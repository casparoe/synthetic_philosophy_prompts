And here is one student's answer, copied exactly: The principle of alternative possiblities — PAP — says you're only morally responsible if you could have done otherwise. Frankfurt's counterexample has Jones deciding whether to lie, and Black waiting in the wings with a device that can force Jones to lie if he shows any sign of backing out. But Jones goes ahead and lies on his own, so Black never steps in. Even though Jones couldn't have done otherwise, he still seems blameworthy, so PAP is false. Then there's the intervention-latency problem. If Black's device needs to detect a neural precursor before it activates, then right before that precursor Jones technically could have done something different — he could have avoided generating the signal. That's a flicker of freedom. But I think Frankfurt handles this with his distinction between robust alternatives and mere flickers. He said that only robust alternatives — where you can actually bring about a different outcome — matter for PAP, and these tiny precursor flickers don't count. So the latency worry doesn't really save PAP, because the micro-alternative isn't the right kind. Actually, wait — if the precursor is determined by prior causes outside Jones's control, then he doesn't even have that flicker. So Frankfurt's case works perfectly: there's no alternative at all, robust or otherwise, and Jones is still responsible. That's why PAP is false, at least in the version that requires robust alternatives, which is the version Frankfurt was always attacking. One mistake people make is mixing this up with Frankfurt's hierarchical model of agency from his later work, where second-order desires identify with first-order ones.
