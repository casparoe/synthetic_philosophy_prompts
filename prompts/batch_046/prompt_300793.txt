hey, gradign papers for my phil of action seminar and need a sanity check. here's the prompt i gave students:

"Explain Galen Strawson's Basic Argument for the impossibility of moral responsibility. Be precise about why determinism is not the core issue, and about the role of being causa sui. Then evaluate whether a libertarian who accepts that choices can be genuinely uncaused can still escape by claiming present-tense ownership of mental states is enough."

one answer (paraphrased, not quoted):

Strawson's Basic Argument starts with the idea that you're only truly responsible for an action if you're truly responsible for the mental nature that led to it. If you weren't responsible for being the kind of person who'd do that, then you can't be blamed or praised. From there he asks how you could be responsible for your mental nature. Either you deliberately shaped yourself, which requires already having a mental nature to do the shaping, or your nature was caused by genetics and environment, which you're not responsible for. So there's a regress or circularity.

The key thing is that determinism is just one version of the problem. Even if quantum mechanics introduces genuine randomness, you're still not the cause of the mental framework that interprets those random events. So standard incompatibilism about determinism misses the deeper point.

Now, can a libertarian escape? I think yes. A view like Kane's says we perform self-forming actions that aren't determined but are still ours. Even if we didn't create our initial nature, we can endorse or reject mental states in the present moment, and that's enough for responsibility. So Strawson sets the bar too high by demanding that we be causa sui in the sense of being self-created from nothing. A libertarian only needs present-tense ownership, not total self-creation. Therefore the argument doesn't show responsibility is impossible, just that it's not absolute in the way Strawson wants.
