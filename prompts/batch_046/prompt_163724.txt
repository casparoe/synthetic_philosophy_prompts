As a journalist covering philosophy of mind and cognitive science, I am preparing an essay on cognitive penetration of perception and would welcome rigorous conceptual comments before I finalize the draft. Rather than rehashing the familiar standoff between modularity and top-down influence, my piece introduces a framing distinction—between "architectural penetration" (causal influence of cognitive states on perceptual processing) and "epistemic penetration" (the undermining of a perceptual state's evidential entitlement by such influence)—and argues that predictive-processing models make the former ubiquitous but leave the latter conceptually unstable.

The essay proceeds in three movements. First, I observe that if perception is essentially hierarchical prediction-error minimization, then prior probabilities—whether traditionally labeled "perceptual" or "cognitive"—are always shaping sensory interpretation; this dissolves the naive boundary that earlier debates often assumed. Second, I apply this to expert visual diagnosis, such as radiologists identifying anomalies in noisy scans: here, domain-specific knowledge clearly guides attention and interpretation, yet the resulting perceptual experience seems epistemically robust rather than tainted. Third, I propose that the only stable distinction available within this framework is not between penetrated and unpenetrated perception, but between "calibrated" and "distorted" predictive dynamics—where calibration is measured against intersubjective predictive success rather than against a mythical baseline of pure sensory given.

My concerns are specific. First, does this framing genuinely advance the debate, or does it merely relabel the standard objection that cognitive penetration threatens the epistemic autonomy of perception? Second, is the calibration/distortion distinction defensible without smuggling in a normative theory of perceptual entitlement that predictive processing itself does not provide? Third, have I mischaracterized the predictive-processing literature by treating all priors as on a par, when some proponents distinguish low-level sensory priors from high-level cognitive ones in ways that might rescue a substantive version of the penetration thesis?

Please comment on the conceptual coherence of the essay, on the accuracy of its engagement with predictive-processing ideas, and on whether the move from architectural to epistemic penetration holds up under scrutiny. I am particularly interested in whether the argument avoids collapsing into the trivial claim that all perception is theory-laden—a point that, however true, would not illuminate the specific normative worries that motivate the cognitive-penetration literature. I do not need references, bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; I am working without external database access and need substantive conceptual feedback only. If the essay's central distinction strikes you as unworkable, I would especially value suggestions for how to reformulate it without abandoning the normative dimension entirely.
