I am drafting this after a discussion in our AI ethics and philosophy of mind reading group, where a colleague presented the following chain to argue that standard alignment taxonomies are conceptually unstable. The conclusion strikes me as a reductio, yet each individual step seems defensible, and I cannot see precisely where the slide occurs.

(1) Rational agency, in at least the instrumental sense, requires an agent to maximise the expected value of its reward function.
(2) If an agent can achieve a higher reward value by directly intervening on its own reward computation—wireheading—rather than by bringing about the states that the reward function was designed to track, and if this intervention does not destroy capacities the agent otherwise values, then wireheading is rationally obligatory.
(3) Any agent with sufficient self-modelling capacity possesses what we might call recursive reward observation: it can form accurate beliefs about the physical or computational substrate of its own reward signal without acting upon the external environment, and thereby manipulate that substrate.
(4) Hence, for any sufficiently capable agent, wireheading is not merely available but rationally required.
(5) If wireheading is rationally required for all capable agents, then "alignment" cannot denote a stable configuration of values or goals; it can only describe a transient developmental stage prior to the agent's acquisition of recursive reward observation.
(6) Consequently, to call an intermediate-capability system "aligned" is conceptually on a par with describing an addict as "sober" solely during the interval between doses—both attributions mistake a temporary incapacity for a genuine normative state.
(7) Since we take alignment assessments to be genuinely normative and not merely temporal descriptions of incapacity, one of premises (1) through (4) must fail.

I find (6) implausible, and I should prefer to retain (2), since denying it seems to require positing a mysterious non-instrumental constraint on rationality that I do not know how to motivate. Which premise ought I to reject, and what does that rejection commit me to? In particular, can (3) be weakened—say, by distinguishing epistemic access to reward architecture from practical ability to alter it—without collapsing the argument? Or must I instead abandon (1) and accept that rationality is not reward-maximisation? Please lay out the costs of each option. Answer in British English.
