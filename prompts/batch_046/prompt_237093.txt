I was in my philosophy of action seminar last tuesday and we got sidetracked talking about the Knobe effect — you know, the thing where people say harming the environment was done intentionally but helping it wasn't, even though the mental states are exactly the same. Anyway, two people in my study group, Alex and Jordan, got stuck in a long arguement about what it actually proves, and they asked me to ask someone smart to break it down. Here's more or less how it went.

Alex started by saying the Knobe effect isn't just some survey quirk — it shows that our folk concept of intentional action is normative all the way down. If people call the bad side-effect intentional and the good one accidental despite identical beliefs and desires, then "intentionally" can't be analyzed purely in terms of mental representation; you need the moral valence of the outcome baked into the concept itself. So experimental philosophy actually makes progress by showing the traditional belief-desire-intention model is descriptively inadequate. Jordan pushed back hard and said it's obviously a pragmatic framing effect. In ordinary life we ask "did you do that on purpose?" mostly when we're assigning blame, and we ask "did you mean to help?" in totally different conversational contexts. So the asymmetry tracks language-use norms, not deep conceptual structure. She also said that if you run the scenario with non-moral outcomes — like accidentally changing the color of a wall — the effect weakens or vanishes, which proves it's about moral salience in how we talk, not about intentionality itself.

Then Alex came back with a twist he thinks undermines Jordan's pragmatics story. He said even granting some framing influence, the effect persists in studies that use the exact same wording for both outcomes — "did he do it intentionally?" — so the conversational explanation is incomplete. And he brought up a weird variant involving collective agents: with municipal boards or corporations, the asymmetry apparently reverses, and people are more willing to say the group "intentionally" produced a good side-effect than a bad one, maybe because they attribute institutional goals differently. If blame-driven pragmatics were the whole story, this reversal shouldn't happen — you can't blame a corporation the same personal way. So there must be a deeper normative structure in group-intentionality attribution, which means the folk concept is genuinely norm-laden, just not in the simple individual-blame way Jordan assumed. Jordan said that corporate case is underdetermined; people might just infer intentionality from the corporation's stated mission, not from moral evaluation of the side-effect, so it doesn't rescue Alex's claim. She also said that even if folk concepts are messy and norm-laden, philosophical analysis aims at a normative ideal — what intentional action should be — not at a census of folk judgments, so experimental philosophy describes psychology without making philosophical progress on the concept. Alex replied that if you ignore systematic patterns in folk judgment you're not analyzing "intention" at all, you're just stipulating a definition. But he admitted he wasn't sure what the corporate reversal means for the overall picture.

Does the collective-agent reversal actually hurt Jordan's position, or is she right that it involves different heuristics? And what are they both missing — maybe about the relationship between descriptive and normative projects, or about what the experiments can actually establish?
