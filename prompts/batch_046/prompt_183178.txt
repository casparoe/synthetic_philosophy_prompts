As a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, I recently encountered a popular technology article summarizing Douglas Hofstadter's discussion of tangled hierarchies and the so-called inviolate level, using Escher's Drawing Hands as the central illustration. The article's account went roughly as follows. Escher's image depicts two hands sketching each other into existence, which the author treats as a direct visual analog of logical self-reference. Hofstadter, according to the piece, named this structure a tangled hierarchy and argued that every layered system—whether a formal language, a biological brain, or a running computer program—contains an inviolate level that the system itself can never fully represent or transcend from within. The article further claimed that Hofstadter derived this constraint directly from Gödel's incompleteness theorems, interpreting them as a general proof that complete self-modeling is impossible in any domain. Finally, it applied the idea to software architecture: a running process can inspect its own memory or source code, but it can never model the interpreter, operating system, or hardware layer that executes it, because that layer remains permanently outside the system—just as the sheet of paper on which Escher's hands are drawn remains outside the image. The conclusion was that this is not merely an analogy but a necessary feature of any hierarchical architecture.

The account feels intuitively compelling to me, especially the computing analogy, but also philosophically shaky—particularly the slide from a visual artwork to a formal proof, and from arithmetic to physical computation. I am unsure which parts are faithful to Hofstadter, which are standard simplifications, and which are missteps. Could you audit this summary? Please separate (a) what is essentially accurate about Hofstadter's view, (b) what is a defensible simplification for a general audience, and (c) what is specifically wrong or overextended—especially regarding whether the inviolate level is a logical, physical, or metaphorical claim; whether Gödel's results apply broadly to software systems in this direct way; and whether Escher's image is best understood as depicting a logical paradox, a recursive loop, or a metaphor for the limits of representation. If any part of the summary commits a category error—say, treating a feature of formal arithmetic as an architectural constraint on running code—please identify it clearly. I am not asking for a bibliography or reading list; I am trying to calibrate whether the software analogy holds up or collapses under scrutiny.
