Design a classroom activity for a 15-person upper-level philosophy seminar (75 minutes) that makes participants feel the force of Plato’s Meno problem—why knowledge is more valuable than true belief—without using the slave-boy example or standard virtue-epistemology framing. Instead, build the session around a “degraded protocol” simulation: pairs receive corrupted navigation instructions pointing to a hidden object in the room; in round one, some pairs happen to decode the correct location (mere true belief) while others understand the underlying error-correction algorithm (knowledge). Both groups find the object and earn participation points, but in round two the corruption pattern shifts unpredictably, so only the algorithm-understanders can adapt. Provide full rules, materials (envelopes, a classroom map, two different corruption keys), the payoff scheme tied to seminar credit, and a structured debrief that maps round one and round two onto true belief versus knowledge, including what to say only after participants have experienced the failure. Predict what will go wrong the first time—likely that students conflate “understanding the protocol” with general intelligence rather than treating it as an epistemic status—and be explicit about which feature of the Meno problem the physical simulation cannot reproduce (probably the intrinsic normative claim that knowledge is better, as opposed to merely being more instrumentally robust). State what would change your mind about whether this design genuinely captures the problem, and identify where your reasoning about the pedagogical mapping is most likely to fail.
