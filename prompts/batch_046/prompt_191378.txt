I've been trying to map the disagreement over when the Anthropocene begins, and the more I read, the more it feels like a stalled debate masquerading as an empirical dispute. On one side are the "Great Acceleration" advocates who point to the mid-twentieth-century spike in radionuclides, plastics, and concrete; on another are those who place the boundary at 1610 with the Columbian Exchange and its biotic homogenization; and on a third are the traditionalists who want 1784 and James Watt's steam engine as the marker of fossil-fuel metabolism. All three camps agree on the underlying physical and historical records—the disagreement is never about whether the Orbis spike happened, but about which signal counts as epochal. At the same time, the International Commission on Stratigraphy has resisted formalizing any of these boundaries, which raises a parallel question about authority: is the resistance scientific, or is it a form of disciplinary boundary-policing that treats human history as outside geology's proper domain?

My working hypothesis—shaped by reading some institutional-analysis work—is that this is not primarily a factual disagreement but a conflict over what an epoch-boundary explanation must do. Geologists traditionally require a Global Boundary Stratotype Section and Point, a physically traceable marker that operates independently of human intention; yet every Anthropocene proposal is selected precisely because it tracks human intentionality—whether that's carbon combustion, colonial botany, or nuclear testing. So the debate may be verbal at the level of "Anthropocene" (are we describing a rock layer or diagnosing a civilizational trajectory?) and substantive at the level of evidentiary standards (does a boundary need to be visible in every sediment core, or only in the aggregate climate record?). There's also the "who decides" dimension: climate scientists, historians, and stratigraphers are using the same word but answering different questions—one about Earth-system state change, one about moral responsibility, one about taxonomic legitimacy. I want a diagnosis of the stall itself: is it that the question is ill-posed because it asks geology to do ethical work, or that we lack a conceptual distinction—perhaps something like "chronological sovereignty" versus "stratigraphic description"—that could separate the geological claim from the historical-normative claim? And what would actually count as progress—would it be a formal result showing that no single GSSP can capture a distributed, agent-driven transition, or an institutional redesign that splits naming authority between geologists and historians? Please answer in American English, don't survey the positions for their own sake, and tell me what kind of disagreement this really is and whether anything short of redefining the practice of stratigraphic naming could resolve it.
