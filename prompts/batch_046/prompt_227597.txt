I would like your careful adjudication of a disagreement that arose during a recent graduate seminar I led on moral luck and responsibility. Two advanced students—whom I will call Dr. Reeves and Dr. Sato—debated the following case and its conceptual implications.

Consider two corporate executives, A and B, who each approve an identical supply-chain contract without investigating labor conditions. Both rely on the same audit report, share the same cognitive bias (over-trust in third-party verification), and have the same accessible reasons to investigate further but fail to do so. Unbeknownst to either, one contract binds a factory with hidden forced labor, leading to severe harm once discovered; the other binds a factory with only poor working conditions, leading to moderate harm. The divergence is purely a matter of hidden structural conditions, not of any difference in deliberation, foresight, or will.

Dr. Reeves argued that this exemplifies resultant moral luck in its purest form. Because A and B exercised identical control over their decisions and possessed identical mental states, they are equally blameworthy for their negligence. The greater harm caused by A is, Reeves insisted, entirely outside her control, and therefore cannot retroactively worsen her moral character or augment her internal culpability. To think otherwise, Reeves claimed, is to confuse moral assessment with consequentialist accounting.

Dr. Sato responded that Reeves operates with an unduly atomistic conception of moral agency. Even bracketing outcomes, Sato argued, moral identity is partly constituted by one's actual causal relations to others. A stands in a different relational position: the victims' justified resentment is directed at her specifically, the community's practices of accountability must address her specifically, and her own retrospective self-understanding is shaped by having caused severe rather than moderate harm. Sato therefore proposed that we distinguish sharply between internal blameworthiness (which is indeed equal) and external accountability or moral standing (which differs). A bears a heavier burden of moral repair and occupies a different position in the moral community, even if her will was no worse.

Reeves countered that this distinction collapses upon inspection. If accountability practices are not merely instrumental but partly constitutive of what blameworthiness means in a social world, then separating the internal from the relational is an abstraction that misrepresents moral life. Moreover, Reeves added, Sato's view risks making moral status depend on fortune in precisely the way the control principle was meant to forbid: A's moral identity becomes hostage to hidden structural conditions she never chose.

Sato's final reply was that Reeves begs the question by assuming the control principle applies to moral identity in the same way it applies to choice. Perhaps, Sato suggested, we are responsible not only for what we will, but for who we become in relation to others—including relations formed through actions whose outcomes we do not fully control. Reeves answered that this is merely to rename the problem rather than solve it, and that if we accept Sato's view, we must abandon the idea that moral equality has any substantive content.

Please adjudicate this exchange step by step. For each move—Reeves's appeal to the control principle, Sato's relational-constitution claim, Reeves's charge of collapse, and Sato's final defense—indicate whether the argument succeeds on its own terms, where it relies on an unstated premise, and whether either party conflates distinct concepts (for instance, culpability, accountability, and moral worth). Then state who is more right overall, and identify what both parties appear to miss—particularly regarding whether their framework can accommodate cross-contextual moral variance, that is, cases in which the same agent, through identical decisions, produces both severe and moderate harms in different contexts, and whether such variance undermines or supports the distinction between internal and relational responsibility.
