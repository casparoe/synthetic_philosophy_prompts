I work as a software engineer focusing on AI alignment and formal verification, and recently I have been thinking about the limits of formalizing ethical constraints in autonomous systems. In particular, I have been studying Gödel's first and second incompleteness theorems and their standard interpretations beyond pure arithmetic. My working assumption is that any formal system expressive enough to encode basic arithmetic—including, I suppose, a constitutional framework for multi-agent AI or a formal specification of reward functions—must contain statements that are true within the system's language but unprovable by the system itself. Extending this idea, I have been treating normative domains analogously: if we formalize human ethical constraints, there ought to be true moral propositions that lie permanently outside the reach of that formalization.

This leads me to an architectural question I have not been able to resolve. If Gödel's results genuinely apply to ethical formalizations in this direct way, then any sufficiently expressive value-alignment framework must be either incomplete—failing to capture all true moral facts—or inconsistent should it attempt to prove them. Given this, should we deliberately design AI systems that lack full self-reference regarding their own constitutional rules or reward specifications, thereby avoiding the Gödelian trap? Or should we accept that there will always be Gödelian moral truths inaccessible to formal derivation and instead build in some non-formal, intuitive oversight mechanism to handle those gaps? I have encountered references to the Löbian obstacle and reflective agent architectures in this context, but I am unclear whether Gödel's theorems actually license these design conclusions for normative rather than mathematical domains.

Could you help me untangle this? Specifically, I want to know whether the analogy between arithmetic incompleteness and formal ethics is sound, whether there really are unprovable true normative statements in any constitutional AI framework, and what—if anything—this should change about how we architect alignment systems in practice. Should we prioritize escaping self-reference, accommodating inevitable formal gaps, or is the entire line of reasoning a category error?
