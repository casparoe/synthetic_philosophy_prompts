I've recently found myself defending a specific reading of Nāgārjuna's Madhyamaka: namely, that the catuṣkoṭi (tetralemma) is not merely a dialectical device for dismantling opponents' metaphysical commitments, but constitutes the complete logical architecture of śūnyatā itself. On this view, emptiness does not point to some hidden, ineffable reality behind appearance; rather, it is exactly the fourfold negation—things are neither existent, nor non-existent, nor both, nor neither—and this negation is not a provisional step toward synthesis but the final descriptive account of conventional existence. I take this to avoid both eternalism and nihilism not by transcending logic but by exhausting its available positions, and I think it predicts that any attempt to formalize Madhyamaka in standard bivalent logic will necessarily misrepresent the view.

What would genuinely count against this interpretation? I want the defeaters sorted by severity, not just the familiar charge that it collapses into nihilism. For instance: if textual or philosophical evidence showed that Nāgārjuna intended the tetralemma purely as a soteriological exercise without metaphysical commitment, would that be fatal, or could the logical-architectural reading absorb it? What about formal results—say, if paraconsistent logic demonstrates that the fourfold structure can be rendered consistent without requiring emptiness, or conversely, if it collapses into logical triviality? Would comparative-philosophy results matter, such as showing that later Madhyamaka thinkers like Candrakīrti or Śāntarakṣita explicitly reject the tetralemma as a metaphysical framework in favor of purely pragmatic deconstruction? I also want an honest assessment of whether defenders of this reading have insulated it by invoking the emptiness of emptiness—so that even the fourfold negation must itself be emptied—rendering it immune to any counterexample, and whether that insulation is philosophically legitimate or makes the view unfalsifiable. Which of these defeaters would a committed proponent concede as damaging, and which would they dismiss as category errors arising from applying the wrong logical framework?

Finally, are there predictions this reading makes about, say, the possibility of conventional truth, the status of causal relations, or the limits of conceptual thought, that rival interpretations—whether anti-realist, therapeutic, or mystical—do not share, and that therefore expose it to distinctive risk? I want to know whether this position actually risks anything, or whether it has been qualified into a view that no observation or argument could touch, and whether that is a problem for a metaphysical claim of this kind.
