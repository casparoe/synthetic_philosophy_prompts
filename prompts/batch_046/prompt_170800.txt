I am a clinical ethics fellow who, only last month, sat with a couple carrying a late-onset hereditary neurodegenerative mutation as they decided whether to conceive using preimplantation genetic diagnosis; since that consultation, the procreation asymmetry has become personally urgent rather than an abstract classroom puzzle. Next week I must lead a single fifty-minute session for first-year medical students—none with prior philosophy training—on precisely this asymmetry: why it can be wrong to bring a suffering person into existence, yet not wrong in any parallel sense to fail to bring a flourishing person into existence, even when the welfare probabilities are symmetrical. I do not want the explanation itself; I want the architecture of an explanation. First, what must come before everything else—should I open with the intuition that harming an existing child has a victim while failing to create a happy one does not, or with the formal distinction between person-affecting and impersonal reasons—and then in what exact dependency order should the remaining conceptual pieces be introduced? Second, I need one carrying example that can sustain the full argument: a concrete reproductive-choice scenario, possibly the genetic-testing case itself or something structurally analogous, and I want to know what properties it must possess and what it must avoid; in particular, should I reject the familiar deaf-child versus hearing-child framing, and if so, why is it the wrong vehicle? Third, map the misconceptions these students will almost certainly generate: I expect they will reduce the asymmetry to the banal claim that causing suffering is always bad without seeing why the benefit side lacks a structural counterpart, and that they will imagine the argument requires weighing actual people against non-existent ones. For each misconception, specify the exact point in the sequence where it should be intercepted and the precise move that defuses it. Fourth, provide one diagnostic question for the end of the session that would reveal whether they have grasped the asymmetry itself rather than memorized its slogan. Finally, state explicitly what must be left out at this level—perhaps the non-identity problem, modal realism about possible people, or the full technical axiological formulation of the asymmetry—and say why each omission protects the lesson rather than impoverishing it.
