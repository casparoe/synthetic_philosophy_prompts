I chair an interdisciplinary grant-review committee, and we have begun receiving proposals that build in deliberately burdensome methodological conditions—mandatory multi-site replication, expensive open-data infrastructures, or physically arduous data-collection protocols—apparently intended to signal serious commitment rather than to serve direct scientific needs. Please translate the handicap principle into an operational decision procedure my committee can apply repeatedly: list the ordered questions to ask, specify what counts as a sufficient answer at each stage, identify the points where the procedure stops with rejection or approval, and flag the points where judgment must replace calculation. The procedure should distinguish credible costly signals of rigor, where the cost is borne differentially by high-quality proposals, from wasteful spectacle or performative handicap. Then apply it to two proposals: (a) a psychology study requiring every participant to complete a six-hour in-person cognitive battery with no remote option, justified solely as an investigator-commitment signal; and (b) a climate-science study allocating 40 percent of its budget to redundant real-time sensor verification, justified as a costly anti-fabrication signal. Present the procedure in numbered steps, apply each case in one paragraph, and keep the entire response within 400 words.
