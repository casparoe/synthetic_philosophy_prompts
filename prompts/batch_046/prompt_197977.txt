I'm looking for the established term, if any, for a specific tension I keep noticing between Buddhist anatta and Western accounts of personal identity. The familiar linear story is that mental states form a causal chain, and psychological continuity explains why I prudently care for my future states rather than treating them as strangers. But if you actually examine how mental states arise—say, the decision to sit in meditation emerges simultaneously from bodily discomfort, a remembered instruction, and ambient noise—the causal ancestry looks more like a branching web than a single thread. Meanwhile, ordinary practical life still treats the future as singular and "mine": I save for retirement and feel remorse for past acts, not distributed sympathy across causal branches. So the metaphysics is distributed and non-linear, yet the normative stance remains unitary and directed. People call nearby things "the bundle problem" or "narrative self-construction," but neither captures this precise mismatch between network-like causation and linear practical identification. Does this configuration have a name in Abhidharma psychology, in philosophy of mind, or in the personal-identity literature? If not, what are its nearest neighbors, and where exactly does it diverge from them?
