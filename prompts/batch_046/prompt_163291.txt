Question: In Carroll's dialogue, the Tortoise accepts premises A and B, accepts the logical principle Z (if A and B are true, then Z is true), yet refuses to accept Z. Achilles then adds Z to the premises, but the Tortoise demands a further principle Z1 linking A, B, and Z to Z, and so on. Explain the philosophical problem about deductive justification that Carroll is dramatizing. Then evaluate the response that distinguishes, in modern proof-theoretic terms, between premises in the object language and rules of inference that license structural moves: does this distinction genuinely block the regress, or does the Tortoise's challenge reappear at the meta-level?

Student answer: The dialogue dramatizes the problem of rule-circularity. Every deductive step requires a logical rule to license it, but if that rule must be added as an explicit premise, then applying it requires yet another rule, generating an infinite regress. Carroll's point is that deduction can never be fully justified, since there is always an ungrounded step remaining. Achilles represents the formalist strategy of making every rule explicit inside the argument, but the tortoise shows this strategy collapses into regress. The modern distinction between derivation rules and premises does stop the regress formally, because rules are structural and are not the kind of thing that need premises to be applied. Yet this only pushes the problem up one level: at the meta-level we still need justification for adopting those particular rules rather than others. Therefore Carroll's dialogue ultimately demonstrates that all logical reasoning rests on norms that cannot themselves be logically justified. The tortoise is essentially advancing an intuitionist critique of formalism, insisting that reasoning must be grounded in direct rational insight rather than in mechanical rule-following. The analogy with Hume's problem of induction is apt here—both show that a fundamental mode of reasoning lacks a non-circular foundation—though Carroll applies it specifically to logical form rather than to empirical generalization.
