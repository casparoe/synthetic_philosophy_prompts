I'm drafting a proposal for a philosophy workshop on conceptual problems at the intersection of historical consciousness and personal identity. My half-formed idea: Nietzsche's tripartite distinction among monumental, antiquarian, and critical history in his essay on the uses and disadvantages of history for life is almost always interpreted as a taxonomy of collective historiography, but applying it rigorously to first-person autobiographical memory exposes a structural gap. Critical history, as Nietzsche describes it, requires dissolving the past's authority to serve present vitality; yet standard narrative-identity theories require some continuity of the past self for moral agency. This suggests there might be an unrecognized intermediate mode—call it therapeutic dissolution—that severs harmful autobiographical continuities without the total destructive force of critical history or the preservative burden of the antiquarian mode. I need the proposal to state the precise conceptual question, show why existing readings of Nietzsche's framework leave the first-person application open, outline a comparative method (mapping Nietzsche's criteria for life-promoting history onto current conditions for narrative identity), specify what success would look like (a principled distinction between destructive and therapeutic dissolution that avoids metaphysical commitments about the self that Nietzsche rejects) and what failure would look like (if the distinction collapses into one of the three standard modes or requires empirical psychology beyond conceptual analysis), and keep the scope realistic for a single workshop paper.
