As a doctoral student in philosophy of history assisting with course design, I am drafting the final examination for an upper-level MA seminar on perpetrator studies. The course has centered on Christopher Browning’s analysis of Reserve Police Battalion 101 and Daniel Goldhagen’s argument about eliminationist antisemitism among ordinary Germans, but in my discussions with fellow students I have noticed that they consistently reduce the debate to a false binary—situation versus culture—that can be answered by picking one author and summarizing their evidence. I need an essay question that makes this recitation impossible. Specifically, the prompt should embed a conceptual problem of explanatory overdetermination: whether situational mechanisms such as peer pressure, bureaucratic distancing, and careerist calculation operate at a different analytical level—individual psychology and institutional structure—than dispositional mechanisms such as ideologically saturated worldviews and affective hatred, and whether recognizing both requires abandoning the claim that one explanation must be primary. To prevent students from retreating to familiar summaries, the question should present a counterfactual analytical tool rather than an empirical prediction: ask them to imagine an identically structured reserve police unit composed of conscripts from a society lacking any deep tradition of antisemitic ideology, deployed in the same occupied region with the same bureaucratic duties in 1942, and to use this scenario not to prove either Browning or Goldhagen correct, but to articulate precisely what kind of explanatory claim each author is making—causal, constitutive, or normative—and whether the counterfactual undermines one claim, both, or neither. The exam is intended for students who can write for 120 minutes, and the question should carry 40 marks. Please provide the essay question itself worded directly for the students, followed by a model answer of approximately 800 to 1000 words that demonstrates the specific conceptual moves a top answer must make, and a marking scheme that allocates the 40 marks across five must-hit criteria. For each criterion, include the point value and a one-line note on the typical error that costs marks, such as treating the counterfactual as an empirical forecast rather than an analytical device, or conflating causal explanation with moral exculpation. The model answer should avoid inventing archival specifics and should show how a philosophically sophisticated response can restructure the debate without relying on obscure historiographical references.
