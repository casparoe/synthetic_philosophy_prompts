I have spent thirty years calibrating physical instruments, and since retiring I have been trying to apply the same discipline to language. The argument below seems to prove that Japanese indirectness and Dutch directness are ethically on a par with white lies, which I find implausible, yet I cannot locate the exit. (1) A speech act is ethically defective in proportion to the divergence it introduces between the speaker's private cognitive state and the informational state it produces in the hearer—call this the calibration gap. (2) White lies open a large calibration gap by substituting false or diluted content for the speaker's actual belief. (3) Japanese indirect refusal—stating that a request might be somewhat difficult when the speaker means an absolute refusal—opens an equally large gap: the literal propositional content is false relative to the speaker's intention, and correct uptake requires the hearer to apply a high-context decoding filter that the speaker knows may misfire. (4) Dutch directness—stripping away the hedges and face-preserving attenuation expected in many other cultures—opens a gap of opposite sign: the hearer, calibrated for buffered signal, decodes hostility or coldness exceeding the speaker's actual emotional state. (5) Therefore both norms, like white lies, systematically decouple transmission from the speaker's unmediated mental state. (6) But treating both dominant communicative systems as systematically immoral is absurd. (7) Hence the calibration principle in (1) must fail, and white lies are not wrong merely because they create divergence. I want to keep (1) if possible, and I also want to preserve (3) and (4) as accurate descriptions of cultural encoding. Which step should be rejected? Is the flaw an equivocation in "calibration gap" between deceptive falsehood and conventionally expected signal modulation? Or does (5) illegitimately aggregate opposite-signed gaps into a single moral failure? What does abandoning (1) cost me if I still wish to distinguish deception from directness?
