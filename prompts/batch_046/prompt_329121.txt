I'm drafting a dissertation proposal in experimental philosophy focused on what the Knobe effect reveals about the concept of intentional action. The standard asymmetry—where a harmful side effect of an action is typically judged intentional while an otherwise identical beneficial side effect is not—has been interpreted as showing that moral evaluation shapes intentionality judgments, as an artifact of how vignettes frame normative outcomes, and, in some recent work, as a special case of broader causal-attribution patterns.

A less developed but increasingly relevant line distinguishes this "side-effect asymmetry" from a proposed "means-end asymmetry": the question of whether harmful means adopted to achieve a valued end are judged intentional in ways that beneficial means are not, and whether this pattern diverges from the side-effect case in ways that bear directly on theories of moral responsibility. Meanwhile, methodological debates persist over whether the effect survives when outcome descriptions are stripped of normative language, and over whether the phenomenon is robust across non-Western linguistic communities or non-verbal task paradigms.
