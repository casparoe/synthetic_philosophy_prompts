I was talking with friends last weekend about whether it is wrong to pay someone to stand in line for you, or to hire a “professional mourner” for a funeral, and we kept running into the same shape of argument. Someone says that putting a price on a thing does not merely motivate people differently; it turns the thing into something else—waiting in line becomes labor rather than shared inconvenience, grief becomes a service rather than a bond. Then someone answers that money is only a tool, and you can always add rules: you may pay for line-standing but not resell the spot; you may hire a mourner only if she never knew the deceased; you may trade carbon offsets for personal moral failings but only up to a fixed cap. Each rule sounds reasonable alone, yet together they let the price mechanism swallow the domain except for a shrinking list of protected zones, and eventually those zones feel like special product categories—“premium grief support,” “ethical queue-access,” “certified non-commercial adoption brokerage.” I have noticed this same pattern in debates about paying college athletes, about apps that sell priority access to public parks, and about markets for organ donation with “altruistic” framing requirements.

I have no formal background in philosophy or economics, though I have been reading older discussions of exchange and money on my own, and I wonder whether the pattern I am describing—this gradual expansion through individually reasonable exceptions that do not block commodification but actually formalize it—has an established name, or whether it connects to a specific distinction about natural and unnatural exchange. I am not looking for a bibliography; I want to know whether thinkers have already carved out this exact move, where boundary rules become part of the pricing structure rather than external limits on it. If a concept exists, I want to understand how it differs from ordinary “market creep” or from the simple claim that money corrupts values, because in my examples the corruption is not happening in spite of the rules—the rules are what enable it. If there is no precise term, I want the nearest concepts, how each falls short, and where the edges of my pattern really lie: does it require that the exceptions themselves become marketized, or does it apply even when the protected zones remain genuinely outside the exchange? I care about the carving—what this is, what it is not, and where its boundaries are—far more than I care about a bare label.
