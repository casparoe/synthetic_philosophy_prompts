In my graduate seminar on philosophy of mind we have been tracking an impasse that has persisted for roughly two decades in the literature on layered representational architectures, though it rarely receives sustained attention outside a small cluster of cognitive philosophers. The dispute concerns what I will call the careenium—the postulated intermediate representational stratum that mediates between low-level neural dynamics and high-level cognitive processing—and within it, the so-called simmballs: discrete, structurally atomic symbolic tokens that carry semantic content. One camp, which I will call the substrate-pushers, maintains that when a simmball is activated within the careenium it exerts genuine downward causal influence on the physical substrate: the symbol literally pushes the neurons around, altering firing patterns in a manner not reducible to the substrate’s own dynamics. The opposing camp, the shadow-theorists, grants that simmballs are real patterns in the careenium, but insists they are causally inert epiphenomena; all causal work is done bottom-up, and attributing efficacy to the symbols is merely a useful fiction of our explanatory practices.

What makes the stall so frustrating is that both sides accept the same neurocomputational data, the same formal models of the careenium, and the same criterion of predictive adequacy. They agree on every empirical fact I can think of, and yet neither has budged on the core claim. My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is not empirical at all, but rather turns on a hidden conceptual divergence about what downward causation requires in a layered system—perhaps one side demands that the symbol function as an irreducible causal node, while the other treats any reducibility to substrate dynamics as a defeat for symbol efficacy. But I am not sure whether this is correct, or whether the dispute is verbal (they are using “causation” in different technical registers), or whether they are actually answering different questions—one about ontology, the other about explanatory strategy—under the single heading of “do symbols push the substrate around?”

So I want a diagnosis, not a survey and certainly not a bibliography. Is this a substantive disagreement disguised as empirical, a verbal dispute about the word “causation,” or a case of two research programs answering different questions under one name? Given your diagnosis, is there any conceptual distinction, formal result, or shift in framing that could genuinely move the debate, or should we conclude that the impasse is permanent because the question is ill-posed? I want you to engage concretely with what each side actually says about the careenium and the causal role of simmballs, and I want an honest answer about whether progress is even the right expectation here.
