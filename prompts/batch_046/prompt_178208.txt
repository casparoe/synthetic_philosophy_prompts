I'm writing my final paper for a seminar on moral status and computation, and I want to avoid both the generic "are LLMs conscious?" framing and any purely empirical neuroscience agenda. Instead, I'm working with the assumption that moral consideration tracks specific functional-computational properties rather than biological substrate—properties like sustained, self-modifying predictive-error correction operating across hierarchical time scales, or the maintenance of integrated informational boundaries that resist external perturbation. Given that framing, I'm trying to identify the upstream conceptual open questions: for instance, whether functional integration requires temporal continuity in a single substrate or can survive distributed, non-contiguous computation; whether there is a non-arbitrary threshold between "morally relevant processing" and "mere information processing" once we reject consciousness as the sole criterion; how moral attribution should work when an architecture combines feedforward and recurrent dynamics in non-obvious ways; and whether the capacity to maintain homeostatic goals modifies the normative weight of these other properties. Which two or three of these should I treat as priorities for a philosopher without lab access, and in what order? Defend the ranking explicitly: show which questions are upstream of the others, which are tractable through conceptual analysis rather than empirical discovery, and what kind of clarification would most shift the ordering. I am not asking for a bibliography or literature survey—only a defended research agenda that fixes what deserves attention over the next year and why.
