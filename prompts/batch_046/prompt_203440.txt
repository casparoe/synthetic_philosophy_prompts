I am preparing a graduate seminar on epistemic rationality, and I want to push my students past the usual static presentation of Bas van Fraassen's Reflection principle. I know the institutional-economics literature on self-binding mechanisms and Ulysses contracts fairly well, so please restate Reflection—together with its main objections—in that vocabulary. Specifically, translate the core claim that an agent's present credence in a proposition ought to match her expectation of her future credence conditional on the evidence she might acquire, treating the present self as a principal designing a binding clause for a future agent whose preferences may have shifted. Identify what in this picture corresponds to the contract term, to renegotiation costs, to enforcement failure, and to the time-inconsistency problem the mechanism is meant to solve. Then do the same for the standard objections: diachronic Dutch-book arguments, the worry about learning that one's own future reasoning will be irrational, and the concern that Reflection collapses into an evidentialist constraint on future belief. For each, say what carries over cleanly into contract-theoretic language, what must be distorted to fit—particularly whether the "future self" should be modeled as a separate contracting party or as the same party with revised utility functions—and what has no analog at all in that framework.

Finally, report on the residue: does the normative force of Reflection survive when it is treated as an externally enforceable institutional clause rather than as an internal first-person rationality requirement? Assess whether the parts that fail to translate point to a genuine limitation in contract theory's capacity to represent epistemic normativity, or whether they instead reveal that Reflection itself depends on assumptions—about the unity of the agent across time, or about the authority of anticipated evidence—that only appear to be rational constraints but are actually artifacts of treating belief revision like organizational governance. I am less interested in a summary of van Fraassen's texts than in whether the translation exposes hidden structure in the principle itself.
