Consider the cluster of interpretive and philosophical problems surrounding Rousseau’s argument against theatrical spectacle in his Letter to d’Alembert on Spectacles and its uneasy relationship to his broader political and autobiographical writings. Rather than asking for a survey or a bibliography, I want a defended agenda: among the unresolved tensions below, which two or three open questions or lines of work are actually worth sustained philosophical attention, in what order of priority, and for what kind of payoff?

The tensions I have in mind are these, but treat them as candidates rather than obligations:

First, the consistency problem: Rousseau condemns theater for cultivating amour-propre through artificial representation and passive spectatorship, yet he constructs the Confessions as a highly staged, rhetorical self-display meant to win public judgment. Is this tension best read as a genuine contradiction in his thought, a strategic exception justified by the confessional genre’s moral purpose, or as evidence that “representation” operates differently in theatrical and autobiographical contexts?

Second, the civic-participation problem: Rousseau’s anti-theater argument is anchored in Geneva’s specific social structure, where he claims direct civic participation is still possible. Does his critique depend on an empirical, historically local claim about Geneva, or does it rest on a conceptual claim that any representational medium necessarily substitutes spectacle for action? The answer determines whether his argument has any traction outside the small-republic context.

Third, the gendered-foundation problem: Rousseau’s ideal of civic virtue is explicitly masculine and embedded in Geneva’s patriarchal civic order. If we reject that gendered foundation, does the core of his anti-theater argument collapse, or can a non-gendered version survive as a critique of spectacle and mediated emotion? How much interpretive work is required to separate the two?

Fourth, the extension-to-modern-media problem: Some interpreters apply Rousseau’s critique to modern mass media, algorithmic recommendation systems, or immersive digital environments. Is this extension philosophically legitimate, or does it smuggle in anachronistic assumptions about scale, interactivity, and the absence of collective ritual?
