I'm grading a take-home exam for my advisor's philosophy of mind seminar, where I'm serving as a PhD student TA. The exam asked: Explain Dennett's rejection of the Cartesian theater, describe how the multiple drafts model replaces it, and clarify whether this eliminates all unified conscious presentation or merely redistributes it. In doing so, distinguish fame in the brain from stardom in the brain, and discuss whether Dennett's narrative self is causally efficacious or merely a fiction without causal power.

Here is one student's answer: The Cartesian theater is Descartes's idea, tied to the pineal gland, of a central location where sensory information converges into one unified stream for an inner observer. Dennett rejects this and offers the multiple drafts model: parallel processes generate competing narratives, and consciousness is just the version that achieves widespread influence—fame in the brain—rather than being spotlighted in a privileged location, stardom in the brain. There is therefore no canonical story, only temporary coalitions. Dennett treats the self as a center of narrative gravity, but this is purely a useful fiction with no causal powers, like a character in a novel. Consequently, the model eliminates unified presentation entirely and replaces it with functional fragmentation: drafts may influence one another, but there is no real coherence in consciousness.
