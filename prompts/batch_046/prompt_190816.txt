Here are my verdicts on when it is acceptable to invoke something like "national character" or collective temperament, and I want to know whether they fit a single principle or just reflect inconsistent biases. I think it is clearly wrong to explain one German colleague's tidy desk by saying "Germans are orderly." I think it is somewhat defensible, though still risky, to say "Japanese business culture tends to prioritize consensus" when explaining why a particular merger moved slowly, provided you immediately ground the claim in institutional history rather than psychology. I think it is perfectly acceptable to note that "the Icelandic literary tradition preserved more medieval sagas per capita than other Nordic regions," since that is a statistical claim with identifiable causal mechanisms. But I think it is wrong, even playfully, to say "Italians are emotional" about one emotional Italian friend, because the generalization is being applied to an individual in a way that doesn't invite revision. Meanwhile, I think it is genuinely informative—not offensive—to say "this neighborhood has a culture of not calling the police" when the claim is about locally sustained norms with specific incentive structures, not about ethnicity. Is there one principle that captures this pattern—perhaps something about whether the claim is reversible, causal rather than essentializing, or offered at the right level of aggregation? Or do these verdicts collapse into "acceptable when I sympathize with the group, unacceptable when I don't"? Please state any candidate principle precisely, say which of my verdicts it saves and which it must overturn, predict its verdict on a case I have not listed (for example, attributing high Finnish standardized-test scores to a pervasive national temperament of perseverance rather than to teacher-training policy), and answer in American English.
