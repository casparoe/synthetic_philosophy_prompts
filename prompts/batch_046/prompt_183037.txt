Consider the following notes for an essay tentatively titled "Virtue as Calibration: Reconceiving Character in Light of the Situationist Critique." The central thesis is that the situationist challenge targets not virtue ethics as such, but a specific "dispositional essentialism" that identifies virtues with stable, cross-situationally uniform behavioral tendencies. In place of this model, I propose understanding virtue as "calibration competence": not a fixed pattern of conduct, but a reliable capacity to align one's affective, attentional, and practical responses with the normative demands of a particular context. On this account, high behavioral variability across situations is compatible with—indeed, sometimes indicative of—virtuous character, provided the variability tracks genuine differences in situational normativity rather than arbitrary environmental influence. The notes then outline three internal tensions. First, whether calibration competence merely renames practical wisdom (phronesis) and therefore offers no genuine theoretical advance over standard Aristotelian resources. Second, whether the proposal over-intellectualizes virtue, sacrificing the motivational and emotional integration that virtue ethicists regard as essential. Third, whether calibration competence can be observationally distinguished from mere social adaptability or impression management, and whether this threatens to make the concept empirically unfalsifiable. I would welcome critical comments on the following: whether this reconceptualization genuinely engages the situationist empirical literature or merely relocates the problem; whether the distinction between stability of calibration and stability of behavior can be maintained without circularity; and whether insulating virtue ethics from cross-situational behavioral predictions in this way is a legitimate theoretical maneuver or an ad hoc defense that changes the subject. I am especially interested in whether there is a decisive objection showing that the calibration model either fails to preserve the distinctiveness of virtue ethics or succeeds only at the cost of rendering the view empirically empty.
