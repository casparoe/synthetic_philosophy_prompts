After a long and somewhat heated argument with a friend about whether technology will inevitably outlast its creators, I keep returning to von Neumann’s idea of the universal constructor. My friend insists that because a self-replicating machine can copy both its physical form and its instruction set, technological survival is essentially guaranteed once such a system exists—errors can be corrected by redundancy, and evolutionary selection takes care of the rest. I think this misses a deeper conceptual problem internal to von Neumann’s own framework. Could you explain, in precise conceptual terms, the tension between syntactic replication and semantic preservation in that model? Specifically, I want to understand how von Neumann’s separation between the universal constructor (the mechanism that builds) and the description tape (the instructions it reads) bears on whether a descendant system counts as the “same” technology or as something functionally new. In particular, please address whether the architecture implicitly requires an external, invariant reference frame—such as a stable environment or an uncorrupted master copy—to prevent what might be called semantic drift: the gradual reinterpretation of functional specifications across generations, in which each constructor reads not exactly the same tape but a version whose operational meaning has shifted with its context. Does von Neumann’s framework contain the resources for self-correction of such drift without appealing to an outside designer or maintainer, or does it instead show that true technological “survival” in the sense of preserving original purpose is impossible without continued human involvement? I am not asking for a bibliography, page references, or citations; I want a conceptual analysis that treats the constructor, the tape, and the evolutionary mutation mechanism as interacting parts of a single argument about autonomy, continuity, and the limits of self-replication.
