Please explain the conceptual tension that arises when the scale argument for insect sentience is combined with a threshold-based rule for moral patienthood. On the standard formulation, the argument observes that although the probability that any individual insect is sentient may be very low—perhaps well below one percent—the astronomical number of insects alive at any moment means that the aggregate expected moral weight of insect welfare could exceed that of all vertebrates combined. Now introduce the following constraint: rather than aggregating expected value continuously, our moral framework assigns status only to beings for whom our credence in sentience exceeds a specific minimum threshold (say, five percent), and this threshold applies per-capita rather than to populations as collective units. Under this rule, insects might remain below the threshold regardless of how many exist, which appears to neutralize the scale argument entirely. Your task is to analyze whether the scale argument genuinely conflicts with such threshold-based patienthood, or whether the two can be reconciled—for example, by treating a large aggregate population as a single distributed moral unit, by arguing that epistemic humility should erode our confidence in the threshold boundary itself, or by distinguishing between aggregation at the level of expected suffering and aggregation at the level of status assignment. Be precise about where in the reasoning the scaling factor operates, and address whether the argument survives if we replace point-estimate probabilities with broad confidence intervals that overlap substantially with the threshold.
