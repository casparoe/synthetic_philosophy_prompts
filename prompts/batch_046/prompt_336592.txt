Consider labor-market regulations—commonly called right-to-disconnect statutes in several jurisdictions—that penalize employers for systematically contacting employees outside contracted working hours, even when the employee retains formal freedom to ignore the message and faces no explicit penalty for non-response. The practice targets not direct coercion but the structural expectation of perpetual availability.

What conception of liberty—positive, negative, or republican—does this regulatory choice presuppose? Can a purely negative-liberty framework, which treats unfreedom solely as actual interference or credible threat, justify penalizing communications that the recipient is technically at liberty to disregard? Or does the statute implicitly invoke republican freedom, treating the employer's standing power to intrude as domination regardless of whether it is exercised? Might it instead rest on a positive-liberty claim about the effective conditions for self-determination—securing the practical capacity to plan leisure and family life—or does that framing collapse into an impermissible paternalism about how workers should use their autonomy? And is this approach philosophically consistent with contract-law doctrines in adjacent domains, where the formal presence of an option to refuse is typically treated as sufficient for voluntary consent?
