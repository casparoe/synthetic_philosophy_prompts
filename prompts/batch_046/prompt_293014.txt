I've been in this transitional justice reading group for like two years and we're stuck on the same loop about amnesty-for-truth bargains, especially comparing South Africa's TRC with Argentina's CONADEP and the Rwandan gacaca courts. We all agree on the basic historical record—who recieved amnesty, roughly how many perpetrators testified, what the final reports said about systemic violence versus individual acts. The pro-amnesty folks keep saying democratic consolidation wouldn't have held without trading prosecution for disclosure; the other side keeps arguing the "truth" we got was just strategic disclosure shaped by what perpitrators needed to say to avoid jail, so it actually buried the structural stuff rather than exposing it. We've read the same post-commission surveys, we've hashed out the moral arguments about retribution versus stability, and literally nobody's moved an inch.

So I'm trying to figure out what kind of stall this actually is. Is there a hidden empirical disagreement doing all the work—like, do we actually know whether strategic testimony produces reliable knowledge about institutional harm, or are we both just assuming different things about how memory and incentive interact? Or is it a verbal disagreement about what "reconciliation" even means, where one side measures it by stable institutions and the other measures it by some kind of moral repair that amnesty makes impossible? Or are we just hitting bedrock intuitions about whether punishment is non-negotiable after atrocity, and all the talk about democratic consolidation is post-hoc dressing? Given whatever diagnosis you think fits, what would actually count as progress here—a specific kind of study, a conceptual distinction that splits the question, or an admission that no argument should be expected to move anyone? I don't want a winner; I want to know what kind of disagreement we're actually having and whether there's any move that isn't just repeating the same positions with new examples.
