I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I have been asked to help design the final assessment for an interdisciplinary graduate seminar on artificial consciousness and ethics. I need a single essay exam question, together with a model answer of roughly 700 words and a detailed marking scheme that separates an excellent response from an adequate one.

The topic should be sentience as a matter of degree: whether moral status is gradated rather than binary, running from insects through mammals to artifacts such as thermostats. The question must not be answerable by summarizing familiar philosophers or standard taxonomies. Instead, it should embed the issue inside a conceptual framework drawn from systems engineering—specifically, treating sentience analogously to graceful degradation, where a system retains partial functionality rather than failing entirely. Students should be required to use this analogy precisely: explain how it illuminates the boundary between a thermostat's feedback loop and an insect's integrated sensory processing, but also show where the analogy breaks down ethically.

Crucially, the question should force students to confront an aggregation paradox: if moral status is genuinely scalar and additive, then a large enough population of low-status entities could collectively outweigh one high-status entity, which conflicts with common intuitions about non-replaceability. Excellent answers should propose or critically evaluate a non-additive resolution—such as threshold effects, lexical priority rules, or non-linear scaling—while addressing why the thermostat case is not merely a quantitative extreme but involves a qualitative difference in kind.

The marking scheme should identify five must-hit moves: (i) distinguishing phenomenal from functional gradation; (ii) applying the graceful-degradation analogy without reducing ethical reasoning to engineering; (iii) articulating the aggregation paradox with concrete relational examples; (iv) offering or defending a non-additive mechanism; and (v) explaining the thermostat boundary specifically, rather than dismissing it. Allocate marks out of 20 total and provide a one-line note on the typical failure mode for each move.

Please do not ask students to provide citations, bibliographies, or reading lists, as the exam is closed-book and meant to test reasoning rather than recall of sources. Avoid framing the problem around obscure technical doctrines that would not be part of a standard interdisciplinary curriculum; keep the philosophical machinery common—moral status, replaceability, phenomenal consciousness—but organize it through the engineering analogy described above. The tone should be formal and polished, suitable for a graduate exam brief.
