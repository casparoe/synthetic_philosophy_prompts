Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between two opposing camps on state apologies for historical wrongs.

Camp A holds that state apologies are morally required and conceptually coherent: a contemporary state can sincerely apologize for past wrongs committed by its institutional predecessors because collective moral agency extends diachronically through sustained constitutional and civic identity, and such apologies perform non-substitutable work—restoring the normative standing of victims, acknowledging the specific moral character of the harm, and signaling a collective commitment to shared principles that material reparations alone cannot fully achieve.

Camp B holds that institutional apology is either conceptually incoherent or structurally pernicious: collective agents lack the mental states and unified moral subjectivity required for genuine apology, and even if the conceptual hurdle could be cleared, formal apologies function as "moral licensing" mechanisms that diminish political will for material redistribution, substitute symbolic recognition for structural change, and impose burdens of forgiveness upon victim communities without altering the conditions that produced the harm.

Write the document they should have produced together.

First, provide a numbered list of claims both sides can sign, including about: what a state apology is (as an institutional speech act distinct from personal regret); what historical conditions must obtain for an apology to be appropriate (e.g., institutional continuity, identifiable wrong and affected parties, acknowledgment of specific harm rather than generic lament); the shared normative and empirical claims both accept about particular historical cases (without asserting a universal causal law); and the shared rejection of clearly inadequate positions (e.g., that an apology can fully substitute for material repair, or that present citizens bear absolutely no institutional relationship to past state actions).

Second, present a disagreements table. For each camp, state in wording that side would accept for its own view: (i) the conditions under which a state possesses the moral standing to apologize; (ii) whether apology carries independent moral weight beyond instrumental effects on reconciliation or redistribution; (iii) how to assess the risk of moral licensing and what weight it should carry in policy design; (iv) the appropriate structural relationship between expressive apology and material reparation.

Third, identify the cruxes—the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side. I expect at least two: one concerning diachronic collective identity (whether constitutional and institutional continuity can ground the moral continuity necessary for expressive acts like apology, and what kind of continuity is sufficient); and one concerning the political psychology and causal effects of apology (whether formal state apologies reliably diminish, have no effect on, or enhance subsequent legislative and budgetary commitments to redistributive repair).

Fourth, for each crux, specify a concrete comparative case, institutional design, or line of argument that both sides agree bears on it, and record in advance what each camp would predict about that evidence. For the identity crux, both might agree that comparing sustained institutional reckoning with discontinuous regime-change contexts bears on whether continuity is necessary; state each side's predicted interpretation without inventing data. For the licensing crux, both might agree that examining legislative and budgetary trajectories following formal apologies in specific national contexts bears on the question; state each side's predicted pattern of interpretation. Do not cite invented statistics; describe only the kind of evidence both would accept as relevant, with each side's anticipated reading.

Finally, note whether any portion of the dispute is verbal rather than substantive—e.g., whether disagreement about "sincerity" collapses once "institutional sincerity" is defined in terms of credible commitment mechanisms and sustained policy alignment rather than mental-state analogies—and if so, say precisely which part and why.

Please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know this topic, and do not present any contested philosophical claim—such as the existence of diachronic collective moral agency, the inevitability of moral licensing, or the substitutability of symbolic and material repair—as if it were the consensus view.
