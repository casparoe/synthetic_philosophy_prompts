Please argue that if you actually take Hofstadter's strange-loop picture of the 'I' to its logical end—where the self is just a symbol that loops back and points at the system generating it—then you don't end up with one tidy 'me' in the drivers seat. You end up with a mess of competing sub-loops: emotional ones, narrative ones, bodily feedback loops, all generating their own self-referential symbols and none of them having a built-in right to be the 'real' I. So the model doesn't preserve a singular self; it quietly dissolves into a kind of federated committee that only feels like one person because the loudest loop drowns out the others. Make the case that this dissolution is a feature, not a reductio, and deal with the obvious pushback that people genuinely expereince themselves as unitary, not like a crowd arguing in a room. Maybe throw in why a small loop (like hunger) can temporarily hijack the whole system without there being any central referree to stop it.
