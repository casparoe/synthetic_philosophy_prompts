I have been revisiting Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach in connection with a cognitive-architecture framework I have been trying to formalize, which I call autoreferential predictive binding. The core idea is that a system can achieve self-modeling not by encoding an explicit symbolic sentence that refers back to its own syntactic structure, but by using hierarchical prediction-error minimization to stabilize a meta-representational attractor. Functionally, this produces a loop in which the system's model of itself continuously updates through its own outputs, yet there is no literal Gödel-style self-referential formula embedded in any underlying formal language.

This brings me to a confusion about the philosophical weight of Hofstadter's analogy. As I understand it, Gödel's first incompleteness theorem applies specifically to formal systems capable of representing arithmetic and containing explicit mechanisms of self-reference, demonstrating that such systems must contain true but unprovable statements. Hofstadter then draws an analogy—perhaps more than a metaphor—between this logical structure and human consciousness, describing self-awareness as arising from a "strange loop" in which representational capacities turn back upon themselves. But if my framework can generate the functional signature of such a loop—stable self-modeling that influences its own dynamics—without ever instantiating a formal self-referential statement, then it seems that the Gödelian obstacle to a complete, closed-form theory of mind has been circumvented. Specifically, if consciousness does not literally require a Gödel-numbered sentence in neural syntax, but can emerge from purely statistical, non-symbolic recursion, then what remains of the claim that self-awareness is necessarily tied to logical incompleteness?

I want to ask whether philosophers of mind interpret Hofstadter's strange loop as a literal claim about the logical architecture that minds must implement, or as a heuristic about hierarchical levels of abstraction. If the former, then architectures like autoreferential predictive binding appear to falsify the idea that self-awareness necessarily involves Gödelian unprovability. If the latter, I am unsure what philosophical constraint the analogy actually imposes: it would seem to be an evocative structural illustration rather than a live argument against naturalizing consciousness. Could someone clarify whether the Gödelian analogy in GEB is meant to show that any complete science of self-awareness is formally impossible, or merely to illuminate how self-reference can emerge across levels of description—and, if it is only the latter, why the analogy continues to be invoked in debates about whether a fully explanatory theory of mind is achievable?
