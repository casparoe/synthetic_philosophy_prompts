Since my grandfather's death eighteen months ago, I have been developing a screenplay drawn from his work as a regional labor organizer in the mid-twentieth century, and the project has made me acutely aware that I am not merely adapting history but restructuring a real life for dramatic effect. Within the philosophy and ethics of historical fiction and biographical dramatization—particularly regarding obligations to the deceased—what are the principal open questions, normative tensions, and lines of practical inquiry, and which should take priority for a practitioner who must make concrete choices about fabrication, compression, and narrative causality? I am not asking for a literature survey, but for a defended agenda: rank the two or three problems that should most govern a dramatist's decision-making, defend the ranking with explicit criteria (for instance, tractability for a working writer, severity and irreversibility of harm, or how many downstream artistic and ethical choices depend on resolving the issue), and address the trade-offs between them.

To ground the discussion, I want to focus on three interrelated tensions that have become central to my own thinking, though I welcome others if they are genuinely upstream of these. First, there is what I will call the distinction between representational harm—direct falsification of biographical fact—and participatory harm, which occurs when a dramatist casts a real person as an agent within a causal narrative they never inhabited, thereby restructuring not just what happened but how a life is understood to have unfolded; I want to know whether participatory harm is conceptually distinct or collapses into misrepresentation, and whether it carries independent moral weight. Second, there is the temporal and relational decay problem: do obligations to the dead diminish with chronological distance, with the death of all immediate survivors, or with the transition from private memory to public record, and what does that imply for dramatizing figures who are neither famous nor fully forgotten? Third, there is what I term the restoration framing—when a biopic or historical drama justifies its liberties by claiming to honor, rescue, or correct the record about its subject—and whether this framing genuinely mitigates harm or instead constitutes an epistemic corruption of collective memory by substituting a narratively satisfying arc for the irreducible contingency of a real life.

Of these, which line of inquiry should I treat as upstream of the others, which are most tractable without access to archival research I do not possess, and which are overrated in current debate? Please defend your ordering with reference to what actually changes in a writer's practice if the question is settled one way rather than another. Finally, please say explicitly how confident you are in this prioritization, and identify the single point—whether conceptual, normative, or empirical—about which you are least sure, explaining what kind of consideration would alter your view.
