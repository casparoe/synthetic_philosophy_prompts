In Jaspers' analysis of collective responsibility, metaphysical guilt is said to arise not from particular criminal acts but from a failure of solidarity with those subjected to injustice, and it can therefore attach to a collective even after the individual perpetrators are dead. If this form of guilt is genuinely non-individual and persists through institutional continuity rather than personal culpability, then it seems to function less like a private moral stain and more like a structural liability that survives regime change. Given that framing, should we understand intergenerational transmission of such guilt on the model of an amortizable social debt—where each successive generation inherits a proportionally diluted share—or rather as a non-depreciable obligation that applies in full to every member of the continuing collective regardless of birth cohort? Specifically, does the logic of metaphysical guilt permit any calculus of dilution based on temporal distance from the original failure, or does it require that every generation that participates in the collective's continuity bear the guilt entirely and without gradation?
