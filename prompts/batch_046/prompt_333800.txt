A recent essay I encountered summarized the so-called "species problem" in philosophy of biology as follows. The biological species concept fails for asexual organisms and hybrid zones; the phylogenetic species concept fragments continuous evolutionary lineages into arbitrarily small units; and morphological classification depends on observer-dependent judgments of similarity rather than on mind-independent boundaries. From these failures, the essay concludes that biologists should abandon the search for any unified species concept and treat "species" as a purely instrumental term lacking metaphysical weight. It then extends this dissolution directly to ethics: if Homo sapiens has no sharp biological boundary, then arguments for human moral exceptionalism grounded in species membership automatically collapse, leaving only a gradient of cognitive capacities as the legitimate basis for distributing moral status. I suspect this reasoning moves too quickly between descriptive taxonomy and normative ethics, and that it may misrepresent how philosophers of biology actually understand taxonomic pluralism, but I am unsure exactly where the errors lie. Is this summary fair? Please distinguish which parts accurately reflect the state of the biological debate, which parts conflate taxonomic practice with metaphysical conclusions about the reality of species, whether the failure of a single unified concept genuinely entails that species terms carry no metaphysical commitment whatsoever, and whether the ethical conclusion follows without importing a hidden normative premise about the irrelevance of biological kind membership to moral status.
