hey, I'm prepping for a seminar on speech acts and performative utterances and I wrote down my current takes to see if they're coherent. Can you look them over and tell me which ones actually conflict and how badly?

Here's what I think:
1. Austin's core idea—that saying can be doing—is right, but he overstates how much convention is required. A lot of performative force just comes from people recognizing that you have the authority, not from shared ritual rules.
2. I still use Searle's five-way taxonomy, but I think he left out a class I call "ground-laying" utterances—like when a chair says "we're now in open session" before a vote. It doesn't change anyone's rights by itself, but it makes the later votes count. Not quite a declaration, not quite expressive.
3. I think misfired performatives aren't permanently broken. If everyone involved later treats the act as done, the social fact gets repaired. So the line between misfire and success is mostly about uptake, not about getting the formula exactly right.
4. But for big institutional stuff—court rulings, legislation—I think performative force is totally conventional. You can't repair a botched statute by collective agreement; the rules have to be satisfied first.
5. Promises bind you even if you made them insincerely, because the obligation comes from the hearer's reliance, not your inner state. But declarations—like "I now pronounce you married"—are different: if the officiant secretly lacks authority, it's void even if everyone claps, because authority has to be genuinely present, not just socially recognized.
6. I also think two people can invent thier own private performative system—make up a ritual that creates obligations between just them—with zero wider social recognition.

So, do these hang together? I'm worried that 3 and 5 pull in opposite directions on whether social uptake can override formal failure, and that I'm being inconsistent about convention between 1 and 4. Are there other tensions I'm missing?

Please rank them by severity, suggest the cheapest fix for each, and tell me which single revision would clean up the most problems. Also, please seperate what's settled in the philosophy of language literature from what's genuinely contested—don't present a contested reading as the consensus view.
