Consider this argument about corruption, clientelism, and whether Western standards are universal. (1) Corruption is the exchange of official discretion for a private benefit that falls outside the public justification of the office. (2) In a clientelist system, a mayor directs public contracts to supporters in exchange for votes; this is corruption. (3) In many societies, presenting a gift to an official before a routine request is obligatory etiquette; the official expedites the request, and both parties understand this as reciprocity rather than secrecy. (4) In Western democracies, a legislator grants access and attention to major donors who fund her reelection; she exercises discretion over which voices shape policy. (5) By (1), both (3) and (4) involve exchanging discretion for private benefit—social harmony or electoral survival—outside the office’s public mandate. (6) Therefore, if (1) is applied consistently, democratic representation and standard gift-giving etiquette are both corrupt. (7) But treating democratic representation as structurally corrupt is a reductio; hence (1) must be abandoned in favor of relativism, where corruption is whatever violates local norms of legitimate exchange. (8) Yet if corruption is purely local, a society that openly auctions judicial verdicts has no corruption, which is equally absurd. (9) So either (7) fails and we need a universal criterion that distinguishes (2) from (3) and (4) without begging the question, or the error lies elsewhere.
