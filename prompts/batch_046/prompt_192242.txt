I've been developing an argument for a seminar paper on distributive justice, and a line of reasoning I worked out with a colleague now strikes me as suspicious because the conclusion is far stronger than I intended, though each premise seemed defensible when I wrote it. The argument attempts to show that prioritarianism—taken here as the view that the moral importance of benefiting someone is determined by a strictly decreasing weight function applied to their absolute welfare level—cannot be confined to income redistribution but necessarily extends to leveling down positional goods and even innate capacities. The steps are these: (1) Prioritarian evaluation applies a weight w(f) to any welfare gain, where w is monotone decreasing in the recipient's baseline welfare, so benefits to the worse off always carry greater moral weight. (2) If the principle is to be domain-general rather than arbitrarily restricted, it must apply to any causal determinant of welfare, including health status, educational credentials, and cognitive ability, since these generate welfare differences just as income does. (3) Consider a case where person A and person B are both above a catastrophic threshold but A enjoys a substantial welfare advantage solely because of a non-transferable positional good—say, a cognitive capacity that yields high-status employment. If reducing that capacity in A (without pushing A below the threshold) eliminates the welfare gap, the prioritarian weighted sum improves, analogous to a progressive tax. (4) Therefore, prioritarianism requires us to diminish or suppress such advantages whenever doing so improves the weighted distribution, even if the advantaged party receives no direct compensation. (5) But this implies we have positive reason to level down talents, credentials, or health in the well-off whenever the worst off would thereby gain, which is morally repugnant. (6) Consequently, prioritarianism must either be restricted ad hoc to money, or it is false.

I want to preserve both (1) and (5) if possible: the intuition that benefiting the worse off matters more seems correct, and the intuition that we should not suppress others' talents also seems correct. So I need to know exactly where the chain breaks—whether in the move from resources to capacities in (2), in the analogy between taxation and suppression in (3), or elsewhere—and what accepting that break costs for the overall view. Please diagnose the failure, explain why the faulty step looked plausible, and price the alternatives.
