I recently adopted a largely plant-based diet and have been reflecting on whether religious traditions contain resources for criticizing modern industrial animal agriculture, which led me to Maimonides' view that animal sacrifice was not an ideal form of worship but a divine concession to the Israelites' deeply ingrained attachment to sacrificial practices. I have constructed the following argument and now find it suspicious because the conclusion licenses far too much. (1) If God legislates by accommodating human weakness rather than prescribing the ideal, then moral law should adapt to deeply rooted practices rather than demanding their abolition. (2) Maimonides treats animal sacrifice as precisely such an accommodation: the Israelites could not abandon it abruptly, so God permitted it with restrictions while directing it toward true worship. (3) Modern industrial meat consumption is similarly a deeply rooted cultural and economic practice that many populations would struggle to abandon abruptly. (4) Therefore, by Maimonides' reasoning, moral or religious law should concede to industrial meat production with regulatory restrictions rather than demanding veganism or abolition. (5) But this conclusion is absurd: it would justify accommodating any entrenched harm, from slavery to ecological destruction, provided it is deeply rooted. (6) So one of the premises must fail. Which step should I reject? In particular, is the error in assuming that divine accommodation of religious practice generalizes to moral accommodation of secular practices, or does Maimonides' distinction between ideal worship and permitted practice contain a hidden normative criterion that blocks the analogy? If I want to preserve his insight that law must sometimes work with human weakness, what does that commit me to regarding modern animal ethics?
