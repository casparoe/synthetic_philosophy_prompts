I'm an analyst for a watchdog NGO and every quarter I have to sign off on reports flagging specific government moves as democratic backsliding or not. The board hates false positives (calling normal partisan politics backsliding) but also hates missing the slow, structural stuff. I need a reusable decision procedure — a flowchart in words, really — that I can run in about 20 minutes per case without library access. Ground it in whatever political science actually distinguishes backsliding from constitutional hardball, not just vibes.

The conceptual twist I want baked in: don't just ask if one institution got weaker. Ask whether the change collapses "institutional redundancy" — the number of genuinely independant veto points that could block executive overreach — but selectively, so that bodies currently constraining the ruling coalition are captured or defanged while parallel bodies that don't constrain them stay untouched. That's my proposed line between hardball and backsliding, but I need it turned into ordered tests.

Give me:
- A checklist with steps in sequence, marking exactly which ones are judgment calls I have to initial.
- How to apply it to court restructuring, media-regulator capture, and emergency powers that outlast the crisis.
- Three worked cases: (1) a government expands the top court and packs it with loyalists; (2) a government overhauls public-media funding but applies identical rules to every outlet; (3) a left-leaning coalition gerrymanders districts in ways that violate neutrality norms but claim to expand representation for marginalized voters. Case 3 should come out "technically yes, backsliding" by the procedure even though the politics might feel different — I want that tension visible.
- Where the procedure will definitely misfire, especially in federal or coalition systems where blame is diffuse.
- What it flat-out cannot do.

Write the answer in American English, no bibliography or citation requests, and flag the steps where I'm just dressing up a judgment call as procedure.
