A university behavioral-policy review board has issued the following rulings: (1) it approved an opt-out retirement-savings program with one-click reversal and full disclosure; (2) it rejected a hidden checkout-delay algorithm that added friction without notifying users; (3) it approved mandatory large-print sugar-labeling at point of sale without altering purchase procedures; (4) it rejected a disclosed smart-fridge lock that required a five-minute mindfulness exercise to open after 10 p.m.; (5) it approved a 24-hour cooling-off period for high-interest loans paired with a brief literacy quiz, with easy cancellation.

Now it faces a proposal for a university-wide browser extension, automatically enabled for all students during exam periods, that blocks entertainment and social-media sites. The extension displays a prominent banner explaining the mechanism, permits instant disable via a single toggle, and collects no personal data.

What should the board decide if it wishes to be consistent? Identify the closest precedent, articulate at least two plausible governing principles that both accommodate the five rulings but yield opposite predictions for this proposal, state which principle you think best captures the board's implicit logic, and recommend a verdict with reasoning. If the precedents genuinely underdetermine the case, specify what further decision—about which kind of intervention—would be required to settle the matter.
