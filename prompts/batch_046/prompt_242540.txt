You advise a clinical phenomenology unit that records first-person reports from patients emerging from deep sedation or dissociative episodes. Staff research assistants—without philosophy training—must run a reusable, ten-minute coding procedure on any report expressing sustained, calm uncertainty about whether the speaker is currently awake or still dreaming, especially when the speaker frames this in terms of personal transformation. The procedure must classify each report as (A) a philosophically coherent expression of dream-skepticism structured like Zhuangzi’s butterfly problem; (B) benign confusion that lacks that specific philosophical architecture; or (C) a clinically significant reality-testing failure requiring immediate referral to a senior clinician. It must be grounded in the specific logic of the butterfly scenario—not the generic “I might be dreaming” worry, but the deeper instability that arises if transformation between waking and dreaming is possible, leaving the identity of the self who asks “which am I?” in question—while remaining executable by non-specialists who must initial the form and cannot consult a philosopher.

Design a three-layer decision procedure in ordered steps. Layer 1, the Transformation-Articulation Test, asks whether the speaker distinguishes a change in appearance or state from a change in the identity of the first-person subject; specify the exact verbal markers that count as sufficient, the point at which this layer stops with a definitive code, and where it hands off because the distinction requires interpretive judgment. Layer 2, the Persistence-of-Self Test, asks whether the speaker maintains a continuous first-person reference across the hypothesized transformation; again specify sufficient answers, stop conditions, and judgment hand-offs. Layer 3, the Pragmatic-Grounding Test, requires the speaker to perform three basic orientation actions—state the current date, identify the purpose of the room, and name one visible object—without internal contradiction; specify what counts as passing, what counts as failing, and the precise node where failure automatically triggers code (C) regardless of the philosophical sophistication of the report.

Then apply the procedure to three reports. First: a patient calmly states that “the self that was sedated may have transformed into the self asking now, like one form becoming another,” correctly performs all three orientation actions, and asks whether philosophy distinguishes this feeling from ordinary confusion—showing articulation of transformation with preserved self-reference and intact grounding. Second: a patient reports that “the ceiling tiles are moving like butterflies,” expresses uncertainty about which state is real, but cannot state the year and contradicts himself about whether he is in a hospital—showing confusion without philosophical structure and failed grounding. Third: include one case that produces an answer the unit will dislike—specifically, a report that articulates transformation and self-persistence with near-philosophical precision, using language very close to the butterfly problem’s structure, yet contains a subtle contradiction in the orientation task that forces the procedure to assign code (C) and mandate clinical referral, despite the staff’s strong intuition that this is a “Zhuangzian” case rather than a pathological one.

Finally, state explicitly what the procedure cannot do: it cannot determine whether any metaphysical transformation has actually occurred, it cannot validate or invalidate the patient’s philosophical insight, and it cannot settle reports that pass Layer 1 ambiguously or fail Layer 2 in ways that blur the line between poetic expression and disorientation. Flag those boundary conditions clearly, indicating exactly where the assistant must escalate to the senior clinician rather than complete the form alone. Keep the language formal, avoid any citation requests, and do not quote any source verbatim.
