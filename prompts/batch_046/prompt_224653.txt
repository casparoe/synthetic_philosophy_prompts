I serve as the metadata-review officer for a regional oral-history archive holding approximately twelve thousand interviews recorded between 1920 and 2010. We are migrating to a new discovery platform, and our board—historians, librarians, and community representatives—has adopted a policy grounded in the social construction of kinds: race, gender, and occupational classifications are not natural divisions but institutionally sustained categories that acquire reality only through collective acceptance and administrative repetition. To this they have added a specific constraint they call indexical fidelity, which holds that a retrieval kind (the category a researcher uses to locate material) and a kind-in-use (the classification actually operative for the speaker at the moment of recording) are ontologically distinct practices. Conflating them is not merely anachronistic; it collapses two different institutional realities into one and misrepresents both the historical record and the present retrieval system.

My two cataloguers, who have no formal background in philosophy or social ontology, must apply this framework to every donation batch—roughly fifty interviews—within twenty minutes per batch. The board reviews only contested cases, and my sign-off is required for any suppression or translation that involves harm-related descriptors. I need a reusable decision procedure they can run in sequence, with explicit stop-points where the determination is factual and where it becomes a value judgment that must be flagged for human decision.

The procedure must specify, in order:

1. How to determine whether a kind-term appearing in a transcript, donor form, or speaker self-description is functioning as a kind-in-use (historically operative classification) or as a modern retrieval label that has been anachronistically projected onto the record.
2. A decision tree for each descriptor: preserve verbatim in a locked historical-metadata field; translate to a contemporary retrieval equivalent with an explicit mapping note that records both the original term and the translation rule applied; flag for board review with a standardized justification form; or suppress with documented reasoning.
3. The precise boundary at which the procedure ceases to be a factual determination and becomes a normative judgment—specifically, when preserving a harmful historical term for fidelity to the kind-in-use conflicts with avoiding present harm through retrieval access—and how that boundary should be marked in the documentation.
4. Documentation standards that allow a future researcher to reconstruct both the original institutional practice that sustained the kind-in-use and the current retrieval practice that sustains the kind-in-archive.

Then apply the procedure to three worked cases, including one where it produces a result I anticipate the board will reject because it privileges historical fidelity over present harm-avoidance:

Case A: A 1954 interview with a Black jazz musician who describes himself using a term that was then a community self-identifier within his local scene but is now widely considered a slur; the donor form lists his race using an obsolete census category that has since been administratively retired.

Case B: A 1982 interview with a factory worker who refers to herself using a marital-status term that implied economic dependency, and lists her occupation using a domestic category, despite being the household’s primary wage-earner; modern retrieval taxonomies offer separate categories for marital status and for wage-labor versus domestic labor.

Case C: A 1968 interview in which a speaker describes money not as state currency but as a social score within a cooperative credit system—an institutional kind that structured exchange in his community but has no equivalent in our modern economic taxonomy.

The procedure should also state which conceptual distinctions it must drop to remain usable in twenty minutes, where it is likely to misfire (for example, with hybrid or contested terms that function differently across race, gender, and economic domains), and which cases must be escalated to board sign-off rather than resolved by cataloguer judgment. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
