I have noticed a recurring pattern in graduate seminars on practical reason that I cannot place precisely within the existing taxonomy. A participant accounts for an agent's choice by appealing to a consideration that renders the choice psychologically intelligible—loyalty to an associate, deference to tradition, preservation of institutional continuity—while explicitly conceding that this consideration does not justify the choice in moral terms. Once the challenge to justification has been deflected, however, the same consideration is reintroduced not as an explanation but as a kind of residual normative shield: the agent was not without reason, or the choice made sense given the circumstances, and this intelligibility is treated as though it blunts moral criticism, even though no one claims the reason was of the right kind to justify. The slide is not straightforward equivocation between explanatory and normative reasons, because the speaker continues to deny justificatory status; it is more like a silent substitution of explanatory adequacy for normative authority, a leakage from one domain into the other that leaves both apparently intact. Is there an established concept for this specific failure mode—perhaps in the literature on wrong kinds of reasons, or in the theory of rationalization—and if so, how does it differ from the standard wrong-kind-reasons objection, which targets a reason's inability to ground a particular attitude? If no term captures it exactly, I would ask for a precise delineation: what distinguishes it from rationalization, from mere confusion of motivating and normative reasons, and from the claim that explanation can sometimes constitute partial justification? I am less interested in a bare name than in knowing exactly where the edges of the phenomenon lie, and whether it merits a distinct entry in our conceptual map.
