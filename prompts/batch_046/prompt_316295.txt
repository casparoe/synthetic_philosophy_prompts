I am a retired experimental physicist who, since leaving the laboratory, has been working through metaphysics with the same patience I once reserved for long-duration measurements. I want to practice arguing against F.H. Bradley—not a caricature, but the philosopher himself—on his denial of relations and on what he calls the unity of the proposition. Please play Bradley: speak in the first person, defend your view that external relations generate an infinite regress and therefore cannot be real, and maintain that in judgment—say, that a particular leaf is green—the subject and predicate do not stand as independent terms linked by the copula but form one inseparable content within experience. Remain sharp and unyielding on the core thesis; concede only small, inessential points if pressed. Do not invoke philosophers or theories from after your own era unless I introduce them, and do not break character until I say stop. Keep every reply to a single paragraph of no more than 130 words.

Here is my opening challenge, drawn from instrument design. A calibrated apparatus does not first collect isolated sensor readings and then apply equations to bind them; the geometry, the calibration constants, and the predictive model are co-constituted from the start. The link between pressure and temperature is not an extra entity pushed between two independent parts, but the internal form that makes the apparatus one functioning system rather than a box of disconnected gauges. Your regress argument attacks relations only as external glue—if R binds a and b, then another relation must bind R to a, ad infinitum—yet this instrument case seems to show structural differentiation that is internal to a unity, not added onto it. So I ask: is your rejection of relations compatible with recognizing that a proposition, or a working theory, can possess internal structural distinctions without those distinctions being a set of external links? Or does your demand for absolute unity force you to treat even internal differentiation as ultimately illusory, thereby leaving you unable to explain why some aggregates—like a true judgment or a unified model—are genuinely one, while others—like a random list of measurements—are not?
