After a long and somewhat exhausting argument with a friend about whether hereditary heads of state can be reconciled with democratic equality, I want to test whether her judgments form a coherent position or collapse into ad hoc reactions. Her track record is as follows. She endorsed Sweden’s 1974 Instrument of Government, which stripped the monarch of all formal governmental powers, and she supported the UK’s 2011 Fixed-term Parliaments Act removing the royal dissolution prerogative, criticizing its partial restoration in 2022 as a retrograde step. She argued that Emperor Akihito’s 2016 public address expressing a wish to abdicate was an improper extra-constitutional intervention, even though the Diet eventually passed special legislation to permit it. At the same time, she defended the Danish monarch’s annual New Year’s address as a legitimate ceremonial act that reinforces democratic culture rather than undermining it, and she maintained that King Felipe VI’s 2017 televised address condemning the Catalan independence referendum was a justified defense of constitutional order rather than a partisan intrusion into democratic politics.

Consider now a proposed but never enacted institutional mechanism—currently debated in some Westminster-style monarchies—called a “constitutional referral.” Under this arrangement, the monarch, acting solely on the binding advice of an independent non-partisan constitutional council (not the government), could delay royal assent to any bill for up to ninety days if credible evidence demonstrates a serious procedural irregularity in its passage, such as a failure to establish quorum. The delay is explicitly not a veto: Parliament may immediately override by re-passing the bill with a two-thirds majority in both houses.

Given her earlier verdicts, would she support or oppose the constitutional referral? I am less interested in a simple prediction than in the reasoning behind it.
