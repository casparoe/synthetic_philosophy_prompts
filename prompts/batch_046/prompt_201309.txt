In discussions of personal identity, a debate has stalled that I keep encountering in philosophy readings: can an "I" be distributed across multiple brains? Inspired by Hofstadter's claim that the self is more like a replicable pattern than a single substance locked in one skull, some argue that if two brains maintain continuous, bidirectional access to one shared stream of experience and memory they constitute a single distributed self; others insist this describes only two intimately linked persons, not one plural "I." We share the same basic neuroscience and agree that consciousness can in principle split or merge, yet in every discussion the positions never shift. Is this stalemate actually about hidden empirical questions—say, which neural structures or information-integration patterns count as the substrate of selfhood—or is it a normative disagreement about what we ought to preserve, or even a verbal dispute over whether the word "I" can apply to plural instantiations of one pattern? Or are the sides answering different questions altogether, one about metaphysical composition and one about practical identity? Diagnose the disagreement using the concrete commitments each side actually holds, and tell me whether any conceptual distinction, empirical finding, or reframing of the question could genuinely move it forward, or whether we should expect the impasse to remain unavoidable.
