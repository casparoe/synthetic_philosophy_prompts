In cyclical philosophies of history—those that model civilizations through successive phases of growth, maturity, decay, and renewal—there is a recurring defensive pattern I want to identify precisely. When a civilization persists in institutional stagnation for far longer than the theory predicts, without yielding either collapse or the regenerative crisis required for renewal, the account often introduces an “inertial suspension”: an auxiliary mechanism, such as ritualized bureaucracy or extractive administration, that freezes the decaying phase indefinitely. The macro-law is preserved intact; only the expected transition is postponed rather than denied. Linear-progressive accounts facing analogous stagnation tend to invoke a mirror-image move, treating visible stasis as a “latent accumulation” of unseen forces that will eventually produce a breakthrough, thereby preserving directional progress without observable advancement. In both cases, the theory absorbs a prolonged deviation by inserting a non-transformative holding pattern rather than restructuring its core dynamics. Is there an established concept—perhaps in philosophy of science or historiography—for this specific maneuver? If so, how exactly is it distinguished from a standard ad hoc rescue or from a genuinely productive auxiliary hypothesis? If not, can you define the phenomenon precisely, specify the boundary conditions under which a suspension counts as theoretical refinement rather than evasion, and identify what kind of historical evidence would genuinely threaten the underlying law rather than merely extending its latency?
