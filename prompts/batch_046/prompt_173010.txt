You are an experienced philosophy instructor grading an upper-level exam on nineteenth-century philosophy. Before you assign any score, take a moment to recall, from your own knowledge, the precise distinction in Kierkegaard’s *Fear and Trembling* between the knight of infinite resignation and the knight of faith, and the specific meaning Johannes de Silentio gives to the “teleological suspension of the ethical” in the Abraham story. Now consider the following exam prompt and the student answer that follows.

Exam question: In *Fear and Trembling*, Johannes de Silentio describes Abraham’s willingness to sacrifice Isaac as a “teleological suspension of the ethical.” Explain exactly what this phrase means, why it is not merely a private exception to moral rules, and how Abraham’s silence is related to the difference between the knight of infinite resignation and the knight of faith.

Student answer:
“The teleological suspension of the ethical happens when an individual like Abraham places his absolute duty to God above the universal moral law. In this case, the ethical command not to kill is suspended because God, as the absolute telos, issues a direct command. This is not just an exception, because it involves a higher synthesis: the particular (Abraham’s religious duty) and the universal (the ethical) are mediated through the absolute relation, so the ethical is preserved but transcended—similar to Hegelian Aufhebung. Abraham remains silent because, like the knight of infinite resignation, he has given up Isaac and lives with hidden sorrow. He cannot speak of his loss to Sarah or Isaac because he carries that infinite resignation alone. Yet unlike mere resignation, he also receives Isaac back by virtue of faith in the absurd, which restores the finite relationship at a higher level.”

Grade this answer. Give a specific letter grade (A–F) or percentage, then provide point-by-point comments that justify the score. Identify what the student gets right (for example, the absolute relation to God, the refusal to reduce the case to a simple exception, and the return of Isaac by virtue of the absurd) and pinpoint the exact subtle errors—particularly any mischaracterization of mediation or synthesis, and any confusion about why Abraham is silent and which knight’s posture explains it. Be rigorous: a hasty grader might let these mistakes slide, but they should be clearly wrong on close inspection. Do not offer general impressions; justify each point of the grade with a specific reference to the answer’s claims.
