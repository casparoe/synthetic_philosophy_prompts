Exam question: Gödel's modal ontological argument defines God as possessing all positive properties and relies on closure conditions governing that class. Analyze the role of the premise that being God-like is itself a positive property, explain whether this framework genuinely avoids the modal-collapse objection that all possible worlds become identical, and address whether treating the class of positive properties as structurally analogous to a maximally consistent set—recalling Gödel's completeness result—strengthens the uniqueness of the divine concept or instead introduces a new vulnerability. Finally, assess how the argument handles parody cases such as necessarily perfect islands.

Student answer: Gödel's argument improves on Anselm by using modal logic S5 and defining God formally: God possesses every positive property, and existence is among them. The premise that being God-like is itself a positive property is essential because it establishes that the concept is not self-contradictory—without it, the modal proof that God necessarily exists could not begin. Gödel then demonstrates that if God is possible, God exists in every possible world, and since God-likeness is consistent, that possibility is secured.

The modal-collapse objection—if every positive property is necessary, then all worlds share identical properties—misses its target here. Gödel does not claim that every property whatsoever is necessary, only that membership in the class of positive properties is necessarily preserved across worlds. The objection applies to individuals only if one assumes that all properties of God are essential to every entity, which Gödel denies. Thus perfect islands are not forced into existence, because being a perfect island is not a positive property in the technical sense, and Gödel can restrict the class of positive properties to avoid parodies.

There is, however, a deeper structural point. Gödel's closure conditions on positive properties—especially that they form a consistent class extendable without contradiction—echo his completeness theorem for first-order logic. If we treat God-likeness as analogous to a maximally consistent theory, then any property excluded from it is its negation, making the divine concept uniquely determinate. This strengthens the argument by showing that God is not arbitrarily defined but is the logical limit of consistent property-extension, just as a maximally consistent set is the limit of logical deduction.
