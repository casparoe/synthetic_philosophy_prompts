I'm on the allocation committee for the Meridian Foundation's long-horizon science fund. We've always sized grants with standard expected-value math — linear weights, arithmetic mean returns — but one senior fellow just proposed we switch to a Kelly-style geometric framework for the whole portfolio. Her claim is that since we're a perpetual endowment, our real objective isn't arithmetic social return but the geometric growth rate of impact over centuries, so each grant should be sized as a fraction of capital using log-utility weights. She also wants us to resolve reviewer disagreement by taking the geometric mean of their probability estimates rather than averaging them arithmetically, arguing that multiplicative aggregation respects independence and avoids the dilution bias when one reviewer is near-certain and another near-zero.

The committee is split. The conservative wing says this is risk-seeking in fancy clothing, and that geometric averaging of scores will systematically suppress controversial high-variance projects because one low score pulls the product toward zero. They want linear aggregation and a hard 3% cap on any single grant. There's also a strange philosophical wrinkle hanging over us: if geometric rationality is correct for capital, shouldn't we apply it to our own deliberation time? That is, should committee attention be allocated multiplicatively — spending exponentially more hours on proposals with exponentially higher potential — instead of giving every application the same two-hour review? Nobody knows how to operationalize that, but it's become a kind of reductio people keep throwing around.

Can you draft a short internal memo recommending whether we adopt the Kelly-geometric protocol for grant sizing and reviewer aggregation, with clear criteria staff can actually apply? I need a bottom line — yes, partial, or no — plus the reasoning, and an honest treatment of the strongest argument on the losing side. Please also address whether the "attention allocation" analogy is a serious extension of the framework or just a reductio, and whether geometric averaging of reviewer beliefs is defensible or just smuggles in hidden risk aversion. Write it like an internal policy doc, not an academic paper; no need for citations or a bibliography. I also want you to check whether there's a typo in my summary of the conservative position — I think I said "seperate" somewhere but I'm not sure where.
