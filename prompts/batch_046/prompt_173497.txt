I'm taking a graduate course in philosophy of biology that centers on the species problem, particularly the tension between treating species as natural kinds versus treating them as spatiotemporally bounded individuals. In a recent seminar on ontological individuality, a classmate proposed that we understand a biological species through the analogy of a corporation: discrete membership lists, the capacity for fission or merger, and persistent organizational identity despite complete turnover of constituent parts. She then applied this to synthetic chimeric organisms—entities engineered with genomes drawn from multiple lineages—to argue that they do not blur species boundaries any more than a corporation absorbing subsidiaries blurs its corporate identity. I worry the comparison succeeds only by importing legal-personhood and intentional-design structures that evolutionary dynamics do not possess, but I want a disciplined audit rather than an impressionistic objection.

Please respond as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction or conclusion. First, provide the explicit correspondence table mapping corporate features onto biological species features. Second, identify the breakdown where biological reproduction lacks contractual incorporation or centralized governance. Third, specify what the analogy quietly smuggles in about agency, normative status, or design that distorts evolutionary processes. Fourth, evaluate whether the conclusion—that chimeric organisms pose no serious challenge to species individuality—survives once the disanalogies are visible. Fifth, suggest whether a different analogy—perhaps drawn from distributed computing or meteorological systems—would do less misleading work in thinking about engineered genomic chimerism.
