The heart of virtue ethics, from Aristotle through to contemporary philosophers such as Rosalind Hursthouse, is that moral character is built from robust, global traits — honesty, courage, temperance — that show up consistently across very different situations. A truly honest person is honest with strangers, with friends, and under pressure, in a unified, cross-situational way. The situationist critique, drawing on classic experiments such as Milgram’s obedience studies and Darley and Latané’s bystander-intervention research, demonstrates that small changes in context produce large shifts in behaviour, so people lack the consistency virtue ethics requires. More recent personality science, especially the CAPS framework developed by Mischel and Shoda, confirms that behaviour is governed by stable if-then signatures rather than by global traits. Consequently, there are no character traits of the kind virtue ethics needs. Some defenders retreat to local traits — being honest-at-work or courageous-in-sport — but this fragments virtue into situation-specific habits and abandons the Aristotelian vision of an integrated soul. Once virtue is broken up this way, virtue ethics collapses into a form of behavioural conditioning, and the situationist critique succeeds as a straightforward empirical refutation.
