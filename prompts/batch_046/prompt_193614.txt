Two colleagues in my engineering team—both without formal philosophy training—got into a disagreement after one casually referenced Benatar's asymmetry. The first argued that if the absence of pain is genuinely good even when there is no subject to enjoy that absence, then introducing any conscious being carries an unavoidable moral liability: that being will suffer some harm, and there is no corresponding benefit to weigh against it, because the absence of pleasure in non-existence is not a deprivation to anyone. From this he concluded that antinatalism follows, and he pushed the point further: if we ever build an artificial system with phenomenal consciousness, the same logic should make us hesitate to instantiate it, since we would be creating harm with no compensating good for the counterfactual non-existent system. The second colleague replied that this treats Benatar's asymmetry as a decision rule about creation rather than an evaluation of interests, and that phenomenal consciousness—not mere functional complexity—is what makes harm and benefit attach to a subject; applying the asymmetry to hypothetical AI therefore confuses suffering with system failure. The first responded that if phenomenal consciousness is the sole criterion, then the asymmetry actually undermines the case for procreation more strongly: pleasure is good but not good for anyone in particular, whereas suffering is bad for the specific subject who experiences it, so any life is a net moral gamble with no upside that benefits a missing person. The second said this inverts Benatar's point, which is meant to show that we wrong existing people by harming them but do not wrong non-existent people by failing to benefit them, not to establish a baseline of non-existence that receives the good of absent pain; therefore the asymmetry blocks an obligation to create, but does not generate an obligation not to create. They could not agree whether the first was correctly deriving an antinatalist conclusion or importing a hidden premise about risk and obligation.
