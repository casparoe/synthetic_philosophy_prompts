So I was arguing with a colleague over coffee about dispositions, and I found myself defending this view I'm now calling the situated-potential account — probably not original, but I think it's sharper than the usual framing. Basically, I think having a disposition is never a monadic property of an object alone; it's always a triadic relation involving the object, the trigger, and whatever background conditions are currently masking or enabling the manifestation. So the classic fink cases and the standard mask anomolies aren't weird edge cases that need patching — they just make explicit what's structurally true all along. The sugar cube's solubility isn't robustly "in" the cube; it's relational, holding only when the anti-dissolution enchantment (or chemical blocker, whatever) is absent. I'm drawn to this because it seems to dissolve the old debate about whether dispositions need catagorical bases, but I'm also worried it's just contextualism with extra steps, and that I've qualified it into unfalsifiability — if every failure to manifest gets explained away by some hidden contextual factor, does the view actually risk anything?

So here's what I want: what would genuinely count against this situated-potential picture? Sort the defeaters for me, from fatal to merely awkward, including formal results or scientific practices that presuppose dispositions are intrinsic, and also tell me what a committed defender of the traditional intrinsic view would themselves concede counts against them — not just what critics say. And be honest: has this kind of view already been insulated from refutation in a way that's a problem for metaphysics?
