I'm on the advisory board for our county's pilot health-alert app, just a small group that advises the health director on rollouts. The app pulls wastewater data and a couple anonymous symptoms people log, then sends users either a color code — green, yellow, red — or an exact percent risk for catching the flu-like virus going around now. Half the board wants the exact numbers because more information is always better. One guy keeps citing Gool's theorem — I think he means Good's — which I understand to say that free information can't hurt your decision on average. The other half says a precise 23% risk makes people do weird things, like skipping a cancer screening because they feel probably fine, or flooding the ER over nothing. I also keep worrying about what I call second-order ignorance: if you know your own risk but don't know what everyone else knows, you misjudge how contagious you'll be in a crowd, and the whole contact network shifts in ways the model didn't predict.

We need to send the director a short written opinion by Friday. It should recommend one concrete option — colors-only, full numbers, or a hybrid with seperate conditions — include a time frame for review, and take a real stance rather than laying out options and leaving her to pick. Please engage the Good's-theorem argument seriously, not just dismiss it, and include an honest treatment of the strongest point on the losing side. Since I'm not academic, write it like you're explaining to a smart neighbor, but keep it formal enough for the director to act on. I want a definately committed answer, and please point out where my own reasoning is weakest. Don't ask me for citations or reading lists — I wouldn't know how to find them anyway.
