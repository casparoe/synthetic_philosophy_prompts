I don't have an academic background, but I've been working through my own idea about how people learn to be good by reading about other people's lives. It started with Plutarch's Parallel Lives, where he pairs figures like Alexander and Caesar not to crown a winner but to let their differences illuminate each other. From that I developed a more specific claim: moral learning through biography operates by virtue calibration, where the reader adjusts an internal scale of moral weights by comparing paired lives that share a common achievement but diverge in temperament and failure mode. Crucially, this calibration only works if the biographies preserve moral noise—the unresolved contradictions, the virtues that become vices in different contexts, the failures that don't get redeemed by the narrative. If you smooth a life into hagiography, or if you present a single hero without a counterpart, you remove the comparative signal and the reader just imitates rather than calibrates. So my position is that paired, unsanitized biography is uniquely suited to moral education because it forces active calibration rather than passive admiration. What would count against this? I want the evidence and arguments that should actually move me: psychological findings on exemplar learning, historical cases where comparison led to worse judgment, philosophical objections to the idea that morality can be calibrated like a scale, and whether the concept of moral noise collapses into ordinary complexity. Please rank the defeaters from decisive to awkward, say which ones defenders of this view could concede without destroying the position, and assess whether the view risks being insulated by qualifications—if it has been, is that a weakness? Also, please clearly separate what is settled among people who study this from what remains genuinely contested, and avoid presenting a disputed view as the consensus.
