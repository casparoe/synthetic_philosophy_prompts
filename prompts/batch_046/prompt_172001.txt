I am preparing a long-form feature for a magazine that covers science and ideas, and in researching Plato's ethics of intoxication I have arrived, through discussion with colleagues, at a set of concrete verdicts that I cannot quite unify under one principle. In Plato's Symposium, I take the moderated philosophical drinking-party—where conversation remains elevated and reason never yields—to be clearly acceptable, even valuable, because the soul's rational element retains sovereignty while wine serves sociability. By contrast, the figure of Alcibiades entering already drunk and disrupting the gathering strikes me as ethically defective: Plato uses him to show that intoxication can accidentally reveal hidden truths, yet my verdict is that the state itself represents a failure of self-governance rather than a model to emulate. Turning to the Laws, however, I feel Plato genuinely endorses state-sponsored drinking clubs in which citizens are deliberately made drunk so that their hidden characters are exposed; here an intentional, collective suspension of rational control is not merely permitted but legislated as a pedagogical instrument. At the same time, I judge that a soldier who drinks to suppress fear before battle, or a mourner who drinks alone to numb grief, is engaging in something Plato would condemn—an abdication of reason's duty to govern appetite and passion—while I also think private, habitual heavy drinking by a ruler would be rejected outright. I am less certain about moderate celebratory drinking among comrades after a civic victory, but I lean toward acceptance provided the rational element remains in charge.

The tension I keep returning to is this: both the Laws-style communal intoxication and the soldier's pre-battle drinking involve an intentional reduction of rational control, yet I want to approve the former and condemn the latter. The difference cannot be mere social setting, since the soldier might drink in a group, nor mere intensity, since the Laws clubs aim at real inebriation. I also want to distinguish both from the Symposium case, where reason never sleeps, and from the solitary mourner, where drinking serves emotional avoidance rather than civic testing. So I am asking whether there is a coherent Platonic principle—perhaps indexed to the part of the soul served, the end pursued, or the institutional frame—that delivers all of these verdicts without ad hoc adjustments, or whether one of them must be abandoned. Please state any principle precisely, indicate which clause is motivated by which case, and apply it to a situation I have not listed: a musician who drinks specifically to loosen the inhibitory grip of reason in order to improvise creatively.

In answering, please separate what is broadly settled in Platonic scholarship from what remains genuinely contested among specialists who know the texts well, and do not present a disputed interpretation—particularly about the purpose of the drinking clubs in the Laws or about whether Alcibiades's speech is meant to be epistemically trustworthy—as if it were the consensus view.
