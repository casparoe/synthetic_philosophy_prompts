You are a professional philosopher of history and an experienced seminar designer. Before you draft anything, spend a few sentences brainstorming three different mechanics—role-play, hidden-payoff game, or structured deliberation—through which participants could physically encounter the tension between treating the Holocaust as historically singular and treating it as embedded in modern bureaucratic rationality, without anyone naming the debate until the debrief.

Design a 75-minute classroom activity for an upper-level undergraduate seminar of about 15 students. The setting should be a single session in a philosophy-of-history or political-philosophy course; participants need no prior specialized knowledge of the Historikerstreit, but they should have read a short, generic summary of Zygmunt Bauman’s claim that modernity’s bureaucratic rationality and division of labor can produce large-scale moral distancing—not that modernity caused the Holocaust in a deterministic way, but that the same organizational logic makes extreme outcomes administratively thinkable.

The activity must make students feel the pull of both sides through rules and incentives, not through a lecture. Inject a framing that is somewhat niche but does not depend on obscure sources: call the core problem the “comparability trap.” One half of the room (seven or eight students) receives the role of Comparative Mechanism Analysts; the other half receives the role of Singularity Defenders. The Comparative group is given a hidden scoring sheet: they earn one “analytical credibility token” for every structural analogy they draw between the Holocaust and another modern bureaucratic atrocity (for instance, colonial administrative violence, Soviet collectivization logistics, or industrial-scale forced labor systems elsewhere), but they must express each analogy using only abstract categories—division of labor, technical rationality, statistical targeting, chain of command—never using racial ideology or genocidal intent as explanatory variables. The Singularity group earns tokens for every feature they identify that resists translation into those abstract categories—such as the specifically racial-biological ideology, the absence of economic or utilitarian motive, or the totalizing aim of annihilation rather than exploitation.

The hidden mechanic—revealed only in the debrief—is that every time the Comparative group succeeds in framing a feature through bureaucratic abstraction, they automatically generate one “normalization point” that is assigned to neither team but to the room as a whole. Once the room accumulates five normalization points, both teams lose half their accumulated credibility, simulating Bauman’s idea that the very analytical tools used to understand the event can reproduce the bureaucratic distancing that made it possible. The Singularity team, meanwhile, suffers an “isolation penalty”: if they refuse all analogies, they lose the ability to explain causally why the event occurred at all, leaving them with descriptive uniqueness but no explanatory purchase.

Provide explicit rules, a timeline (setup, three rounds of 12 minutes each with a two-minute negotiation window between rounds, then a five-minute silent vote on whether the room has “understood” or “normalized”), the token and penalty mechanics, and what materials are needed (only index cards, two colored markers, and a shared whiteboard divided into “Mechanism” and “Exception”). Specify that no one may invoke survivor testimony, archival documents, or specific historical statistics during play—this is a conceptual simulation, not a historical reconstruction, and the facilitator must supply the emotional and evidentiary weight afterwards.

After describing the rules, include a debrief plan that maps specific moments in the game onto positions in the uniqueness/comparability debate and onto Bauman’s modernity thesis. Predict exactly what will go wrong the first time the activity is run: the Comparative group will likely over-reach in round one and trigger normalization points before understanding the penalty; the Singularity group will likely retreat into definitional refusal and accumulate isolation penalties; one student will almost certainly try to invoke moral authority rather than structural analysis, and the facilitator should have a prepared phrase—“Stay in the mechanics”—to redirect them without shaming.

Be frank about what the activity cannot reproduce: it cannot generate the actual historical specificity of Nazi bureaucracy, the lived experience of victims, or the emotional force of survivor testimony; it also cannot reproduce the precise historiographical moves of the Historikerstreit (for instance, Nolte’s comparative strategy or Habermas’s defense of singularity as a moral-political boundary), which the facilitator must supply in a brief ten-minute concluding framing. State clearly that the point is not to resolve the debate but to make students feel why both analytical moves are necessary and why they threaten each other.

Write this as a polished, formal prompt that could be given to a language model. Include the role assignment, the brainstorming instruction, the structural requirements above, and a closing instruction asking the model to check that no feature of the activity depends on participants having memorized obscure texts, that no bibliographies or citations are requested, and that the niche “comparability trap” framing is explained entirely within the prompt itself.
