I serve on my university's Committee on Institutional Recognition and Communal Equity, and we must reach a concrete decision before Thursday's spring convocation session. The case before us concerns Marcus Chen, a graduating senior who founded a biotechnology venture now valued at approximately forty million dollars and who has received a national undergraduate research prize. Under long-standing policy, the university issues a public press release, hosts a named ceremony, and lists the honoree prominently on the institutional website. This year, however, the Student Welfare Council has petitioned us to suspend the individual ceremony and replace it with an anonymized 'Community Contribution Acknowledgement' that celebrates the graduating cohort without naming exceptional performers. They argue, drawing on the norms of Jantelagen and the dynamics of tall-poppy syndrome, that highly visible celebration of outlier success produces measurable psychological harm—particularly among first-generation and underrepresented students—by converting merit into a status hierarchy that undermines collective belonging. The Office of Advancement and several faculty senators counter that suppressing public acknowledgment violates the meritocratic ethos embedded in the American Dream narrative, discourages high aspiration, and misattributes structural inequality to individual visibility. They further contend that private recognition alone fails to provide the aspirational mirror that allows peers to imagine similar trajectories for themselves.

I am writing, in my capacity as a philosopher on the committee, to request a formal written opinion for circulation among the members. The opinion should recommend a concrete course of action—approve the traditional ceremony, approve with specified modifications, or reject in favor of the collective model—together with a clear time frame for implementation and for subsequent review. It should treat the strongest consideration on the losing side with genuine philosophical seriousness rather than dismissing it as mere sentiment or bureaucratic overreach. In particular, please engage the tension between what I shall call allocative visibility (the distribution of credit according to causal contribution) and expressive visibility (the use of public honor to signal which values the institution wishes to cultivate). The recommendation need not resolve every theoretical ambiguity, but it must be actionable by administrators who are not philosophers.

Please write your response in British English, observing British spelling and usage conventions throughout, though my own framing is in American English.
