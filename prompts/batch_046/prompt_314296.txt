I'm a PhD student in philosophy of history, and I'm stuck on something that feels like a contradiction in my department's norms. Historians routinely reject explicit counterfactual reasoning — asking 'what if the telegram had arrived an hour earlier' gets you dismissed as doing fiction, not scholarship. Yet those same historians constantly invoke inevitability: they'll say the fall of Rome was structurally inevitable, or that decolonisation was unavoidable after 1945, without ever spelling out the counterfactual baseline that makes 'inevitable' true. So there's this institutional practice: counterfactuals are banned, inevitability is standard. What philosophical commitments about causation and explanation does that practice actually presuppose? Does it require some kind of structural determinism that renders explicit counterfactuals redundant, or can a probabilistic, interventionist view of historical causation accomodate both? And if we replaced the implicit theory with one that treats inevitability as just high counterfactual robustness — meaning an outcome survives most minimal rewrites of initial conditions — what would actually have to change in peer review, syllabus design, or archival method? I don't need a reading list, just want to know whether the practice is philosophically coherent or just a disciplinary habit wearing philosophical clothes. Please answer in British English.
