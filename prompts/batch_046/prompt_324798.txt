In my philosophy of mind seminar we have spent several weeks on the old dispute over whether dreams are genuine experiences, focusing on Malcolm’s argument that sleep removes the conditions for applying experiential concepts and Dennett’s counter-proposal that dreams might be loaded into consciousness upon waking like cassette tapes, leaving no experiential residue during sleep itself. Despite broad agreement about the phenomenology of recall, the unreliability of dream reports, the suppression of motor output in REM sleep, and the absence of any behavioral criterion for “experiencing” during unconsciousness, the two camps—those who treat dreams as real phenomenal events and those who treat them as retrospective narratives constructed at awakening—have not shifted at all. My working hypothesis is that the impasse is not really about sleep neuroscience but about a hidden disagreement over whether “experience” requires a functional accessibility relation to the subject’s cognitive economy, or whether phenomenal presence can float entirely free of access conditions; yet neither side seems able to defend their criterion without begging the question against the other. Is this diagnosis correct—do we have a bedrock conceptual clash masked as an empirical dispute about sleep—or is there instead a disguised disagreement about, say, whether cortical integration persists in REM in a way that would license one criterion over the other?
