I have been studying proposals for citizen assemblies selected by lottery, and I designed a hypothetical municipal system to test my understanding. In this system, 120 residents are drawn at random to form a zoning assembly. These 120 then vote internally to select 12 who will draft the actual regulations. Additionally, if 30 members of the original 120 petition for it, any decision by the 12 must be put to a city-wide referendum rather than taking effect immediately.

For a community education course I am helping to facilitate, I was asked to evaluate a fellow learner's written response to the following prompt: What democratic values does this layered design try to balance, and where does it risk undermining the justification for using random selection in the first place?

Below is the learner's answer. The answer reads:

This proposal tries to combine descriptive representation with deliberative efficiency. The initial lot of 120 gives every resident an equal chance to be included, which addresses the democratic deficit of self-selection in volunteer assemblies. The second-stage election of 12 from within the group introduces competence-based filtering without reintroducing campaign finance, since the electorate is small and already representative. Because the 120 remain randomly drawn, the peer-vote doesn't destroy statistical representativeness; it just shifts who holds the pen within a mirror-like group. The recall provision—30 members triggering a referendum—acts as a safeguard against groupthink, letting the broader assembly veto outliers. The design also fits Rousseau's idea that a randomly selected subset can carry the general will of the whole if the original pool was legitimate, so the 12 can bind the 120 without violating democratic equality. The main risk is capture: studies of mini-publics show that internal voting tends to favor the educated and articulate, so the 12 may skew toward the city's professional class despite the random origin. A deeper problem is that allowing a referendum overturn undermines the epistemic rationale for delegating to a small panel; if every decision can be appealed to the whole city, the 12 have no real authority. Still, the design improves on pure election by removing campaign dynamics from the first gate.
