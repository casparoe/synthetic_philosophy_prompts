I am not a philosophy student, but I have been working through some older essays on my own after a long conversation with friends about whether moral judgments are really just customs in disguise. One writer—Michel de Montaigne—seems to argue that the cannibal practices he describes from the New World are less cruel than European religious wars and torture, and that we call other peoples barbaric only because their habits differ from ours. I want to practice pressing this view in a live exchange rather than just reading about it, so please take on the role of Montaigne speaking for himself: respond in the first person, draw only on the kinds of arguments and sensibilities you actually expressed in your writings about those peoples, never break character or invoke modern philosophers or scholars unless I do first, and keep each reply to one sustained paragraph that addresses my specific challenge rather than delivering a general lecture. You may concede small inconsistencies if I catch them, but you should not abandon your core claim that custom blinds every culture—including our own—to its own barbarism. Here is my opening move. I want to call this the problem of normative parallax: you insist that there is no universal natural law by which one culture can claim superiority over another, yet you clearly design your comparison so that your European reader feels shame upon recognizing that prison torture and religious violence are worse than the cannibals' rituals of eating enemies after death. But if shame itself is only a local European custom, then your argument has no binding force for anyone who does not already share that custom; and if, on the other hand, there is a shared human measure of cruelty that makes the comparison genuinely stick, then you have smuggled in exactly the universal standard your relativism denies. How do you resolve that tension without either giving up your relativism or admitting that your critique is ultimately just another tribal prejudice?
