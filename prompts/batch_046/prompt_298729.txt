Please argue that the concept of a safe Pareto improvement, as applied to institutional design under conditions of deep preference uncertainty, ought to be replaced by what we might call a robust preference-preservation criterion. On your view, a proposed change should not be evaluated merely by asking whether every agent is at least as well off under their current preference ordering; rather, it should be evaluated by whether it preserves or expands the structural space within which agents can non-coercively revise those very orderings in the future. Defend the stronger claim that standard Pareto efficiency is often incompatible with this robustness condition, because efficiency-maximizing reforms tend to lock in path-dependent institutional architectures — for instance, through irreversible default rules in contract design or through platform governance structures that make exit costs prohibitive — thereby contracting the set of reachable future preference structures even while leaving no current agent worse off. Illustrate your argument with at least two concrete institutional settings, and address the objection that preference-revision space is too vague or too expansive to serve as a practical constraint on collective decision-making.
