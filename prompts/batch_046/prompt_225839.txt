I am presenting a paper at an interdisciplinary seminar on philosophy of history and technology. My thesis is that Nietzsche's framework of monumental, antiquarian, and critical history—developed in his reflection on the uses and disadvantages of history for life—does not exhaust the modes of historical consciousness available today, and that algorithmic curation and generative AI have produced a distinct hybrid I call "recombinant history." In this mode, the preservationist accumulation characteristic of the antiquarian is fused with the aspirational, exemplary projection characteristic of the monumental, but it systematically avoids the destructive, life-affirming rupture that defines the critical. I argue that this hybrid explains a specific contemporary paralysis: culture now archives, remixes, and projects the past at unprecedented scale without ever allowing it to genuinely challenge or overturn present forms of life. The audience will be about twelve people: half are specialists in nineteenth-century German philosophy who know the Untimely Meditations closely and are deeply suspicious of anachronistic applications of Nietzsche to technology; the other half are media theorists and philosophers of technology who doubt that pre-digital conceptual schemes can illuminate algorithmic culture without reducing technology to an illustrative footnote. Both groups care about textual fidelity, conceptual precision, and whether my argument offers something that existing accounts of digital memory and algorithmic culture do not already cover. Finally, identify the single objection I am most hoping nobody raises, explain precisely why it is dangerous for my argument, and suggest how I might survive it if it comes.
