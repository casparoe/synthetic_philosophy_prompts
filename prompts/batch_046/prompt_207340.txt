You are a senior philosopher of language and hermeneutics with deep familiarity in both analytic and continental traditions, and you have advised interdisciplinary research groups before. Before answering, take a moment to brainstorm: list the major open questions in the debate over authorial intention and textual meaning, then identify which ones are upstream of practical interpretive work versus which ones are mostly internal to theory. You are advising a working group of five researchers — two philosophers of language, two legal hermeneutics scholars, and one literary archivist — who have 18 months and a modest grant to investigate one focused problem: how the concept of authorial intention should function in institutional practices of interpretation, specifically in legal statutory interpretation and in archival cataloging of ambiguous manuscripts. They do not need a survey; they need a defended agenda.

Your task: identify the three or four open questions or lines of work in the authorial-intention debate that are most worth this group's limited effort, rank them by priority, and defend the ranking. The ranking should specify: (a) which questions are upstream — that is, settling them changes how the others are approached; (b) which are tractable within 18 months by philosophers and legal scholars rather than requiring empirical archival discoveries; (c) which carry real practical weight for the group's institutional settings (courts, archives) versus which are mostly ornamental in theoretical debates; and (d) what concrete result over the next year would cause you to reorder them.

In constructing your agenda, inject and work with the following distinction, which the group is testing: the difference between causal-intentional origin (what mental state actually produced the text), normative-intentional constraint (what an interpreter is entitled to treat as binding on meaning), and semantic residue — those structural patterns in a text that generate stable interpretive effects regardless of whether they match either origin or constraint. Ask specifically whether resolving the status of semantic residue is upstream of the other two, or whether it is a derivative problem. Use American English. Think step by step, and do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Do not quote from texts without verification; if you reference specific claims from named thinkers, paraphrase them. Focus on reasoning and dependency relations, not on summarizing well-known positions.
