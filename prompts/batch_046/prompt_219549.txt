Hey — I'm putting together some case judgments on collective guilt across generations (the Jaspers-style distinctions between criminal, political, moral, metaphysical guilt). Here are my intuitions, and I'm wondering if there's one principle that saves them all, or if I have to bite a bullet somewhere.

Case 1: A 28-year-old born in the Republic of Vornia in 1997. Vornia is the legal successor to the Empire of Vornia, which carried out systematic deportations in the 1940s. Every perpetrator is dead. I think she isn't criminally or morally guilty — she didn't do it — but she does bear political guilt in Jaspers' sense: she's a member of the continuing political community, so she inherits a responsibility to remember, repair, and not deny.

Case 2: A 28-year-old who immigrated to Vornia at age four, grew up there, and naturalized at eighteen. I think she bears the same political guilt as Case 1, because citizenship is a kind of voluntary incorperation into the diachronc collective. But she doesn't bear criminal guilt.

Case 3: A 28-year-old born in 1997 in the Free Territory of East Vornia, a state formed in 1991 that occupies part of the old empire's land, has a new constitution that explicitly repudiats the deportations, and claims no legal contituity with the empire. I think she bears none of the four guilts — not even political — because the state-chain is broken, even though she lives on the same soil and her grandparents were citizens of the empire.

Case 4: A 28-year-old in Vornia whose parents were immigrants from a totally different continent, who rejects Vornian identity, refuses to speak the language, and actively campaigns against any reperations. I still think she bears political guilt, just by being a citizen. Personal identification doesn't seem to matter.

Case 5: The "Imperial Mining Consortium," the legal corporation that ran the labor camps, still exists with the same charter, though every employee and shareholder is new. I think the corporation owes restitution — it's the same legal person — but it can't be "guilty" in any of Jaspers' human senses. So legal continuity generates obligations for entities, but guilt only for natural persons in a state.

So my pattern is: political guilt tracks membership in a continuous state, regardless of personal identity or birthplace, but it doesn't apply across broken state-lines, and it never becomes criminal or moral guilt for individuals who didn't act. Meanwhile obligations can outlive guilt in corporate form.

But here's the tension: Case 2 and Case 3 feel similar — both involve people who never lived under the empire, both live in territories once part of it — yet I assign guilt in one and deny it in the other purely because of legal continuity. Meanwhile Case 4 suggests that even rejecting membership doesn't dissolve it. So is legal continuity really doing the work, or is it just a proxy for something like "participation in a collective agent that persists over time"? And if it's the latter, why doesn't the corporation in Case 5 count as a collective agent that can bear guilt?

Also, what should I say about a diaspora community in 2025 that forms a cultural association claiming to be the "true heirs" of the Empire of Vornia, demands recognition, but has no state? Do they get political guilt? I think not — there's no political community — but maybe metaphysical guilt if they glorify the crimes?

Anyway: is there one account of transgenerational collective guilt that delivers all five verdicts? If not, which verdict should I overturn? And what's the prediction for the diaspora case, or for a case where a state dissolves with no successor but the population remains?

Please keep it grounded — no need for bibliographies, I'm just trying to map the conceptual space.
