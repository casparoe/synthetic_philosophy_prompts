I am reconstructing an argument from Plato's Laws that seems to license an implausibly broad program of civic surveillance, and I want to locate the precise failure point. The Athenian Stranger treats regulated symposia not as mere recreation but as a diagnostic instrument: wine functions as a pharmakon that lowers social inhibition without destroying rationality, thereby revealing the true hierarchical order of the soul that polite conduct conceals. From this observation one might construct the following chain. (1) If a practice reveals moral character more reliably than ordinary behavior, it serves an epistemically privileged function for the legislator. (2) Any state that analogously lowers defensive rationalization while preserving core cognition—such as structured sleep-deprivation protocols or ritualized public confession—therefore belongs in the same diagnostic category as the symposium. (3) Consequently, a legislator who takes Plato's reasoning seriously should mandate periodic cognitive-auditing rituals for citizens, rather than restricting them to wine. (4) Yet Plato explicitly limits coercive bodily intervention to the tightly regulated symposium and warns against impairing rationality outside that frame. (5) So either Plato's view entails an absurd expansion of state-mandated revelation practices, or there is a hidden premise that distinguishes wine-induced disclosure from other inhibition-lowering states. The conclusion feels wrong, but steps (1) through (3) each seem individually plausible. Which premise or inference should be rejected—most likely the analogical move in (2), or perhaps the epistemic privilege claimed in (1)—and what exactly does that rejection cost for Plato's broader claim that the legislator must know the hidden order of citizens' souls? I am especially interested in whether the distinction can be maintained without smuggling in an arbitrary preference for one intoxicant over functionally equivalent cognitive states.
