I recently read an essay in a popular philosophy magazine that summarized Marx’s account of exploitation and alienation for a general audience. The author presented exploitation as essentially a moral complaint about unfair wages: capitalists pay workers less than the full value those workers create, and this judgment only holds if the labor theory of value is correct; once that economic framework is abandoned, the critique collapses into mere sentiment. As for alienation, the essay treated it as essentially a psychological condition—something like modern burnout or job dissatisfaction—where workers feel disconnected from what they do. The author added that Marx intended this diagnosis mainly for industrial factory labor, and that it functions as an emotional add-on to the economic argument rather than an independent structural analysis. From these two points, the essay concluded that Marx’s overall critique is doubly vulnerable: it rests on an obsolete economic theory and on subjective moods that empirical studies of workplace happiness can easily undermine.

I am writing a course paper that touches on the 1844 Manuscripts and on later analytic reconstructions of Marx’s normative economics, and I want to audit this summary before I rely on it. Could you separate what is accurate, what is a common but misleading simplification, and what is a misrepresentation that a careful reader of Marx would reject? In particular, does the conclusion—that the critique collapses if the labor theory of value fails and if workers report satisfaction—actually follow from the parts that survive correction? I am also interested in whether the essay’s framing ignores Marx’s distinction between formal and real subsumption of labor under capital, and whether that distinction changes how we should assess the scope of alienation beyond the factory floor. Please treat this as an accuracy audit rather than a request for a bibliography or reading list: I want to know which parts of the summary I can trust, which would distort Marx’s position if repeated, and whether the formal-versus-real subsumption point exposes a further gap in the popular account.
