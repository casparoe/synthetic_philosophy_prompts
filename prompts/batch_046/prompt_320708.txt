In rationalist and decision-theory discussions, Buridan's ass is routinely mapped onto algorithmic deadlock: the claim is that a rational agent with exactly equal utility for two options behaves like a comparison loop with no tie-breaker, hanging until an exogenous randomizer—a coin flip, a noise term, or a hard-coded lexicographic rule—is injected. This analogy is often used to license the conclusion that "pure" choice without prior preference is incoherent, and that all real decision-making must bottom out either in hidden inclination or in arbitrary noise. How far does the algorithmic-deadlock analogy actually hold? Also assess whether the conclusion that preference-less choice is impossible survives the breakdown of the mapping, or whether the analogy was carrying the argument all along. If there is a better analogy for symmetric resolution—perhaps spontaneous symmetry breaking in physics, or the phenomenology of arbitrary decision—sketch it and say whether it does better.
