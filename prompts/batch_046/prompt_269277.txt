I'm preparing the final assessment for a graduate seminar on historical epistemology and the politics of knowledge production, and I want one essay question that treats archival absence not as a simple evidentiary gap but as an active, structurally produced feature of record-keeping systems. The question should present a concrete scenario—specifically, a nineteenth-century colonial census that meticulously records land tenure and tax liability for male heads of household according to patrilineal inheritance rules, while containing no field for women's agricultural labor, no category for kinship obligations that fall outside the nuclear household, and no notation for communities that refused registration—without requiring students to cite specific scholars or texts by name. Instead, students should be required to analytically separate three distinct modes by which silence operates in such an archive: first, silencing as deliberate destruction or suppression of existing records; second, silencing as categorical omission hard-wired into the bureaucratic form itself, where the categories of collection determine what can be seen; and third, silencing as an epistemic mismatch, where the archive's classificatory logic is structurally incapable of registering a practice or relation that existed in a form foreign to that logic.

Please write the exam prompt itself (calibrated for a 45-minute in-class essay with an expected response of roughly 800 words), followed by a model answer of approximately 600 words, and a marking scheme that distinguishes an A-level response from a B-level response through specific, named moves. An A answer should keep the three modes rigorously separate, apply each precisely to the census scenario, and articulate what is methodologically and politically at stake in choosing one mode over another—for instance, recognizing that categorical omission does not necessarily entail intentional suppression, and that treating absence as proof of absence risks reproducing the archive's own epistemic violence. A B answer, by contrast, should be marked down for collapsing the distinctions, treating all silence as monolithic, or conflating the lack of data with proof that the practice did not exist. The marking scheme should also flag specific errors, such as attributing agency to bureaucratic routine without justification, or failing to address how the historian's own methodological choices replicate or resist the archive's silences. The entire exercise should be answerable by a student who understands the conceptual architecture but should be unanswerable by anyone who has merely memorized standard labels about "archive and power."
