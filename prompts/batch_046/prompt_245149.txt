I'm taking a course on comparative poltics and my roommate keeps insisting that "democratic backsliding" is just a partisan label for "the other side won." She mentioned her professor's working group has been tracking actual cases for years, and so far they've said yes, clear backsliding, for Hungary's court-packing and media-authority consolidation, Poland's judicial disciplinary chamber and capture of public broadcasting, and Turkey's 2017 presidential-system overhaul with its civil-service purges. But for the US after 2020 they said no — they treated state-level fake-elector schemes and partisan election-board takeovers as "acute democratic stress" but not backsliding, since federal checks ultimately held and there was no durable instutional capture.

Next they're weighing a hypothetical case they take seriously: Norvalla, a parliamentary democracy with fully competitive elections, intact courts, and unchanged civil-liberties protections, has just created a "Constitutional Safeguard Office." The PM appoints its members for twelve-year terms, and the office can annul any parliamentary statute that conflicts with a vaguely defined "national strategic interest"; it has already struck down one opposition-backed labor law. Predict whether her working group will call this backsliding or not. Lay out the two or three implicit rules that could explain their earlier four verdicts — maybe one centered on horizontal-accountability capture, one on electoral-competitiveness erosion, one on liberal-norm decay — say what each predicts for Norvalla, then take a committed position on what they'll actually reccomend and why. Also name the one further case — say, a country with systematically rigged elections but fully independent courts and media — that would finally settle which rule they're running.
