ok so i've been trying to work out whether Sen's entitlement view actually wipes out the category of "natural famine" entirely, and i think i've got an argument that it does, but the conclusion feels wrong in a way i can't pin down. here's the chain:

1. Sen's core claim is that famine is never caused by a decline in food availability (FAD) but always by a collapse in people's entitlements — the legal and economic mechanisms that let them command food.

2. In Bengal 1943 aggregate food supply was actually higher than in some non-famine years; people died because wages collapsed and prices spiked, cutting off access.

3. During the Irish famine exports kept leaving while the poor lost purchasing power; same pattern.

4. In the Great Leap Forward local procurement and denial of relief turned harvest failures into mass death, so the proximate killer was instutional, not meteorlogical.

5. So in every well-studied case, entitlement collapse is present and FAD is either absent or not sufficient to explain mortality.

6. Now, if X is always the real cause and Y is never sufficient, then Y is never part of the causal story at all.

7. That means drought, flood, or crop failure are never causes of famine — only institutional failures are.

8. But consider a small isolated community with no state or market, hit by a drought that wipes out their crops. They starve. There's no government or exchange system to fail, yet they've clearly lost access to food.

9. If we apply Sen's framework consistently, we have to say their entitlements collapsed too — they lost command over food — which makes this a political or institutional failure.

10. But there's no politics or institution here. So the framework either misclassifies a pure natural disaster as a crime, or it stretches "entitlement" so thin that it just means "ability to eat," which makes the theory empty.

11. Therefore Sen's view proves too much: it dissolves the natural/political distinction and forces every starvation event into the "political crime" box.

Which step should I reject? I really want to keep (1)-(5) because they seem empirically solid. The conclusion (11) feels like it must be wrong — there should be room for genuinely natural famines. But if I drop (6) or (7) i'm not sure i'm being consistent with Sen. And (8)-(9) seem like a valid counterexample. So where's the hidden mistake — is there an equivocation in "entitlement," a scope error in (6), or am i wrong to think (8) is a counterexample?
