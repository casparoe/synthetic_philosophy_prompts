I'm grading answers for a small seminar on strategic reasoning (the kind of material discussed in long-form rationalist forums). The exam question was: "Historical analogies like Munich (1938), Vietnam (1965–73), and the Thucydides trap (Athens/Sparta) are frequently used to frame contemporary strategic choices. (a) Briefly state what policy lesson each analogy is typically taken to support. (b) Critics argue that all three mislead in structurally similar ways. Identify one shared logical weakness of analogical reasoning that applies to Munich and Vietnam, and then explain whether the same weakness applies to Graham Allison's 'Thucydides trap' framing. (c) Finally, address the objection that rejecting analogies leaves policymakers with no guidance: is there a defensible middle ground between treating history as a template and ignoring it entirely?"

Here is the student's answer:

"(a) The Munich analogy (1938) is usually invoked to argue that accommodating an expansionist power—giving Hitler the Sudetenland—only invites further aggression; the lesson is that deterrence and early resistance are necessary. The Vietnam analogy (mid-1960s onward) is used to warn that limited military commitments can slide into open-ended quagmires with ambiguous objectives and no clear exit strategy, counseling caution about intervention in civil conflicts or asymmetric wars. The Thucydides trap, drawn from Allison's reading of the Peloponnesian War, suggests that when a rising power threatens to displace a ruling one, the resulting fear and structural stress make armed conflict highly likely regardless of specific intentions; the lesson is usually that great-power transition must be managed with exceptional care to avoid catastrophic war.

(b) For Munich and Vietnam, the shared weakness is indeed decontextualization: analysts map surface features—appeasement dynamics, guerrilla warfare—onto present cases without accounting for changed institutional or technological environments. The same flaw applies directly to the Thucydides framing, which is essentially the security dilemma projected onto great-power politics: as China arms and expands, the US feels threatened and responds, creating a spiral that makes conflict inevitable. Because Thucydides himself warned that analogies are unreliable—he insisted that each war has unique causes—the framing named after him is self-undermining; if we take his methodological caution seriously, we should reject the trap as just another misleading comparison between ancient Greece and modern states.

(c) Rejecting analogies entirely does not leave policymakers blind; they can instead rely on structural models that identify causal mechanisms—like power-transition dynamics—without pretending that history literally repeats. The middle ground is to treat historical cases as sources of hypotheses rather than templates, testing whether the mechanism (fear of displacement, domestic pressure for escalation) is present now rather than assuming similarity because the past 'looks like' the present."
