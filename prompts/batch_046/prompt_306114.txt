A philosophy of logic blogger recently summarized Lewis Carroll’s 1895 Mind dialogue What the Tortoise Said to Achilles in the following way. First, Carroll shows that deductive validity requires accepting not only premises but also rules of inference—specifically, that one must accept a principle linking premises to conclusion (for example, that A and B together entail Z). Second, if one tries to justify that principle by turning it into an explicit premise, one generates a new gap between the expanded premises and the conclusion, producing an infinite regress of ever-higher rules. Third, the blogger claims this demonstrates that every formal system contains an ungrounded inference license: a primitive rule that cannot be derived within the system but must be accepted without proof. Fourth, the blogger asserts that this is essentially the same phenomenon Gödel formalized decades later—namely, that formal systems are inherently incomplete because they cannot license all of their own inferential moves. Finally, the blogger concludes that Carroll was therefore an early anti-formalist: human reasoning transcends any formal system because we can immediately see the validity of an inference without reducing it to premises or rules.

I am unsure how much of this to trust.
