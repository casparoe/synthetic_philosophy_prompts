A history podcast summarized Hannah Arendt's account of the Eichmann trial like this: she arrived in Jerusalem expecting a monstrous anti-Semite and instead found a dull, career-obsessed bureaucrat who seemed proud of his efficiency and showed little personal hatred toward Jews. From this she coined "the banality of evil," by which she meant that in modern times evil no longer requires satanic motives or deep ideological hatred; it is carried out by ordinary, thoughtless people who follow administrative rules and never examine what they are doing. The host went further, claiming that Arendt therefore believed the Holocaust was caused mainly by modern bureaucratic systems rather than by anti-Semitism, and that she essentially excused Eichmann by treating him as an unthinking cog who could not be blamed because he lacked malicious intent. The host also suggested that Arendt intended this as a universal theory of all evil, but added that it applies only to modern societies, since in older societies evil was always personal, passionate, and deliberately intended.

I feel this captures something real about her argument, yet I suspect it distorts her in ways that matter—particularly the claim that she denied the role of ideology or that she excused Eichmann. Could you audit this summary point by point? Which parts are accurate to Arendt's view, which are common popular distortions, and what would she likely object to? Specifically, does her concept of "banality" apply to all evil or only to a particular kind, and does the host's conclusion that bureaucracy rather than hatred caused the Holocaust actually follow from her analysis? I am not looking for a bibliography, just an assessment of whether this is a fair rendering I can rely on.
