Here's a puzzle for you. In large-scale disaster relief, agencies like FEMA or major NGOs allocate budgets by trying to maximize expected lives saved—if the same resources could rescue a hundred people in one region or five in another, they send the aid to the hundred. But in clinical mass-casualty triage, the standard protocol tells a medic to stabilize the one patient bleeding out right in front of them, even if that burns supplies that could have saved three others waiting nearby. So we count numbers aggressively in funding rooms and seem to ignore them at the bedside. What philosophical commitments does each practice presuppose? Does the funding model strictly require that numbers count in the Taurek sense, or is it just about tractability and measurability? And does triage actually deny that numbers matter, or does it hide a different consistency condition? Reconstruct the implicit theory behind each, say whether they're genuinely in tension or can be reconciled under one view, and sketch what a relief-and-triage system built on the idea that "the numbers don't count" would have to change. I'm especially interested in whether the practice is philosophicaly underdetermined—maybe both patterns are compatible with several theories and the apparent conflict is superficial.
