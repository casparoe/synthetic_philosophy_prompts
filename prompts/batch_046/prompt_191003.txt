Assume the persona of a professional philosopher of mind and practicing poet, equally at home with the literature on perceptual psychology and with formal verse. Before you compose anything, take a few minutes to brainstorm how the architecture of a blank-verse sonnet—specifically the volta between the octave and the sestet—can be made to perform philosophical work rather than merely contain it. Think step by step about how syntactic and imagistic repetition can carry an argument.

Your task is to write a 14-line poem in unrhymed iambic pentameter, divided into an octave (lines 1–8) and a sestet (lines 9–14), on a phenomenon I will call stubborn-boundary penetration. The concept is a specific, less-discussed asymmetry within the broader debate over cognitive penetration of perception. Ordinarily we ask whether belief or expectation can alter literal seeing—whether expecting a face in noise makes one see a face. But consider the reverse-direction case: a perceiver, through prolonged conceptual training, comes to treat a particular shadow not as a mere absence of light but as a bounded object with edges, weight, and resistance. Later, through further conceptual revision—say, accepting a strictly physicalist ontology—she sincerely updates her belief: the shadow is only absence, nothing more. The cognition revises downward; yet the perceptual boundary does not. She still experiences the same contour, the same presence, the same refusal to be seen through.

Write in the first-person voice of this perceiver, speaking after the belief has already changed. The octave should present the perceptual experience with the language of objecthood and boundary. The volta at line 9 must announce the revised belief explicitly—stating that she now knows the shadow to be mere absence—yet the sestet must echo, with minimal alteration, the same sensory descriptions and syntactic patterns used in the octave. The formal repetition should do the argumentative labor: the unchanged meter and mirrored imagery demonstrate that cognition has failed to penetrate the perceptual habit, even though that habit was originally formed through cognition. Do not resolve the tension in the final lines; end on the persistence of the boundary. Make the rendering precise enough that a specialist in the cognitive-penetration literature recognizes the specific asymmetry—belief updated without corresponding perceptual revision—rather than only the generic theme. Identify which phrases in the octave you will mirror in the sestet, then compose the poem.
