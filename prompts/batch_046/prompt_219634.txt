I have just come out of a long, somewhat circular argument with a close friend about Confucian role ethics. She kept reducing the entire framework to what she called virtue ethics with family values, and I found myself unable to articulate precisely why the relational structure of obligation is philosophically distinct from character cultivation rather than merely sociologically different. Rather than continuing the debate informally, I want to convert our disagreement into a rigorous assessment instrument.

Please act as a professional philosopher specializing in comparative moral philosophy and an experienced exam designer for an upper-level undergraduate seminar. Before drafting anything, take a moment to brainstorm: what is the precise philosophical crux that separates role-based moral reasoning from character-based virtue ethics? How can a question be constructed so that a student who has merely memorized labels such as ren, li, xiao, yi, and de will fail, while a student who grasps the internal architecture of role-obligation will succeed? Think step by step, and ensure that your design rewards philosophical reconstruction over label recognition.

Then, design a take-home essay examination for third- or fourth-year philosophy students: 72 hours, 2,000 words, worth 50 marks in a 100-mark course. The prompt should inject a niche but philosophically central tension. Ask students to examine whether the Confucian concept of quan—discretionary weighing or contextual adjustment within role obligations—commits the tradition to a form of moral particularism that rejects universal moral principles altogether, or whether it operates within a framework of universalizable role-rules that merely allow for contextual adjustment. Students must clearly distinguish between role-obligation (yi) and virtue-cultivation (de), and they must address how role-conflict—for instance, when filial duty conflicts with civic responsibility—is resolved without appealing to an external, non-relational moral principle imported from outside the role structure.

Provide the following in a polished, formal format:

1. The exact exam prompt, with clear instructions, word count, time limit, and the specific conceptual task.
2. A model answer that demonstrates the four specific moves a top response must make: (a) a structural distinction between relational roles and character virtues, explaining why roles generate obligations rather than merely expressing cultivated traits; (b) an analysis of quan as internal to role-reasoning rather than as an arbitrary exception to fixed rules; (c) an evaluation of whether this practice entails moral particularism or can be reconciled with universalizable principles internal to the role framework; (d) a clearly defended thesis on whether role ethics ultimately needs or can dispense with universal principles, supported by reasoning rather than assertion.
3. A marking scheme allocating marks across these moves—suggest approximately 10 marks for the structural distinction, 15 for the analysis of quan, 15 for the particularism evaluation, and 10 for thesis, coherence, and critical engagement—together with a list of specific errors that should cost marks. These should include: conflating ren with Aristotelian eudaimonia or benevolence; treating quan as mere personal preference or arbitrary exception-making rather than disciplined contextual judgment; importing Kantian universalization uncritically as the only possible alternative to particularism; reducing role-conflict resolution to a simple hierarchical ranking without philosophical justification; and failing to distinguish yi from de.

The question should be unanswerable by reciting a summary of Confucian ethics. It should separate students who understand the philosophical architecture from those who have only memorized the vocabulary.
