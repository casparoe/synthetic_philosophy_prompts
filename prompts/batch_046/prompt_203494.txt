I'm designing a single ninety-minute session for an interdisciplinary reading group—philosophers and cognitive scientists, about half of whom have never formally encountered anthropic reasoning—and I want them to leave grasping precisely why the presumptuous philosopher's inference is presumptuous, not merely counterintuitive. Rather than opening with urn models or cosmological constants, I want the session carried entirely by one concrete scenario I have constructed: a remote research archive maintains two competing institutional histories. History A holds that the archive was founded in 1950 with a permanent staff of twelve; History B holds that it was founded in 2000 with a staff of two hundred. Every present employee knows only that they are currently employed there, that the archive has operated continuously, and that the present census matches exactly the staff size predicted by whichever history is true. I want to use this to build the distinction between self-sampling and self-indication assumptions.

Plan the explanatory arc: what conceptual dependency must be in place before indexical evidence can even be introduced, and why ordinary Bayesian updating over objective frequencies is insufficient for it; the precise sequence in which self-sampling and self-indication should be presented so that neither collapses into common sense or into the other; the necessary features the archive scenario must have—specifically why it must turn on observer-counting rather than on simple frequency or base rate—and the exact point at which the analogy is most likely to break or mislead; the misconceptions this particular audience will bring or form during the session (for instance, treating self-indication as a disguised version of the base-rate fallacy, or treating self-sampling as subjective Bayes with a flat prior over worlds) and the specific stage in the sequence where each should be headed off; one check-for-understanding question at the end that forces them to apply the distinction to a modified version of the archive case—say, one in which the staff discover a third competing history—rather than merely reciting the definitions; and what formal machinery—explicit reference-class framing, observer-moment weighting, or the connection to the doomsday argument—should be openly excluded at this level and acknowledged as such.
