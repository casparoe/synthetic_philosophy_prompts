But I think both miss an intermediate mechanism I'm tentatively calling "honor-cost inversion": a practice hangs on not because it's seen as moral, but because quitting it carries a bigger status-hit than keeping it up—until some exogenous shock (market integration, new technology, war) flips the calculus so that quitting becomes the honorable move and persisting looks pathetic.

Can you write an essay weighing whether norms like slavery and dueling end through rational argument, economic interest, or honor dynamics? Take Williams and Appiah seriously, but also evaluate whether this "honor-cost inversion" adds anything distinct, or whether it's just Appiah with extra steps. Reach and defend a real conclusion: which factor is primary, and can a culture deliberately engineer the inversion, or does it have to wait for outside shocks? I'd also like you to compare slavery and dueling directly—economic interests loom huge for one and seem minimal for the other—so your view should explain both, not just the easy case.
