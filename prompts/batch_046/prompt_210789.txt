I cover philosophy and political theory for a magazine, and I am drafting a long-form essay on Machiavelli’s pairing of fortune and virtù. Before filing, I want a consistency check on my interpretive commitments. Here is where I stand.

First, I read virtù not as moral virtue but as a flexible, improvisational capacity—closer to skilled adaptation than to any fixed list of traits. Second, I take fortune to be genuinely irreducible: it is the structural residue of events that resist rational anticipation, not merely hidden determinism or “bad luck.” Third, I accept Machiavelli’s hydraulic image—preparation can channel fortune but never eliminate it, so some contingency always remains. Fourth, I think virtù can be institutionalized: a well-ordered republic can display collective adaptive capacity even when individual rulers lack personal virtù. Fifth, I hold that this institutional strength only delays collapse—every political order eventually meets an overwhelming contingency that exceeds its adaptive range, so long-term stability is conceptually impossible. Sixth, I believe moral blame for political failure should be suspended when outcomes are clearly driven by this irreducible contingency, since blame requires control. Seventh, and more speculatively, I am drawn to the idea that at the limit fortune and virtù collapse into one another—what we call fortune is just virtù that has not yet been recognized or structurally incorporated.

Do these hang together? I suspect the institutionalization claim and the inevitable-collapse claim pull in opposite directions, and that the irreducibility claim is hard to square with the collapse-into-one-another thesis. I also wonder whether the tension between personal improvisation and collective institutional virtù is only apparent. Please rank the real tensions by severity, indicate for each what the cheapest revision would cost me, and identify the single change that cleans up the most problems. I am not sure which commitment I would be willing to drop.
