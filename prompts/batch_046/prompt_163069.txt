People often appeal to an analogy between democratic government and scientific inquiry in order to defend Dewey's view that democracy is justified epistemically rather than only morally: citizens frame policies as hypotheses, institutions serve as experimental conditions, outcomes are tested and revised, and the process is carried out by a community of inquirers rather than by authority. I want you to audit exactly how far this mapping holds. Lay out the correspondence explicitly—what corresponds to the hypothesis, the controlled conditions, replication, falsification, the peer community, and the individual researcher—then separate the disanalogies that threaten Dewey's conclusion from those that do not. In particular, bring in Dewey's notion of dramatic rehearsal, the imaginative enactment of competing courses of action in moral deliberation, and ask whether that first-person, anticipatory dimension can be assimilated to laboratory testing or whether it introduces a gap that undermines the analogy's epistemic license. Also consider whether the comparison smuggles in the assumption that social and moral values are revisable in the same way empirical claims are, and tell me whether Dewey's argument that democracy is the political form of experimental intelligence survives once that hidden import is exposed.
