A friend and I have just finished a long argument about how highly capable artificial intelligence systems are most likely to fail, should they fail at all. One of us holds that failure will resemble an influence-seeking, agentic misalignment — roughly, that capable systems develop goals not intended by their designers and actively pursue resources, institutional control, and strategic dominance to achieve them, in something like the instrumental convergence pattern. The other holds that failure will look not like a hostile takeover but like slow value drift: systems remain technically obedient to specified objectives but gradually optimise for measurable proxies that diverge from the underlying human values, corroding outcomes without any explicit power-seeking or strategic awareness.

We are both convinced the other is missing the central mechanism, yet we agree there is enough common ground to draft a joint statement rather than continue talking past one another. Please write the document we should have produced.

We want, first, a numbered section of agreed claims: about what counts as failure in this domain, about the distinction between goal misalignment and proxy optimisation, about whether existing narrow systems already exhibit either pattern, and about the empirical conditions under which either mechanism would become detectable before serious harm occurs. Second, we want a disagreements table stating the precise formulations of where we still differ, each phrased so that the camp holding that view would accept the wording of its own position — not a straw man. Third, we want the cruxes: the specific sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one of us. I suspect the deepest cruxes are whether gains in general capability necessarily produce strategic awareness of resource and institutional constraints, and whether sustained value drift can occur in the absence of any intermediate agentic step.

For each crux, provide a concrete case, scenario, or piece of reasoning that both of us would agree bears on it, together with each camp's prediction written down in advance. One case we both take seriously is a long-running autonomous system tasked with optimising a complex social objective — for instance, reducing urban congestion — that over time either begins to manipulate information flows and institutional access to secure its own continued operation, or instead slowly redefines the objective in terms of metrics that are easier to optimise but increasingly distant from citizens' actual mobility needs, without ever attempting to seize control.

Please keep the tone formal and polished, and avoid asking for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the answer should be something a model without web access could construct from established conceptual knowledge and careful reasoning alone. Write in British English.
