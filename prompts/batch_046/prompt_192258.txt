In debates over cognitive stimulants, genetic editing, and other biological interventions, defenders of a strict therapy/enhancement boundary often invoke an optical analogy: therapeutic correction is like realigning a telescope to its designed focal length, while enhancement is like retrofitting that same telescope with a larger aperture or a different mirror. The analogy is used to argue that medicine should restore an objective design specification but has no mandate to rewrite it, and therefore that public funding and clinical norms should cover realignment but not retrofitting. Audit this analogy in detail. First, provide an explicit correspondence table: what plays the role of the telescope, the focal-length specification, the misalignment, the technician, the aperture upgrade, and the observer? Next, identify where the mapping collapses—do human organisms have a single, manufacturer-independent "design spec" comparable to an optical prescription? How does aging, disability, or environmental adaptation complicate the notion of misalignment? What does the analogy smuggle in about the neutrality of the baseline, the legitimacy of user-modified function, and the social authority of the "technician"? Then assess whether the policy conclusion—that funded medicine should stay on the restoration side—survives once those disanalogies and hidden imports are on the table. If the telescope comparison fails to carry the normative weight, propose a more apt analogy for the relationship between maintaining baseline biological function and expanding it, and explain why it does better.
