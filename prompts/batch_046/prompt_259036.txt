I have been working through the reasoning behind Kelly betting and the broader principle of geometric rationality—the view that a rational agent should maximize the geometric mean of returns rather than the arithmetic mean, thereby avoiding strategies that guarantee long-run ruin. The standard presentations all seem to assume repeated, independent wagers placed against a fixed bankroll, with losses and gains scaled proportionally. But I am trying to apply this logic to a specific, more constrained scenario of my own design, and I am unsure whether the usual conclusions still hold. Imagine I have a single initial capital reserve that I must distribute across five successive, independent wagers that resolve one after another in strict order. The critical twist is an irreversible entry threshold at each stage: if my capital ever drops below that threshold, I am permanently excluded from all remaining wagers, not merely continuing with a smaller stake. Given this sequential, path-dependent structure with what amounts to an absorbing floor, what are the most important implications of Kelly-style geometric rationality for how I should size my allocations? In particular, should the geometrically optimal wager remain a constant fraction of my current capital, or does the presence of that irreversible exclusion point demand a dynamically conservative adjustment—perhaps treating the floor as a boundary condition that modifies the growth-rate objective itself? I am especially interested in whether this undermines the familiar intuition that overbetting is self-correcting through geometric decay, since in my scenario the decay is not gradual but catastrophic and discontinuous.
