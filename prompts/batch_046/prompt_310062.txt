Hey, I'm a retired marine biologist who got into philosophy books after retirement— I keep finding these consciousness debates fascinating but also kind of slippery. So I was reading about illusionism, the idea that phenomenal consciousness is literally an illusion: there are no real 'what-it's-like' properties, just our cognitive system misrepresenting it's own representational states as having some mysterious phenomenal glow. That part I think I get, though it feels a little harsh to say we're all systematically wrong about our own minds.

But then I started wondering about this other angle—let's call it 'phenomenal projectionism.' The idea is that we don't actually misrepresent internal states; instead, our brain's self-monitoring system projects qualitative properties onto its own physical processes, kind of like how we project redness onto an apple. The redness isn't in the apple, and the 'feeling' isn't in the neuron, but the projection isn't exactly a false belief either—it's more like a useful framing device that lets the organism navigate it's internal environment.

Can you compare illusionism and phenominal projectionism? Specifically, do both deny that phenomenal properties are intrinsic to mental states? Does projectionism avoid saying that everyone is deluded about their first-person experience, which seems like a big cost for illusionists? And which view handles the explanitory gap better—if either does? I'm also curious whether projectionism just collapses back into illusionism once you unpack it, or if there's a real conceptual difference in how they treat first-person reports. No need for obscure citations, just a clear conceptual comparison is plenty.
