Bradley's regress argument and the broader problem of how a proposition achieves internal unity without collapsing its parts into an undifferentiated whole. For my final project I have arranged to interview a philosopher who has recently advanced a revived Bradleyan position against modern structured-proposition theories: she holds that the familiar Russellian reply, which distinguishes a relation in its relating capacity from that same relation treated merely as a logical term, does not genuinely halt the regress but only pushes it back one step, since the act of relating that is supposed to unify subject and predicate must itself either be included within the proposition—thereby generating a further need for unification—or remain external, leaving the internal unity unexplained. She extends this critique to contemporary appeals to ontological dependence and grounding, arguing that if grounding is itself a relational tie, then any proposition that asserts a ground relation would itself require a further unifying act, producing a vicious circularity rather than a termination point. Include a further question that probes whether her rejection of relational unification ultimately commits her to a Bradleyan absolute in which all propositional differences are internal modifications of a single underlying reality rather than genuinely composed parts, with a follow-up that asks how false propositions or contradictory beliefs are possible on such a monistic picture if there are no independent entities to stand in incompatible relations.
