I am auditing a seminar on embedded rationality this term, and in the readings and discussions I keep encountering a specific argumentative oscillation that I have not been able to name. The setup is always the same: someone points out that an agent physically embedded in its environment cannot contain a complete, accurate model of its own decision procedure without altering the very process being modeled, so in practical planning the agent must treat portions of its cognition as fixed parameters rather than as objects of direct choice. That limitation is usually presented as a hard structural boundary. But then, within the same conversation, the speaker slides into holding the agent responsible for failing to overcome that boundary—criticizing it for not anticipating its own updates, or blaming a person for not reasoning their way out of a cognitive bias—without acknowledging that the embeddedness constraint has just been invoked to show why such self-modification is structurally unavailable. I have seen this happen in debates about AI alignment, where an embedded optimizer is said to be unable to fully model its future weights, yet is still faulted for the blind spots that result, and in more casual philosophy-of-mind discussions about human agency, where our inability to step outside our own reasoning is first offered as an excuse for a biased judgment and then ignored when we assign blame for not having corrected it.

The two moves are not strictly contradictory, but they feel like a bait-and-switch: embeddedness is treated as an immovable wall when it serves to block one kind of self-modification argument, then treated as a surmountable obstacle when we want to preserve accountability. I am not sure whether this is just ordinary inconsistency, a variant of the motte-and-bailey, or something more specific—perhaps a failure to keep first-order planning and second-order evaluation in separate registers. Is there an established concept in the literature on embedded agency or bounded rationality for this particular pattern of invoking a structural self-modeling limit and then quietly dropping it? And if there is no established term, could one be coined and defined in a way that clearly distinguishes it from plain equivocation, from the standard distinction between descriptive and normative capacities, and from the familiar claim that agents can revise their reasoning frameworks over time? I would want the boundary conditions specified: exactly when does treating embeddedness as a constraint become incompatible with treating it as a failure of effort, and where is the line between legitimate second-order revision and illegitimate demands for first-order omniscience?
