I have been trying to settle my own view on what an emotion fundamentally is—whether it is essentially a kind of judgement, a form of perception, or a distinctive feeling—and I find myself with a cluster of verdicts about particular cases that I cannot easily reconcile. I should be grateful if you could help me see whether there is a coherent principle behind them, what it implies for cases I have not mentioned, and which of my intuitions I ought to abandon.

Consider the following. First, a subject in prolonged sensory deprivation reports a diffuse, objectless dread that carries almost no bodily arousal—no racing heart, no muscular tension—yet insists that the state is unmistakably emotional and not merely a mood or a cognitive disturbance; I am inclined to agree that this is an emotion in a particularly pure form. Second, an expert chess player, upon suddenly seeing a hidden tactical line, experiences a brief warm bodily sensation together with a sense of structural closure, but explicitly denies that she is evaluating the position as good or bad for her; I tend to think this is not an emotion but a sophisticated perceptual achievement with affective colouring. Third, a person who has cultivated, over many years, a calm and sustained benevolence towards strangers describes it not as an occasional wave but as a background orientation that is genuinely felt yet involves no fluctuation in arousal and no occurrent judgement; I want to call this an emotional disposition that can also be an occurrent state when attended to, even though it lacks the turbulence we usually associate with emotion. Fourth, a neurological patient can correctly judge that a situation is dangerous, reports the appropriate physiological arousal, yet says that there is no felt quality connecting these elements—I know I should be afraid and my heart is racing, but I feel nothing; here I am firmly convinced that the emotion is absent, which suggests that feeling is essential. Fifth, someone experiences intense grief upon learning of the death of a fictional character, knowing full well that the character does not exist, and reports that the grief is qualitatively identical to grief over a real loss except in its object; I take this to be a genuine emotion, not a pretence, which seems to show that a judgement of reality is not necessary. Sixth, a mathematician describes a specific affective tone that accompanies her recollection of a proof's elegant resolution—an experience she describes as tied to the logical structure itself rather than to any personal gain, and which she feels without making any propositional judgement that the proof is good in a self-interested sense; I am genuinely torn here, for part of me wants to classify this as an aesthetic emotion analogous to grief, while another part wants to say it is a form of cognitive satisfaction with emotional residue.

These verdicts pull in different directions. The first and fourth cases suggest that an occurrent felt quality is necessary and perhaps sufficient, yet the third seems to allow an emotion without turbulence or arousal, and the fifth allows an emotion directed at a known non-existent object. Meanwhile the second and sixth both involve affective colouring attached to cognitive or perceptual achievements, but I want to deny it is an emotion in the second case and remain uncertain in the sixth.

Could you therefore set out the principle, or a small set of rival principles, with sufficient precision that I can apply them? For each clause, please indicate which of my cases motivates it, and please state clearly what your account predicts for a case I have not listed above—namely, the case of someone who, through prolonged practice, can observe her own anger arising with full bodily and phenomenological presence yet maintain a complete absence of any judgement that she has been wronged, and who reports that the anger is present but not mine in any evaluative sense—together with at least one other case not in my list. Most importantly, please be explicit about which of my six verdicts each principle is forced to overturn, and defend that choice rather than disguising it. I should also ask that you present your answer in continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as one might write in a letter or email, and that you avoid offering bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, since I am working without access to external sources.
