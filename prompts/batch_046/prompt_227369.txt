I was asked to deliver the opening remarks for a workshop titled "Evolution, Sympathy, and Secular Obligation," convened jointly by our departments of philosophy and evolutionary biology. The audience is split between specialists—historians of Victorian science and moral philosophers who have taught Darwin's moral psychology for decades—and non-specialists, including theologians studying naturalistic ethics and graduate students who know Darwin mainly through popular summaries that conflate evolutionary ethics with ruthless competition. After a lengthy discussion with a colleague who argued that Darwin's account of morality is purely descriptive and therefore leaves normative ethics entirely untouched, I want to craft an address that treats Darwin's ambivalence as the structural spine of the event rather than as historical ornament.

I need you to write a ten-minute speech in which Darwin's views on morality and religion do genuine structural work: the address should center on the tension in Darwin's own texts between his descriptive genealogy of the moral sense in The Descent of Man—where he traces moral feeling to evolved social instincts shared with cooperative animals—and the private uncertainty he voiced in correspondence about whether explaining morality through natural selection diminishes its authority over the individual conscience. You should open by clarifying for the general audience that evolutionary origins do not license selfishness, then deepen the point for the specialists by showing that Darwin himself was genuinely unsure whether reason's extension of sympathy beyond the immediate group to universal obligations could retain normative weight once morality was understood as an adaptive trait. The speech should conclude not with a resolution but with a framing question for the workshop—what it means for secular ethics if the origin of our moral instincts and the authority of our moral judgments are not the same thing—without offering bibliographies, reading lists, or citations to secondary literature, and without treating Darwin's position as more settled than his texts actually allow.
