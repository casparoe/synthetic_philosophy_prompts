You are advising a philanthropic research board that must decide how to distribute a three-year grant program aimed at clarifying the normative underpinnings of existential-risk mitigation. The board is specifically focused on the debate—associated with Samuel Scheffler's afterlife conjecture—about why human extinction is wrong in ways that exceed the sum of deaths it directly causes. Within this debate, distinguish two interpretive lines that are rarely separated with precision: the project-continuity view, on which the primary harm is the truncation of present agents' meaningful activities that presuppose a future audience or continuation; and the value-accumulation view, on which the harm is the non-existence of all future well-being that would otherwise have occurred. Now inject the following complication: suppose one argues that the project-continuity view tacitly requires a transgenerational identification condition—present agents must be able to psychologically identify with specific future strangers for the truncation to constitute a harm to them—whereas in replacement scenarios, in which causal history is altered so that entirely different future people exist instead of the ones originally anticipated, no such identification holds, and therefore the harm collapses into the mere loss of value rather than a harm to present persons. Given this framing, what are the principal open questions, conceptual distinctions, or lines of philosophical work that remain unresolved in this area? More specifically, which two or three should the board prioritize if its criterion is not academic completeness but actionable guidance for allocating resources between near-term risk reduction and far-future institutional capacity-building? Defend your ordering by specifying the evaluative criteria you employ—such as tractability within a three-year horizon, independence from contested empirical psychology, downstream influence on policy design, or dependence on resolving prior metaphysical questions about personal identity across generations—and articulate the main trade-offs among them. Conclude by identifying what empirical finding or conceptual clarification would most plausibly cause you to revise your ranking.
