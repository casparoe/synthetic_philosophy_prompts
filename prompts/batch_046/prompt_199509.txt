I'm in a philosophy of moral psychology seminar and we've been going back and forth on virtue ethics vs the situationist critique. I noticed a pattern in how defenders respond that I can't find a label for, and I'm wondering if it's a recognized failure mode or just sloppy arguing.

The pattern goes like this: a study finds that people who act bravely in physical emergencies often freeze or defer in bureacratic ones—say, refusing to sign off on a dangerous but profitable policy. The virtue ethicist replies that courage isn't one thing; it's 'courage-in-emergencies' vs 'courage-in-institutional-contexts', and since the study only tested the first, it doesn't touch the second. Okay. But then, when the same ethicist later describes a manager who did speak up as 'a courageous person,' they don't append the institutional qualifier—they treat the trait as carrying across domains and explanitory why he spoke up, even though they just said cross-domain application is illegitamate. So the granularity gets invoked to block counterevidence but ignored when making positive attributions or predictions.

I've seen this happen with honesty, compassion, and patience too. It's not quite the old 'no true Scotsman' move, because they're not denying membership in the category; they're multiplying the categories on demand. And it's not standard theoretical refinement, because refinement usually makes predictions sharper, not selectively blurryer. Is there a name for this specific asymmetry—using contextual fragmentation defensivly but not predictively? And if there's no established term, how should I carve it: is it a kind of equivocation, a protective strategy for dispositional concepts, or something else? I want to understand where its edges are—when does legitimate context-sensitivity turn into this one-way elasticity?
