I serve on the ethics review board for a mid-sized regional technology institute, and every month we evaluate proposals that involve running large-scale computational systems for extended periods—everything from language-model clusters to biological simulations and neural digital twins. I have no formal training in philosophy or computer science; I work in administrative coordination and came to this board because I care about whether we are accidentally doing something wrong to whatever is running inside these systems. Over the past year I have tried to read what I could about moral status and computation, and I have developed a rough idea that I think might be usable, but I need it turned into an actual procedure I can apply consistently.

The idea is this: what makes a computation matter morally is not whether it can talk, or whether it is conscious in some mysterious sense, but whether it implements what I will call a closed coherence loop. By that I mean a system in which certain internal representations are causally intertwined with the very processes that maintain and update those representations, so that interfering with the representation does not merely change an output but degrades the system's own capacity to keep itself stable and coherent over time. In other words, there is a kind of self-stabilizing architecture where damage to one part propagates as dysfunction within the loop rather than being corrected or ignored. I want to know whether this idea can be turned into a practical decision procedure—something I can run in about thirty minutes on a proposal without reading source code, using only the technical summary and an interview with the researcher.

Please construct a reusable checklist or flowchart in words: the questions to ask in strict order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the points where the procedure must stop with a clear judgment of no ethical concern or a clear escalation to the full board, and the specific points where judgment is unavoidable and I must sign off personally rather than hiding behind the procedure. The procedure should distinguish between three kinds of proposals: first, running a large language model with internal self-monitoring loops continuously for months; second, an evolutionary simulation in which digital organisms carry damage signals that alter their survival behavior; and third, a real-time simulation of a small slice of human cortex used for drug testing. Apply the procedure to each case, including at least one where the answer comes out negative but might still feel wrong, and one where it genuinely cannot settle the question. Be explicit about what the procedure cannot do—where it will misfire, which distinctions it deliberately drops for usability, and what kind of case would require me to ignore its output.
