I am a high school student working on a paper about philosophical zombies for my philosophy class, and I have been reading about Chalmers's conceivability argument. I understand that if zombies are epistemically conceivable—if we can imagine a world physically identical to ours but lacking conscious experience—then, using a two-dimensional semantic framework, they should also be metaphysically possible, which would mean physical facts do not necessitate phenomenal facts. But this is where I get stuck. If a zombie is defined as being physically identical to a conscious being in every primary-intension respect—same brain states, same behavior, same causal roles—then it seems like neuroscience could never detect one, because every empirical test would come back identical. So is the zombie argument actually an argument against the possibility of a scientific theory of consciousness, rather than an argument for property dualism? And when I combine this with the phenomenal concept strategy—the idea that our concept of consciousness is anchored in direct first-person acquaintance rather than in functional or physical descriptions—it looks like we end up with a skeptical problem about other minds rather than a metaphysical problem about the nature of consciousness. We have direct knowledge of our own experience, but no third-person way to verify anyone else's, and the zombie possibility just makes that gap permanent. Am I conflating the epistemic gap with the metaphysical gap, or does the combination of the conceivability argument and the phenomenal concept strategy genuinely push us toward skepticism about other minds? I want to make sure I understand the distinction between primary and secondary intensions before I write my conclusion, because right now it feels like the argument collapses the two together.
