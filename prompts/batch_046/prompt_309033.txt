Here's an argument I constructed for a seminar on transformative choice, and now it strikes me as suspicious because the conclusion seems to undermine all rational life-planning. (1) A rational choice requires comparing expected outcomes using the chooser's current evaluative framework. (2) In a genuinely transformative experience—such as becoming a parent, undergoing a profound religious conversion, or acquiring a radically different sensory modality—one's first-order preferences change, and, more importantly, one's meta-preferences about which values are authoritative also change; call this recursive preference restructuring. (3) Consequently, the transformed self may disavow the authority of the pre-transformation framework entirely. (4) So any evaluation of the transformative option must either apply pre-transformation standards (which the future self rejects) or post-transformation standards (which the present self cannot access). (5) Therefore, no rational agent can ever deliberately choose to undergo a transformative experience. (6) Yet people clearly do make such choices rationally—advising a friend to become a parent, for instance, is not necessarily irrational. (7) So one of (1) through (4) must fail. (8) Abandoning (1) severs rational choice from evaluative comparison altogether; abandoning (4) requires an account of cross-temporal authority that avoids both paternalism toward one's future self and complete fragmentation of agency. I'd prefer to keep (2) and (3), since recursive restructuring seems descriptively accurate, but I want to know whether I'm forced into (8)'s second horn, or whether there's a hidden slip—perhaps an equivocation on "access" or a false dichotomy—between (2) and (4).
