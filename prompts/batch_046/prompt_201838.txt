I am designing a unit for an upper-level seminar on moral responsibility and have noticed that students frequently invoke the maxim that hypocrisy automatically cancels one's standing to blame, applying it broadly—for instance, to dismiss a climate activist's criticism of institutional carbon emissions on the grounds that she herself travels by air. Rather than allowing the slogan to function as a conversation-stopper, I want students to treat it as a family of precise normative claims that must be disambiguated before evaluation. Could you produce a formal conceptual analysis, structured as a numbered menu of at least five distinct readings, that specifies for each: (a) the exact sense of "standing" at stake—whether moral authority, non-arbitrary entitlement, conversational propriety, or efficacy in motivating reform; (b) the scope of hypocrisy that triggers cancellation—whether it requires targeted hypocrisy (blaming another specifically for fault F while being guilty of F oneself) or extends to structural hypocrisy (benefiting from systemic conditions one condemns without direct personal commission of F); and (c) whether that particular reading actually warrants dismissing the activist's blame, or whether it leaves her criticism intact. For each reading, indicate whether it is trivial or tautological, substantive and defensible, or false but widely assumed. Present this as a polished disambiguation suitable for seminar handouts, without requesting bibliographies, author attributions, or citations to specific texts.
