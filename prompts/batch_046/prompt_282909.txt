I am a doctoral student in moral philosophy, and in my advanced seminar on agent-centered ethics we have spent the better part of a term stuck on what I can only describe as an unmoving dispute over Bernard Williams's integrity objection, centered on the familiar scenario—often associated with the one-thought-too-many worry—in which a person saves a spouse without weighing comparative reasons and is praised precisely for not having engaged in such weighing. The literature seems to have sorted itself into two stable camps: those who interpret the case as demonstrating a structural failure in aggregative moral theories, specifically that agent-relative reasons cannot be captured within a neutral calculus of overall good, and those who read it as a psychological observation about the corrupting effects of calculative reasoning within intimate relationships. What frustrates me is that both camps now accept that special relationships generate non-fungible obligations and that excessive deliberation can damage moral character, yet they continue to talk past one another—one treating the point as normative and the other as descriptive—without any apparent mechanism for resolving whether the objection targets the decision procedure itself or merely the phenomenology of acting from love.

My working hypothesis is that the stall is not about empirical psychology or even about normative ethics in the usual sense, but about an unacknowledged disagreement over transparency conditions in practical reasoning: whether moral agency requires that one's motivating reasons be available for third-person scrutiny and justification, or whether there exists a legitimate form of epistemic opacity—perhaps specific to intimate contexts—in which the absence of explicit weighing is itself a feature of sound practical cognition. I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict: is this a substantive disagreement about what counts as a moral reason, a verbal confusion arising from using integrity to cover both structural and psychological phenomena, or a case of two groups answering different questions—one about correct decision theory, the other about the appropriate structure of deliberative consciousness—under a single heading? And given whatever diagnosis you offer, could a distinction between moral transparency and epistemic transparency in practical reasoning, or perhaps a formal model that assigns domain-specific decision costs to different kinds of relationships, actually move the debate, or is the impasse a sign that the underlying conflict concerns bedrock intuitions about partiality that admit of no further argument?
