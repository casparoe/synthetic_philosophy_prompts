I'm trying to determine what Gödel's incompleteness theorems actually license when they are taken outside mathematics, and I've formed tentative verdicts on several well-known invocations, but I cannot tell whether they cohere. Here are my judgments, with one case I keep reversing.

When a physicist argues that any formalized "theory of everything" must contain true physical statements it cannot prove—because Gödel applies to any consistent, recursively axiomatizable system rich enough to encode arithmetic—I think this overreaches. Physics may lack recursive axiomatizability, and physical truth is not syntactic provability. Verdict: illegitimate application.

When a philosopher of mind claims that human mathematicians can recognize the consistency of formal systems that those systems cannot prove consistent, and therefore human cognition transcends algorithmic computation, I feel the argument misidentifies what Gödel establishes. The second theorem concerns formal derivability within a system, not epistemic access by an agent. Verdict: illegitimate, though for a different reason than the physics case.

When an AI safety researcher claims that a sufficiently powerful autonomous system cannot reliably verify its own consistency without an external auditor, citing Gödel's second theorem as a structural constraint, this strikes me as a legitimate analogical use. The system's self-model need not be identical to arithmetic, but the dependency on an external validator captures the theorem's logic. Verdict: legitimate.

When someone applies Gödel to ethics, arguing that any consistent moral framework must leave some actions neither obligatory nor forbidden—morally "undecidable" within the framework—I think this is a category error. Moral norms are not recursively enumerable formal sentences in the required sense. Verdict: illegitimate.

There's a case I genuinely cannot settle: a mathematician argues that Gödel's results do not apply to quantum mechanics because quantum theory is formulated in continuous mathematics (real and complex analysis) rather than discrete arithmetic, and therefore lacks the representational machinery for self-reference. Part of me thinks this is a valid restriction; part of me thinks self-reference can be reconstructed in continuous settings, or that any physical computation ultimately discretizes. Verdict: unresolved.

Finally, when a historian of ideas says Gödel contributed to the decline of logical positivism by showing that formal verification of scientific theories was impossible in principle, I think this confuses syntactic incompleteness with empirical underdetermination. Verdict: illegitimate, but on historical-interpretive rather than logical grounds.

Is there a precise principle—perhaps involving the presence of recursive self-reference, the distinction between syntactic derivability and empirical or normative evaluation, and the conditions for analogical rather than literal application—that captures why I accept the AI case but reject the mind, physics, and ethics cases? I want the principle stated with enough precision that each clause is motivated by one of my verdicts, and with an honest statement of which verdict it forces me to overturn—particularly whether my distinction between the mind case and the AI case survives, or whether both should fall together. I also want to know what the principle predicts for a case I have not listed: the application of Gödel's second theorem to constitutional law, where a legal system contains explicit rules for its own amendment and thus seems to model self-reference institutionally rather than arithmetically.
