I'm preparing interview questions for a jurisprudence podcast, and next week's guest is a philosopher who defends a reconstructed Fullerian position: she holds that the eight principles of law's internal morality are not merely moral constraints or conditions of practical efficacy, but conceptual prerequisites for legal authority itself. On her view, Hart's separation thesis fails not because every law embodies justice, but because a system of rules that is radically unstable, opaque, or retroactive cannot function as law in the full sense—it collapses into mere coercive command, lacking the procedural rationality that makes it possible for subjects to treat legal directives as distinctive reasons for action. I want a sequence of eight to ten questions that does not let her restate this framework but forces her to defend it at the joints.

Please begin by asking how she responds to Hart's classic charge that Fuller's principles are just conditions of efficiency—why she thinks describing them as "conceptual prerequisites for authority" avoids reducing law to a well-run machine. Then move quickly to the hardest case: a legal regime that is perfectly general, clear, prospective, and consistent in its administration, yet systematically enforces grave injustice; does she call this "law" in a defective analogical sense, or deny the title altogether, and what does that do to her claim that she is not smuggling morality into the concept? I want at least one question on retroactive legislation, since Fuller's own examples cluster there but modern constitutional orders routinely permit transitional justice measures—ask whether her view can accommodate targeted retroactivity without losing the conceptual boundary she wants to draw. Another should press whether her reconstructed view collapses into Raz's service conception of authority, and if she distinguishes them by saying her procedural conditions are constitutive rather than instrumental, include a follow-up keyed to her likely answer: ask whether that distinction survives once we observe that many durable legal systems have endured long stretches of partial opacity or frequent change without losing their authority in the eyes of their subjects.

For each major line, include a follow-up tied to her most probable reply: if she appeals to the "capacity to guide conduct" as the marker of authority, ask what empirical or conceptual evidence could show that subjects are guided by pure coercive fear rather than by recognition of legal authority; if she insists that unjust but procedurally sound rules remain law, albeit defective law, ask what work the "defect" does if it carries no necessary moral condemnation. End with a direct question about what would count against her reconstructed Fullerian view—whether it is an empirical finding about how people actually relate to legal rules, a conceptual argument that authority can exist without procedural rationality, or a demonstration that her position cannot distinguish law from other stable systems of social coordination like effective bureaucratic administration or well-organized criminal syndicates.
