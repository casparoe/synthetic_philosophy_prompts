Read Jorge Luis Borges's "The Library of Babel" through the philosophical lens of actual infinities — specifically Hilbert's hotel, Cantor's transfinite set theory, and finitist objections to completed infinity. The story presents a universe of interlocking hexagonal galleries that together contain every possible book of a fixed length and alphabet, arranged without discernible order yet claimed to be complete. Rather than treating the library as a simple metaphor for epistemic limitation, analyse its architecture as a proposed actual infinite: does Borges present the library as a potential infinity, always extendable but never fully present, or as a Cantorian completed totality? Examine how specific details — the repetition of identical volumes save for one letter, the librarians' belief that a master catalogue exists somewhere in the hexagons, and the despair that no search can be exhaustive — interact with Hilbert's hotel (for instance, whether a new volume could be added to a fully occupied gallery without displacing another, or whether an infinite number of librarians could be accommodated in an already-infinite space) and with Cantor's demonstration that an infinite set can be placed in one-to-one correspondence with a proper subset of itself. Then consider whether the library's claim of completeness is vulnerable to a diagonal argument: construct a hypothetical volume that differs from every entry in any proposed catalogue on at least one page, and ask whether Borges's text anticipates this vulnerability or implicitly suppresses it. Finally, evaluate what the story contributes to the philosophical debate that formal proofs about infinity do not: does the librarians' lived experience of searching through an unending but locally finite space dramatise an epistemic or psychological cost of accepting actual infinity that Hilbert's hotel and Cantor's arguments leave unaddressed?
