As a doctoral student in philosophy of biology, I have been struck by a persistent institutional bifurcation in how evolutionary theory is taught and applied, and I am trying to determine what philosophical commitments about the ontology of selection make that split coherent. In standard graduate curricula, gene-level, individual-level, and group-level selection are presented as formally distinct frameworks—Hamilton's rule is taught as kin-selection arithmetic, multilevel selection theory as a variance-partitioning formalism, and evolutionary-game dynamics as individual-strategy optimization—without any required adjudication of whether these are competing metaphysical claims about the actual units of selection or merely convenient descriptive registers at different scales. By contrast, in applied wildlife-management protocols, funding priorities for endangered populations are routinely determined by genetic effective-population-size thresholds (Ne), a metric that treats genetic viability as the fundamental currency of evolutionary persistence, even when the legislative and public-facing justification invokes the preservation of a species-level entity or an ecosystem-level functional role. The pedagogical architecture thus treats selection levels as pluralist and interchangeable, while the policy architecture treats genetic-level metrics as the decisive constraint, allowing group-level and individual-level language to function as rhetorical or tractable framing.

What implicit theory of selection units does this bifurcation presuppose? Does it depend on a pragmatic pluralism in which gene, individual, and group are not competing ontologies but context-relative heuristic frames? Or does it conceal a reductionist commitment, using genetic metrics as the real evaluative standard while permitting multilevel descriptions to serve politically palatable purposes? Reconstruct the philosophical theory that makes this institutional split rational, carefully distinguishing what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates; then say whether the split is genuinely philosophically underdetermined, compatible with several rival ontologies of selection, or whether it collapses into inconsistency once its hidden commitments are made explicit. Finally, indicate what would have to change in either graduate pedagogy or conservation funding practice if we replaced that implicit theory with a consistently multilevel ontology in which selection levels are hierarchically nested with genuine, irreducible causal autonomy at each level, rather than treated as interchangeable descriptive lenses.
