i was discussing with a friend who's taking a course on buddhist ethics and we got stuck on this merit-transfer ritual they do in some theravada temples in sri lanka and thailand. you know the one—family pays monks to chant, they write the dead relative's name on a slip of paper and burn it or float it away, with the idea that merit reaches them in whatever rebirth-state they're in now, maybe a hungry ghost or a deva or even an animal. the monks are adamant about anatta, no enduring self, but they still act like the name points to the correct recipient of this specific merit. if you ask them about personal identity in a philosphical discussion, they'd deny there's any substantial self linking the dead uncle to the being recieveing it now.

so what implicit theory of identity is the institution actually running on? does it presuppose a conventional stream of mental continuity, or just a causal chain of karmic results? and if you take abhidharma seriously—only momentary dhammas exist, no stream—would the practice collapse, or can it be reconstructed without smuggling in a self? also, the same tradition teaches that moral responsibility doesn't carry across lives since there's no self to bear it. doesn't that conflict with assuming enough continuity for merit-transfer to make sense? i'm trying to figure out whether there's a coherent view licensing both, or if the practice is just holding together contradictory commitments without realizing it.
