I run a monthly discussion group for philosophically curious non-specialists—lawyers, engineers, and writers—who have read the popular introductions but have never taken a formal logic course. Next month we are working through problems of personal identity, and I want to build the session around Dennett’s brain-transplant scenario: a brain removed from a body, placed in a vat, receiving radio signals that produce a complete experience of walking through a garden, while a duplicate brain remains in the original skull. Rather than guiding them through the standard “am I Hamlet or Yorick?” resolution, I want to teach the underlying conceptual machinery that makes the puzzle genuinely bite—specifically, the distinction between descriptive self-knowledge (knowing every fact about one’s body and history) and indexical self-location (knowing where one is from the first-person perspective), and how a subject can possess perfect descriptive information yet still be systematically wrong about her own spatial origin.

Plan the explanatory arc for a single ninety-minute session. What idea must be firmly in place before the brain-vat scenario even registers as a puzzle about identity rather than as generic skeptical paranoia—should I begin with a simple physical split, with the difference between knowing-that and knowing-where, or with something else? Choose one concrete carrying example that modifies Dennett’s original story just enough to isolate the indexical failure: for instance, a case in which the brain’s sensory feed is accurate about every object, color, and sound in the garden, but the signal origin is misrouted so that the subject correctly identifies everything around her yet mislocates herself relative to the vat. In what precise order must the components be introduced—the brain-computer interface, the duplicate brain in the skull, the radio delay—so that the audience does not collapse the distinction between “where is my brain?” and “where am I?”?

Map the misconceptions they will bring or form in sequence: the intuition that consciousness is tied to the body because “I feel my feet,” the assumption that duplicate brains necessarily guarantee duplicate people, the confusion of first-person location with third-person brain-location, and the slide into treating the whole thing as an external-world skepticism argument rather than as a puzzle about de se reference. For each, specify exactly where in the sequence to head it off—not merely “address it,” but which prior conceptual layer should already be established so the correction lands cleanly. Include one check at the end—a specific question or miniature thought experiment—that would reveal whether they have grasped the indexical-descriptive discrepancy rather than merely being able to retell the story.

Finally, tell me openly what I should refuse to explain at this level. I anticipate they will ask about technical analyses of centered worlds, about actual split-brain patients, or about whether radio signals could in principle carry phenomenal content; I want to know which of these digressions would genuinely reinforce the dependency structure you have built and which would muddy it, and how I should deflect the latter politely without making the audience feel dismissed.
