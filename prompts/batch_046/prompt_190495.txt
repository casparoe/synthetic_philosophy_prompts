I am a doctoral student in metaphysics serving as a teaching assistant for an upper-division undergraduate seminar on ontology and material constitution. For the midterm, I asked students to evaluate the classic statue-and-clay case, and I am now grading submissions. One answer strikes me as conceptually fluent and well-structured, yet I suspect it smuggles in two or three subtle confusions—perhaps a mischaracterization of the single-object reply, or a slide between synchronic constitution and diachronic identity—that a hurried reader might miss. I would like a precise, point-by-point assessment rather than a general impression.

Here is the exam prompt I assigned: At noon, a sculptor shapes a lump of marble into a statue of David. The statue occupies exactly the same region as the marble, and every physical particle composing the marble composes the statue. By 3 p.m., a vandal squashes the statue flat; the marble survives, reshaped, but the statue is destroyed. Some philosophers argue that this divergence in persistence conditions shows the statue and the marble are not identical. Others reply that we are describing one object—the marble—under two different sortals, and that multiplying entities violates parsimony. Write an evaluation of this dispute. Your answer should: (i) reconstruct the argument from modal or persistence differences to non-identity; (ii) present the strongest version of the "one object, two descriptions" reply and identify where it fails; and (iii) explain what the constitution relation, as distinct from identity, is supposed to contribute, and whether it genuinely resolves the tension or merely renames it.

The student submission (approximately 650 words) reads as follows. "The case of David and the marble presents a standard puzzle about material constitution. At noon, the statue and the marble share every spatial part and every categorical physical property. By 3 p.m., however, the marble survives being squashed while David does not. If the two are identical, then by Leibniz's law they must share all properties, including modal ones such as the capacity to survive flattening. Since they differ in this modal property, non-identity follows validly.

The opposing view—the single-object reply—treats 'statue' and 'marble lump' not as referring expressions for distinct entities but as different modes of presentation of one substance, analogous to how 'Hesperus' and 'Phosphorus' name the same planet under different descriptions. On this account, there is only the marble; 'statue' merely applies an artistic description to the marble when it instantiates a certain form, just as 'morning star' applies an observational description to Venus. Once we recognize that these are Fregean senses attached to a single referent—the marble—the conflict in persistence conditions dissolves, because the underlying substance survives, and we simply cease applying the artistic description.

Against this, the non-identity theorist appeals to constitution: the statue is constituted by the marble without being identical to it. This relation is best understood as a form of temporal identity applied across stages. Just as a person is constituted by her body over time without being reducible to any particular set of cells, so the statue is constituted by the marble at noon without being reducible to that particular arrangement of particles. Constitution therefore allows shared matter at a time with divergent histories, without positing two substances in one place. Nevertheless, I think this resolution is largely verbal. The constitution theorist is really saying that there is one enduring substance—the marble—described now materially and now artistically, with 'constitution' naming the diachronic relation between its temporal parts. If so, then the modal differences are not differences in real properties but differences in how we describe the same substance across its history. The appearance of coincidence is just the appearance of one object being described both materially and formally. Thus, while the argument from modal properties initially looks compelling, it relies on treating descriptive modes as ontological differences. Once constitution is recognized as diachronic identity applied synchronically, parsimony is preserved and the puzzle dissolves."
