As a retired crystallographer who has recently begun studying philosophy of mind through an adult-education seminar, I have found myself returning to analogies from structural science to make sense of functionalism and multiple realizability. In our discussions, a fellow participant advanced the comparison that mental states stand to neural substrates as crystal structures stand to their constituent atoms: just as the face-centered cubic lattice can be realized by sodium chloride, copper, or aluminum without altering its geometric description, so too can a belief or a pain be realized by silicon chips, neural tissue, or some other substrate without altering its psychological description. The analogy is meant to do substantial argumentative work—namely, to establish that psychology enjoys the same kind of descriptive autonomy that crystallography enjoys over chemistry, and that demands for neurobiological reduction miss the point in much the same way that demanding a chemical account of every cubic crystal would miss the structural point.

I would like a careful audit of how far this comparison actually holds. Please provide an explicit correspondence table—what in the mental domain plays the role of the lattice, the atoms, the bonding forces, the macroscopic properties, and the descriptive language of crystallography—and then identify the precise points where the mapping breaks. In particular, I want to know whether the analogy quietly imports assumptions about the causal efficacy, evolutionary origin, or normative evaluation of mental states that have no counterpart in crystal structures; whether the autonomy being claimed for psychology is genuinely parallel to the autonomy of a structural science, or whether it smuggles in a stronger metaphysical independence than is warranted; and whether, once these disanalogies are on the table, the functionalist conclusion about multiple realizability survives, collapses, or requires significant reformulation. If the crystal-structure analogy ultimately fails to carry its intended weight, I would also welcome a suggestion for a more appropriate analogy drawn from the physical sciences that might better illuminate the functionalist claim without importing misleading features.
