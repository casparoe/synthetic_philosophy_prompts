I'm currently working through the literature on experimental philosophy for an advanced seminar on action theory, and after discussing the original asymmetry cases with colleagues, I've come to defend a fairly strong interpretation of what they show: the effect doesn't merely demonstrate that normative considerations bias our attributions of intentional action, but that the folk concept of intentional action is constitutively normative—such that describing an outcome as intentional already involves an evaluative stance that purely descriptive, belief-desire-causal accounts of action explanation cannot accommodate. On my view, this undermines the possibility of a fully value-neutral psychology of intentionality, and its defenders should treat the result as conceptual rather than as a performance error, a conversational artifact, or a culturally local quirk.

That said, I want an honest map of how exposed this position actually is. What would have to be true for it to be wrong? I'm interested in which observations, arguments, or formal results should move a committed defender, versus which ones they could dismiss as peripheral. For instance, if the asymmetry disappeared entirely under mechanistic explanatory priming, or if it failed to replicate in languages where intentionality is grammatically marked independently of outcome evaluation, how damaging would that be? I've also been developing a hypothetical extension to collective agents—corporations and algorithmic decision systems—where the normative asymmetry might invert: harmful collective outcomes are often explicitly foreseen yet rarely called intentional, while beneficial ones seem to attract the label more readily. If that inversion holds conceptually, does it undermine my claim that the folk concept is uniformly normative, or can it be accommodated by distinguishing individual from collective agency? More generally, has the constitutive-normativity view been qualified into near-unfalsifiability—say, by restricting normative constitution to "thick" moral contexts but allowing thin evaluative ones to operate descriptively—and if so, is that insulation a theoretical virtue or a defect?

Please lay this out as a ranked assessment of exposure: distinguish fatal defeaters from merely awkward ones; say what a defender of the view should concede as genuinely counting against it versus what they would likely reject; identify whether the position risks anything substantive or has been protected by ad hoc qualifications; and assess whether, under pressure, it collapses into a rival account—perhaps a Gricean pragmatic explanation, or a hybrid descriptive-normative model—rather than standing as an independent thesis. I want an evaluation of what the view actually risks, not a defense of it.
