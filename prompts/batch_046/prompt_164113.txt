I've been working through the decision-theoretic derivations of the Born rule in Everettian quantum mechanics, and I keep encountering a specific argumentative structure that I can't place precisely. The setup is familiar: an agent faces branching outcomes, and an author argues that rational preference ordering requires weighting branches by some measure. The author then introduces a symmetry constraint—often involving the indistinguishability of branches related by a unitary transformation that leaves the physical state invariant—together with an expectation formalism for comparing actions. From these, they conclude that the rational weights must be proportional to the squared moduli of the amplitudes. But on closer inspection, the expectation operator or the utility aggregation rule has already been defined relative to a branch-counting or amplitude-weighted measure. The derivation therefore doesn't extract the probabilistic weighting from pure rationality principles; it embeds the weighting into the decision-theoretic machinery and then exports it as a derived result. This is not the broad objection that Everettian mechanics assumes probabilities somewhere in its ontology; it's specifically about calibrating the formalism of expected utility to the very measure it claims to justify. Does this configuration have an established name in the literature—perhaps as a particular species of circular reasoning or formal misalignment? How should it be distinguished from other circularity charges, such as assuming the Born rule in the definition of the state space or in the dynamics? And if there is no dedicated term, how might one delineate this failure mode so that it applies only when the measure is hidden inside the decision rule rather than in the physical premises?
