I am a Ph.D. student in comparative philosophy working on classical Chinese theories of death, personal identity, and ethical transformation. In a recent seminar on Daoist ethics, two interpretive camps have been arguing past each other about Zhuangzi’s attitude toward mortality, and I want to replace the circular exchange with a formal adversarial collaboration document that both sides could actually sign. The first camp, which I will call the Transformative Dissolutionists, holds that Zhuangzi’s doctrine of cosmic transformation (huà) entails that death is not a harm and that any sustained grief or attachment to a particular living form constitutes a failure of the perfect equanimity (zhizhi) achieved through knowledge of the Dao. The second camp, the Embodied Continuity Readers, holds that while death is indeed a transformation within the cosmic process, Zhuangzi permits a non-possessive, aesthetic form of sorrow—what we might term transformative mourning—that acknowledges the specific relational form now dissolved without resisting the process itself, provided it remains below an equanimity threshold that separates bounded acknowledgment from disruptive, possessive grief.

Please draft the joint statement both camps could endorse after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. The document should begin with a numbered section of agreed claims, covering at minimum: the textual basis for transformation in the Zhuangzi; the distinction between possessive clinging and non-possessive acknowledgment; the agreement that the well-known anecdote of Zhuangzi’s response to his wife’s death is the central test case; and the shared acceptance that the metaphysical claim that all things are one in transformation must be interpreted rather than ignored. This should be followed by a disagreements table stating each camp’s precise position in wording its own members would accept—for the Dissolutionists, that any evaluative attachment to a particular form after death contradicts perfect knowledge; for the Continuity Readers, that transformative mourning is compatible with equanimity so long as it does not treat the transformation as a loss of being itself. You should identify at least two cruxes: first, whether the equanimity threshold is a legitimate interpretive construct or a modern psychological imposition on classical Daoist thought; second, whether the metaphysical unity of transformation eliminates the basis for particular relational acknowledgment or only eliminates possessive attachment. For each crux, specify the concrete case or argument both sides agree bears on it (for instance, the drumming anecdote or the metaphysical unity passage), record each side’s explicit prediction about what that evidence demonstrates, and note whether any shared criterion exists that could actually move either camp. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; work solely from conceptual reasoning and widely available knowledge of the text.

Present the agreed claims as a numbered list and the disagreements in a table with columns for the crux, the Dissolutionist formulation, and the Continuity formulation. End the response with a single line beginning Core residual disagreement: that summarizes in one sentence the single remaining dispute that survives the collaboration.
