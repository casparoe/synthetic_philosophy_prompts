I am working through a course on the ethics of historical attribution and have become stuck on how philosophical models of collective responsibility apply to the First World War. The Fischer controversy—centred on the historian Fritz Fischer’s argument that the German leadership deliberately pursued a general European war in 1914, supported by archival evidence of expansive war aims drafted that autumn and strategic planning during the July Crisis—is usually debated as a question of documentary proof. Rather than asking merely who caused the conflict, I want to compare two conceptual frameworks for what it actually means to hold a nation responsible.

The first framework is diachronic institutional culpability: it treats a nation-state as a persisting moral agent when its governing institutions maintain intentional continuity over time, so that decisions taken by the Kaiserreich in July 1914 can be attributed to “Germany” as a unified subject. On this view, Fischer’s evidence demonstrates a collective national will, and blame inheres in the institutional lineage that connects the 1914 decision-makers to the modern state. The second framework is synchronic nodal attribution: it denies that “Germany” is a singular moral bearer. Instead, blame attaches only to specific institutional nodes—the General Staff, the Foreign Office, the chancellor—evaluated individually. Under this model, Fischer’s archive reveals distributed culpability among nodes, but aggregating these into a national verdict is a category error; the nation is at best a causal locus, not a responsible agent.

Please compare these two frameworks with direct reference to Fischer’s findings about strategic intentions and war aims in 1914. Then apply both to the following counterfactual: suppose that in January 1913 the constitutional structures of the German Empire had been replaced by a democratic republic, yet the identical individuals who held the chancellorship and chief of staff retained their posts with unchanged strategic intentions and authored precisely the same July Crisis decisions. Would diachronic institutional culpability still assign blame to “Germany”, or would the institutional rupture shift attribution? Would synchronic nodal attribution yield an identical verdict regardless of constitutional form? Which framework better captures our ordinary practice of blaming nations for wars, and where does each framework fail?
