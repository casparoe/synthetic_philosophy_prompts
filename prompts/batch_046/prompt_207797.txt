I am writing as a doctoral student in philosophy of language and clinical neuropsychology. Our rehabilitation team repeatedly faces the same decision: whether an ambiguous vocalization or gesture from a minimally responsive patient should be interpreted as an intentional speech act with specific propositional content, or dismissed as a non-intentional reflex. I want Davidson’s radical interpretation—specifically the interdependence of meaning and belief, the principle of charity, and the triangulation condition—translated into a reusable procedural checklist that a non-philosophical clinical team can run in about fifteen minutes per session. The deliverable should be a flowchart in words: ordered diagnostic questions, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, explicit stop-points yielding “do not interpret,” and explicit hand-off points where the remaining issue is clinical values rather than interpretive fact. The procedure must handle hard cases in which charity forces attribution of apparently implausible beliefs, and cases in which the shared perceptual environment is disrupted (for example, sensory isolation or equipment-mediated interaction that breaks triangulation). Please apply the procedure to three cases: (1) a patient who produces the same syllable each time a painful stimulus is applied; (2) a patient who emits a novel sound sequence only in the presence of one specific caregiver; and (3) a case in which the logic of interpretation forces attribution of a false belief about the immediate physical environment—an answer the team will likely reject—while remaining internally coherent. Conclude by specifying exactly what the procedure cannot determine and where it is most likely to misfire, particularly regarding the indeterminacy of translation when only a single interpreter is available.
