Write a short story that engages with the philosophical question of what, if anything, makes an original artifact more valuable than a perfect forgery when the two are physically and perceptually indistinguishable. Rather than treating this as an abstract puzzle, embed it in a concrete institutional setting: a near-future conservation institute that operates under the legal doctrine of Causal Unbrokenness, which asserts that an object’s cultural value is grounded in an uninterrupted causal-historical chain connecting it to its creator’s original act of production.

Within this world, introduce a niche restoration technology—mnemonic grafting—that does not merely replicate surface features but reconstructs and embeds the artist’s decision-pathway, including hesitations, revisions, and emotional inflections, into a new substrate. The protagonist, a senior conservator, is tasked with applying this technique to a masterpiece destroyed in an archival fire. She finds that the grafted reconstruction reproduces not only the finished work but also an intermediate layer in which the original artist corrected a mistake that had haunted him; the reconstructed object thus contains a resolution the historical original never achieved. The institute’s authentication protocol demands that she classify the result either as an original (if intentional continuity is sufficient) or as a forgery (if physical continuity is necessary).

Write the narrative from her first-person perspective as she prepares her submission, allowing the philosophical conflict to emerge through her interactions with colleagues, the technical reports she writes, and her private examination of the reconstructed surface. The story should ask whether authenticity resides in material lineage, in recovered intentionality, or in some third property—perhaps the vulnerability of the object to further alteration—and should resist a tidy resolution. Please do not include a bibliography, cite specific philosophers by name, or rely on obscure theoretical terminology that requires outside knowledge; the concepts should be fully intelligible from the narrative and institutional rules you establish.
