Write a deadpan parody of an agony-aunt philosophical self-help column—approximately 650 words, formatted as three letter-and-reply exchanges—titled “Dear Autonomia: On the Preservation of Human Activity in the Age of Synthetic Labor,” and published in the invented venue The Post-Productivity Quarterly. The voice should be that of an invented consultant philosopher, “Dr. H. V. Arendt-Whitman” (affiliated with the imaginary Institute for the Study of Durable Worlds), writing with bureaucratic warmth and absolute conceptual rigidity. The satire should target the real tendency in post-work philosophy and policy discourse to salvage the “meaning of work” by reclassifying structural unemployment as a higher form of human flourishing—specifically, the habit of conflating Hannah Arendt’s tripartite distinction among labor (the cyclical biological maintenance performed by the animal laborans), work (the world-building fabrication of homo faber that produces durable objects), and action (praxis: plural, speech-based appearance in a public realm) with modern metrics of productivity, emotional labor, and “human capital.”

The humor must arise from applying these categories with grotesque precision to absurd scenarios rather than from misrepresenting them. Invent two letter-writers: first, a former “bullshit jobs” auditor—specialized in generating compliance documentation for other non-essential roles—who has been replaced by an automated auditing system and now seeks guidance on whether this displacement allows him to graduate from labor into true action; second, a care-worker whose emotional labor (previously involving attachment-based interaction with vulnerable adults) is now performed by a large language model fine-tuned on attachment theory, leaving her wondering whether her remaining “unpaid presence” counts as world-building work or mere biological maintenance. The columnist’s replies should stay entirely in character: deadpan, never winking, using no real scholars’ names (only invented institutional affiliations), and avoiding any bibliography, citation, or reference to specific editions or page numbers. Each reply should exaggerate the real feature of the discourse being satirized—the impulse to rename economic displacement as voluntary “post-labor praxis”—by pushing Arendt’s logic to a reductio: for instance, defining public action so administratively that it requires triplicate forms filed in a civic square, or treating the loss of durable world-building as an opportunity to embrace the “pure cyclical metabolism” of unemployment.

Keep the tone polished, formal, and conceptually legible: a specialist should recognize that the parody is fair—that labor, work, and action are being kept rigorously separate, that the distinction between the private realm of necessity and the public realm of appearance is being invoked correctly, and that the satire is directed at the rationalization of automation rather than at Arendt herself. Do not ask for quotes from actual texts; paraphrase any philosophical distinctions without quotation marks. The piece should be meaningful without web access: it should depend only on widely memorized conceptual architecture, but arranged in a sufficiently niche configuration—synthetic emotional labor, the auditing of bullshit jobs, bureaucratized praxis—that it does not read like a generic essay on robots and jobs.
