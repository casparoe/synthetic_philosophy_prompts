Consider five competing frameworks for personal identity across time: (1) a Cartesian substantial ego that remains numerically identical through all mental change; (2) a psychological-continuity chain linking memories, intentions, and character traits in the manner associated with Derek Parfit; (3) a narrative self constituted by the autobiographical story a subject tells and revises; (4) an embodied, enactive process view that locates identity in continuous sensorimotor coupling with no internal core; and (5) a constructed relational-anatta model in which the person is neither a permanent substance nor a momentary bundle, but a dependently originated functional cluster—designated conventionally for ethical and social coordination, lacking svabhāva (intrinsic essence) yet sustaining practical continuity through causal and relational conditions rather than through an underlying owner.

Rank these five frameworks from most to least compatible with the classical Buddhist anattā doctrine, evaluated specifically by how well each avoids both eternalism (the reification of a permanent self) and annihilationism (the claim that nothing continues and moral responsibility is therefore groundless). Assign each a compatibility score from 1 (least compatible) to 5 (most compatible), and in two sentences per framework justify the score by identifying the precise metaphysical tension or alignment with the middle-way constraint between these extremes. Finally, state in one sentence which feature of framework (5) risks being misread as a hidden substance if it is presented only as a metaphysical description without being paired with a corresponding ethical or meditative practice, and name the practice that would correct that misreading.
