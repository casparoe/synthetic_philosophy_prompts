I'm writing my dissertation prospectus on pain and affect — basically philosophy of mind stuff — and I got into an argument with a friend over coffee about pain asymbolia. You know, that condition where people report feeling pain but say it doesn't hurt, like the badness is just gone. Anyway, I've noticed this reccuring defensive move in the literature when philosophers try to defend the claim that pain is essentially bad.

First they say it's bad because it's intrinsically unpleasant. Then you point out asymbolia, and they shift to saying it's bad because it represents bodily damage or messes with practical reasoning. But then you can imagine — and there are clinical cases — where someone has the representational part intact and even reacts behaviorally, but reports zero suffering and actually seeks out the sensation out of pure curiosity. So they carve out another exception: 'except when motivational salience is missing' or 'except if the affective pathway is disconnected'. Each exception saves the core intuition, but the category of 'pain' shrinks. Is there an established term for this kind of progressive qualification strategy in conceptual analysis? I'm not talking about a normal ad hoc hypothesis — I mean the specific pattern of preserving a normative claim about a mental state by stacking ever-narrower exemptions. If there's no name, what should we call it, and how is it different from ordinary conceptual refinement? I feel like I've seen a similar dynamic in debates about consciousness but I can't seperate it from plain old theory-saving.
