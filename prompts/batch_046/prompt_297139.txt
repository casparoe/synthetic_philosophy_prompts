The target is the problem of old evidence: the formal difficulty that if evidence E is already entailed by one's background knowledge K, then P(E | K) equals 1, so Bayes' theorem yields no increase in the posterior P(H | K ∪ E) for any new hypothesis H that predicts E, despite the strong intuition that learning H explains E should still confer some confirmation. To avoid a generic nerd joke, embed the punchline in the following niche framing: imagine a subjective Bayesian who has always known that her antique pocket watch stops exactly every Tuesday at noon (E). The joke should turn on whether her credence in H updates, given that E was already in her prior, and should specifically invoke either the logical-probability resolution (that P(E) can be less than 1 relative to a different epistemic state) or the background-knowledge-partitioning move (redefining K to exclude the entailment of E). It must make sense to someone familiar with the old-evidence literature but should not rely on named citations, bibliographies, or obscure technical jargon beyond standard Bayesian notation. Please present it in a concise, seminar-appropriate form.
