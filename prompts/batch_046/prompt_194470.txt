Compare the combination problem that confronts constitutive micropsychism—how micro-level subjects with primitive phenomenal properties could jointly constitute a unified macro-subject—with the threshold problem that confronts strong emergentism about consciousness—why consciousness should arise suddenly at a particular level of organizational complexity rather than gradually or not at all. For each problem, distinguish three layers of difficulty: the compositional layer (what entities or properties enter into the relation), the qualitative layer (whether the result preserves, transforms, or replaces the original properties), and the explanatory layer (whether the account predicts the absence of consciousness in simpler or differently organized systems). Then evaluate whether the micropsychist strategy of treating macro-subjectivity as fundamental—thereby dissolving combination rather than solving it—merely relocates the explanatory burden to accounting for the organized appearance of micro-subjects, and whether the emergentist strategy of positing a novel kind of property at the threshold faces a structurally parallel relocation regarding the organizational conditions that trigger emergence. Which relocation is more damaging to the respective view, and does either strategy genuinely resolve its problem or only rename it?
