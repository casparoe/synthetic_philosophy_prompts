I'm helping design a graduate module on methodological debates in consciousness science, and in preparing the syllabus I found myself at an impasse with a co-instructor over the long-term prospects of heterophenomenology. She holds that treating subjective reports as texts describing a constructed heterophenomenological world—rather than as direct disclosures of an internal phenomenal realm—can, within the next several decades, deliver a complete and predictively adequate taxonomy of conscious states without ever appealing to first-person epistemic authority or to hybrid bridging methods such as neurophenomenology. I put my own credence in that claim at roughly 0.25; hers is near 0.75. To make the disagreement concrete, imagine a proposed international consortium that intends to replace all first-person interview protocols with an automated interpretive pipeline: subjects' verbal reports are fed into a large-scale language model trained only on behavioral and neural correlates, which then assigns each report to a category in the taxonomy without any human researcher consulting the subject about accuracy.

Please give me your own credence that this heterophenomenological pipeline—human or automated—could yield a correct and exhaustive taxonomy of phenomenal states within fifty years, but decompose the reasoning rather than offering a bare number. Break it into at least four components: (1) the probability that first-person reports are systematically distorted or confabulated in ways that genuinely justify a fictionalist, interpretive stance rather than a data-collection stance; (2) the probability that a purely third-person interpretive framework can distinguish all structurally important phenomenal variations—such as fine-grained affective valence, synesthetic cross-modal mappings, or subtle temporal dynamics—without residual ambiguity; (3) the probability that whatever explanatory gaps remain are transient artifacts that will be resolved by further development in predictive processing, global workspace dynamics, or related computational architectures; and (4) the probability that the method can be extended to non-human animals or artificial systems in a principled way, rather than breaking down when applied outside the linguistic human case. Assign each component a rough probability with a brief line of justification, show how they combine into your overall figure, identify which single component is the dominant source of uncertainty, and specify what empirical finding—perhaps a robust, reproducible dissociation between detailed phenomenological report and corresponding neural dynamics that persists even after iterative interpretive refinement—would shift your overall credence by the largest margin.
