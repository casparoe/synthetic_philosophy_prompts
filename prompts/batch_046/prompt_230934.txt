okay so my study group came up with this argument that geometric rationality (like Kelly betting) actually makes moral progress impossible, and i cant figure out where it slips. heres the chain:

(1) if you're rational you maximize the geometric mean of your outcomes across worlds, not the arithmetic mean.
(2) now suppose you care about N people equally. then your "portfolio" includes all of their welfare levels in each world.
(3) to be geometrically rational about the whole portfolio, you maximize the product of welfare levels across people and worlds, weighted by probabilities.
(4) that means you maximize the expected log of the product, which is the same as maximizing the sum of expected logs of each person's welfare.
(5) but log is unbounded below: if there's any world where even one person's welfare is zero, the whole product is zero in that world, dragging the geometric mean to zero.
(6) so any gamble that risks even one person's welfare being zero has geometric value zero, no matter how huge the gains for everyone else are.
(7) therefore a geometrically rational social planner should reject every innovation, policy, or bet that carries a nonzero chance of catastrophic harm to anyone.
(8) but that means we should never do anything risky at all, which is absurd.
(9) so geometric rationality must be the wrong criterion for social decision-making.

the conclusion seems wrong but every step looks okay? which premise should i actually reject? i really want to keep (1) and (4) if possible. also, is there a hidden move in (3) where treating group welfare like a capital portfolio sneaks in an assumption about how welfare aggregates?
