I have been trying to work out my own view on how Buddhist ethics relates to consequentialist reasoning, and I want to see whether my case judgments hang together or whether I am smuggling in assumptions that conflict. Here are my verdicts.

First, a monk violates the Vinaya prohibition on eating after noon in order to feed a traveler who is starving. I think this is not merely permissible but ethically required: the immediate reduction of suffering outweighs the institutional breach, and the monk’s intention is compassionate rather than indulgent.

Second, a bodhisattva deliberately lies to a tyrant in order to conceal the location of refugees. I think this is clearly right, even though lying ordinarily generates negative mental formations and adverse karma.

Third, a practitioner spends years in deep jhāna, eliminating her own suffering entirely, but never assists others. Another practitioner forgoes advanced meditation to perform manual labor that alleviates widespread suffering but never achieves deep mental states. I think the second practitioner is ethically more advanced, despite possessing less internal purity.

Fourth, a monastery conducts a large-scale merit-making ceremony that burns extensive incense and directly harms countless insects, arguing that the collective spiritual benefit for thousands of participants justifies the harm. I think this is wrong: the violence is intentional and proximate, and merit generated indirectly cannot cancel direct injury.

Fifth, a teacher tells a struggling student that their doctrinal understanding is already complete, knowing it is false, in order to preserve the student’s confidence and prevent them from abandoning practice. I think this is not fine, even though the intention is compassionate and the long-term outcome might be beneficial.

Sixth, a donor gives a large sum to an inefficient charitable organization that saves many lives through sheer scale, rather than to a highly efficient organization that saves fewer lives per dollar but maximizes impact per unit of giving. I think the donor to the inefficient but large-scale charity has done the better thing, because the scope of compassion matters more than optimization.

Is there a single coherent principle—perhaps drawing on cetanā, upāya, or the weighting of karmic results—that captures all six verdicts? If not, which verdict should be overturned, and why? Please provide either one principle or a small set of competing candidates, stating precisely which clause is motivated by which case above. Give your verdict on at least one further case I have not listed—for instance, a surgeon who knows that operating on a patient will save that patient’s life but will cause her severe psychological trauma that prevents her from saving dozens of future patients. Finally, please state honestly which of my six verdicts your preferred principle cannot accommodate, and whether accommodating it would require an implausible complication.
