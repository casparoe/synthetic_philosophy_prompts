Consider the following line of argument about material constitution. The familiar statue-and-clay case is usually presented as a challenge to mereological essentialism: if clay is shaped into a statue and then melted, the statue is destroyed while the clay remains, so they must differ. Yet this framing assumes that constitution is a static, one-time relation between an object and the sum of its parts at a given moment. I want to suggest that for artifacts, constitution is better understood as a dynamic, protocol-governed relation that I will call constitutive maintenance. On this view, the clay does not simply constitute the statue in virtue of being arranged statue-wise; rather, the statue exists only so long as there is an ongoing satisfaction of a maintenance protocol—whether that involves preserving the original material, allowing gradual replacement of parts, or substituting materially different components—where the protocol itself is fixed by the artifact's kind and the intentions of its maker. If this is right, then the standard puzzle conflates two distinct failures: the failure of material persistence (the clay is melted) and the failure of protocol satisfaction (the maintenance conditions are violated). A bronze statue gradually replaced with steel according to a conservation plan might persist, not because bronze and steel share some hidden essence, but because the replacement falls within the protocol. Conversely, if the original clay is melted and reshaped into a bowl without reference to the artistic protocol, the statue is destroyed even though all the original material is present in a new arrangement. The central question, then, is not whether the statue is identical to the clay, but whether the constitution relation survives disruptions that violate the maintenance protocol.

I am interested in whether this reconceptualization is coherent, whether it genuinely resolves anything in the original debate, and whether it collapses into an existing metaphysical view—perhaps a form of four-dimensionalism with a functional overlay, or an application of a Baker-style constitution theory restricted to artifacts—without adding explanatory value. In particular: does treating constitution as protocol-dependent change the conditions under which we should say the statue survives part-replacement? Does it help with the case where the clay is replaced particle by particle, or does it merely rename the problem? And is there a principled way to distinguish the maintenance protocol of an artifact from the biological homeostasis of a living organism, or does the analogy break down?

Please comment on the accuracy of this framing, on its philosophical interest, and on any weaknesses in the reasoning—especially whether the notion of a maintenance protocol can be made precise without circular appeal to the very artifact identity it is meant to explain.
