Consider the following essay outline on the problem of dirty hands in politics. I distinguish two modes of moral contamination in public life. The first, which I call transactional dirt, corresponds to the familiar scenario: a leader makes a discrete decision—authorizing deception, ordering lethal force, breaking an alliance—to avert catastrophe. Here there is a clear agent, a calculable trade-off, and a specific wrong that can be weighed against a specific good. The second mode, which I call constitutional grime, is not anchored to any single decision. It arises when officials continuously maintain institutional structures—border regimes, fiscal architectures, security apparatuses—that predictably generate severe harm as a routine byproduct of normal operation. The harm is diffuse, cumulative, and embedded in the system rather than triggered by an exceptional act.

The standard dirty-hands defense holds that extraordinary circumstances may license extraordinary wrongs, on the condition that the agent bears the moral residue. But this defense seems tailored to transactional dirt: it relies on the drama of exception, the visibility of choice, and the possibility of personal atonement. Constitutional grime offers none of these. There is no heroic moment of decision, no clear point at which one might refuse and thereby stop the harm. One does not choose to maintain the tax or immigration system in the same direct sense that one chooses to authorize a strike. Yet the cumulative damage may far exceed that of discrete acts. This produces an asymmetry: discrete immoral actions are debated and sometimes excused, while continuous structural complicity is treated as mere administration. I suggest that the asymmetry is unstable. Either the dirty-hands justification extends to institutional maintenance—implying that officials are permanently licensed to preserve harmful systems—or it does not extend, which would imply that the gravest moral burden of politics lies not in exceptional choices but in ordinary governance.

Please comment on the coherence of this distinction and on whether the argument that constitutional grime undermines the standard dirty-hands defense succeeds. In particular, is there a morally relevant difference between discrete decision and structural maintenance that I have overlooked, and does the absence of a clear refusal point weaken or strengthen the moral excuse for maintaining harmful institutions? Please also state what would change your assessment—whether showing that structural complicity is categorically different from discrete wrongdoing, or demonstrating that continuous participation actually generates a heavier rather than lighter moral obligation. Note where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
