Consider the following examination question and a student’s response, which applies debates about historical objectivity to a concrete methodological scenario.

Question: Leopold von Ranke urged historians to recover the past as it actually happened, an ideal often rendered in the German phrase wie es eigentlich gewesen. In his study What Is History?, E. H. Carr contended that the historian’s selection and arrangement of evidence is inseparable from interpretation, thereby undermining Rankean objectivity. Hayden White, developing a structuralist analysis of historical writing, argued that historical narratives are constituted by linguistic tropes and modes of emplotment that are not themselves determined by empirical evidence. Imagine a research group that uses automated text-mining to extract every dated mention of a particular event from a digitized archive and arranges these mentions in strict chronological order, producing a narrative without any human selection of significance. A defender claims that this algorithmic procedure finally achieves Rankean objectivity, since the machine applies no interpretive framework.

In your answer, (a) explain Carr’s critique of Rankean objectivity and assess whether algorithmic extraction resolves the problem Carr identifies; (b) explain White’s analysis of narrative form and assess whether an automatically generated chronology escapes his critique; and (c) state one point of convergence and one point of divergence between Carr and White concerning the role of empirical evidence in historical writing.

Student answer:
Ranke believed that the historian should act like a mirror, reflecting the past without adding anything of his own, which is summed up in his phrase about showing things as they actually were. Carr rejects this mirror theory in What Is History? by arguing that facts never speak for themselves; historians must choose which facts are important and fit them into a causal or narrative pattern. This means that history is nothing but interpretation, and there is no past that exists independently of the historian’s account. Applying this to the algorithm, Carr would still object, because the programmers decided which archives to digitize, which keywords to use, and how to define an event-mention, so interpretation has only been moved upstream.

White takes a different but related line. He claims that all historical writing is structured by deep linguistic patterns. Specifically, historians organize their material through four prefigurative tropes—romance, tragedy, comedy, and satire—which shape the meaning of the events before any evidence is examined. Because these tropes are not empirically verifiable, an algorithm that lists events in chronological order avoids White’s problem entirely: it uses no narrative mode at all, so there is no hidden literary structure distorting the record. Both Carr and White agree that pure objectivity is impossible, but they differ on whether evidence can constrain interpretation. Carr thinks that once a historian selects a problem, the available sources still limit what can be said, whereas White thinks that the archival record only supplies the content and never determines the form, so the same evidence could support romance or tragedy equally well. Therefore, neither thinker would accept the algorithmic defense, though Carr would locate the remaining bias in the design choices and White would deny that the chronological list is even a history in the proper sense.
