I've been thinkng about this since arguing with my flatmate over coffee — she's on the disability office committee at our uni and was explaining this funding rule they have. Basically students apply for adjustments (extra exam time, note-takers, whatever) and list what they think they need, but then the office tracks actual take-up across the term. If you requested extended deadlines but never actually used them, they log that as a revealed preference against the adjustment and reduce your allocation the next year, saying the non-use proves you never really valued it. Meanwhile in my welfare-economics module we learnt that revealed-preference theory only gives you ordering, not intensity, and that failing to take something up might just be stigma, hassle, or changed circumstances — not proof that your true utility was lower all along. So what theory of value is that office running on? Does it presuppose that actual behaviour reveals stable, transparent preferences about your own welfare, maybe even cardinal ones? Or can a modest ordinal view explain the gap some other way? I want to know whether the practice strictly needs that stronger psychological commitment, or if it's compatible with several rival theories of utility. And if we replaced their implicit model with the economists' cautious one — that choice only reveals ordering under specific constraints — what exactly would have to change in how they measure need and distribute resources?
