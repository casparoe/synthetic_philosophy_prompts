Assume that Søren Kierkegaard, having observed the transformation of the public sphere since the mid-nineteenth century, is asked to apply his critique of the press and the leveling of the crowd to the contemporary environment of algorithmically curated digital platforms. Rather than treating these platforms as merely accelerated newspapers, frame the problem through the following distinction: in the print culture he diagnosed, leveling operated synchronously and uniformly, with a single public absorbing individuals into an anonymous mass that spoke collectively without individual commitment; today, recommendation algorithms construct asynchronous, predictive publics that anticipate each user's preferences, fragmenting the crowd into personalized feeds while optimizing engagement through invisible metrics.

How would Kierkegaard likely assess whether this shift from synchronous to asynchronous predictive leveling weakens or intensifies his original diagnosis? Address three specific points. First, would he interpret algorithmic fragmentation—where every user inhabits a distinct information environment—as a restoration of particularity and active choice, or as a more insidious form of leveling, because the algorithm pre-calculates desire and replaces the individual's passionate commitment with passive, pre-formatted consumption? Second, apply his distinction between the crowd, in which truth is diluted by numerical strength and no one bears responsibility, and the single individual who stands in inward relation to the eternal: how does this distinction hold when there is no unified crowd but millions of algorithmically isolated micro-publics, each convinced of its own particularity? Third, consider the invisibility of the curating mechanism—there is no identifiable editor, only an optimization function—relative to his critique of the press as an abstraction that severs speech from action: does the absence of a human editorial agent exacerbate the abstraction, or does the personalization of content restore a direct, unmediated address that the mass press destroyed?

In responding, reconstruct his reasoning through the conceptual vocabulary central to his analysis—leveling, the crowd, inwardness, passion, and individual responsibility—without providing bibliographies, citations, or references to specific editions. Conclude by stating whether this modern environment would compel him to revise his claim that the press makes everyone an expert on everything precisely by making no one responsible for anything, or whether the predictive structure of algorithmic media confirms that diagnosis in an altered form.
