I'm putting together a little packet for my aestetics reading group — we just finished arguing about aesthetic testimony and I'm still not settled on whether it's possible to genuinely learn that something's beautiful just from being told, or if you're always just holding a kind of rented belief until you feel it yourself. There's this framing I like: testimony in art doesn't exactly transfer knowledge, it delegates perception, like you're letting someone else see for you and then borrowing their reaction without owning it. I want a poem, maybe 16 lines, split into three stanzas, with the same word at the end of each stanza but that word needs to shift meaning — first literal seeing, then recognizing/understanding, then being exposed or witnessed. The speaker should be in front of a specific minimal work, maybe a grey monochrome canvas or one of those white rooms with a single bulb, recieving testimony from a friend about its emotional weight. The turn should happen in stanza two when they realize the borrowed feeling isn't taking root, leaving them with this hollow authority. Don't resolve it; just hold the tension between wanting shared taste and knowing that unfelt belief is just a placeholder. Make it strange and actually poetic, not a textbook summary.
