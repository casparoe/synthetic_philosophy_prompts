As a retired experimental physicist who has only recently begun working through philosophy with any seriousness—spurred, I confess, by a long winter with Plutarch's Parallel Lives and a growing irritation with modern self-help biography—I find myself stuck between two camps that seem to talk past each other on whether studying specific lives is a legitimate mode of moral learning. On one side stand the Plutarchan exemplarists: they hold that biography is not mere illustration but a distinct moral technology, one in which hagiographic selection and narrative coherence are functional features, not bugs, because they enable emotional identification and allow the reader to isolate virtues and vices through the pairing of parallel Greek and Roman figures. On the other side stand the critical narrative skeptics: they maintain that biography is irreducibly corrupted by selection bias and narrative closure, that hagiography substitutes admiration for structural analysis, and that any moral claim drawn from a curated life collapses under what I will call the counterfactual biography test—imagining the same behavioral trait embedded in a different institutional context or narrative arc and finding it no longer clearly virtuous.

I want you to draft the joint statement these two camps should have produced after a year of good-faith adversarial collaboration. The document should begin with a numbered section of claims both sides can sign—addressing at least what Plutarch actually does structurally in pairing lives, the psychological reality of emotional identification with narrated figures, the unavoidable selectivity of biographical evidence, and the limits of causal inference from a single life—followed by a disagreements table that phrases each remaining dispute in wording its own side would accept for its position. Then identify the cruxes: I take one to be whether narrative closure is epistemically necessary for moral calibration or merely an aesthetic distortion that hides confounding variables, and another to be whether the counterfactual biography test is a legitimate check on moral exemplarity or an impossible standard that no historical life could survive. For each crux, specify a concrete case or comparative pair both sides agree bears on it, recording in advance what each camp predicts the case will show. Please treat this as a constructive text both parties could sign, flag any disagreement that turns out to be purely verbal and dissolve it explicitly, and do not include bibliographies, citation lists, or requests for further reading—the aim is to capture the conceptual architecture of the dispute itself, not to inventory sources.
