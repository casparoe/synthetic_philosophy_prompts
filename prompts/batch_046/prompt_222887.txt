I am a retired molecular biologist who, since leaving the laboratory, has been studying philosophy of science and rational-choice theory in some depth. I currently serve on the Ethics and Protocol Review Board of a university-affiliated palliative-care research institute. At this week's session we must render a written opinion on a proposed randomized clinical study—designated Protocol 7-B—that compares standard exhaustive informed-consent disclosure against an algorithmically truncated "satisficing" disclosure model. In the experimental arm, patients are presented with a dynamically reduced set of treatment options calibrated, the investigators claim, to each patient's measured cognitive load and an adaptively lowered aspiration threshold; the theoretical justification draws on bounded-rationality accounts of decision-making under stress, arguing that full disclosure produces informational overload that undermines rather than enhances autonomous choice. Critics on the board maintain that treating descriptive cognitive limits as normative design parameters collapses the distinction between explaining how patients decide and prescribing how they ought to be informed, and that institutionalizing truncated disclosure for a vulnerable population sets a precedent for lowering epistemic standards in clinical ethics. I am drafting the board's formal recommendation memo—approve, reject, or approve subject to enforceable conditions—for circulation before Thursday's vote. The memo should present a concrete course of action with a clear timeline for implementation or revision, treat the opposing view with genuine intellectual charity rather than dismissing it, and avoid any request for bibliographies, external citations, or further reading lists, since I need reasoning I can weigh entirely within the document. Write in a tone that respects conceptual rigor—this is not a popular essay—but remember that my training is in empirical science, not in philosophy, so ground the argument in the structure of the decision problem itself rather than in disciplinary name-checking.
