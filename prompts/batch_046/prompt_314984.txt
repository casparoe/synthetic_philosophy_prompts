Write a short story, composed in British English, set during a philosophy tutorial at a provincial university where the tutor announces that there will be a surprise examination at some point before the end of term. Rather than treating the announcement as a mere logic puzzle, the students become absorbed in a disagreement about what "surprise" actually denotes in this context—whether it refers strictly to the failure of a rational agent to predict an event, or whether it requires a specific phenomenological state of violated expectation that can persist even when the event is logically anticipated. One student should defend the view that once the class reaches common knowledge that the exam cannot fall on the final day—because then it would not be surprising—the logical space for surprise collapses iteratively, leaving no possible day for the exam to occur; another should object that this argument illegitimately assumes that holding a belief about Friday eliminates the visceral anticipation that makes Friday's arrival genuinely unexpected, and that people routinely experience surprise despite having formed correct beliefs. The narrative should weave in the tutor's quiet observation that the same ambiguity between predictive belief and lived expectation appears in how citizens respond to political forecasts or algorithmic recommendations, though the story should not resolve this parallel explicitly. The plot should turn on the revelation that the examination was in fact administered on Tuesday morning, before the students' reasoning had concluded, leaving them to wonder whether their failure to anticipate it was a failure of logic or of collective attention. Keep the dialogue intellectually precise but naturally spoken, avoid offering a simple moral resolution, and allow the philosophical tension to linger as the characters recognise that their reasoning about belief and expectation might extend far beyond the classroom.
