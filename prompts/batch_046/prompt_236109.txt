As someone who spends more time constructing narrative architectures than resolving syllogisms, I have been trying to formalize the intuition that drives my current novel: that societies do not so much collapse as they are translated into forms we fail to recognize. The argument below, which I drafted for a lecture I never delivered, now strikes me as suspicious precisely because it concludes that the distinction between collapse and transformation is itself a fiction—perhaps a comforting fiction, but a fiction nonetheless. I want to preserve the idea that there is a meaningful difference between the end of the Classic Maya court system and the mere evolution of modern bureaucracy, yet I also want to hold that persistence conditions for societies are institutional rather than territorial or demographic. Here is the chain.

(1) A society S at time t is identical to society S' at time t' only if S and S' share at least one core institutional function—redistributive storage, legal adjudication, or ritual legitimation—that remains continuously operative across the interval.

(2) The palace economies of the Late Bronze Age, the Classic Maya polity, and the Rapa Nui ceremonial complex each lost every institutional function that had defined them within roughly one to three generations.

(3) By (1), none of these societies persisted; they were replaced by social organizations that shared neither personnel, practices, nor structural purposes with their predecessors.

(4) Apply (1) consistently to the present: the shift from agrarian, kin-based production to digital, algorithmic coordination over the last two centuries involved the complete cessation of every core institutional function that defined pre-industrial societies.

(5) Therefore, by the same reasoning, modern industrial and post-industrial societies are not transformed versions of earlier ones but entirely new entities that replaced them—meaning the transition was not change but collapse followed by succession.

(6) But we do not describe the Industrial Revolution or the digital transition as collapses; we treat them as internal developments of a continuous civilization.

(7) To avoid that conclusion, one might weaken (1) so that societies can survive the total loss of defining institutions, provided territorial continuity or demographic overlap remains.

(8) Yet if territorial and demographic continuity alone are sufficient for persistence, then Easter Island after the moai-building era and the Classic Maya lowlands after court abandonment should also count as the same societies in reduced form—precisely the conclusion (1) was meant to avoid.

(9) Hence (1) either forces us to call every major transformation a collapse, or it forces us to abandon the distinction between collapse and transformation altogether.

Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If I must choose, I would rather keep (1) and (3) intact, even if it means accepting (5) and reclassifying modernity as a post-collapse reconstruction. But I want to know whether there is a hidden slide—perhaps in the scope of "core institutional function" or in the analogy between ancient and modern transitions—that allows me to keep my narrative distinction without paying that price.
