Consider a normative designer who advises synthetic societies on whether to institutionalize guilt or shame as the primary moral emotion. Her verdicts to date are these: in a small face-to-face honor village that replaced communal shame with legal guilt for corruption, she endorsed keeping shame for violations of collective obligations; in a modern corporation whose executive committed fraud, she held that guilt-based liability is correct and public shaming must be barred to permit rehabilitation; in a transitional state that used pillory for domestic violence, she rejected the practice, arguing that shame drives private harm underground and that confidential guilt-based accountability is required; and in an open-source collective where a maintainer abused contributors, she endorsed transparent reputational degradation—an institutional form of shame—over private remorse, on the ground that the injury was to collective trust rather than to discrete individuals.

She is now advising the Consensus, a large-scale virtual culture of millions with no embodied interaction, in which all reputation is algorithmically transparent and automatically adjusted, and in which restorative protocols can precisely return an offender's standing to its prior value. A member has deliberately introduced a subtle bias into the shared decision-making algorithm, harming no specific person but distorting collective outcomes for months. The Consensus can engineer either guilt—private algorithmic counseling that lowers an internal moral-health score without altering public reputation—or shame—an automatic, visible reputation reduction paired with a precise algorithmic path to full restoration through collective service.

Predict her recommendation. Identify the candidate rules that fit her four verdicts—such as one keyed to social scale and anonymity, one keyed to whether status is precisely restorable, and one keyed to whether the harm targets collective fabric or discrete individuals—say what each predicts for the Consensus, and argue for your best guess, noting which of her earlier cases is the closest analog. Then specify the single further case—varying either scale, restorability, or the nature of harm—that would most clearly distinguish the rules. Finally, address whether her framework permits a culture to genuinely choose between guilt and shame, or whether structural features determine the answer.
