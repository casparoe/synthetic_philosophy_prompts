I am currently enrolled in a graduate seminar on philosophy of mind and cognitive science, where we recently discussed the extended mind thesis in the context of long-term human-machine cognitive integration. After class, two of my fellow students, Mara and Julian, became entangled in a disagreement about whether a philosopher who has relied for two decades on a continuously available, highly personalized artificial reasoning assistant could legitimately be said to have that system as part of her extended mind. I am writing to ask you to adjudicate their exchange, because neither seemed satisfied with the other’s rebuttals, and I want to understand exactly where the reasoning holds or collapses.

Mara opened by pressing the functional-parity intuition associated with Clark and Chalmers: if the assistant is always accessible, highly reliable, automatically consulted during deliberation, and its outputs are trusted without independent verification, then it plays exactly the same functional role as biological long-term memory or internal reasoning routines. She argued that denying it constitutive status merely because it sits outside the skull is an arbitrary internalist prejudice. Julian replied by invoking the coupling-constitution distinction—coupling is necessary but not sufficient for constitution. He granted the functional similarity but insisted that phenomenological transparency matters: the philosopher remains constantly aware that she is querying an external device, whereas biological memory retrieval typically lacks that explicit mediating awareness. Mara countered that transparency is a matter of degree, not kind, and that Clark’s later integrationist framework emphasizes trust, accessibility, and mutual adaptation rather than phenomenological invisibility. She added that internal retrieval is itself often mediated by conscious effort and reconstruction, so Julian’s transparency criterion fails to distinguish the cases cleanly.

Julian then introduced a more specific concern, which he called the problem of diachronic authorship. He claimed that for a process to be genuinely part of one’s mind, the system must be able to maintain a continuous claim of authorship over its contents across temporal gaps—sleep, distraction, or interruption—without requiring an external mechanism to regenerate or validate them. Biological memory survives sleep because consolidation is endogenous; the AI assistant, however, operates via stochastic algorithms running on remote servers, so the philosopher does not author the inferential steps—she merely selects among outputs generated by an independent causal process. Mara responded that this draws an illusory boundary: biological consolidation depends on environmental inputs, sleep architecture is partly externally regulated, and neural plasticity itself requires external stimulation. She accused Julian of smuggling in a hidden internalist commitment to endogenous causation that the extended mind framework was designed to dissolve. They reached an impasse, with Mara insisting that Julian’s authorship condition is ad hoc, and Julian insisting that Mara’s parity argument ignores the causal independence of the external generator.

Please adjudicate their dispute point by point. For each major move—Mara’s parity appeal, Julian’s transparency objection, Mara’s integrationist rebuttal, Julian’s diachronic-authorship argument, and Mara’s final charge of ad hoc internalism—indicate who makes the stronger point and why. In addition, identify what both disputants appear to be missing: for instance, whether there is a relevant distinction between the assistant’s role in storage versus in generative reasoning, whether the temporal scale of integration changes the normative stakes, or whether either side has addressed the possibility that extended cognition might come in degrees rather than as an all-or-nothing constitution claim. Do not provide a bibliography or reading list; I am interested only in the conceptual adjudication.
