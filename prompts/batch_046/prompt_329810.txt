I am working through a problem in the ethics of rescue that sits between Taurek's anti-aggregationist position and standard additive accounts of moral weight, and I am trying to determine what principle, if any, captures my own reflective verdicts across structurally varied cases. I tend to reject the view that numbers never matter, but I also resist the idea that they always matter in a simple additive fashion, particularly when other structural features—relational proximity, assigned duty, or the physical properties of the good being distributed—are present. Here are my judgments, presented concretely.

In a standard capsized-ferry scenario, I can make one trip to rescue either one stranger immediately to my left or five strangers slightly farther to my right, all facing comparable danger; I think I should go right, and I would regard saving the one as a serious moral failure. If, however, the one to the left is my sister and the five to the right are strangers, I think I should save her; here the relational fact seems to block aggregation rather than merely outweigh it, and abandoning her to save the strangers strikes me as betrayal rather than tragic necessity. The same blocking effect occurs if the one is a patient to whom I have a specific, assigned duty of care—say, I am her designated lifeguard—while the five are unassigned swimmers; my prior commitment nullifies the numerical disparity. Now consider distributive dilution: I possess one unit of antidote that can save one person with near certainty, or I can divide it five ways so that each of five people has a twenty percent chance of survival. Here I am genuinely uncertain, but my inclination is to administer the full dose to the single person, which suggests that aggregation may fail when it requires fragmenting an indivisible good in a way that eliminates any certain rescue. In an epistemically opaque variant, I can clearly see one definite victim to my left, but to the right there is only fog where there may be five people, or two, or none; I think I should rescue the visible individual rather than gamble on an unseen aggregate, implying that aggregation requires adequate epistemic access to the numbers. Finally, suppose the visible victim is someone I already rescued from a different accident yesterday; I do not think this prior saving diminishes her claim now, so if she competes with five strangers, I still lean toward saving the five—prior rescue does not create a moral discount.

These six verdicts feel coherent in pairs, but they are hard to hold together. Cases one, five, and six treat numbers as significant, yet cases two and three allow relational and role-based considerations to nullify them entirely, and case four makes me doubt whether aggregation survives when the resource is physically non-aggregable without destroying its efficacy. Is there a unified principle—perhaps one that distinguishes between additive moral weights and structurally constrained obligations—that can accommodate all six without ad hoc adjustments? Or must I drop one or more verdicts for the sake of simplicity, and if so, which should go—the dilution intuition in case four, or the epistemic constraint in case five? If a principle can be formulated precisely, I also want to know what it predicts for a case I have not listed: suppose I am one of five equally positioned rescuers, each facing one visible victim nearby, but we could coordinate to abandon our individual charges and collectively save a distant cluster of ten people. Would the principle require us to coordinate and save the greater number, or does it permit each of us to honor our local obligations?
