I keep encountering the phrase “this is the best of all possible worlds”, and each time it seems to do a different job. Last week, after a friend missed a train connection but then stumbled into a conversation that changed her mind about her career, she said it with a kind of relieved wonder — not sarcastically, but as if the mishap and the discovery were somehow linked in an optimal package. Another time, I heard it used to dismiss complaints about suffering, as though any objection to the way things are must be a failure to see the grand design. I have no formal training in philosophy, but I want to understand what precise claims are hiding inside this one sentence, because the people using it rarely seem to mean the same thing.

Could you separate out the distinct readings as a kind of menu? I am especially interested in distinctions that are easy to miss: for example, is the claim that our world maximises some overall value across all logically possible worlds, or only that it is the best among worlds that are genuinely accessible from our actual circumstances and choices? Does it require that every particular evil is a necessary ingredient of a greater good, or can it allow that some evils are genuinely bad yet still consistent with the world being best overall? And when someone applies it to the outcome of a specific decision they made — saying that, given they acted as they did, this result is the best available — is that the same metaphysical thesis, or a much weaker, almost practical claim about acceptance and regret? For each reading, please say whether it is a near-tautology, a serious and defensible position, or likely false, and indicate which version would actually justify comforting someone after a loss, and which version risks sounding like complacency about avoidable harm. Please answer in British English.
