I was working through Orwell’s political writings with a friend who studies rhetoric, and we kept circling back to a tension that feels like it collapses his whole project if pushed hard enough. Here is the argument we reconstructed, and I cannot see the exit. (1) In “Politics and the English Language,” Orwell claims that political writing corrupts language by making it euphemistic, and that this corruption is not accidental: terms like “administrative relocation” do not merely disguise forced displacement, they prevent the speaker from fully conceiving the violence involved. (2) If a vocabulary is structurally euphemistic in this way, then anyone who uses it—including critics who adopt its terms to argue against the policy—thinks within categories that already sanitize the harm; the language deterministically constrains conceptualization. (3) Orwell proposes escaping this trap by adopting plain, concrete, Anglo-Saxon-derived prose and rejecting “ready-made phrases.” (4) But the vocabulary of “plain English,” “concreteness,” and “Saxon words” is itself embedded in a specific political and cultural tradition—English empiricism, anti-bureaucratic populism, a particular national self-image—that has its own euphemistic history; calling a critique “obscurantist” is often a move to dismiss structural analysis using exactly this “plain language” frame. (5) So either Orwell’s escape vocabulary is just another politically loaded framework that deterministically constrains its users (making his proposal self-defeating), or the determinism in (2) is false and vocabulary does not actually trap thought. (6) Yet Orwell repeatedly suggests that bad language makes bad politics inevitable, which sounds like a strong determinist claim. (7) Therefore, either Orwell must concede that his own essay participates in the corruption it denounces, or he must weaken (2) so radically that language reform becomes merely decorative rather than politically necessary. I want to keep (1) and (3)—the idea that euphemism corrupts thought and that clarity is a moral duty—without ending up with (7). Which step should I reject, why did it seem plausible, and what does giving it up cost Orwell’s broader view of language and power?
