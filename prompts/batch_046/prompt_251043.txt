I am a doctoral student in political philosophy studying institutional failure and distributive justice. For the past several years I have been working through what might be called the post-Sen famine literature: Amartya Sen's entitlement-based account of the Bengal famine of 1943, which he and others have extended by analogy to the Irish famine of 1845–1852 and to the Great Leap Forward famine of 1959–1961, set against the food-availability-decline revisionists who maintain that in at least some of these episodes aggregate caloric supply collapsed and that framing the event primarily as an entitlement failure is therefore misleading. Despite extensive empirical work on mortality figures, grain procurement policies, and rural labor markets, the normative and causal framing seems frozen. Those in the Sen tradition tend to classify these episodes as structural political crimes—systematic failures of entitlement protection for which governing institutions bear direct culpability—while the revisionists tend to treat them as natural or systemic disasters that were severely exacerbated, but not solely caused, by mismanagement. Both sides acknowledge governance failures; neither has persuaded the other.

My working hypothesis is that the stall is conceptual rather than empirical. I suspect the two camps are implicitly operating with different definitions of what a famine is—one treating it as a demographic mortality event caused by food supply dynamics, the other treating it as an institutional failure of entitlement protection—so that their evidential standards and causal attributions are incommensurable rather than contradictory. But I am unsure whether this diagnosis is correct. Could there instead be a hidden empirical disagreement about, for example, the elasticity of rural labor markets in wartime Bengal or the precise caloric deficit in Henan Province during 1960 that archival work could in principle resolve? Or is the disagreement actually normative—about the threshold of government culpability for mass starvation—disguised as a dispute about causation?

I want an honest diagnosis of the structure of this disagreement, supported by what proponents on each side actually say and do, not a literature survey. Is it verbal, substantive, normative, or a case of two distinct questions—one about proximate causal mechanisms, one about institutional responsibility—being run together under the single heading "what caused the famine"? If it is conceptual, what distinction—perhaps between proximate causation and structural culpability, or between famine as event and famine as institutional failure—would disentangle it? And given your diagnosis, what would count as genuine progress: a formal model of entitlement collapse that both sides could accept, a previously unarchived dataset resolving the supply question, a normative framework for assigning political culpability for starvation, or an argument that the framing itself is ill-posed and should be abandoned? Please rank the tensions by severity if there are multiple, and be frank about whether anything short of abandoning one of the framing concepts could actually move the debate.
