I recently began serving on an interdisciplinary committee that evaluates long-range institutional risk assessments, and I have become preoccupied with an epistemic disagreement that none of our statistical protocols can resolve. When our group adopts a normative framework for weighing distant future harms—particularly one that requires us to take seriously concerns raised by dissenting stakeholders—some members argue that we should apply self-sampling reasoning: because we are one reasoning group among many possible ones that could have reached reflective equilibrium, our confidence in this framework should be treated as a random draw and therefore discounted as presumptuous unless we possess framework-independent justification. Other members argue for self-indication: the very fact that our particular framework has survived sustained critical scrutiny and remains stable under institutional stress-testing is evidence that such frameworks are genuinely viable, not that we are outliers sampling from a vast space of failed philosophies. I want to pitch a four-month research fellowship that moves beyond the standard cosmological framing of the presumptuous philosopher and asks whether there is a principled distinction between self-sampling and self-indication when the domain is normative judgment rather than physical existence. The existing philosophical literature addresses individual rational failure extensively, yet those treatments concern temporal inconsistency or motivational conflict within a single agent, not the collective epistemic position of reasoning groups selecting among stable normative frameworks. I need the proposal to frame the precise question—whether the reference class for sampling should be defined by physical observers, by agents at reflective equilibrium, or by frameworks that survive explicit institutional audit—explain why both purely statistical and purely ethical treatments leave it open, describe a conceptual approach that makes progress without requiring empirical data collection, and specify a realistic deliverable for committees like ours: decision criteria that distinguish legitimate collective confidence from the error of the presumptuous philosopher. I also want to inject an unconventional angle: rather than treating the weighing of dissenting objections as a separate distributive problem, I propose using it as a diagnostic tool, since deciding whose disagreement counts forces us to specify the reference class explicitly. The proposal should state what success would look like—likely a framework paper with operational criteria—what would count as failure, and why philosophical analysis rather than cognitive science should handle this conceptual work. Can you draft this as a formal one-page proposal with a clear bottom line and scope appropriate to the timeline?
