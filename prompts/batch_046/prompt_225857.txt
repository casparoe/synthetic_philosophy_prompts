I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and at a roundtable last month two ecologists — Dr. Ren and Dr. Kessler — got into an arguement about whether tearing down half a boreal forest for solar farms actually lowers wild-animal suffering. Ren said yes, definitely: wild lives are net-negative on average (predation, parasites, starvation), so cutting habitat cuts census, and that's like pulling tickets out of a suffering-lottery. Kessler pushed back that fragmentation doesn't just scale populations down; it restructures harm. Edge habitats spike stress in survivors, reproductive compensation means females have more litters that mostly die young, and mesopredator release creates new predation hotspots. So suffering per capita can jump even if headcount drops. Ren answered that Kessler was smuggling in anti-natalist logic — counting lives that never get born as losses — when welfare ethics should only weigh actual experienced harm. Kessler said he wasn't counting ghost lives; he was tracking the real, path-dependent suffering of survivors and the boom-bust cycles fragmentation creates. Then Ren raised trophic cascades: if connectivity breaks and apex predators vanish, herbivores suffer less predation, maybe that's a welfare gain. Kessler said that just shows Ren's census model is broken, because welfare is spatially heterogeneous and non-additive across trophic levels — you can't sum individual experiences when one species' suffering is structurally coupled to another's presence. They turned to me and asked who was right. Please adjudicate point by point: who's correct on reproductive compensation, on the census argument, and on whether trophic cascades actually help Ren's case? And say what thier both missing, especially about whether ecosystem welfare can be summed across species at all. I keep going back and forth.
