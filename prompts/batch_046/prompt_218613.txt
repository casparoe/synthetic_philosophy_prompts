I'm running a 75-minute session for an advanced philosophy of action seminar—fourteen juniors and seniors who have encountered Davidson's account of weakness of will in passing but have little background in behavioral economics. Last week we argued about whether hyperbolic discounting is merely a cognitive bias or reveals something about the structure of diachronic agency, and I want an in-class simulation that lets students feel the fracture from the inside rather than observing it as data. To avoid a routine marshmallow-test replay, build the activity around a somewhat niche framing: the immediate reward should carry an "abandonment option value"—the hidden value of being permitted to drop the long-term project entirely—so that participants discover they are often willing to pay more to eliminate the quitting option than to secure the delayed payoff itself. They should experience this reversal without anyone naming hyperbolic discounting or akrasia in advance; the labels and formal mapping come only in the debrief.

Please design a runnable activity—roles, rules, physical materials, a timeline within the session, and a payoff scheme using only printed time-credits or token candy with no real money—in which students split into planners who set binding group rules at t1 and actors who face altered incentives at t2, with a hidden exchange-rate decay that makes future credits less valuable in a non-linear way. The mechanics should force participants into the reversal themselves: the planner's resolution should be genuinely undermined in the actor phase not by ignorance but by a shift in evaluative perspective, and there should be a social-observation layer in which partners can see but not enforce choices, so the failure feels like a breach of an implicit contract with one's past self. Include a debrief that maps specific moments onto Davidson's divided self and onto the formal property of hyperbolic curves, lists what will predictably go wrong on the first run—for instance, students gaming the hidden rate, or a planner refusing to participate—and is frank about which phenomenological feature, namely the sense that the akratic act remains genuinely mine despite being contrary to my best judgment, the rules cannot reproduce and I must supply verbally. The group is seated at one large table; I have no projector.
