I've been turning over those old lifeboat puzzles—strangers deciding who must be sacrificed so the rest survive—but I keep wondering whether the problem shifts when the passengers are not strangers at all, but family members bound by a promise made years earlier: specifically, that the youngest child would always receive the last ration if circumstances ever turned this dire. Write a villanelle about this exact situation. Use the poem's two repeating refrains to carry the two competing obligations—one about honoring the promise, one about saving the greatest number. Let the form's circular repetitions mirror the way the moral argument keeps returning to the same point without reaching a harbor, and end in the final quatrain with both claims still standing, unresolved. Do not turn it into a sermon about duty or survival; keep the tension open, as if the boat itself has not yet decided which way to drift.
