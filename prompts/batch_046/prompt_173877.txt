A university’s tenure review uses a three-tier evaluation—strong recommendation, recommendation, or denial—plus a formal “indeterminate” category for candidates whose work straddles established disciplinary boundaries. When a case lands in that zone, the committee does not vote but remands it to a sub-panel that must apply a weighted rubric to force a binary promotion decision within thirty days; if the rubric produces a tie, denial is automatic. For staff hiring, by contrast, the same institution employs a strict qualified/unqualified dichotomy with no intermediate status and breaks ties by seniority. What theory of vagueness does the tenure protocol implicitly presuppose—epistemicism, that there is a hidden sharp boundary the rubric is trying to discover; supervaluationism, that the category is genuinely indeterminate until a precisification is imposed; or something that treats vagueness as a practical failure rather than a semantic one? Reconstruct the implicit view, check whether it is consistent with the staff-hiring binary, say what would have to change in the tenure process if the institution adopted the rival theory, and indicate whether the practice is philosophically underdetermined. Respond as a numbered list of exactly five items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
