Design a 50-minute seminar activity for about 16 philosophy undergraduates that makes them feel the force of Al-Ghazālī’s occasionalist challenge to necessary causation—specifically, the gap between observed constant conjunction and an inferred necessary connection—without lecturing about it first. The activity should use structured roles and a small-stakes prediction game (no real money) in which students initially believe they are producing outcomes through their own actions, develop informal “laws” linking their inputs to results, and then discover that every correlation they observed was in fact mediated by an unseen agent operating behind the scenes. Include: the physical setup and materials; explicit rules, turn order, and how collective or individual points depend on correct causal predictions; the exact mechanism and moment at which the hidden mediator is revealed; a debrief that maps specific moments of the simulation onto Al-Ghazālī’s distinction between apparent (customary) and real (divine) causation, and onto the broader problem of induction; what will predictably go wrong the first time it is run (for example, students guessing the mediator prematurely, or the facilitator accidentally signaling through timing); and an honest admission of which feature of occasionalism—such as the metaphysical claim that created beings possess no causal efficacy whatsoever—the activity can only gesture at and the facilitator must supply verbally. Do not request bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
