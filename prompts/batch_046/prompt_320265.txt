Design a 75-minute participatory simulation for 14 students in an upper-level philosophy seminar on Nietzsche's genealogy of morality, with the goal that participants feel—before the facilitator names it—how ressentiment operates structurally: not as private envy but as a creative inversion that replaces a hierarchy of strength with a moral economy in which weakness becomes merit and strength becomes a debt owed to the weak. Structure it as two consecutive rounds with hidden role cards and a token economy. In Round 1 (the noble phase), students earn visible status tokens only through direct competitive achievement—claiming resources, displaying risk, and openly ranking performance—tracked on a public leaderboard. In Round 2, silently change the rules: status tokens are now earned by auditing others’ achievements, issuing formal accusations of excess or arrogance, and demonstrating one’s own lack of competitive assets (e.g., having suffered setbacks or refrained from risk); those who continue Round 1 behavior lose tokens automatically, and students who invent new virtue-terms—such as purity or humility—that did not exist in Round 1 gain bonus tokens. Use only paper tokens, role cards, and a whiteboard; payoffs are symbolic status marks, not money. After both rounds, run a 15-minute debrief mapping Round 1 onto master valuation (good/bad) and Round 2 onto slave valuation (good/evil), pinpointing the moment participants stopped competing for goods and began competing for moral authority. Predict one failure mode—students interpreting Round 2 as egalitarian fairness rather than value-inversion—and specify how the facilitator should correct this without lecturing, for instance by asking participants to name the virtues they invented and trace their origins. End with an honest limitation: the simulation can reproduce the reactive mechanism of score-settling but cannot reproduce the historical, world-reshaping creativity of slave morality that generates an entirely new conceptual vocabulary; the facilitator must supply that distinction verbally, clarifying that ressentiment is not merely reactive punishment but the birth of a new moral language.
