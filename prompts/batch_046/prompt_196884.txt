Consider the track record of a university aquaculture-welfare oversight board: (1) it approved non-invasive cortisol monitoring in farmed salmon exposed to chronic low-level noise; (2) it rejected electric-shock nociception tests in shrimp that offered no direct therapeutic benefit to the subjects; (3) it approved comparative behavioral-enrichment studies in octopuses and lobsters; (4) it rejected limb-autotomy analgesic trials in crabs despite promised post-study veterinary care; and (5) it approved heart-rate-variability crowding studies in adult trout past reproductive age. The board now receives a proposal to induce targeted nociceptor knockdown in adult zebrafish, then subject them and wild-type controls to high-density crowding with invasive endpoint tissue sampling, in order to isolate which neural pathways drive long-term welfare deficits for future stock improvement; the individual subjects receive no direct benefit and will be euthanized for analysis. Predict the board’s verdict, identify the competing principles implicit in its record—such as a direct-benefit constraint, a tiered species-status rule, or a proportionality-of-knowledge standard—state which earlier ruling is the closest analog, and specify the further case that would best force a choice between those principles. Give a committed answer with reasons, not a menu of options.
