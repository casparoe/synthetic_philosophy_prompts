Construct a four-node sequential-bargaining scenario in the style of the centipede game, set in an ordinary commercial-negotiation context—say, a vendor and a purchaser revising delivery schedules—without invoking demons, perfect predictors, or science-fictional technology. The design must hold constant common knowledge of rationality and of the basic payoff ordering, but must include exactly one isolating feature: a narrowly scoped, realistic information asymmetry about one party's secondary payoff at a later node, where the existence of the asymmetry is common knowledge but its precise resolution is not. Engineer the payoffs and information conditions so that, with the feature present, rational play reaches node two and only then collapses via backward induction, whereas removing the feature restores immediate termination at node one. Present the decision tree with explicit ordinal or cardinal payoffs, specify the epistemic conditions at each node (who knows what about whom, and that they know it, etc.), and show step-by-step why induction is blocked at nodes one and two but applies at node three. Then identify explicitly: (a) which feature does the isolating work, (b) what the verdict becomes if that feature is stripped away while everything else is held fixed, and (c) how a defender of universal backward induction would attack your case—whether by arguing that the feature violates the common-knowledge requirement, that it implicitly introduces bounded rationality in disguise, or by some other line. The case should be suitable for explaining to an audience familiar with basic game theory but without formal epistemic logic, and it should be no longer than a concise paragraph for the scenario itself, with the analysis following.
