I'm taking a seminar on theories of well-being and have been arguing with friends about Nozick's experience machine, but I find my own gut judgments about modified versions of the case don't line up cleanly with either hedonism, desire-satisfaction, or the standard "contact with reality" objection. Here are my verdicts, and I'd like to know whether they cohere around a single principle or whether some have to be sacrificed.

In the classic irreversible machine—perfect simulated bliss, complete disconnection from reality, and no awareness of the artifice—I feel strongly that I should not plug in, and that a life spent entirely inside would be deficient in well-being even though phenomenally identical to a real life. In the "intermittent window" variant, where I spend eleven months in an indistinguishable simulation and then spend one fully conscious month in reality, with the option to repeat, my resistance weakens: if the outside world were bleak, I think I might rationally choose the cycle, yet I still feel the simulated months are somehow "discounted"—they contribute less to my well-being than real months, though I'm not sure whether that's because of ignorance, lack of achievement, or something else. In the "hidden contribution" variant, I'm immersed in pleasant experience but unaware that my brain is actually directing remote research drones that solve real scientific problems; here the existence of genuine real-world achievement makes the option feel much more acceptable, perhaps even good, yet I also feel that the ignorance undermines the value of that achievement for me, which sits uneasily with my willingness to accept the eleven-month ignorant stretches in the window case. In the "collective transparent" variant, my family and I are all in a shared simulation, fully aware of the setup, genuinely interacting with one another's minds through the interface; this feels closest to permissible—maybe even flourishing—yet I still think it is inferior to being embodied together in reality, even though I struggle to name what's missing. Finally, in the "reversible five-year" variant, I plug in, experience five years of rich, difficult, growth-filled virtual life, then unplug with intact memories and no elapsed real-world time; I think I would do this, but I also think those five years "don't fully count" toward my life's narrative weight in the way real years do, which puzzles me because I value narrative continuity highly.

Is there a coherent account—perhaps involving authenticity, causal efficacy, narrative ownership, or a pluralist combination—that captures all five verdicts without ad hoc adjustments?
