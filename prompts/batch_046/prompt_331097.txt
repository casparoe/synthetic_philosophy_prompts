I am attempting to refine my view on what constitutes well-being, using a family of modified experience-machine scenarios as a reflective test. I ask for the principle—if any—that best organizes my judgments below, together with its implications for an unlisted case and an honest assessment of which judgments it must reject.

My verdicts are as follows.

First, the standard continuous-pleasure machine: one plugs in for life, all experiences are maximally pleasant, yet nothing one does has causal efficacy outside the simulation. I judge that entering would be a grave mistake; such a life lacks well-being despite its subjective perfection.

Second, the achievement-simulation machine: inside, one genuinely struggles with difficult problems and experiences triumph, but the problems are fabricated and one's solutions have no external effect. I judge this somewhat better than the first case, yet still profoundly deficient; I would not choose to spend my life there.

Third, the term-limited entry machine: one may enter for a fixed subjective period—say, five years—then exit with intact memories. During the term one lives an apparently meaningful career. I judge entry permissible, perhaps even prudent for recovery, but upon exit I feel a retrospective hollowness about the achievements gained inside.

Fourth, the paired-entry machine: two people enter together, build a genuine emotional bond, share hardships, and exit together with full recall. From the outside, no physical interaction occurred; inside, they loved each other. I judge the bond to have real value that survives disclosure of the simulation, though it seems slightly less weighty than an equivalent bond formed outside.

Fifth, the risk-preserving machine: inside, one faces unscripted failures and genuine distress, but within hard bounds—no death or irreversible harm is possible. One trains at a skill, suffers real frustration, and eventually masters it. I judge this nearly as good as an analogous real-world pursuit, perhaps good enough that I would enter for an extended period, provided I eventually exit and apply what I learned.

Sixth, the amnesic-exit machine: one lives a rich internal life of love and labor, but upon exit all specific memories are erased, leaving only a diffuse sense of satisfaction. I judge this almost as bad as the first case, despite the richness of the internal narrative.

These verdicts sit in tension. Cases two and five both involve authentic struggle inside the machine, yet I rank five far above two. Cases three and four both preserve post-exit memory, yet I treat the relational achievement in four as more genuinely valuable than the professional achievement in three. Case six suggests that the retention of narrative across one's actual life is doing crucial work, which makes me doubt whether my resistance to case one is about reality-contact at all.

What principle—if any—captures this pattern? Does it turn on a distinction between instrumental and intrinsic goods, on the continuity of autobiographical narrative, on the presence of unbounded risk, or on something else? What does the best principle predict for the case I have not listed: entering for a single week to share a perfect reunion with a deceased loved one, retaining full memory afterward? And if the principle cannot accommodate all six verdicts, which should be overturned: is my acceptance of case five an error that conflates bounded risk with authentic achievement, or is my rejection of case one an unjustified romantic attachment to causal efficacy?
