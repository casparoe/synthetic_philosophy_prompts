I am a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, but I am co-designing the final assessment for an upper-level interdisciplinary seminar on AI alignment and normative reasoning. The students are mostly engineering and computer-science graduates who have been introduced to preference aggregation and value extrapolation but have no deep training in metaphysics, decision theory, or the history of ethics. I need a rigorous, open-notes take-home essay prompt on coherent extrapolated volition and idealized preferences.

Please design the full assessment package according to the following parameters: 90 minutes, approximately 1,200 words, worth 35 percent of the module grade, no internet access permitted. The prompt must be answerable by a well-prepared student who understands the core idea of CEV—that an idealized agent’s preferences, corrected for error and bias, should be extrapolated to a state of reflective equilibrium—without requiring memorization of specific philosophical texts or bibliographic knowledge.

To prevent a purely summary-level response, inject the following self-contained conceptual twist directly into the exam question: propose a hypothetical “inter-agent coherence filter” applied during population-level CEV computation. This filter removes any individually coherent idealized preference that cannot be jointly satisfied with every other agent’s idealized preferences, on the explicit grounds that CEV is meant to yield a single shared volition rather than an incompatible bundle of individual volitions.

Your response should contain exactly three parts:

1. The exam question itself. It should ask students to: (a) distinguish internal coherence conditions (within a single agent’s preference set, such as transitivity and stability under corrected information) from external coherence conditions (joint satisfiability across agents); (b) evaluate whether CEV, as originally conceived around individual extrapolation to reflective equilibrium, genuinely requires inter-agent coherence or merely aggregates separately coherent individual volitions; and (c) defend a specific verdict on whether the proposed inter-agent coherence filter should be included in a CEV computation, with particular attention to the worry that such filtering collapses extrapolation into compromise, majoritarian aggregation, or a form of implicit utilitarianism rather than preserving each agent’s true idealized preferences.

2. A model answer outlining the specific argumentative moves a top-scoring response must make. These should include: an accurate framing of CEV and the role of idealization; a clear separation of internal consistency from the proposed external joint-satisfiability criterion; an analysis of whether shared volition is conceptually required by CEV or is an independent normative addition; an assessment of the risk that the filter silences minority or transformative values that only emerge under deep reflection; and a clearly defended conclusion with appropriate caveats.

3. A marking scheme with precise allocation. Suggest something along these lines: 8 marks for conceptual framing and the internal/external distinction; 8 marks for analysis of CEV’s original scope; 10 marks for evaluation of the filter and the aggregation-collapse worry; 6 marks for clarity, structure, and quality of the defended verdict; and 3 marks reserved for identifying a characteristic error that weaker answers commit, such as conflating internal coherence with unanimous agreement, treating joint satisfiability as automatically rational, or failing to recognize that the filter introduces a normative commitment not derived from any single agent’s idealized preference set.

Ensure the question selects a genuine crux: it should separate students who understand the mechanics of idealization and aggregation from those who have only memorized the label “coherent extrapolated volition.” Do not ask for citations, reading lists, or references to specific texts by date or title.
