A friend walked me through an argument that uses the typogenetic DNA-Gödel isomorphism and now I'm stuck because the conclusion seems to swallow all of biology. (1) DNA operates as a typogenetic formal system: its sequence encodes the polymerase and ribosomal machinery that reads and copies it, structurally parallel to Gödel numbering where a formula describes its own proof-theoretic properties. (2) Any consistent formal system capable of this kind of self-reference can represent arithmetic, so Gödel's first incompleteness theorem applies: there exist true statements about the system that are unprovable within it. (3) Therefore there must be true statements about DNA replication—say, about whether a particular mutation will eventually break the replication loop—that are determinately true but not derivable from the biochemical rules governing the cell. (4) But every observed replication event is fully accounted for by mechanistic chemistry; there are no "undecidable" biochemical gaps, only deterministic or probabilistic physical processes. (5) So if (1)-(3) are right, mechanistic biology is systematically incomplete whenever self-reference is present, which includes not just DNA but any neural architecture that models itself. (6) Applied consistently, this means no physical subsystem of a brain can be mechanistically closed either, generating an infinite regress: each layer that models the layer below introduces its own Gödel sentence. (7) The anti-mechanist takes this as proof that self-modeling minds transcend physical law; I think it just shows the analogy was pushed too far, but I can't locate the slip. I'd rather keep (1) and (4) than (7). Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost—does it break the isomorphism, weaken Gödel's applicability, or force me to accept mysterious gaps in replication?
