I'm preparing a magazine feature comparing two factory towns. In Town A, civic leaders spent years promoting a shared ethic of thrift, early labor, and deferred gratification—treating these values as a direct investment that would attract employers and raise wages. The culture shifted noticeably, yet employment stayed flat. In Town B, no one promoted any collective values; a logistics firm relocated there for tax breaks, and now local wages are up. Defenders of Town A's program say the values were "necessary but not sufficient," but I can't tell whether that's a real causal claim or the kind of after-the-fact storytelling associated with Weber's explanation of capitalism. Can a culture of shared values actually drive economic change the way a tax incentive does, or does treating values as a lever always confuse background conditions with real causes? What's the intellectually honest way to report Town A's outcome without either dismissing culture entirely or giving it credit it never earned?
