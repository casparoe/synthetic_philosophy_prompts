As a retired biologist who has only recently begun studying philosophy of science and mind, I find myself returning to Ian Hacking’s idea of looping effects—the way classifying people can change the people classified, so that the classification itself becomes unstable. I understand that Hacking contrasts psychiatric “interactive kinds” with the “indifferent kinds” of natural taxonomy, but I want to test whether that distinction holds up once institutional incentives enter the picture. Give me an example of a conceptual issue in psychiatric classification in which a diagnostic category was introduced primarily for administrative or epidemiological tracking rather than for clinical discovery, and in which the subsequent allocation of resources—whether disability benefits, pharmaceutical research funding, or therapeutic access—altered the self-understanding and reported symptomatology of those falling under the label, thereby feeding back into revised diagnostic criteria. I am particularly interested in whether this loop differs in kind from analogous feedback in biological taxonomy—for instance, when conservation funding changes the observed behavior of a protected subspecies and prompts taxonomic revision—or whether both are instances of the same institutional framing effect. I ask for an example that is not the standard introductory cases of autism spectrum conditions or attention-deficit classifications; I want something that forces the analysis into the weeds of how resource allocation shapes phenomenology. I do not need citations, a reading list, or references to specific DSM editions; I am asking for conceptual clarification that a reader without library access could follow and evaluate on its own terms.
