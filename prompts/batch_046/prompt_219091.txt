In longtermist and existential-risk discussions, a recurring analogy compares living in a simulated universe to running inside a sandboxed computation on temporary hardware: just as an administrator might terminate a sandbox process without moral obligation to the virtual agents within it, an external simulator could shut down our cosmos without wronging its distant future inhabitants. This comparison is often used to license the view that extreme prioritization of the far future—sometimes characterized as fanaticism about distant generations—is rationally unwarranted unless one can first exclude being in such a disposable environment. Audit this analogy in detail: lay out the explicit correspondences between sandbox termination and cosmological closure; identify the disanalogies that bear on moral status, causal continuity, and the epistemic position of agents inside the system; specify which properties of computing infrastructure are being implicitly imported; and determine whether the conclusion—that ontological uncertainty about simulation should systematically dampen longtermist commitment—survives once the mapping failures are accounted for. If the analogy collapses or carries hidden premises, indicate what comparison, if any, better captures the normative stakes of acting for the far future under radical ontological uncertainty.
