Having spent thirty-five years in condensed-matter physics and only recently turned to philosophy in retirement, I find myself caught between the tidy explanatory hierarchies I once took for granted and the strange loops I now encounter in texts like Hofstadter's. In a reading group I joined last autumn, a colleague kept returning to an analogy to defend a strict, downward-only reductionism: the levels of scientific explanation are like the floors of a well-designed library, with each subject—thermodynamics, chemistry, neuroscience—assigned exclusively to one floor, catalogued by rules that never leak upward, and separated by fireproof doors that prevent any book from residing on two levels at once. The point, she said, was that mental properties, if real, ought to sit on their own floor, fully determined by the physics below but never reaching back to alter the shelving rules; any talk of downward causation or self-reference is just a confusion about which floor one is standing on.

I want to audit this library analogy carefully, because it seems to do a great deal of argumentative work. Could you lay out the correspondence explicitly—what plays the role of the floors, the books, the catalog system, the librarians, and the fire doors—then identify the precise points where the mapping fractures? In particular, I am wondering how it handles cases where the observer is part of the system observed, or where the description of a level requires vocabulary drawn from another. Some members of my group have introduced Escher's Drawing Hands as a counter-image: each hand draws the other, violating any inviolate separation between creator and created, suggesting that in consciousness or in scientific practice the levels are tangled rather than layered. Does this image genuinely undermine the library analogy, or does it merely describe a different kind of architecture altogether? Furthermore, I have been reflecting on a niche physical case—quantum measurement, where decoherence does not fully eliminate the entanglement between apparatus and system, producing a kind of feedback loop that looks structurally similar to a strange loop. If we inject that physical instance into the audit, does it strengthen the tangled-hierarchy objection to reductionism, or is it a category error to import an artistic image of self-reference into a domain governed by unitary dynamics? Finally, once the disanalogies are on the table, does the conclusion that reductionism is safe—no downward causation worth the name—survive, or was the library analogy carrying the argument all along?
