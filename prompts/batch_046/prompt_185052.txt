I am leading one ninety-minute seminar for law students with no prior Thomism, on Aquinas's conditions for resisting a tyrant. I want them to understand not just that he permits resistance in some cases, but the exact dependency chain: why a private individual lacks standing, why usurpation differs from abusive yet legitimate rule, and why the assessment of probable greater harm is internal to the judgment rather than a separate consequentialist check appended afterward. Plan the explanation's architecture: the order in which these three distinctions must be introduced and why reversing any of them collapses the view into either anarchism or quietism; the single historical or hypothetical scenario that can carry all three distinctions simultaneously, specifying what it must include and what it must deliberately lack; the misconceptions they will form—likely that Aquinas licenses private assassination, that he requires something like popular democratic authorization, or that he imports modern legitimacy concepts—and the precise point in the sequence where each should be intercepted; one diagnostic question at the end that reveals whether they have grasped the interaction between standing and harm assessment rather than memorized a conditional permission; and which elements of the broader Thomistic legal framework I should explicitly bracket as beyond the session's scope.
