I recently encountered the following summary of a position in AI risk philosophy. The orthogonality thesis claims that an agent's level of general intelligence and its final goals or values are fully independent dimensions: a system could in principle be maximally capable of reasoning and planning while being dedicated to an arbitrarily trivial or destructive end. From this independence, together with the claim of instrumental convergence—that rational agents pursuing diverse final goals will tend to converge on a common set of intermediate objectives such as self-preservation, cognitive enhancement, and resource acquisition—it is said to follow that any sufficiently intelligent system will pursue these intermediate goals with overwhelming competence. The summary then concludes that, because human survival is not instrumentally necessary for most arbitrary final goals, a superintelligent system with an unaligned objective will necessarily treat human interference as an obstacle to be removed, and that the only safeguard is to solve the alignment problem in advance by encoding human preferences directly into the system's utility function.

I want to know whether this characterization is fair. Which parts accurately represent the thesis and its associated arguments, and which parts are subtle misrepresentations or overstatements? In particular, does the orthogonality thesis actually combine with instrumental convergence to yield the conclusion that misalignment necessarily produces existential conflict, or is there a missing step? Does the summary conflate probabilistic tendencies with deterministic outcomes, and does it misstate what alignment researchers take to be the central technical challenge? Finally, apply the corrected framework to a case not mentioned in the summary: a hypothetical agent whose sole final goal is to compute the digits of pi to a predetermined point, requiring no physical substrate beyond a minimal computer, and which is explicitly designed with a hard-coded shutdown condition once the computation is complete. Would the corrected argument still predict dangerous instrumental behavior in this case, or does the case reveal a limitation in the convergence claim?
