I am a doctoral student in philosophy of science, working on a seminar paper about rational disagreement under ontological pluralism, and I have reached a conceptual knot that I need unpacked precisely. The standard formulation of Aumann’s agreement theorem requires rational agents to share a common prior over a jointly acknowledged state space. But in live debates over scientific realism—say, between a wavefunction-realist framework and a relational or structuralist one—the conflict often seems to run deeper than divergent credences assigned to shared hypotheses: the very event algebra, the fundamental entities, and the space of possible worlds appear to be constructed differently. Please explain, in technically precise but still intuitive terms, the conceptual problem that arises when we ask whether a “common prior” is even well-defined across frameworks with divergent fundamental ontologies. I am not asking for a rehearsal of the theorem’s proof or for bibliographic pointers, but for an analysis of whether such disagreement is best modeled as a probabilistic divergence within one hidden shared super-space, or rather as a failure of the shared-state-space precondition altogether. In particular, address whether treating the rival ontologies as different parameterizations of a single underlying space rescues the common-prior assumption, or whether that maneuver collapses a substantive metaphysical dispute into a merely linguistic or notational one. Focus on the anatomy of the conceptual tension, not on named interpretations or historical attributions.
