You are a senior constitutional-design advisor specializing in democratic selection mechanisms—elections, sortition, and tenure rules—retained by a parliamentary reform commission. The commission reviews about six proposals per year to change how members of a specific legislative body are selected or retained: for instance, replacing an appointed chamber with citizens selected by lot, imposing strict lifetime term limits, or requiring prior elected service as a qualification. Each review lasts three months, must yield a recommendation defensible to parliament, and cannot invoke partisan preference or vague democratic sentiment. The commissioners are legally literate but not philosophers; they need a reusable decision procedure—an ordered sequence of tests with explicit stop conditions—grounded in democratic theory (representative, participatory, and epistemic legitimacy; descriptive representation; accountability; and structural corruption) but stripped to operational form.

Before constructing anything, briefly list the four or five load-bearing conceptual distinctions your procedure will rely on, and state why each must survive simplification.

Then build the procedure itself. For each step, provide: the precise question; what counts as a sufficient answer, including any quick indicator or comparative benchmark; whether the answer is an institutional fact, an empirical comparison, or an irreducibly normative judgment; the stop conditions (output "reject," "accept with conditions," "refer to plenary judgment," or "proceed"); and the dependency—why this step must precede the next. The procedure must explicitly handle these hard cases: (a) a body whose primary function is technical oversight rather than lawmaking; (b) a proposal that improves descriptive representation but severs the electoral-accountability chain; (c) a proposal that eliminates careerism but also eliminates institutional memory; and (d) a proposal applying different selection mechanisms to different chambers within the same system. Flag every point where judgment cannot be eliminated, especially when empirical evidence is ambiguous or when normative weights conflict (e.g., equality of access versus continuity of expertise), and note where the procedure is likely to misfire.

Next, apply the procedure to three concrete proposals, running the steps in order for each:

1. Replace a regional upper chamber—currently elected with staggered six-year terms—with a body of 150 citizens selected by stratified random lot, serving one non-renewable three-year term, with no prior office-holding requirement.
2. Impose an absolute lifetime term limit of ten years on all members of the lower legislative house, with no grandfathering clause.
3. Design this case yourself: it should be a proposal a reform-minded commission would typically favor—such as introducing partial sortition or hybrid selection—but the procedure must yield a negative or heavily qualified recommendation that the commission will find unwelcome. Make the unwelcome result explicit, identify the exact step that produces it, and explain the judgment call driving it.

For each application, state where the procedure stops or stalls, give the recommendation, and identify the non-eliminable judgment that shaped the outcome.

Finally, state clearly what the procedure cannot do: which categories of proposals lie outside its scope; which normative disagreements it assumes away rather than resolves; and what change in political or institutional context would cause the same inputs to produce a different output. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Think carefully and be precise about where the procedure ends and human judgment must take over.
