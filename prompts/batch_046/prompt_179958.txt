I am a high school student working on an independent writing project about how institutional language shapes what societies treat as real. I would like you to compose a satirical parody of a natural-history museum exhibition catalog—specifically, the deadpan, authoritative curatorial voice used for diorama labels, taxonomic plates, and visitor-guide transcripts. The catalog should present three social kinds—race, gender, and money—as if they were recently catalogued biological species, complete with invented Latin binomials (for example, Homo categoricus, Genus performativum, and Moneta fiduciaria), evolutionary origin narratives, anatomical descriptions, and documented habitat ranges. At the same time, every entry should quietly disclose that the "species" possesses no physical substrate and is maintained instead by court rulings, pronoun conventions, banking protocols, and legislative amendments. The humor should arise from the institutional voice earnestly applying biological taxonomy and conservation biology to phenomena that resist material observation, rather than from caricaturing any named individual scholar. Include a reference to an imaginary "Plate IV" morphological diagram, a curatorial note on "conservation status" that alludes to contemporary policy debates rather than biological extinction risk, and an original conceptual frame—call it "institutional morphology"—to describe how these kinds preserve their structural shape in the absence of any natural substrate. The piece should run approximately 600 words, remain readable to someone who has never studied social-construction theory, yet be precise enough that a reader familiar with the literature recognizes the satire as conceptually fair rather than straw-manning. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations to specific authors; the argument should stand entirely on the internal logic of the parody.
