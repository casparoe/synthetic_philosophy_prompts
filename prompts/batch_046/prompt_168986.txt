I'm taking a philosophy of science course and we've been discussing how unrealistic assumptions function in scientific modeling—particularly the kind of defense associated with Friedman, where predictive accuracy can excuse false premises. But in the literature and in seminar discussions I've noticed a more specific defensive pattern that doesn't quite match that story. Researchers construct models built on extreme idealizations—frictionless surfaces, perfectly rational agents, populations of infinite size—and then treat the extremity of those assumptions not merely as a simplifying device, but as an explanatory resource. When empirical predictions fail, the failure is attributed not to a flaw in the core mechanism, but to the presence of interfering conditions that the idealization correctly set aside. Over successive rounds, the model accumulates exceptions: it applies except in small populations, except with bounded rationality, except when transaction costs are nonzero. Each exception is individually reasonable, but collectively they mean that almost any observed outcome can be reconciled with the model by locating the appropriate deviation from purity. This seems different from adding an auxiliary hypothesis in the Duhem-Quine sense, because the adjustments aren't modifying the core theory; they are specifying conditions under which the pure mechanism is masked. And it seems different from Friedman's F-twist, since predictive success isn't doing the work—rather, the unrealistic assumption itself becomes a kind of unfalsifiable shield.

Is there an established term for this specific accumulation—using the very extremity of an idealization to immunize a model against empirical mismatch by treating all deviations as contamination from interfering conditions? If there is one, how exactly does it differ from a Lakatosian protective belt or from ordinary ceteris paribus qualifications? If there isn't, could one coin and define it precisely, with boundary conditions that distinguish it from standard immunization strategies and from legitimate scope-limitation? I want to be careful about the carving: I'm not asking about idealization in general, or about all unfalsifiable theories, but specifically about cases where the unrealistic premise functions as both simplification and permanent excuse, and where the list of interfering conditions grows in an ad hoc fashion without ever threatening the core mechanism.
