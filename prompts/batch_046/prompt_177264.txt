I'm working on a short essay for a course on early American moral philosophy, and I'd like comments on its central framing before I commit to the draft. The essay examines Benjamin Franklin's well-known scheme for moral perfection in the Autobiography — the thirteen virtues, the weekly rotation, and the small notebook of black marks tracking failures. Rather than treating this as a straightforward exercise in virtue ethics or proto-utilitarian self-cultivation, I want to argue that the scheme operates as a kind of moral accounting technology. The grid of virtues arranged in a fixed temporal cycle, combined with the daily recording of lapses, transforms character from an internal state into a quantifiable flow of behaviors: each failure is entered like a debit, each week of progress like a tentative credit, and the self functions simultaneously as accountant, ledger, and audited firm. I suggest this formal structure borrows less from classical virtue theory than from the logic of double-entry bookkeeping — a practice Franklin knew intimately — and that it secularizes the Puritan spiritual diary without retaining its theological endpoint.

My specific concern is whether this technological framing overreaches. Franklin never describes his method in commercial terms, and he explicitly abandons the pursuit of perfection once he realizes the asymptotic difficulty, keeping the practice alive only as a habitual discipline. I want to ask whether this asymptotic failure is structurally necessary — whether the moral perfection goal functions not as an achievable telos but as a regulatory fiction that prevents the accounting system from ever closing its books — or whether it simply reveals the incoherence of applying quantitative tracking to qualitative character. I'm also unsure whether the comparison to Puritan spiritual accounting holds given Franklin's deistic distance from Calvinist self-scrutiny, and whether my reading risks anachronistically projecting modern self-quantification onto an eighteenth-century moralist.

Could you comment on the coherence of this framing? In particular, I'd welcome your assessment of whether the bookkeeping analogy genuinely illuminates Franklin's rotating grid and asymptotic stopping point, or whether it imports features that break down on closer inspection. I'm also interested in whether there is a better analogy for what Franklin is doing — perhaps closer to Stoic prosoche or to Enlightenment projects of human perfectibility — and whether the technological interpretation can survive once the disanalogies between financial ledgers and moral self-tracking are fully on the table. I do not need bibliographic suggestions or reading lists; I'm working mainly from the Autobiography and common knowledge of Franklin's context, and I want to know whether the argument holds up on its own conceptual feet.
