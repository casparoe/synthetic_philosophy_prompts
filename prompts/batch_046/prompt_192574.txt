Please help me understand a conceptual issue in the following paraphrased passage from Part V of René Descartes' Discourse on Method. Descartes asks the reader to imagine that there exist machines with the same organs and external form as a monkey or some other non-rational animal. He maintains that, were such machines to exist, we would possess no means of recognizing that they were not in every respect of the same nature as those actual animals.

Many readers take this to be a flat denial that animals possess any form of consciousness. I want to examine the reasoning more precisely. Is Descartes advancing an epistemological claim—that we simply lack access to any hidden mental life behind behavior—or an ontological claim, that the absence of rational thought entails the complete absence of sensory or affective awareness? Furthermore, does the clock analogy depend on an implicit binary between full rational souls and purely mechanical bodies, one that excludes any intermediate gradations of awareness? And does that binary become unstable if we apply the same reasoning to human beings, since a sufficiently complex machine might replicate not only animal behavior but also human speech and reasoning without possessing a soul? Would such a case not push Descartes toward a functionalist interpretation of mind that his own substance dualism is designed to resist?

Please explain this tension in American English, and avoid reducing the passage to a simple assertion about animal suffering. I am particularly interested in where the analogy strengthens his position and where it relies on an unstated assumption about the visibility, or invisibility, of mental substance.
