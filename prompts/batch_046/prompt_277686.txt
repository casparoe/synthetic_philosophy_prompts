I'm preparing a podcast interview with an unnamed philosopher defending a specific, recently articulated view on manipulation and persuasion. Her position is that the distinction is not a matter of autonomy-subversion—bypassing rational capacities or operating covertly—but rather what she calls "contextual fidelity": manipulation is wrong because it severs "preference-context bindings," treating a person's desire as portable into social roles or situations where it was never formed, whereas legitimate persuasion respects those bindings. She allows that disclosure failures are merely epistemic wrongs, not constitutive of manipulation, and she anticipates objections about advertising and therapeutic nudging by insisting they only become manipulative when they involve "binding acknowledgment" failure—deliberately transplanting preferences across contexts without recognizing the original binding. I want a sequence of interview questions that presses this framework where it is weakest, turning on its actual content rather than generic concerns about free will. The questions should anticipate her standard deflection—that worries about covert influence confuse disclosure ethics with preference-binding ethics—and include the follow-ups that make that deflection progressively harder to maintain. Include at least one question testing whether her account can distinguish manipulation from clumsy but well-meaning persuasion in intimate relationships, one probing whether "binding acknowledgment" presupposes an implausible sociology of preference formation, and one asking what empirical or phenomenological evidence could disconfirm her claim that people experience manipulation primarily as context-violation rather than autonomy-violation. End with a question about whether her view implies that large-scale democratic persuasion is inherently manipulative, since such campaigns routinely address citizens outside the local contexts in which their political preferences originally formed. Keep the sequence tight enough for a forty-minute conversation, and flag which questions are designed to expose internal tension in the view versus which invite her to extend it.
