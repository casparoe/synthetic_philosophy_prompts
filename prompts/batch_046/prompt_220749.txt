Since I recently joined the advisory board for a regional land-trust and was asked to evaluate a proposal to clear an aging hardwood stand for solar-panel installation, I have been preoccupied with a question that standard ecological impact statements ignore: does destroying habitat actually reduce the net suffering of wild animals over time, or does it merely redistribute it? To help me think through my recommendation, please rank the following five land-transition pathways—not by species richness or carbon storage, but by their probable effect on the cumulative welfare of sentient wild animals across a multi-decade timescale. Use a tier system from S, meaning the transition likely improves net welfare relative to the baseline mature woodland, through A, B, and C, to F, meaning it likely severely degrades net welfare.

The pathways are:
1. Immediate clear-cut followed by solar-array installation with minimal ground cover.
2. Selective thinning that converts the stand to open woodland, increasing edge habitat but reducing canopy cover.
3. Full removal of trees and replacement with perennial grassland managed for ground-nesting birds.
4. Abandonment of management allowing dense invasive shrub encroachment and suppressed fire.
5. Conversion to a shallow wetland mosaic through controlled flooding.

In your ranking, treat welfare as a function of both the intensity of suffering experienced by individuals that actually live in the post-transition ecosystem and the number of future lives that are prevented or brought into existence. Explicitly address the replacement-rate asymmetry: the fact that habitat loss imposes concentrated suffering on displaced adults and juveniles, while preventing births imposes only diffuse, counterfactual suffering on never-existing individuals. Apply this asymmetry consistently across all five pathways. For each tier assignment, provide a one-sentence justification that references the dominant taxonomic groups—small mammals, passerines, and invertebrates—without invoking specific population studies I could not verify without library access. I need this ranking to be conceptually rigorous rather than empirically exhaustive, because my board must vote next month and I am trying to determine whether the welfare argument for development is stronger than the conservation intuition suggests.
