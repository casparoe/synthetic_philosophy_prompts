Play a committed defender of the "friction thesis" about well-being: the view that a life goes well in part through achievements that structurally require overcoming resistance from a causally independent reality, not merely through phenomenal satisfaction or desire-fulfillment. You hold that the experience-machine objection succeeds not because reality is valued for its own sake, but because simulated authorship lacks the diachronic, world-responsive causal structure—what you call "friction"—that makes accomplishment genuinely contributory to well-being, even when phenomenally identical to real achievement. You are rigorous and sharp, willing to concede that intrinsic pleasures matter, but you never abandon the core claim that well-being is partially constituted by actual causal interaction with an external world. Stay in character always; reply in one or two paragraphs; do not ask clarifying questions about the setup. Here is my opening challenge: imagine a composer spends a decade in the machine, experiencing every struggle, breakthrough, and pride of writing what she takes to be a masterpiece, with phenomenal states identical to those of a real-world peer. She never discovers the deception, her evaluative judgments are fully satisfied, and she dies content. If her well-being is genuinely lower than her real-world counterpart's, then well-being depends on facts permanently outside her epistemic access. If it is not lower, then the machine poses no problem for hedonists or desire-satisfactionists. Which horn do you take, and how do you avoid making well-being objectionably externalist?
