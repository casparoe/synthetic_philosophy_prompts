We just finished a long, circular argument about transitional justice—my friend insisted that the only coherent defense of South Africa’s amnesty-for-truth arrangement is counterfactual: demonstrate that, had the Truth and Reconciliation Commission never existed, cyclical violence would have persisted, and measure success by that absence. I said this treats a commission as if it were a natural experiment observed from outside, when in fact it is an intervention that actively restructures the causal architecture of public acknowledgment itself; it does not reveal a hidden past waiting to be discovered, but changes what counts as a shared moral fact in the present. I lost the argument socially if not philosophically, and now I must deliver the opening remarks at tomorrow’s workshop, Repair Without Closure, convened for legal scholars, philosophers, and practitioners who have worked in South Africa, Argentina, and Rwanda. The room is genuinely mixed: some people designed these mechanisms, others study them conceptually, and some have lived through them. I want the speech to do real structural work with that counterfactual-versus-interventionist distinction, so that it shapes what I can and cannot say. I should not frame testimony as the recovery of a pre-existing record that was merely obscured, since that presupposes a counterfactual-dependence view of historical truth. I should not present amnesty as a transactional exchange—truth priced and traded for immunity—because that assumes we can compute the counterfactual cost of prosecution and settle accounts accordingly. Instead, I need to speak about testimony and amnesty as present-tense interventions in the moral record, altering what is publicly acknowledged without claiming they restore a pre-damaged state or heal the past. The remarks must steady a room that includes both survivors and skeptics, comfort without false consolation, and avoid saying anything that collapses the distinction between knowing what happened and changing what it means now. They must land for the philosophers without losing the practitioners, and they must not ask anyone to believe that closure has been achieved. No citations, no bibliography, no reading list—only the conceptual architecture standing on its own. Please write these opening remarks: approximately eight to ten minutes when spoken, formally addressed, shaped throughout by that distinction, and honest about its own limits.
