I have been working through a version of Einstein's hole argument for a graduate seminar on the metaphysics of spacetime, and I have constructed a reductio that now strikes me as suspicious precisely because its conclusion undermines the very framework we use to evaluate determinism. I present it in numbered form and ask where the escape is.

(1) In general relativity, the hole argument shows that if spacetime points possess primitive identity (haecceities) independent of the metric and matter fields, then an active diffeomorphism within a matter-free region produces a distinct solution that agrees with the original everywhere outside that region, violating Laplacian determinism.

(2) The standard resolution, defended throughout the philosophy of physics literature, denies primitive identity to spacetime points: points are individuated solely by their relational properties within the metric-field structure.

(3) Now consider that quantum mechanics treats identical particles via exchange symmetry: swapping two electrons with identical states produces no observable difference. If we apply the same anti-haecceitist reasoning here, particles too lack primitive thisness and are purely relational nodes.

(4) By structural analogy, any localized entity—whether a spacetime point, a quantum particle, or a macroscopic object—can be relocated or permuted within a relational network without altering the physical facts, provided only that internal relations are preserved.

(5) Therefore, if we consistently apply the anti-haecceitist resolution that saves determinism in GR, we must deny primitive identity to all localized entities, including human persons, who are then individuated only by psychological and physical relational continuity.

(6) But if persons lack primitive thisness, then there is no non-derivative fact about whether the subject who undergoes an experience tomorrow is strictly identical to me; identity becomes a purely relational construct.

(7) This eliminates the possibility of genuinely de re obligations or wrongs directed at a specific future subject—there is no specific "me" who can be wronged, only a relational pattern that continues.

(8) Yet our moral and practical reasoning presupposes that we can wrong specific persons across time.

(9) Hence, if we wish to preserve de re moral responsibility and personal identity, we must reject the anti-haecceitist resolution of the hole argument and instead accept that spacetime points possess primitive identity, despite the apparent failure of determinism this entails.

I would prefer to keep (1), (2), and (8), but I am open to being shown that one of them must go. Which step is the weakest, and why did it seem initially defensible? What does rejecting it cost for the metaphysics of spacetime and for our account of personal identity?
