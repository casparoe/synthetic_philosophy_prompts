Here is the package I've arrived at, and I want to know whether it holds together:

(1) I accept Ulysses contracts in personal rationality: it is rational for my present self to bind my future self against predictable weakness of will, provided the binding mechanism remains revocable in principle.

(2) I think constitutional entrenchment is legitimate democratic practice, not a dead-hand violation, but only when it protects procedural conditions for future democratic revision rather than locking in substantive moral or economic outcomes.

(3) I deny that there is any metaphysically deep distinction between intrapersonal precommitment across time and intergenerational collective precommitment; both are instances of the same functional decision-procedure, applied at different scales, and neither requires a unified metaphysical agent.

(4) I hold that implicit consent—remaining within a polity and benefiting from its institutions—adequately legitimizes being bound by constitutional rules that one never explicitly endorsed.

(5) I maintain that rational agency requires treating one's future preferences with equal normative weight; there is no privileged present moment entitled to override later preferences solely on grounds of temporal location.

I'm particularly concerned that (3) and (5) together threaten (1): if future preferences carry equal weight and there is no special metaphysical unity linking me across time, what authority does my present self have to bind my future self? I also worry that (4) undermines the restriction in (2), since implicit consent seems to legitimize any entrenched rule, not merely procedural ones. But perhaps some of these tensions dissolve on closer inspection—maybe the functional identity in (3) doesn't erase relevant normative differences, or the revocability condition in (1) answers the authority problem without invoking temporal privilege?
