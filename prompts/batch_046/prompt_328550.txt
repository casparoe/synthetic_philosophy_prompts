I am taking a course on political philosophy and development ethics, and I have noticed a puzzling asymmetry in how institutions classify mass starvation. International humanitarian law now treats the deliberate starvation of civilians as a war crime, yet domestic famines—whether the entitlement collapse Amartya Sen analysed in Bengal in 1943, the Irish famine under colonial administration, or the excess mortality during China's Great Leap Forward—are consistently archived by states and aid agencies as technical food crises or natural disasters compounded by policy error, rather than as political crimes warranting accountability. At the same time, these same agencies routinely invoke entitlement-based explanations in their reports without allowing that analysis to reshape the institutional response, which remains humanitarian delivery rather than structural or legal redress. What philosophical commitments about causation, state sovereignty, and collective moral responsibility does this practice presuppose? Does it depend on a defensible distinction between direct and structural violence, or is it merely a contingent feature of how sovereignty shields domestic policy from criminal framing? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, and explain what would have to change in international classification frameworks if the underlying view were replaced by one that treats famine primarily as a rights violation rather than a welfare deficit.
