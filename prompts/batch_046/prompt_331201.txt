I was observing a philosophy-of-mind reading group last week where two members, Riley and Taylor, spent half an hour arguing about whether dreams are experiences, using Malcolm and Dennett as background but quickly moving into their own territory. I am not taking sides; I want an impartial adjudication of the specific moves they made. You should treat this as an expert philosopher of mind specializing in consciousness and temporal phenomenology: begin by briefly brainstorming the logical architecture of the dispute, then render your verdict move by move. Their exchange ran as follows. (1) Riley started from Malcolm’s position: during sleep consciousness is suspended, so there is no real-time phenomenology; the rich detail in morning reports is therefore best explained as rapid post-arousal confabulation stitching together memory fragments, not retrieval of a prior experience. (2) Taylor granted that Dennett’s cassette theory allows for construction at awakening, but argued this still yields an experience—just one with a displaced temporal origin; Taylor introduced the idea of retroactive phenomenal binding, where neural integration after the fact can generate a genuinely first-person perspective that is experienced retrospectively, analogous to certain forms of delayed perceptual awareness. (3) Riley objected that allowing displaced origins collapses the boundary between experience and representation, since subliminal priming and implanted false memories also involve post-hoc integration without being experiential; Riley insisted that synchronous presence—being conscious now of a now—is a non-negotiable feature of experience. (4) Taylor replied that synchronous presence is not required, noting that ordinary conscious perception of external events already involves neural delays; Taylor proposed that dreams should be classified as offline experiences—not occurring in real-time consciousness, yet genuinely experiential because they possess first-person authority, emotional salience, and narrative integration that representations like false memories lack. (5) Riley then proposed a middle path: dreams are neither experiences nor representations, but potential experiences—structured mnemonic-affective schemas that become experiential only upon activation by arousal mechanisms, thereby avoiding both Malcolm’s denial and Dennett’s commitment to retroactive construction; Taylor immediately countered that this hybrid merely relocates the problem, since specifying the activation mechanism without explaining how it produces phenomenal presence just pushes Dennett’s question back one step. Please adjudicate each of these five rounds individually. Present your answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences, with no introductory or concluding paragraph: item 1 addresses Riley’s opening confabulation argument, item 2 addresses Taylor’s retroactive-binding move, item 3 addresses Riley’s collapse objection, item 4 addresses Taylor’s offline-experience defense, and item 5 assesses Riley’s hybrid potential-experience view and identifies what both disputants are failing to address about the metaphysics of temporal presence.
