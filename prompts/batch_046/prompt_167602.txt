I'm writing a feature for a science-and-ideas magazine on whether the claim that the world is becoming less violent can be assessed as true or false, and I want a taxonomy of the positions arranged as a decision tree. The audience has encountered the well-known argument that direct interpersonal violence has fallen sharply since the Enlightenment, but not the philosophical objections to measuring it.

Start with this root question: is there a fact of the matter about whether violence is declining? From there, branch on (i) whether 'violence' denotes a single measurable phenomenon or is irreducibly plural; (ii) if singular, whether the correct unit is per-capita direct physical harm, absolute headcount of incidents, or a severity-weighted index; (iii) whether structural, institutional, ecological, or epistemic harm should be included in the baseline; and (iv) whether a downward trend in one metric can be defeated by an upward trend in another, or whether an all-things-considered verdict is required.

Each leaf should carry a name if the position is established (for example, the per-capita direct-harm optimist, the structural-violence pluralist, the metric-eliminativist) or be marked 'unnamed', together with one sentence stating its most awkward commitment — what it must say about colonial mortality statistics, about factory farming, or about comparing a medieval battle with modern surveillance. Flag any pair of leaves that the tree cannot genuinely separate, noting whether they differ only in rhetorical emphasis. Identify any logically possible combination that remains unoccupied, and briefly diagnose whether it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Keep the tree to eight to ten leaves, and phrase every branching question so a reader can answer it without knowing the philosophical labels in advance.
