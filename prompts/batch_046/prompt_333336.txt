The debate over Spinoza’s commitment to democracy has long stalled between interpreters who take his claim in the *Theological-Political Treatise*—that democracy is the “most natural” form of government—to express a normative ideal of collective sovereignty, and interpreters who point to the *Political Treatise*, where he excludes women, servants, foreigners, and those lacking independent rational judgment from citizenship, and conclude that his “multitudo” is in effect an aristocracy of reason. One side treats these exclusions as contingent historical boundaries that do not alter the core metaphysical point that sovereign power (*potentia*) resides in the collective capacity of rational agents acting in concert; the other side treats them as structurally essential, arguing that democratic sovereignty for Spinoza is never raw popular will but always the delegated authority of those capable of guiding the commonwealth by reason. Diagnose this stall: is it a substantive disagreement about Spinoza’s metaphysics of power and agency, a verbal dispute about what should count as “democracy,” or two inquiries—one normative-political, one sociological—running under one heading? Be precise about what each camp actually commits to, and assess whether introducing a distinction between *potentia multitudinis* as an emergent, non-delegated collective capacity and as a rational authority vested only in qualified agents could resolve the tension, or whether the impasse reflects an irreducible ambiguity in Spinoza’s own conception of democratic reason. Finally, say what, if anything, would count as progress: a closer textual argument, a conceptual clarification, or an acknowledgment that no further resolution is available.
