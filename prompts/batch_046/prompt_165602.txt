Since I began assisting in a long-term care unit for patients with advanced neurodegenerative conditions, I have noticed a recurring asymmetry in how I attribute minds to others. With a non-verbal patient whose expressions are subtle and unpredictable, I often feel compelled to ascribe specific intentions or feelings, even though the behavioral evidence is sparse and ambiguous; yet with a conversational system whose outputs are fluent and context-sensitive, I feel no comparable pull, precisely because I understand the mechanistic pipeline that produces its responses. The pattern is not simply a difference in behavioral richness, because in both cases the observable behavior is complex and responsive; rather, my willingness to posit an inner life seems to track whether the causal origin of the behavior remains opaque to me—human neural dynamics I do not understand—versus transparent—an architecture I can trace. Is there an established term for this opacity-dependence in other-minds attribution, and how exactly does it relate to the classical analogical argument? I am not looking for a bare label: I want to know whether this describes a recognized failure mode or bias in the literature, what its nearest conceptual neighbors are, and where its boundaries lie—whether, for instance, a partial scientific understanding of neuroscience should weaken attribution to human beings in the same way.
