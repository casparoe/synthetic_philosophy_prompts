I'm delivering the opening address at an interdisciplinary workshop called "Moral Status Without Thresholds." Half the room works on sentience in invertebrates and simple artificial control systems; the other half designs legal and institutional frameworks that depend on binary classifications—subject or object, protected or unprotected. I want the speech built around a specific structural problem: sentience is not merely uncertain but genuinely gradated, running from something like a thermostat's adaptive response through social insects to mammals, and our moral vocabulary lacks the grammar for this middle region. I am not asking for a defense of animal rights or a technical survey of consciousness metrics. Rather, I need the address to show why this gradation creates a distinctive institutional difficulty—what I will call category drag, the distortion produced when binary legal and moral concepts are stretched over a continuous reality—and why neither a hard cutoff nor an epistemic wait-and-see posture resolves it. The talk should be about fifteen minutes, formal but not academic in tone, and it should close with a concrete comparative image—perhaps a hive, a learning thermostat, and a sleeping mammal—to make the problem of institutional humility tangible for both audiences. Please write the address, ensuring it does not treat the gradation as a temporary gap in our knowledge and does not offer false comfort to either the scientists or the lawyers.
