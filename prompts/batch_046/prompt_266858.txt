In my philosophy of technology seminar, I've been developing the view that widespread reliance on AI for reasoning and synthesis doesn't actually erode human understanding; it merely relocates it. The claim is that understanding was never an internal, self-contained achievement of individual minds, but has always been distributed across tools, social practices, and institutional structures. So when I use a model to structure an argument or summarize a debate, I am not surrendering a capacity I once possessed autonomously; I am extending the same kind of cognitive system that writing, peer review, and reference libraries already constitute. The anxiety about 'epistemic dependence' therefore rests on what might be a myth of the autonomous knower, and AI reliance is structurally closer to using a calculator or collaborating with a co-author than to a novel form of cognitive outsourcing.

What would count against this position? I want the observations, arguments, and distinctions that should genuinely move me, ranked from decisive to merely awkward. For instance, are there empirical findings about skill atrophy or conceptual degradation that cannot be explained away as simple task redistribution? Does a philosophical distinction — perhaps between understanding-that and understanding-how, or between instrumental reliance and constitutive cognitive extension — break the analogy to older technologies? Would it matter if studies showed that users of AI assistants fail not on factual recall but on the independent structuring of problems in unfamiliar domains? And if defenders of this distributed view can always absorb any case of cognitive decline by appealing to system-level understanding, has the position been qualified into unfalsifiability — and if so, is that a serious defect for a claim of this kind?
