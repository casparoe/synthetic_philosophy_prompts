I'm preparing a feature for a science-and-ideas publication on what happens when classical conservative philosophy collides with modern institutional engineering. For one section, I want to stage a conceptual encounter: imagine Edmund Burke—who died in 1797, long before the digital age—were alive to observe a specific proposal now circulating in policy and technology circles. The proposal involves deploying large predictive models to audit legacy legal and social institutions, automatically identifying rules and practices whose original purpose is no longer recoverable from present-day behavioral or administrative data, labeling them functionally redundant, and streamlining them away. Its advocates claim that because institutional memory decays, the old principle—often associated with G.K. Chesterton—that one should not tear down a fence until one understands why it was erected is now obsolete: algorithmic reconstruction can recover or reject lost rationales faster and more thoroughly than human historians. Critics counter that many institutional purposes were never encoded in data to begin with.

Given Burke's well-known commitments—his defense of prescription over abstract reason, his view of society as a partnership linking the dead, the living, and those yet unborn, and his deep suspicion of rationalist schemes that redesign inherited structures from first principles—how would he likely assess this algorithmic audit-and-streamline program? Would he regard machine-reconstructed purpose as an adequate substitute for the gradual, embodied knowledge embedded in tradition? Or would he see the proposal as an especially insidious version of the revolutionary rationalism he warned against—one that mistakes statistical absence for functional uselessness? In framing your answer, please stay close to Burke's actual philosophical arguments rather than to a generic label of conservatism, address the specific tension between algorithmic reconstruction and Chesterton's fence principle, and avoid inventing direct quotations or referencing particular editions of Burke's texts. The goal is not a biography but a rigorous conceptual prediction: what Burke's framework implies about a form of institutional reform he could never have witnessed.
