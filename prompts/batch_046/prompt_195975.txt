Before I commit to any account of how phenomenal and agential unity is structured in split-brain patients, I want the evaluation standards explicit. Write the desiderata: what an adequate theory of conscious unity in commissurotomy subjects must be able to deliver—the phenomena it must explain, the cases it must get right, the distinctions it must respect, and the results or observations it must remain consistent with. For each desideratum, give the motivating case or experimental consideration behind it, including at minimum: (i) the classic lateralized-presentation dissociations in which verbal report and manual response conflict; (ii) the subject's persistent first-person report of being a single, undivided self despite those dissociations; (iii) the cross-cueing behaviors that allow information available to one hemisphere to influence actions guided by the other without direct callosal transfer; and (iv) the implicit integrative capacities observed in affective valence and spatial attention that survive explicit disconnection. Flag any pair of desiderata you take to be jointly unsatisfiable—particularly tensions between fully honoring behavioral dissociation and preserving an unqualified unitary phenomenal field, and between accommodating implicit integration without reintroducing a hidden interhemispheric connector that undermines the surgical premise. Indicate which requirements are non-negotiable for a theory whose purpose is to explain subjective experience rather than merely behavioral architecture, and which might be negotiable if the theory is restricted to explaining decision-making mechanisms alone. Then, briefly, score the main families of existing accounts—unitary-consciousness views, dual-stream or split-consciousness views, and partial-unity or hierarchical-binding models—against your list, keeping the scoring concise since the conceptual weight should fall on the criteria and their conflicts rather than on the evaluation of contenders.
