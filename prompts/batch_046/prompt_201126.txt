As a retired physical chemist who spent three decades studying metastable alloys and phase-boundary dynamics, and who now audits graduate seminars in metaphysics with the puzzlement of someone accustomed to measurable transitions, I keep returning to the statue-and-clay problem with a framing my classmates do not share. In engineering practice, we routinely identify objects—turbine blades, catalytic substrates, structural composites—whose material substrate is almost entirely replaced through scheduled maintenance, yet which retain institutional and functional identity across those replacements by virtue of continuous design specifications, regulatory documentation, and uninterrupted operational roles. This suggests a conceptual distinction between substrate continuity and what I will call engineered persistence: an artifact’s identity is sustained not by the endurance of particular atoms but by an unbroken chain of functional and structural specification. I want to compare the standard philosophical account of material constitution—where the statue and the lump of clay are coincident entities, the statue constituted by but not identical to the clay—with this maintenance-model of artifact identity, in which an object survives complete part-replacement not because a separate constituted entity hovers over the matter, but because the object just is the dynamically maintained configuration governed by its design protocol. Where exactly do these two frameworks diverge in their treatment of destruction, gradual repair, and the conditions under which one object can outlast the turnover of all its parts?

More concretely, I am trying to determine whether the constitution view genuinely explains our intuitions about the statue, or whether it merely labels them with an ontological distinction that does no real work. If I apply the maintenance-model reasoning to the clay statue—treating it not as a static form impressed upon passive matter, but as a configuration that could, in principle, be repaired grain by grain according to the artist’s original specification without losing its artistic identity—does this strengthen the case for saying the statue is distinct from the clay, or does it collapse that distinction by revealing that both are merely processes with different maintenance protocols and different institutional histories? I am not asking for a literature survey; I want a direct, committed judgment. Which of these two conceptual schemes—the constitution-without-identity approach or the engineered-persistence approach—offers the more coherent ontology for artifacts that undergo gradual material replacement?
