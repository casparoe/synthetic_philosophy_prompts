I'm a journalist covering science and ideas, and over the past several months I've been reporting on efforts to redesign civic deliberation using architectures borrowed from adaptive medicine—specifically, multi-arm platform trials with shared control groups, master protocols, and response-adaptive randomization that updates allocation ratios as evidence accumulates. In conversation with both trialists and political theorists, I keep returning to John Dewey's experimentalist account of democracy: the idea that democratic inquiry is not the aggregation of pre-formed private preferences but a collective, open-ended process of problem-solving in which both the situation and the inquirers are reconstructed; his rejection of spectator theories of knowledge; and his claim that values and norms emerge through experimental engagement rather than serving as fixed constraints. I want you to restate Dewey's core commitments about democratic inquiry—treating democracy as a method of social intelligence rather than a decision rule—in the precise vocabulary of adaptive platform-trial design. For each commitment, report what translates cleanly, what requires distortion to fit within concepts like interim analysis, adaptive allocation, mechanism-exploration endpoints, and shared controls, and what has no counterpart whatsoever in that framework. Then provide the residue analysis: is what remains outside the clinical-trial vocabulary—perhaps Dewey's emphasis on the transformation of citizens' characters and social relationships through participatory inquiry—an essential feature that reveals a genuine limitation in using trial architectures as democratic models, or is it merely romantic surplus that obscures the fact that democratic deliberation, like a platform trial, is ultimately an optimization procedure?
