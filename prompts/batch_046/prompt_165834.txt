I advise a vocational-rehabilitation ethics board that must evaluate, in standardized forty-five-minute intake sessions, whether a client’s refusal to enter retraining after disability constitutes Sartrean bad faith—the self-deceptive denial of radical freedom and transcendence over facticity—or an authentic acknowledgment of real limits. Design a reusable decision procedure that a consultant without philosophical training can run: a fixed sequence of interview questions, criteria for what counts as a sufficient answer to each, explicit stop-points where the procedure issues a recommendation versus where it must hand the decision to values-based judgment, and clear flags for cases where ontology underdetermines institutional duty. Keep Sartre’s distinction between facticity and transcendence intact, but translate it into observable indicators of evasion that survive simplification. Apply the procedure to three cases: (1) a former manual laborer with chronic pain who rejects all sedentary options on the grounds that his body has fixed his destiny; (2) a client medically cleared for adaptive work who insists on replicating her exact pre-injury occupational role; and (3) a primary caregiver who declines enrollment because the schedule conflicts with obligations to a dependent—where the procedure should classify the refusal as non-bad-faith despite the board’s policy preference for enrollment, yielding an answer the board will resist. Conclude by stating precisely what the procedure cannot adjudicate, especially regarding the moral weight of caregiving obligations versus self-actualization, and mark every point where Sartre’s framework necessarily leaves the consultant without a determinative answer.
