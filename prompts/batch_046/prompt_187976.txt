I am a third-year student in a philosophy-of-social-science module, and I have been asked to lead a fifty-minute tutorial for first-year sociology students on the ontology of generational categories—specifically, whether labels such as Baby Boomers, Millennials, and Gen Z denote real kinds, or whether they are merely administrative or statistical groupings.

Plan the tutorial's explanatory arc. First, identify the prerequisite idea that must be secured before the students can even understand what is at stake ontologically: I believe they need to distinguish a demographic cohort (a group defined by shared birth years) from a socially constituted kind whose members share properties in virtue of collective recognition or shared formative experience, but I am uncertain whether to introduce Mannheim's concept of generations formed by common historical events, or whether that introduces unnecessary historical baggage. Second, specify the single carrying example around which the whole session should be built. I propose using the widespread claim that Generation Z is more risk-averse and anxious because its members grew up with smartphones. This example must serve to isolate the ontological question from the empirical one: show how the observed trait could be an age effect (all adolescents are risk-averse), a period effect (smartphones have altered behavior across all age groups), or a genuine cohort effect (only those socialized with smartphones during a specific developmental window carry the trait into adulthood). The example must also highlight what distinguishes a cohort explanation from a marketing category such as Millennials (conventionally dated roughly 1981–1996). Third, map the predictable misconceptions in the order the students will raise them, and indicate exactly where in the sequence each should be neutralized: (1) that subjective alienation from one's parents is sufficient evidence of cohort reality; (2) that widespread adoption of a technology creates a shared essence among those who adopted it young; (3) that practical utility for policymakers or advertisers entails ontological robustness. For each, explain whether the rebuttal should come before the carrying example is introduced, during its analysis, or in a dedicated synthesis phase, and how the sequence should absorb the objection without derailing the dependency chain. Fourth, state explicitly what should be omitted and acknowledged as omitted: I intend to leave aside formal regression methods for disentangling age, period, and cohort effects, and I will avoid a full treatment of collective intentionality, but I want confirmation that these omissions are pedagogically defensible rather than hollow. Fifth, design one concluding check—perhaps asking the students to classify the COVID-19 generation as primarily a cohort, a period, or an age phenomenon, and then to state what that classification implies about whether the label names a real kind comparable to Baby Boomer. Finally, specify the response pattern that would indicate genuine understanding rather than mere discomfort or reflexive skepticism: what should they be able to say about the dependency between statistical grouping and ontological commitment?

Please do not include a bibliography, reading list, or required citations; the session has no assigned texts, and the answer should not depend on access to specific sources. Also, avoid precise attributions or dates for particular works; refer to theoretical ideas only in general terms if they are needed for the plan.
