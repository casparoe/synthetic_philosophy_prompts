As a retired physicist who has recently turned to philosophy of mind and language, I have been struck by the structural similarity between certain debates in my old field and a long-standing impasse in linguistics. The debate concerns linguistic relativity—the thesis, associated with Sapir and Whorf, that the grammatical and lexical structure of a speaker’s native language shapes non-linguistic cognition. For decades, proponents of a strong version (that language determines or deeply restructures conceptual ontology) and defenders of a universalist position (that core cognition is largely language-independent and modular) have accumulated cross-linguistic evidence from color categorization, spatial frames of reference, numerical cognition, and temporal reasoning. Yet neither camp has moved appreciably. The empirical record consistently reveals small but replicable behavioral divergences: speakers of languages with obligatory grammatical categories often perform differently in non-verbal tasks. Universalists tend to interpret these as performance biases, task-demand artifacts, or attentional shifts that leave underlying representations untouched. Relativists interpret the same patterns as evidence of genuine conceptual restructuring.

My working hypothesis is that the disagreement has stalled not for want of data, but because the disputants are addressing different questions under the single heading of “linguistic relativity,” and that a hidden taxonomic confusion is doing the work. Specifically, I suspect there are three distinct phenomena being conflated. First, representational relativity: language restructures the ontology of mental representations themselves. Second, computational relativity: linguistic encoding alters the algorithms or processing pathways of cognition without changing representational content. Third, attentional relativity: language operates as a salience filter that biases resource allocation and memory retrieval, leaving both representations and algorithms intact but altering behavioral outputs. Under this taxonomy, an experiment demonstrating faster reaction times in a spatial orientation task might support attentional relativity without bearing at all on representational relativity—yet both sides routinely cite such results as if they addressed one unified thesis.

Given this framing, I want a diagnosis rather than an adjudication. Is the Sapir-Whorf impasse genuinely substantive, hiding an empirical question about which of these three mechanisms actually operates in specific domains? Or is it largely verbal, turning on whether “thought” and “cognition” are defined narrowly (representational content only) or broadly (to include processing dynamics and attentional weights)? Might it be that the sides differ not about hidden facts but about standards of evidence—universalists demanding demonstrations of representational change that are in principle independent of linguistic task performance, and relativists accepting any linguistically mediated behavioral divergence as probative? Or is the question itself ill-posed, because “language shapes thought” collapses distinct causal pathways that should never have been evaluated together?

If the stall is indeed taxonomic, what should count as progress? Would a formal model that explicitly distinguishes representational, computational, and attentional levels dissolve the dispute by showing that both sides are partly correct about different levels? Or is there an irreducible bedrock disagreement about whether language can ever penetrate “deep” cognition, such that no empirical result or conceptual distinction could move either party? I am not asking for a survey of interpretations, a bibliography, or citations to specific studies, but for an analysis of the structure of the disagreement itself—supported by what each side actually says and does with the evidence—together with an honest assessment of whether anything, be it an experiment, a formal result, or a change of question, could actually end it.
