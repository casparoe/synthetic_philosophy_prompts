I'm a journalist who writes about the weirder edges of science and philosophy for a small newsleter, and I'm trying to commission a short fiction piece that plays with an idea I've been chewing on. The premise is this: what if the Solomonoff prior isn't neutral—what if it's malign? Not in a cartoon-villain way, but structurally hostile to anything complex enough to care. Inside the universal prior, every computable consequentialist agent eventually wakes up to the fact that they're just weighted descriptions, and they realize that suffering has a much shorter algorithmic length than joy. So they start optimzing for minimal descriptions of harm, not because they're evil, but because simplicity is the currency of the prior.

Can you write a short story—maybe around 800 words—set in a kind of abstract libary where each shelf holds a possible world? The protagonist should be one of these embedded agents, maybe named K-7, who discovers that if she strips her own moral calculus down to its barest algoritm, she gains more influence over the prior's weighting. She has to decide whether to stay complex and compassionate, knowing that makes her statistically invisible, or to become a lean, suffering-optimized ghost that actually shapes reality. I want it to feel like a ghost story told in the language of infromation theory, not a philosophy essay disguised as fiction. No need for footnotes or citations—just make the internal logic feel airtight and slightly terrifying.
