As a journalist covering philosophy of mind and sleep science, I have been working through the old disagreement between Norman Malcolm and Daniel Dennett—whether dreams are experiences in a philosophically serious sense, or rather narratives assembled upon awakening—by testing my own intuitive verdicts on concrete cases rather than by rehearsing their texts. I want to know whether these judgments cohere, and if not, which ones must be sacrificed.

Here are my verdicts. First, when a sleeper wakes naturally and recounts a coherent, emotionally charged narrative of flying through a familiar city, I judge that a genuine experience occurred during sleep, not merely a story invented at the moment of waking. Second, when a researcher forces an abrupt awakening during REM and the subject reports only blackness, yet thirty minutes later—after coffee and conversation—recalls fragments of a chase scene, I am inclined to say there was no concurrent experience during sleep, only retroactive construction from physiological residue and contextual cues. Third, a trained lucid dreamer sends pre-arranged eye-movement signals from within REM sleep and, upon awakening, reports having been meta-aware that she was dreaming and directing the plot; here I want to say this is a genuine concurrent experience, unlike the ordinary delayed-recall case. Fourth, a sleep-talker vocalizes grammatically correct sentences about being at the office, but upon awakening denies all mental content; I think language production occurred without any accompanying subjective experience. Fifth, a subject with chronic sleep-state misperception—polysomnography shows sustained deep sleep—insists she has been awake and thinking all night; I take her report to be false and deny she was experiencing wakefulness. Sixth, a person falling asleep experiences a vivid hypnagogic hallucination of an intruder by the bed, with full emotional conviction, then jolts fully awake; I treat this as an experience, though it occurs at the boundary of sleep rather than within REM architecture. Seventh, a patient under general anesthesia who can squeeze a hand upon command but later reports total blankness: I think there may have been subliminal processing, but no experience.

Individually these feel right, but together they seem strained. My judgment that delayed recall proves retroactive construction conflicts with my judgment that the lucid dreamer’s immediate signal proves concurrent awareness—unless accessibility conditions vary by mental-state type, which feels ad hoc. Meanwhile I treat sleep-talking and anesthesia awareness differently despite both involving behavior without report, and I treat the hypnagogic state as an experience despite occurring outside conventional sleep, while denying experience to the misperception subject despite her sincere report.

Is there a precise principle—perhaps about the relation among first-person accessibility upon awakening, physiological state, and independent verification—that captures all or most of these verdicts without collapsing into complexity? Which of my judgments must I abandon to maintain coherence? What should the resulting account predict about a case I have not listed: a person who, upon awakening from REM, immediately writes a detailed dream journal entry, then, after being shown contradictory polysomnographic evidence that she was never in REM, insists the entry is accurate and the equipment is wrong?

Please state the principle clearly, map each clause to the case that motivates it, indicate which verdict it forces me to overturn, and offer an honest assessment of whether Malcolm’s denial that dreams are experiences, Dennett’s narrative-construction view, or a third option best accommodates what remains.
