I am working through a difficult section of Aristotle's Politics (Book I, chapters 9–10) on the origins of exchange and the nature of money, and I need help with an interpretive crux. In the passage, Aristotle explains that exchange arose from communal need: one household has surplus grain, another surplus shoes, and both require what the other possesses. He then distinguishes the natural use of a good—wearing a shoe, eating grain—from its exchange use, trading it away for something else. From this need for a common measure comes money (nomisma), a conventional medium agreed upon to stand in for all goods. Yet Aristotle insists that money is "barren" (atokos) and that interest (tokos) is the most hated form of chrematistics, because currency, being a mere convention, has no natural reproductive power; charging for its temporary use is therefore like demanding that a borrowed shoe generate more shoes.

The standard commentary reads this as an economic observation: natural goods can reproduce (grain begets grain; animals breed), but metal coin does not, so treating money as self-multiplying violates nature. However, I want to test a more metaphysically ambitious reading—suggested in some recent Aristotelian scholarship—that takes the sterility claim not as an empirical point about productivity but as an ontological claim about internal ends (erga). On this view, a shoe is completed in being worn, grain in being consumed, and both have a teleological structure proper to their kind. Money, by contrast, has no internal ergon whatsoever: it is purely relational, existing only to stand in for other goods. Consequently, to treat money as possessing its own self-completing activity—namely, generating more money—is not merely greedy but categorically confused: it assigns to a conventional medium the kind of immanent teleological motion proper to living things or well-crafted tools.

Could you help me understand the passage through this lens? Specifically:

1. Does Aristotle's text genuinely support reading the sterility of money as a metaphysical point about the absence of an internal ergon, or does that import modern ontology?
2. If we grant the metaphysical reading, does the wrongness of interest shift from a moral vice to something like a category error—treating a relational medium as if it were a self-completing substance?
3. Where in the broader argument of Book I does Aristotle leave conceptual space for this ontological gap between goods with natural ends and a conventional medium lacking any?
