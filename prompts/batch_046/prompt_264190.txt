I'm a novelist invited to deliver the opening address at the dedication ceremony of the Fernand Braudel Centre for Historical Geography and Climate Studies, an occasion attended by climate scientists, historians, literary scholars, local benefactors, and students with no specialist background. I have recently completed a novel tracing one family's movements across three centuries of gradual coastal erosion, and I want the speech to be genuinely structured by the idea of the longue durée—the deep structures of geography and climate that persist beneath events and individual lives—rather than merely ornamented with it. The concept should constrain what I can and cannot say: I must honour the individual researchers and donors present without implying that human biography is the primary scale on which climate operates, and I must acknowledge present environmental urgency without reducing it to a single dramatic event that a long view can comfortably dismiss. To keep the address from being generic, please build it around a specific conceptual tension I call "the sedimentation of intention"—the notion that individual human intentions accumulate over generations into geographical and climatic structures, which then become the inescapable conditions within which later intentions must form. The audience is mixed, so the speech must land for experts without losing the non-specialists, and it should contain no citations, reading lists, or scholarly apparatus, as I will deliver it entirely from memory. Please write the address in American English, though this request itself is composed in British English.
