I am a novelist in the middle of drafting a long work about moral repair and the possibility of forgiveness without some irreducible residue of the self, and in recent conversations with friends I have kept returning to Daniel Dennett's distinction between cranes and skyhooks, together with the related danger of greedy reductionism, though I want to apply both to a problem they are not usually asked to address.

The difficulty is this: in literary fiction, a character's remorse or love must carry enough normative and emotional weight to drive the plot and move the reader, yet if I construct that inner life entirely from evolutionary, neurological, or social cranes—if every layer of mental explanation is built from the layer beneath without invoking a metaphysical skyhook such as a Cartesian ego, a vitalist spark, or an irreducible intentional essence—the account risks being greedy, not merely illuminating the feeling but dissolving it into mechanisms that leave nothing genuinely human to mourn. I suspect there is a conceptual space between accepting that all mental properties are cranes all the way down and allowing the realist novel to smuggle in a hidden skyhook through the back door of authorial sympathy or narrative framing, but I have not been able to map it with any precision.

Please describe, in continuous prose without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables—written simply as one might write in a long letter—the most important implications of the cranes-versus-skyhooks framework and the problem of greedy reductionism for the craft of constructing fictional interiority. I want to know whether it is philosophically consistent for a novelist to build characters whose choices bear full moral weight while denying any ontological skyhook, or whether the very form of literary realism depends on a kind of explanatory generosity that reductionism cannot provide. Do not supply a bibliography, citation list, or reading recommendations; instead, inject whatever half-formed or unconventional idea is necessary to keep the answer from being a standard philosophical recitation, and frame the implications so that they remain meaningful to someone who must rely entirely on reasoning without searching for sources.
