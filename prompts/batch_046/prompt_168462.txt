Design a 90-minute workshop activity for 12 postgraduate participants on advance directives and the authority of the earlier self, arranged so that the tension between precedent autonomy and present autonomy is experienced through rules and incentives before it is named in discussion.

Divide the group into four triads and assign, within each triad, the roles of T1 (the earlier competent self), T2 (the later self with altered values), and an Interpreter (tasked with applying T1’s directive). Give T1 a sealed scenario card describing a concrete, non-clinical commitment—for example, preserving a collaborative artistic archive—together with a hidden “value card” that encodes one core reason for that commitment. T1 writes a brief binding directive of one sentence, seals it with the value card, and then withdraws from the table.

T2 receives a different hidden value card representing a plausible, coherent value shift (for instance, prioritising broad accessibility over preservation). T2 is then given T1’s directive but is not shown T1’s value card. In round two, T2 may argue that the directive ought to be set aside, using only internal reasons drawn from the scenario itself.

The Interpreter hears both cases in round three, then allocates ten shared points between honouring the directive and overriding it; the triad’s collective outcome depends on this allocation. After the allocation, all three discuss the decision for ten minutes without revealing their hidden cards.

Timeline: round one, 15 minutes (T1 writes and seals); round two, 20 minutes (T2 receives materials and prepares); round three, 25 minutes (Interpreter adjudicates, allocates points, and discusses); round four, 20 minutes (structured debrief).

The debrief must map each round onto philosophical positions—T1 as precedent autonomy, T2 as present autonomy, the Interpreter as substituted judgement versus best interests—identify the precise moment participants felt the directive was legitimate rather than oppressive, predict how the activity will predictably misfire (for example, participants conflating value change with irrationality, or treating the Interpreter as structurally neutral rather than epistemically disadvantaged), and be frank about which feature of the real problem the simulation cannot reproduce—specifically, the irreversible, first-personal dissolution of selfhood that undermines the hidden-card analogy—stating how the facilitator must supply this limitation verbally.

Present the full design, rules, timeline, and debrief in British English, without bibliographies, citations, or reading lists.
