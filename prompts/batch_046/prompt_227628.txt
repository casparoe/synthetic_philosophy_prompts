For my philosophy seminar on the ethics of belief I have been trying to articulate where I stand between James and Clifford, and I am not sure the parts hold together. I accept James's view that in options that are forced, living, and momentous, and where the intellect genuinely cannot decide, we are permitted to let our passional nature determine our belief, and I take this to license religious commitment in conditions of evidential ambiguity. I also accept Clifford's evidentialist principle that one ought never to form a belief without sufficient evidence, but I restrict its scope to beliefs acquired through deliberate, voluntary inquiry, not to those that arise involuntarily through temperament or upbringing. I am convinced that strong doxastic voluntarism is false, so that neither Clifford's negligent shipowner nor James's religious believer directly chooses their belief in the way one chooses an action; nevertheless I want to maintain that the shipowner is derivatively culpable for failing to investigate, whereas the religious believer who stops investigating is not culpable, on the grounds that the shipowner's option was practical and open to further evidence-gathering while the believer's option was genuinely forced and existential. In addition, I hold that phenomenal feelings of intellectual peace, emotional resonance, or what I have started calling doxastic comfort can never serve as epistemic reasons for the initial adoption of a belief, yet they are ethically permissible as sustaining motives for maintaining a belief over time, provided they do not masquerade as evidence. My worry is that my denial of strong voluntarism undermines James's talk of the will to believe, since there seems to be no will involved in belief-formation at all; that my restriction of Clifford's norm to deliberate inquiry makes it unclear why the shipowner's negligence is a failure of belief-formation rather than merely a failure of action; and that my distinction between initial adoption and maintenance seems unstable once I allow affective states to play an ethical role in sustaining belief. I also wonder whether there is an apparent conflict between my acceptance of James's passional grounds and my rejection of doxastic comfort as a justifying reason, but I suspect that conflict might dissolve once the distinction between reason and sustaining cause is properly drawn. Could you examine these commitments, identify which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only appear to be, rank the tensions by severity, and suggest what single revision would clean up the most inconsistencies?
