Over the past year I have become personally invested in the ethics of ecosystem degradation after serving on a regional conservation board that was forced to weigh wetland restoration against agricultural expansion. The debate consistently collapsed into a simplistic binary—either preservation protects sentient wildlife or removal reduces wild suffering by lowering animal populations—without anyone addressing how the internal structure of an ecosystem changes the character of suffering for those who remain. I am now preparing the final examination for my graduate seminar on environmental philosophy, and I need an essay question that refuses to let students recite that binary.

Instead, the prompt should force them to confront a specific conceptual tension I have developed in my lectures: the distinction between sentient biomass reduction and suffering-quality redistribution across trophic levels. The scenario should ask students to consider a degraded ecosystem in which apex predators have been eliminated, mesopredator populations have exploded due to competitive release, and prey species now face prolonged chronic stress rather than brief acute predation—while overall vertebrate numbers have fallen by forty percent. Students must evaluate, using a prioritarian framework that weights intense prolonged suffering more heavily than diffuse brief suffering, whether this transformation genuinely reduces net wild suffering or merely redistributes it into more severe forms, and they must address whether cross-species aggregation of welfare is philosophically defensible when phenomenologies differ radically. The exam is a 90-minute in-class essay; I expect about 1,200 words. Please provide the exam question itself, a model answer of roughly 700 words, and a marking scheme that separates an A response—one that identifies the aggregation problem, applies prioritarian weighting consistently, recognizes that trophic simplification alters suffering quality rather than quantity, and questions whether ecosystem-level welfare is coherent—from a B response that treats the problem as a simple utilitarian calculation of lives saved versus lives lost. The scheme should specify which conceptual mistakes cost marks, such as conflating population decline with welfare improvement, ignoring the phenomenological incommensurability problem, or treating all suffering as fungible across taxa.
