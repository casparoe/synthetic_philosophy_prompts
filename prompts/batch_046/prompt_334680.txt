You are an experienced philosophy educator and curriculum designer. Before drafting anything, take a moment to brainstorm: list three ways a classroom exercise could make students physically or socially experience the pressure to suppress private judgment for group alignment, then select the one that best captures Simone Weil’s concern that political parties replace truth-seeking with collective loyalty and restructure individual reasoning rather than merely organizing it.

Now design a 75-minute, in-person classroom activity for 15 upper-level undergraduates in a political philosophy seminar. The goal is to make participants feel the force of Weil’s critique of political parties—specifically, the idea that parties generate a kind of moral and intellectual gravity that pulls members toward predetermined positions, penalizes independent evaluation, and substitutes collective identity for the pursuit of justice. Participants should encounter this pressure through their own choices, social interactions, and the rules of the exercise itself, not through any lecture or explanation.

Your design must include all of the following:

- A concrete physical layout and setting (e.g., table arrangement, restricted sight lines, hidden information, or designated speaking zones).
- Clear rules for a staged deliberation or simulation, with symbolic or social payoffs only—no real money.
- At least two asymmetric roles that place participants in different positions of information access or social authority.
- A mandatory consensus decision point where the group must produce a unified public stance, but where the rules make it individually costly to register private reservations.
- One invented procedural mechanism—give it a specific name, such as the alignment tariff or another term you coin—that makes the cost of dissent tangible and quantifiable within the game. Define exactly how it works, how it is enforced, and what it costs. This mechanism should capture an aspect of Weil’s analysis (for example, the hidden tax that party loyalty imposes on independent thought) but must not be a standard classroom trope or a well-known simulation device.
- A timeline divided into phases: setup, first round of private judgment, the intervention of collective pressure, a second round under the invented mechanism, and a structured debrief.
- A debrief plan that maps specific moments from the activity onto parts of Weil’s argument: for instance, the moment when participants felt compelled to agree despite private doubts, the formation of an in-group/out-group boundary around a slogan, and the way symbolic rewards reinforced conformity. Also identify the precise moment when the mechanism either succeeded or failed to reproduce the dynamic.
- An honest assessment of what will predictably go wrong the first time the activity is run—such as students breaking character, misunderstanding the invented mechanism, emotional discomfort with enforced conformity, or the facilitator accidentally resolving the tension too early—and specific guidance on how to handle each failure without undermining the point.
- A frank admission of which feature of Weil’s view—perhaps her claim about the near-irreversibility of party conditioning, her theological framing of justice and attention, or her distinction between party loyalty and genuine collective action—this activity cannot reproduce, and a brief note on how the facilitator must supply that missing element in the post-activity discussion.

Do not provide a bibliography, reading list, or citations. Do not include verbatim quotations from any real text; if you reference Weil’s writings, paraphrase them without quotation marks. Do not depend on obscure biographical details, specific publication dates, or little-known secondary literature. Write in a polished, formal tone appropriate for a seminar-design request, and ensure that the invented mechanism is integrated into the rules so that the idea emerges from what happens to the participants rather than from what you tell them afterward.
