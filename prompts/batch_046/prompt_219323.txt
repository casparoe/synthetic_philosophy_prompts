I am a high school student organizing an advanced ethics workshop for my school's philosophy club. I need a structured, runnable classroom activity for about fourteen participants over seventy-five minutes that makes them feel—through rules and real, small stakes rather than through lecture—whether there is a genuine moral difference between lying and misleading. The focus should be on cases where both actions produce the same false belief in the listener and the same practical outcome, yet one involves direct false assertion while the other involves technically true statements arranged so that omission or framing guarantees misinterpretation.

To prevent the exercise from collapsing into a familiar classroom debate, please design it around an invented mechanism I will call the credibility ledger: each student begins with four trust tokens, and the group must collectively advise a hypothetical scholarship applicant (played by the facilitator) through written messages only. If a message contains an explicit factual falsehood about eligibility, the sender immediately loses all tokens and is excluded from further advising; if a message is technically accurate but strategically omits a decisive criterion or frames weak evidence as compelling, the sender loses only one token, yet the applicant still reaches the wrong decision. The rules should be calibrated so that participants discover for themselves whether they treat total token forfeiture for direct lying as categorically worse than the gradual depletion caused by misleading omission, even when both corrupt the applicant's belief in identical ways.

Please include specific roles (for example, primary advisors, an observer-juror panel, and the applicant); a minute-by-minute timeline; the precise audit conditions distinguishing false assertion from strategic omission; and real but modest stakes, such as the winning coalition determining the workshop's closing discussion topic, or each lost token translating to a brief, visible responsibility for the sender. Then provide a debrief that maps key moments—such as the first group that chooses omission to preserve tokens, or the observer who notices the applicant was misled despite every statement being technically true—onto the conceptual distinction between deception by commission and deception by omission. The debrief should also predict likely failures (for instance, students gaming the rules with hyper-technical language, or conflating strategic ambiguity with ordinary politeness) and explain how to handle them without dissolving the exercise. Finally, be frank about which aspect of the philosophical problem this simulation cannot reproduce—such as the speaker's internal mental state or long-term institutional trust erosion—and clarify that the facilitator must supply that dimension in discussion rather than expecting it to emerge organically from the game.
