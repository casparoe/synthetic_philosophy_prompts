I've been working through a variant of the combination problem using a musical analogy that I can't seem to break, and I'd like help locating the fault line. The idea is that a fugue—say, a four-voice Bach fugue—presents a single auditory object only because there is a listener who integrates the independent melodic lines; strip away the listener and you have mere acoustic coincidence, not unity. Transfer this to cognition: (1) If mental unity requires an integrator that binds distributed processes into one phenomenal field, then an ant colony lacks a mind, since no single ant or global workspace integrates colony-wide signals into one subject. (2) The human brain, however, is structurally analogous: it is a fugue of independent neural voices, and Dennett's critique of the Cartesian Theater shows there is no internal listener binding them. (3) So by the same criterion, either both brains and ant colonies lack minds, or neither does. (4) It is not plausible that brains lack minds. (5) Therefore ant colonies have minds, and the distinction between colony-level and brain-level cognition is quantitative, not ontological. I think this proves too much—specifically, it erases the intuition that scale and architecture matter—but I can't tell whether the error is in the fugue analogy, in the claim that brains lack an integrator, or in the disjunction in (3). Please respond as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion: name the flawed step, explain why it looked defensible, say what abandoning it costs for the ant-colony question, and note whether any version of the fugue model can survive.
