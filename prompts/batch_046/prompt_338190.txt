I host a podcast for curious listeners who like serious ideas without the graduate-school jargon, and next week I am interviewing a historian who argues that the "Middle Ages," the "Renaissance," and the "Scientific Revolution" are not rough descriptions of real eras but a kind of invented three-act story—darkness, rebirth, triumph—that got baked into textbooks and never really left. She claims these three labels work like an interlocking fiction: the "Dark Ages" have to exist so the "Renaissance" can be a rebirth, and the "Renaissance" has to exist so the "Scientific Revolution" can serve as the triumphant finale. She also says that what we call the Renaissance was largely a branding exercise for a few Italian city-states, and that if you actually examine the years from roughly 1400 to 1600 you find slow, scattered changes with no clear curtain between acts.

Please write me eight to ten interview questions that go well beyond asking her to restate this view. I want at least one question that presses whether her "three-act play" comparison is just a catchy metaphor or an actual claim about how historians organize knowledge; one that tests her "interlocking fiction" idea by asking what happens to the concept of the Scientific Revolution if we keep the Middle Ages but erase the Renaissance entirely; one that asks why people across different cultures and centuries seem to notice roughly the same shifts around those centuries if the categories are pure invention; one that challenges her "branding exercise" claim by asking whether economic motives for a few banking families can really account for widespread changes in art, philosophy, and scientific practice that do not obviously serve those families; and one that anticipates her standard reply that "continuity doesn't mean nothing changed"—specifically asking how she can identify a genuinely significant change without smuggling back in the very period boundaries she is trying to destroy. For each of these, please include a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give.

Keep the questions formal and polished enough for a podcast that takes ideas seriously, but write them in American English and make them fully accessible to an audience without academic training. Do not ask for citations, reading lists, or bibliographies, and avoid generic questions about her career or how she got interested in history.
