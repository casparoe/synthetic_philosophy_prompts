I serve on a regional urban-planning review board that hears zoning-variance and land-use proposals affecting collective urban space. Over the past two years we have tried to apply Henri Lefebvre's concept of the right to the city as our guiding principle, but in practice we collapse into either ad hoc deference to incumbent residents or supply-maximizing approval with no principled stopping point. I want a reusable decision procedure — a sequence of ordered questions with explicit decision thresholds, stop rules, and clearly marked hand-off points where the answer is a matter of values rather than facts — that a three-person panel (a planner, a legal officer, and an elected community delegate, none with philosophy training) can run in about ninety minutes per proposal, with all three required to sign off.

The procedure should operationalize a specific synthesis I am proposing: the right to the city understood neither as an individual property entitlement nor as an aggregate welfare calculation, but as the collective right to participate in the production of urban space, constrained by two conditions drawn from commons governance — the reversibility of the spatial transformation and the dependency of existing users on the specific spatial configuration being altered. For each step, specify what counts as a sufficient answer, where the evidence must come from (document, testimony, technical report), whether disagreement among panel members forces a procedural stop or a delegated judgment call, and what the panel should record when it invokes the values hand-off.

Then apply the procedure to three proposals, in order:

1. A nonprofit proposes forty units of permanently deed-restricted affordable housing on city-owned land currently used — without formal lease — as a community garden and informal market serving about sixty households. The site is near transit; the garden has operated continuously for eighteen years with no legal tenure.

2. A developer seeks to demolish thirty rent-controlled apartments in a low-rise building to build a forty-story market-rate tower, with a legally binding contract to replace the thirty units at identical rent levels inside the new structure and to provide a public plaza. The replacement is guaranteed, but the construction timeline is four years, requiring current tenants to relocate during that period.

3. A cooperative group wants to convert a privately owned underutilized parking structure into twenty units of cooperative housing with a ground-floor commons open to the neighborhood, funded partly by a city loan requiring repayment within fifteen years.

Please include one case where the procedure yields a result I will dislike — I generally favor increasing housing supply and reducing exclusionary zoning — and identify exactly which feature of the framework drives that outcome. Finally, state clearly what the procedure cannot do: which kinds of disputes it systematically misclassifies, and what principle the board would implicitly be adopting if it overrode the procedure in that unfavorable case.
