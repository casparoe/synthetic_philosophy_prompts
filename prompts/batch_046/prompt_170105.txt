Take on the role of a professional philosopher of science with formal training in poetry. Before composing, do a brief brainstorming step: map out exactly how a sestina’s rotating end-word structure can mirror Quine’s web of belief—identify which positions correspond to statements near the center (highly resistant to change) versus the periphery, and note where the rotation forces a confrontation between the terms analytic and revision.

Then write a sestina—six six-line stanzas followed by a three-line envoi, using the traditional end-word rotation—with these six end-words at the line-ends of stanza 1, in order: belief, web, revision, center, experience, analytic.

The speaker should be a retired field biologist who once treated morphological taxonomy as if its categories were analytically true, and who now, after encountering Quine’s critique of the analytic-synthetic distinction, sees even the central nodes of classification as in principle revisable. Make the form do philosophical work: the migration and return of end-words should track Quine’s claim that experience confronts statements only as a corporate body, not individually, and that so-called analytic truths are merely those nearest the center of the web that we are least willing to revise. In the envoi, place analytic and revision together in the same line to symbolize their entanglement. Keep the tension open—do not resolve it with a sermon or a shrug—so that the rotation itself holds the ambiguity.

The poem should be accurate enough that a specialist recognizes Quine’s position: reference the idea that no statement is fully immune to revision, that logical truths sit near the center, and that reducing all meaning to immediate experience is the second dogma being rejected. Do not include citations, bibliographies, or reading lists; paraphrase Quine freely without using quotation marks. Avoid verbatim quotes from any source. Write with care and precision.
