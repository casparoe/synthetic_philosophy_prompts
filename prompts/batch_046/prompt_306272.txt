Build a decision tree that classifies positions on free will “worth wanting” — specifically, whether and in what form moral agency survives deterministic or mechanistic descriptions of human action — using only concrete commitments, not standard labels like compatibilism or libertarianism. The tree should branch on three successive questions: (1) Is the value of free will reducible to its role in sustaining reactive attitudes (blame, gratitude, resentment), or does it require that agents be ultimate sources of their actions independent of causal chains? (2) If we discovered that deterministic neuroscience fully explained every choice but preserved agents’ capacities for reason-responsive deliberation, should our practices of praise and blame be preserved, revised, or abandoned? (3) Can the relevant kind of freedom be instantiated by collective entities (corporations, democratic assemblies) or artificial systems that lack phenomenal consciousness but exhibit reason-responsive governance?

Each leaf should name a position if one exists in the literature (for example, a reactive-attitudes compatibilism, an event-causal libertarianism, a revisionist eliminativism, or an unnamed hybrid) and state in one sentence its most awkward commitment — especially regarding whether a well-governed AI or institutional body could count as free. Identify at least one logical combination of answers that is currently unoccupied, and diagnose whether it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot cleanly separate. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; phrase every node as a question an informed reader could answer without knowing philosophical taxonomy in advance. Limit the tree to eight leaves or fewer, and specify the audience as philosophy seminar students encountering the debate for the first time.
