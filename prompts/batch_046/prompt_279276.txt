In my bioethics reading group we have been deadlocked for over a year on a specific case: a patient with advanced dementia, no episodic memory of having signed an advance directive, who expresses clear present-tense contentment and asks for care that the directive explicitly refuses. Both sides grant the clinical and psychological facts—the loss of autobiographical memory, the absence of recognition, the presence of apparent well-being—and both agree that unnecessary suffering is bad. The autonomy defenders treat the directive as a binding life-plan authored by a competent predecessor; the welfare defenders treat honoring it as harming a conscious subject who no longer bears any meaningful psychological relation to that predecessor. We have worked through the standard arguments about personal identity in dementia and mapped the empirical literature on narrative disruption, yet neither side has conceded anything. Is this stall substantive—perhaps a disagreement about whether authority belongs to a diachronic narrative thread or to a synchronic conscious subject—or is it verbal, turning on an ambiguity in what “the same patient” or “bind” actually means? Could a hidden formal question be driving the impasse, such as whether a contractual or authorial obligation can survive the dissolution of the party that originally undertook it? Given your diagnosis, what would actually move the debate: a sharper conceptual distinction between kinds of authority, a clinical criterion for when narrative rupture is complete, or an argument that the framing is ill-posed because it presupposes a unitary self where only a cluster of capacities remains?
