Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between two camps arguing about national character: is it a coherent notion at all, and is it ever okay to generalize about peoples?

Camp one — call them the institutional-patternists. They think national character can be salvaged as a thin, descriptive cluster of shared norms and institutional habits, not an essence. They allow probabilistic generalizations if they're domain-specific (say, civic ritual or legal expectation), come with explicit variance disclaimers, and avoid deep psychological claims.

Camp two — the anti-generalizationists. They think the idea is incoherent because within-group variance always swamps between-group differences, and that even "thin" statistical framing smuggles in essentialism and does real harm in policy and discourse.

Write the document both could sign. Structure it like this:

1. Agreed claims — not fluff. Cover the empirical reality of high within-nation variance, the documented history of essentialist abuse, and the fact that institutional differences (legal origin, welfare design, etc.) do produce some measurable behavioral clustering.
2. A numbered disagreement table, with each side's view worded so that side accepts it. Seperate out whether part of the dispute is verbal — e.g., are they using "national character" to mean the same thing? — and the normative split: is generalization always impermissible, or only wrong under power asymmetry?
3. The cruxes — 2-3 sub-questions where settling them would actually move one side. For each, record Camp A's prediction and Camp B's prediction in advance. One crux should be the "dominant cultural script" reframing: is that just national character in new clothes? Another should be the "indexical attribution problem" — the idea that observers always classify other cultures relative to thier own normative baseline, so observed "national traits" might be relational measurement artifacts rather than internal properties.
4. Tests or conceptual distinctions both sides agree would matter, with each side saying what result they'd expect.

Don't ask for a bibliography, don't cite books I need you to know, and don't invoke ultra-obscure theories. Keep it casual — there's room for a typo or two. Make sure the residual disagreement is exact, not vague.
