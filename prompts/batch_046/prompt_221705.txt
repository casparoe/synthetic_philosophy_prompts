I'm working on a chapter about atrocity classification and have noticed a recurring structure in debates over mass violence that I can't name precisely. Consider two analysts who agree on every observable fact—the scale of killing, the organizational patterns, the identity of perpetrators and victims, even the existence of explicit statements—yet one classifies the event as genocide and the other insists it remains a crime against humanity, citing insufficient proof of the specific intent to destroy the protected group as such. The disagreement is not empirical but hermeneutic: the same evidentiary record is read through different standards for inferring mental states. What makes this distinctive is that the categorical split is then treated as a deep moral divergence—the refusal to apply the stronger label is seen not as legal caution but as a failure of solidarity with victims, while applying it is seen by others as an irresponsible escalation that licenses intervention. I have seen this pattern in debates about several twentieth-century cases, and it seems structurally different from ordinary factual disagreement or from simple political bias, because both sides often share normative commitments and differ only in their interpretive rules for mental-state attribution.

There is also a related temporal pattern: once archives eventually reveal clearer intent, earlier reluctance to apply the label is retrospectively condemned as moral blindness, even though the evidentiary standards used at the time were reasonable by ordinary legal measures. This creates an asymmetry in which the same hermeneutic caution is praised prospectively and condemned retrospectively.

Is there an established term—perhaps in the philosophy of law, in genocide studies, or in the literature on categorization—for this specific combination: shared empirical premises plus contested hermeneutic rules for mental-state attribution that drive categorical classification of atrocities, together with the retrospective moralization of those hermeneutic choices? If there is no single term, how should I distinguish this from ordinary evidentiary dispute, from political instrumentalization of labels, and from the well-known politics of naming more generally? I am not looking for a reading list, but for the conceptual carving: what exactly the phenomenon is, what it borders on, and whether it deserves its own name.
