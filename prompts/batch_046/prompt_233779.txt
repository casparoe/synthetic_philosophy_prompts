I have spent forty years studying complex physical systems with nonlinear dynamics and high noise floors, and since retiring I have become preoccupied with a philosophical argument that seems structurally analogous: the decades-long dispute over whether individual acts—purchasing meat, casting a vote, emitting carbon—constitute meaningful contributions to collective harms such as climate damage or industrial animal suffering. The interventionists maintain that moral complicity tracks aggregate causal chains regardless of marginal detectability; the inefficacy objectors insist that responsibility requires a causal lever whose movement actually alters the collective outcome, which in large-scale buffered systems it rarely does. Both camps invoke empirical data on marginal carbon footprints and electoral probabilities, yet the exchange has not advanced in twenty years. I suspect the stall is not ethical but conceptual: the sides may be operating with incompatible ontologies of distributed causation—treating partial causes either as continuous scalar contributions or as threshold-gated triggers—while labeling both positions moral responsibility. Diagnose this disagreement precisely. Is it verbal, a hidden empirical dispute about causal modeling in nonlinear systems, or a clash between two legitimate questions—one about virtue and symbolic alignment, one about efficacy—forced under one heading? Refer specifically to what each side says about marginal effects, buffering capacity, and collective outcomes. Finally, state honestly what would actually move the debate: a formal distinction, an agreed modeling framework, a changed question, or an admission that no resolution is possible because the disagreement is ultimately about values masked as causation.
