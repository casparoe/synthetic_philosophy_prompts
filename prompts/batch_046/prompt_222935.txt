Here is an argument constructed for a seminar on judging historical figures—specifically Jefferson, Churchill, and Columbus—through the lens of the man of his time defence. The conclusion seems to swallow the defence whole, yet each individual step feels difficult to reject.

(1) To blame an agent for violating norm N, the agent must have had access to N as a live option.
(2) Access requires agent-accessibility: the agent could have adopted N without requiring a total reconstruction of their conceptual, practical, and social framework.
(3) For Jefferson, abolitionist norms were agent-accessible: abolitionist texts circulated in Virginia, and rejecting slavery required only extending his own stated commitments to natural rights.
(4) So Jefferson is blameworthy for maintaining slavery.
(5) For Churchill, critiques of imperial racial hierarchy circulated in 1930s Britain; adopting them required political risk but not a total reconstruction of his worldview.
(6) So Churchill is also blameworthy for defending empire.
(7) For Columbus, the norm of indigenous sovereignty was not agent-accessible: adopting it in 1492 would have required abandoning the legal, theological, and cosmological framework of European expansion in its entirety.
(8) So Columbus is excused.
(9) Now apply (2) consistently: any norm whose adoption requires total framework reconstruction is not blameworthy to reject.
(10) But for contemporary agents, adopting norms against factory farming, fossil-fuel dependence, or extreme wealth accumulation requires exactly such reconstruction for most people embedded in those systems.
(11) Therefore, by (9), contemporary agents are excused for failing to meet these demands.
(12) If (11) holds, moral progress becomes impossible to demand: every sufficiently radical norm is excused until it is already dominant.
(13) So (2) must be false, and we should judge all historical figures—including Columbus—by present standards alone.
(14) But then the distinction between Jefferson or Churchill and Columbus collapses, and the defence saves no one.

The author of the argument wants to preserve (1) and (4)—that Jefferson is blameworthy because he genuinely had access—and also wants to preserve some version of the defence for Columbus.
