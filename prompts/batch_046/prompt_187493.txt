Assume the persona of a philosopher-poet well-versed in philosophy of science and metaphysics, particularly in debates about explanatory reduction and emergence. Before composing anything, take a moment to brainstorm how a poem's formal architecture—not just its imagery—can carry philosophical weight, so that the structure itself performs an argument rather than merely illustrating one.

Write a sestina addressing the distinction between cranes and skyhooks as modes of explanation, with explicit attention to what is called greedy reductionism: the mistake of treating the dismantling of an explanatory scaffold as if it were a genuine account of what that scaffold supported. For the six end-words, use in this order for stanza one: ground, scaffold, vanish, build, hang, reduce. Let the sestina's recursive rotation of these end-words embody the iterative, locally supported, bottom-up construction characteristic of a crane-like explanation, where each line rests upon its predecessors without any external suspension. Then let the envoi—where all six words are compressed into the final three lines—enact the greedy reductionist gesture: the scaffold is collapsed, the built structure is said to vanish, and everything is reduced to the ground, leaving the explanatory hang of the skyhook exposed as empty. The poem must be philosophically precise rather than decorative: someone familiar with these ideas should recognize that cranes accumulate support from below, skyhooks pretend to hang explanation from above with no scaffolding, and greedy reductionism confuses destroying the scaffold with explaining the building. Do not include headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables in your response; if you offer any expository remarks, render them as continuous prose in the manner of a letter, though the poem itself should preserve its necessary line breaks. Think carefully about how repetition and rotation can make the conceptual distinction audible to a reader who already knows the theory.
