I'm preparing a position paper for a workshop on pluralism and democratic citizenship, and I'd like a consistency audit of the package before I present. Here is where I've landed:

(1) I accept a form of political liberalism: state coercion must be justifiable to each citizen in terms of shared political values, not in terms of any comprehensive moral or religious doctrine.

(2) From this I derive that democratic equality in shared public institutions—state schools, courts, and administrative offices—requires what I call "civic suspension": citizens and officials should withhold conspicuous religious or cultural markers, because such markers function as status signals that fracture the reciprocal recognition required for democratic deliberation.

(3) I reject assimilation as a legitimate state goal; but I think voluntary cultural hybridization—individuals selectively adopting, combining, or revising practices across traditions—is both permissible and often a valuable expression of autonomy.

(4) I maintain that cultural membership is not fully voluntary: one's initial framework of language, moral intuition, and practical identity is constituted by attachments one did not choose, and one cannot fully renounce these attachments without a form of self-alienation.

(5) Despite (4), I deny that this non-voluntariness justifies group rights that restrict internal dissent or individual exit; the appropriate state protection is external—public funding for minority-language education, religious and cultural institutions, and transmission networks operating through civil society rather than through shared state institutions.

(6) I criticize Anglo-American multiculturalism for treating culture as a private consumer preference to be accommodated case-by-case, and I praise the French republican tradition for recognizing culture as a public good—though I also think the French model wrongly collapses all cultural presence into either private belief or public neutrality, leaving no legitimate space for recognized group presence in civil society.

Please go through these commitments and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only appear to be, and how severely. For each real tension, say what the cheapest revision would be and what it costs; then rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them as equally damaging. Finally, tell me which single revision—dropping or modifying one commitment—would clean up the most tension overall, and whether the remaining package is then coherent or merely less incoherent. I am not signaling in advance which commitment I would be most willing to sacrifice.
