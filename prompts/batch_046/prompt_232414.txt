Write a short story that explores Machiavelli's distinction between fortune (fortuna) and virtù through the eyes of a staff software engineer facing a cascading failure in a global distributed system. The protagonist should have no formal philosophy background, yet find herself confronting an outage that behaves like Machiavelli's raging river—an external, unpredictable force that no runbook or prior architecture can fully eliminate. Rather than treating the crisis as a purely technical puzzle, the narrative should examine how she exercises virtù, understood not as moral virtue but as practical, adaptive excellence: the judgment to violate standard rollback protocols when they become counterproductive, the willingness to improvise circuit-breaker logic from partial telemetry, and the capacity to shape outcomes despite incomplete control. Ground the philosophical tension in concrete engineering details—degraded service meshes, anomalous latency spikes, improvised traffic-shaping decisions—so that the story never becomes a thin allegory. The resolution should remain contingent: fortune should stay partly unmastered, and any success should feel earned through specific actions rather than inevitable, reflecting the idea that fortune governs roughly half our actions while the rest belongs to our own agency. Avoid academic exposition; let the concept emerge through the engineer's internal reasoning about control, contingency, and the limits of planning.
