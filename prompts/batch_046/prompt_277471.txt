I just spent the evening arguing with a friend about whether Aristotle’s critique of chrematistike—wealth-getting that treats money as reproductive rather than as a conventional measure of need—retains any force once modern finance separates risk from usury. I lost part of the argument, but it made me think of Elena. I am delivering the retirement toast for Dr. Elena Vasquez at the university development-fund gala next Thursday. The room will be split: half are economists and ethicists who know Aristotle’s Politics well, the other half are family and board members who do not. Elena spent thirty years running a reciprocal-loan fund that capped returns to actual productive cycles—essentially treating capital as a token of chreia, or need, rather than as barren metal that breeds. I want a five-minute toast that uses Aristotle’s distinction between oikonomia and unnatural acquisition as its structural backbone, not as decoration. The speech must praise her career without ever framing it as “wealth maximization,” since that vocabulary would betray the Aristotelian logic we are invoking. It should quietly answer our argument—whether separating risk from usury actually saves lending from Aristotle’s condemnation—by showing how her practice resolved the tension, without lecturing the family half. Include one concrete image, drawn from her work, that works for both audiences: money as convention versus money as self-generating, such as seed loans tied to harvest calendars rather than compound interest. Do not quote Aristotle verbatim; paraphrase. Do not include a bibliography or ask for one. Keep the tone polished, affectionate, and conceptually rigorous enough that the philosophers in the room recognize the framework while everyone else simply hears a fitting tribute.
