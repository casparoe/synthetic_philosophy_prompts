In philosophy of biology, the debate over biological function is often introduced through an artifact analogy: a heart is like a screwdriver, and its function is fixed by what it was made or selected to do—pump blood, drive screws—regardless of what it happens to do in an abnormal context. Defenders of etiological accounts lean on this comparison to argue that functions are essentially historical, carrying a normative dimension in which malfunction is failure relative to that origin. Critics defending causal-role accounts reply that the analogy illegitimately imports a designer's intention into evolutionary history, and they counter with a different image: a biological system is like a division of labor inside a corporation, where an employee's "function" is just whatever contribution they currently make to the firm's overall capacity, with no reference to the original hiring memo.

Audit both analogies. For the artifact comparison, lay out the explicit correspondence table—designer corresponds to natural selection, intended effect to selected effect, manufacturing defect to dysfunction, repurposing to exaptation—then show precisely where each mapping breaks down, especially around the absence of conscious intent in selection, the multiplicity of selected effects (pleiotropy), and the difference between breaking and being misused. Ask whether the normative judgment that a heart "fails" when it does not pump survives once these disanalogies are visible, or whether it collapses into a mere statistical deviation. Next, audit the corporate-division analogy: show how it handles dysfunction, whether it can distinguish a tumor's contribution to thermoregulation from a heart's contribution to circulation, and whether it loses the very normative force the etiological account was trying to preserve. Finally, say whether either analogy actually carries the philosophical weight placed on it, identify the hidden assumption each smuggles in about the relationship between parts and wholes, and suggest a more accurate comparison—or explain why the debate requires abandoning analogical reasoning altogether.
