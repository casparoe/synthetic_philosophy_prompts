I am writing my dissertation in philosophy of language on whether Wittgenstein’s notion of family resemblance can accommodate highly structured technical registers that lack explicit propositional content. To test my own intuitions, I have been evaluating concrete communicative practices to see whether there is a unifying principle—perhaps a refined language-game criterion—that captures my judgments, or whether I must abandon one of them in reflective equilibrium.

Here are my verdicts:

Case 1: A group of chess players using only standardized algebraic notation to record moves, with no spoken commentary. Verdict: this is a language game, centered on rule-governed notation.

Case 2: Two marine biologists using a proprietary hand-signal system underwater to coordinate specimen tagging, where the signals have no compositional syntax but are mapped one-to-one to actions. Verdict: this counts as a language game, though a minimal one.

Case 3: A religious community using a fixed set of chanted syllables that have no semantic translation in the vernacular but are said to open the ritual space; participants report that altering the syllable order breaks the efficacy of the practice, yet they deny that the syllables mean anything. Verdict: I am inclined to say this is not a language game, because there is no internal rule-structure that can be violated or corrected—only a causal regularity.

Case 4: A team of software engineers using an internal emoji-based shorthand in commit messages that evolves organically, where the meaning of each emoji shifts based on project context and there is no authoritative dictionary, but mistakes are routinely corrected by peers in code review. Verdict: clearly a language game.

Case 5: A therapy session in which the patient and analyst use a shared private vocabulary derived from the patient’s dreams, where terms are defined ostensively within the session and have no stable extension outside it, yet grammatical roles remain intact. Verdict: a language game, but one that strains the idea that family resemblance requires public accessibility.

The tension: Case 2 and Case 3 seem structurally close—both involve non-compositional, context-bound signaling—yet I place Case 2 inside and Case 3 outside. Meanwhile, Case 5 seems to push against the publicity condition often associated with rule-following, yet I want to include it.

Is there a precise principle—stated without appealing to necessary and sufficient conditions—that distinguishes the practices I accept from those I reject? What should it say about the case I keep oscillating on: a silent, collaborative indexing protocol used by archivists, in which pairs assign non-verbal spatial coordinates to unlabelled artifacts, correct each other’s placements through gesture, and maintain no written record of the coordinate system, but treat misplacement as an error that must be repaired? Does this belong inside or outside the family?

Please state the principle, or a small set of competing candidates, precisely; indicate which of my verdicts each can accommodate; specify its verdict on the archivist protocol; and be explicit about which of my judgments—especially on Case 3 or Case 5—must be revised if we prioritize simplicity over saving every intuition. I am not looking for a bibliography or reading list; I am interested in the conceptual architecture alone.
