I serve on my university's Intellectual Property Appeals Panel, which adjudicates ownership disputes falling outside standard patent and copyright categories. We must issue a written opinion on a claim by Dr. Elena Vasquez against Dr. Marcus Chen involving what Vasquez terms a modal contingency scaffold—a structured reasoning framework for collective moral responsibility built from three interlocking conceptual nodes: counterfactual exclusion conditions, aggregated intentionality thresholds, and responsibility diffusion curves. Vasquez presented the scaffold orally at an interdisciplinary workshop, distributing no written materials or slides. Six months later, Chen published an article employing the identical three-node architecture, the same technical terminology for each node, and applications to the same problem class (group omissions in climate governance), though his prose, examples, and formal notation are entirely original. Vasquez alleges misappropriation of an original conceptual artifact; Chen argues that philosophical reasoning structures are unownable ideas, that independent convergence is common in normative theory, and that recognizing her claim would chill collaboration by privatizing shared inferential tools. Our institutional policy covers tangible expressions, data sets, and patentable methods, but is silent on abstract inferential architectures. Draft the panel's formal opinion recommending a concrete decision—recognize the claim, deny it, or recognize it with limited conditions—including any time frame for remedy or procedural follow-up. The opinion should treat the strongest argument on the losing side with intellectual honesty, and it should be composed in American English for circulation before Thursday's meeting.
