I sit on an interdisciplinary ethics panel with a colleague whose track record in evaluating moral testimony is, by every external measure, equivalent to my own. We have assessed five anonymized reports in which a layperson receives guidance on a hard practical decision: in four—where the advisor held clear technical superiority, the recipient suffered acute emotional distress, or the reasoning turned on quantifiable risk—we both agreed that deference was warranted. In the fifth report, advisor and recipient share identical training, emotional stability, and reasoning capacity, but the guidance concerns a ritual obligation specific to a tradition neither belongs to; my colleague insists deference is still required, whereas I deny it. I seek not generic advice but the philosophical structure beneath our disagreement. Respond in exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion: identify the feature—if any—that breaks the symmetry between us in this fifth case without collapsing into mere cultural distance; state whether equal reliability demands that both of us reduce our confidence or allows one to hold firm; articulate the principle that distinguishes the four deference-warranting cases from this one, and whether it can be applied without being ad hoc; specify a further hypothetical scenario that would test your principle against its nearest rival; and state which practical posture—mutual revision, holding firm, or suspension of judgment—best follows from your account.
