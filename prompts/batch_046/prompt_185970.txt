In philosophical and legal discussions of virtual-world harms—such as the deliberate destruction of a long-cultivated virtual artifact or targeted harassment within an immersive environment—the debate over whether these are genuinely real harms appears to have stalled. One side emphasizes that affected users experience measurable psychological distress, that virtual goods carry exchangeable economic value, and that the internal social norms of these worlds treat such acts as serious violations. The opposing side maintains that without direct physical causation or bodily injury, any harm is merely symbolic or derivative, and that attributing full moral weight to virtual events risks a category mistake. Both camps accept the same empirical findings about user psychology and platform economics, yet neither has substantially revised its position in recent years.

My tentative diagnosis is that the deadlock is not about ontology in a straightforward sense but about competing standards for what counts as a sufficient causal pathway: the realists accept mediated causation through representational experience, while the anti-realists require direct physical interaction. Yet I wonder whether a hidden confusion is at work—perhaps one side is asking about the institutional reality of virtual objects (whether they exist within a collectively accepted rule-governed practice that can ground property-like claims), while the other is asking about the phenomenal reality of harm (whether the subjective experience of presence makes the distress structurally comparable to offline harm). Alternatively, the dispute might be verbal, turning on different uses of the term "real," or it might conceal an unresolved empirical question about the mechanisms by which virtual presence generates lasting trauma.
