You are delivering the opening remarks—approximately eight to ten minutes—at an interdisciplinary workshop titled Judgment Without Algorithms, convened for a room split evenly between academic moral philosophers and front-line practitioners: clinical social workers, public defenders, and hospice coordinators, none of whom are professional philosophers. The workshop is structured around the stalemate between moral particularism and moral generalism. Particularism holds that the moral relevance of any feature is irreducibly context-sensitive and that reasons do not aggregate in a linear, additive fashion; generalism holds that defensible moral judgment requires principles, even if only defeasible ones. Your speech must let this disagreement do real structural work rather than serve as ornament.

Specifically, you must navigate a double constraint. For the practitioners, an absence of portable rules feels like institutional abandonment, not philosophical liberation; for the philosophers, any concession to principles-for-practical-guidance risks collapsing particularism into a weak generalism. You are therefore forbidden from offering either a checklist of rules or a romantic celebration of pure intuition. Instead, use the particularist insight about the shape of reasons—how the same feature can count for or against an action depending on its relational context within a situation—to explain what responsible institutional judgment actually looks like: not the application of codified rules, but the cultivation of a perceptual attunement that can be trained, audited, and corrected without being reduced to an algorithm. At the same time, you must directly address the particularist vulnerability to institutional power: if moral judgment is genuinely non-codifiable, how can an institution distinguish legitimate discernment from arbitrary preference?

The audience is mixed: half will recognize references to the holism of reasons and the arbitrariness objection; the other half needs to see why this debate matters to daily decisions about resource allocation, end-of-life care, or discretionary authority. Structure the remarks in three movements: an opening that establishes the shared problem in practical terms; a middle that articulates the particularist account of judgment without surrendering to mysticism; and a closing that proposes a concrete, non-rule-based standard of institutional accountability—something like transparent, revisable reasoning about feature-salience—that both audiences can accept and apply. Do not cite specific authors, provide a bibliography, ask for further reading, or depend on any named theoretical framework beyond the particularist-generalist distinction itself; the argument must stand entirely on its own.
