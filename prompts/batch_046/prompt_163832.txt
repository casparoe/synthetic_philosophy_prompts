Here's an argument I built for a seminar on Scheffler's afterlife conjecture, and now it strikes me as suspicious because it seems to prove too much—probably by sliding between two senses of "value-constitution." (1) The conjecture holds that many activities we deeply value—scientific inquiry, artistic tradition, child-rearing—derive part of their present worth from being embedded in an open-ended human future that extends well beyond our own lives. (2) If an activity's present value is genuinely constituted by its relation to a future state of affairs F, then in circumstances where F is known to be impossible, the value-component tied to F should be absent or severely diminished. (3) Human extinction eliminates F (the open-ended continuation of humanity). (4) So, if extinction is certain, long-term projects should lose the constitutive portion of their value and become, at best, instrumental distractions without deep significance. (5) But consider the parallel case of individual mortality: my own death eliminates F* (the open-ended continuation of my personal projects through my direct agency and witness). (6) By the same reasoning, learning I have only months to live should diminish the constitutive value of my present commitments—parenting, writing, research—to the same degree that collective extinction diminishes collective commitments. (7) Yet this is not what we observe: people facing imminent death often report that their present activities gain intensity and focus, rather than becoming axiologically hollow. (8) So either the conjecture applies symmetrically to individual and collective cases—making it implausible that it explains why extinction is specially bad—or it applies only to the collective case, which requires explaining why collective open-endedness plays a unique value-constituting role that personal open-endedness does not. (9) Any attempt to ground that uniqueness in "the human project" or "civilizational continuity" seems to presuppose the very collective value-structure the conjecture was meant to explain, rather than justify treating the two cases differently. (10) Therefore, the conjecture either collapses into a general claim about all future-oriented value that conflicts with our experience of individual mortality, or it relies on an undefended asymmetry. Where is the slip? Is it in (2)'s move from "constituted by relation to F" to "absent when F is impossible," in (5)-(6)'s analogy between collective and personal futures, or in (9)'s charge of circularity? If I want to keep (1) intact, which downstream rejection costs the least, and what does paying that cost commit me to?
