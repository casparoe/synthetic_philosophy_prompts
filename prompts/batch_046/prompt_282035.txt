As a retired molecular biologist who has recently turned to philosophy of action, I am preparing an interview for a small interdisciplinary podcast with an unnamed philosopher who defends a particular structural account of the moral distinction between doing harm and allowing harm. Rather than grounding the asymmetry in intentions, rights-infringement, or bare consequentialist calculus, she holds that an agent does harm precisely when she occupies an intervention-sensitive node within the causal network producing the harm, such that her withdrawal would alter the system's baseline dynamics; she allows harm when she remains causally external to that self-sustaining loop, even if she could have interrupted it. The view draws on familiar interventionist ideas about causation but applies them structurally to moral agency, yielding non-obvious verdicts—for example, that a physician who discontinues a ventilator may be allowing death even when she physically presses the switch, provided the patient's respiratory system has become the self-sustaining loop and her action is an external interruption rather than an internal causal contribution.

Please draft six to eight interview questions that move well beyond restating this position. I want at least one question pressing the boundary between internal and external causal nodes in clinical settings—specifically, how the view distinguishes active withdrawal of life support from passive non-intervention when both require a physical act. For that question, include a follow-up keyed to her likely reply that the distinction tracks whether the agent's body is integrated into the patient's physiological loop at the moment of decision. I also want one question on how her structural account avoids collapsing into a form of moral luck, since whether one counts as an internal node often depends on contingent facts about how the causal system is arranged rather than on anything the agent controls. Include a follow-up anticipating her defense that structural position is a relational property fixed by the agent's actual causal role, not by fortune. Finally, include a question that asks what evidential or experimental finding—say, from systems neuroscience or clinical ethics—would genuinely threaten her framework, since she admits it is empirically informed but not empirically reducible. The sequence should build: open with conceptual clarification, move to the clinical boundary case, then to the moral-luck objection, and close with vulnerability conditions. Keep the tone formal and polished, suitable for an audience with scientific literacy but no specialist training in philosophy.
