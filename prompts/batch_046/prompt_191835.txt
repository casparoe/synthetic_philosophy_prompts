I'm giving the opening remarks at a workshop honoring the retirement of my doctoral advisor, a philosopher of science who spent forty years studying distributed cognition in research groups and who, in her later work, defended a precise and unorthodox reading of Zhuangzi's view on death. The room is split: half are philosophers and cognitive scientists familiar with her claim that Zhuangzi is not advancing an Epicurean consolation—death is not harmless merely because the deceased no longer exist to suffer it—but is instead rejecting the bounded self as a substance, treating the organism as a temporary clustering of processes that was never an enduring subject capable of being harmed; the other half are family members who loved her but have never read the Zhuangzi. I need a five-minute address that makes her philosophical position do real structural work: it must govern what can and cannot be said about her absence, frame the purpose of the workshop she founded, and avoid all false consolation—no watching over us, no survival of personality—while still offering genuine comfort to the non-philosophers. The speech should weave her empirical work on scientific cognition into her view of mortality so that both halves of the room hear one coherent portrait rather than two disconnected tributes, and it should treat the dissolution she described not as a loss of something precious but as the kind of transformation she always argued cognition itself exemplifies.
