I hold that phenomenal qualities—qualia—are not merely unexplained but conceptually incoherent, in roughly the spirit of arguments that “quine” them. The core claim is that the standard triad—intrinsicness, essential privacy, and causal inertness—cannot be jointly satisfied. If a property is genuinely intrinsic, it must be anchored in physical states; if it is causally inert, it can play no role in generating beliefs about itself; yet essential privacy is meant to guarantee authoritative first-person access. The combination yields a feature that is simultaneously physically embedded and physically disconnected, knowable to its bearer but with no causal pathway explaining that knowledge. So “what it is like” talk refers to nothing that could consistently exist.

What would genuinely count against this, and how exposed is the view? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward: for instance, a demonstrated causal-explanatory role for phenomenal properties that resists functional redescription; a conceptual separation of “intrinsic” from “causally inert” that dissolves the tension; successful third-person prediction of specific phenomenal reports without first-person input; or analogies from mathematics or morality showing that other domains tolerate similarly “inert” intrinsic properties. For each, say whether a committed defender should concede damage or can absorb it by qualification—e.g., redefining the predicted reports as behavioral proxies rather than genuine qualia. Also assess whether this incoherence thesis has been insulated into unfalsifiability (say, by treating any counterexample as “not the real phenomenal property”) and whether that insulation strengthens the critique or vitiates it. Answer in at most 400 words.
