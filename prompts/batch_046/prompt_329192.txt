I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and I got into a weird argument with my editor about whether moral norms ever actually change because people think them through. You know the Bernard Williams / Kwame Anthony Appiah line—that slavery and dueling didn't end because philosophers convinced anyone, but because honor codes and economic intersts shifted? I tried to push that logic onto a new case and now I'm stuck.

Here goes:

1. Every big norm-change is either argument-driven or driven by honor/interests.
2. For slavery and dueling, the argument story is weak; honor and money did the work (Williams/Appiah thesis).
3. So moral argumentation is never the real engine—it's always just post-hoc rationalization.
4. Now there's this niche shift in open-source AI research: people are treating citation of closed-weight models without disclosure as a kind of intellectual dueling offense. It spread fast, and it looks like it was driven by explicit arguments about reproducability and epistemic justice, not by prestige or funding (those followed later).
5. So either this is an exception, or we have to claim the arguments were just window dressing for hidden shifts.
6. But allowing even one exception kills (3)—moral argument can drive change.
7. So if we want to keep the thesis intact, we must deny arguments do real work even here, which feels ridiculous.

I feel like the slip is between (2) and (3), maybe an overgeneralization? Or maybe (4) gets the causation backwards? Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I don't want a bibliography—just tell me where the exit is.
