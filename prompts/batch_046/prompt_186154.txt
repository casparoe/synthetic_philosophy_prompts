I was talking with my cousin last weekend about whether there is literally a little “screen” inside our heads where everything comes together—he kept picturing an audience sitting in a dark theater watching our lives unfold, and I realized that is exactly the image I carry around too, even though it sounds absurd when I describe it aloud. I heard something on a podcast about a philosophical view that rejects that theater picture entirely: instead of one central viewer, consciousness might work like several drafts of a story being edited in parallel, none of them “the official version,” with no auditorium and no single audience member in charge. I would like you to write a deadpan product review—around 600 words, in the voice of a tech magazine such as Wired or Consumer Reports—of a fictional brain architecture called the “Draft-7 Consciousness System,” which is built on that multiple-drafts model rather than on a Cartesian theater.

The review should treat the absence of a central homunculus as a selling point: praise its parallel editing, its refusal to store a master timeline in real time, and the fact that the user never has to maintain one continuous “self” watching from the wings. At the same time, the satire should come from applying ordinary consumer-review criteria—battery drain, user interface, customer support, replay functionality—to these philosophical features. For instance, complain that there is no “replay button” because drafts are constantly overwritten, rate the “user experience” of not having anyone specifically in charge, and include a sidebar noting that warranty claims are complicated because no single draft can be held responsible for a decision. The reviewer should sound genuinely enthusiastic, but the reader should gradually realize that the very idea of a satisfied “consumer” undermines the product being reviewed. Do not name any philosophers or cite specific books; just let the conceptual tension between the theater intuition and the draft model drive every paragraph. Keep the irony sustained and deadpan—no winking—so that a reader familiar with the argument recognizes the satire as fair rather than as a straw man.
