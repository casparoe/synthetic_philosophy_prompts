I'm putting together a little interview series for our dept's podcast programme, Conceptual Mechanics, and next week's guest is a philosopher of design who's just published a monograph on what she calls 'parameter-space essentialism' about letterforms. Her basic claim is that what makes every 'a' an 'a' isn't some static shared shape — it's that the glyph sits inside a bounded region of a continuous design space, the kind mapped out by Douglas Hofstadter's Letter Spirit project. She thinks these regions are held together by 'stylistic gravity', a normative pull that keeps variations recognisable even when the geometry shifts wildly. She's definatly very sharp, but she has this annoying habit of deflecting objections by saying 'that's just noise in the font metrics, not a failure of categorisation'.

I'd like you to draft me about six or eight questions that don't just let her restate the view. I want one that presses her on whether her 'stylistic gravity' can actually distinguish a genuinely malformed 'a' from a different letterform like a Greek alpha, and then include the follow-up she's most likely to give — probably that the boundary is normative, not geometric — followed by a sharper reply to that. Another should tackle variable fonts, where a single glyph can slide continuously between an 'a' and an 'o'; ask whether her essentialism collapses into conventionalism if the same digital object can recieve either categorisation depending on slider position. I also want a question about historical drift: if the essential region is continuous, why did certain medieval blackletter forms drop out of the 'a'-region entirely rather than just sliding to the edge? For each, please sketch the likely answer and then the follow-up that makes the deflection harder to use.
