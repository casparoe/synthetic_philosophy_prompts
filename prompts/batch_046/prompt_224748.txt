Here are notes for a short essay tentatively titled Two layers of wrongness: instrumental corruption versus value parasitism in the wrong-kind-of-reasons problem. I begin with the familiar observation, associated with Bernard Williams, that moral action done for the wrong kind of reason—say, rescuing a stranger solely to collect a reward—fails to manifest the agent's proper moral orientation even when the outcome is correct. Standard treatments tend to classify all such cases under a single heading: the agent's reason is external to the value that justifies the act. I want to argue that this collapses two structurally different failures.

The first, which I call instrumental corruption, is the classic case. The agent's reason is causally detached from the value; she treats the right action as a mere means to an unrelated end. The second, which I call value parasitism, is more subtle. Here the agent's reason is tightly coupled to the value—she tracks it precisely—but only as a necessary condition for some further end that is not itself responsive to that value. Consider a surgeon who performs life-saving operations not because she values her patients' lives, but because her license and professional identity depend on maintaining a high success rate. Patient survival is not an accidental side-effect for her; it is the metric she must optimize. Yet her practical reasoning is anchored in career preservation, not in the patients' welfare. The reason is wrong-kind not because it ignores the value, but because it feeds on it without being constituted by it.

My tentative claim is that value parasitism is not reducible to instrumental corruption. In the first case, the agent could in principle perform the action while being entirely indifferent to the value; in the second, she is calibrated to the value and might even suffer emotional disruption if it is violated, yet her motivation remains structurally misaligned. If this distinction holds, it has implications for how we assess professionals who reliably do good for self-interested reasons that are institutionally embedded: they may deserve less praise than genuinely motivated agents, but perhaps more than those who are merely accidentally beneficial.

I would welcome comments on whether this bifurcation is genuinely substantive or merely taxonomic, whether the surgeon example succeeds in isolating the second layer, and whether recognizing value parasitism helps resolve any traditional puzzles about moral motivation—such as whether an agent who acts from a tightly coupled but non-moral reason can still be said to act for a moral reason in some derivative sense. I am also unsure whether the distinction applies beyond professional contexts, and suggestions for sharpening or testing it, or for relevant objections I may have overlooked, would be particularly useful.
