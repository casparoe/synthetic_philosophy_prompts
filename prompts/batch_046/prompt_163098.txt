I've been following how some cities now run climate citizens' assemblies, where residents are picked at random within census tracts so that the group mirrors local demographics for age, gender, and income, then asked to deliberate on heat-adaptation and flood-planning policies. The selection pool in the program near me requires a fixed mailing address, which means anyone sleeping in shelters, vehicles, or temporary encampments is automatically excluded, even though those same people face the worst exposure to extreme weather. At the same time, the assembly includes teenagers recruited through high school rosters and green-card holders who arrived less than a year ago. I am trying to understand what theory of democratic membership this practice quietly assumes. If belonging to the demos depends on being stably subjected to local law over time, why include very recent immigrants and minors? If it depends on being materially affected by the decision, why exclude the unhoused, who are clearly among the most affected? And if it depends on having a long-term stake in the community, why rely on census demographics that treat a transient renter and a lifelong homeowner as equally representative? I want to know what philosophical commitments about who belongs to the people are built into these selection rules, whether the different criteria for teenagers, immigrants, and unhoused residents presuppose inconsistent theories of democratic boundary, and how much of the design could survive if we replaced the underlying view with a rival one.
