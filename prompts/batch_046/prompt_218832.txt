Next month I present at a philosophy of mind and cognitive science seminar. I defend a modified Gödelian anti-mechanist argument: rather than claiming that human minds can see the consistency of every formal system they are not part of, I argue that any computable cognitive architecture attempting to stabilize a self-referential truth predicate across iterative belief revision will eventually generate a Gödelian sentence that the architecture must either reject on non-rational grounds or accept at the cost of internal inconsistency. The instability is structural, not mystical; it targets the dynamics of self-correcting belief revision, not computation per se.

The audience consists of eight formal epistemologists working on belief revision and bounded rationality, two cognitive scientists who build predictive-processing architectures, and one modal logician specializing in self-reference. They are familiar with the standard Lucas-Penrose objections—system-relativity, the consistency fallacy, and the claim that minds are not formal systems—and are predisposed to treat Gödelian anti-mechanism as settled. The epistemologists care whether my "stabilization failure" is genuinely non-computable or merely a failure of rational updating rules; the cognitive scientists care whether the argument applies to implemented architectures with approximate, probabilistic representations; the logician cares whether my formalization of "stabilization" avoids circularity.
