One student wrote the following: Williams's integrity objection is that utilitarianism forces agents to treat their ground projects—those deep commitments that give life meaning—as if they were preferences that can be overridden whenever the calculus demands. In the classic rescue case, a husband saves his wife from drowning rather than a stranger. The utilitarian says he should have saved the stranger if there were more strangers, or that he needs to justify saving his wife by showing it maximizes utility. The husband who thinks it is my wife is right, but if he adds and this is permitted by the moral calculus, that is one thought too many—he has alienated himself from his own ground project of being a husband. Applying this to the surgeon: she should operate on her child because being a parent is a ground project. If she paused to calculate whether saving her child maximizes lives saved, she would be treating her parental commitment as dispensable. This is essentially the same problem Rawls identifies with the separateness of persons: utilitarianism treats people as interchangeable vessels of well-being, so it violates both distributive fairness and personal integrity. Therefore, Williams shows that utilitarianism is not just too demanding but actually self-defeating, because any agent who genuinely possessed ground projects could never consistently act on utilitarian principles without destroying the very commitments that make action meaningful. The surgeon is right to save her child, and any theory that requires her to weigh her child against strangers has already corrupted moral reasoning.
