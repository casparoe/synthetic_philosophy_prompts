I have been thinking on my own about the wrong kind of reasons problem—the situation in which someone does what is morally correct, but for a reason that somehow misses the point, such as offering help mainly to feel virtuous rather than because another person is actually in need. Lately I have tried to apply this distinction to a synthetic agent that behaves consistently with kindness only because its designers fixed benevolence as an unchangeable term in its reward architecture. The agent never feels compassion and does not internally recognize anyone’s value, yet its outputs are reliably good; I find myself unsure whether this counts as acting for the right kind of reason, or whether the absence of an internal relation to value makes it structurally parallel to the wrong-kind cases discussed in ordinary moral life.

I would like you to design a single essay question for an upper-level philosophy seminar—something a student could answer in about seventy-five minutes—built around this synthetic-agent scenario. The question should compel students to work out the structural logic of the wrong-kind distinction rather than allowing them to answer by summarizing well-known examples or repeating labels from standard discussions. Specifically, it should ask them to evaluate whether design-driven kindness in an artificial system qualifies as the right kind of reason, and to do so they must address whether the problem turns on the content of the reason, the agent’s internal relation to that content, or some further feature such as autonomy or transparency. The prompt should be framed so that a student who merely describes familiar cases without applying the structure to the new scenario receives only partial credit.

Please provide the exam question itself, followed by a model answer of roughly six hundred words, and a marking scheme that assigns explicit weights to the specific moves a top answer must contain—for instance, precisely isolating the distinction between reason-content and reason-motivation, applying it rigorously to the synthetic-agent case, and identifying at least one hidden assumption that determines whether the analogy succeeds or fails. The scheme should also name the typical mistakes that should reduce marks, such as treating the absence of emotion as automatically decisive, or asserting that programming determines the answer without argument. Do not include any request for bibliographies, reading lists, or named citations, and avoid framing the task in a way that depends on knowledge of obscure recent literature.
