As a novelist constructing a philosophical dialogue for a novel set in a declining maritime republic, where unwritten salvage laws persist despite no living citizen remembering their origin, I have become preoccupied with David Lewis's analysis of convention and its limits. I ask you to argue for the following position: Lewis's account of convention as a behavioral regularity sustained by mutual expectations and common knowledge of rationality fails to explain normative persistence in populations characterized by asymmetric memory decay—conditions in which different agents retain disjoint, non-overlapping fragments of the historical path that established the equilibrium, so that no individual holds the full precedent in working memory and the required common knowledge dissolves into distributed, partial awareness. Specifically, defend the claim that under such decay, conventions should be reconceived not as globally coordinated solutions but as "patchwork equilibria" maintained by local repair mechanisms—micro-adjustments made by agents who recognize only adjacent deviations rather than the global regularity. Your argument should explain why the standard Lewisian reply—that conventions require only conditional preferences and mutual expectations, not explicit historical recall—cannot account for the binding force of obligation when agents lack shared access to the equilibrium's origin, and it should clarify what is conceptually gained, and what explanatory power is sacrificed, by treating precedent as a distributed rather than shared object. Please do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; work entirely within the conceptual framework described above.
