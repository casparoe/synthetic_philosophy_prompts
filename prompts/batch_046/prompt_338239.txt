I am working through notes for an essay comparing moral learning in Plutarch's comparative biographies and late antique Christian hagiography, and I would like your critical comments on the argument's coherence, its accuracy with respect to both genres, and whether its central conceptual distinction actually carries explanatory weight. The core claim is that the two traditions manage reader identification in opposite ways. Plutarch pairs figures across Greek and Roman lines so that admiration for any one subject is constantly interrupted by comparison with its counterpart; I describe this as comparative suspension, a state in which practical judgment is trained through unresolved tension rather than through emulation. Hagiography, by contrast, typically isolates a single figure and encourages full narrative identification, only to rupture that identification through miraculous events—bodily incorruptibility, posthumous visions, healings that violate natural law—leaving the reader in what I call suspended identification: emotional attachment persists, but the object of attachment has become morally and psychologically opaque within ordinary human categories. I further propose that both genres generate moral afterimages—durable evaluative impressions that remain after specific narrative details are forgotten—but that Plutarch produces a structural, comparative afterimage, whereas hagiography produces a singular, disruptive one. The essay does not aim to cover all biography or all narrative moral education, but it does claim that this difference explains something specific about how each tradition cultivates practical judgment: Plutarch trains the reader to hold exemplars in productive tension, while hagiography trains loyalty in the face of cognitive rupture. Please assess whether comparative suspension and suspended identification are genuinely different phenomena or merely variants of narrative alienation, whether moral afterimages does conceptual work beyond describing narrative impact, and whether there are clear counterexamples—hagiographic texts that systematically compare saints, or Plutarchan pairs that collapse into singular exemplarity—that would undermine the distinction.
