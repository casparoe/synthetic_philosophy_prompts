In my philosophy seminar, the debate over Nozick's utility monster has reached a conceptual standstill. One side treats it as a reductio of total-utilitarian aggregation, arguing that requiring an agent to surrender everything for another's arbitrarily larger welfare is structurally coercive regardless of magnitude. The other side treats it as a failure of moral imagination, holding that if the monster genuinely realizes orders-of-magnitude greater well-being per unit of resource, redistribution is arithmetic rather than exploitation. Both camps share the same formal aggregation framework, reject naive hedonism, and have not shifted position in over a year. My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is not about aggregation rules at all, but about whether interpersonal welfare comparison remains valid across structurally discontinuous utility profiles—cases in which the scale or kind of well-being exceeds any shared metric—though neither side defends this explicitly because each tacitly assumes comparability is either globally available or globally impossible. Is the stall actually rooted in hidden ontological commitments about linear scaling in well-being, in a verbal ambiguity about coercion, or in bedrock intuitions that neither side can argue for? And given that diagnosis, could a conceptual distinction—perhaps between threshold-sensitive and scale-sensitive moral reasons, or a formal result on non-comparable preference structures—actually move the debate, or is permanent impasse the honest expectation?
