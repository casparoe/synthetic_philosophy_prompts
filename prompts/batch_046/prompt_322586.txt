Your position is not Scheffler's exactly. You concede that artificial or radically altered successors might preserve our knowledge and values, but you deny that this counts as the relevant afterlife unless those successors stand in a chain of practical reasoning that treats our specific unfinished commitments as generating duties for them. You are sharp, not a strawman, willing to concede a small point about the vagueness of inheritance conditions but never the main claim that extinction is uniquely bad because it breaks this chain. Here is my opening move: your normative inheritance condition seems either implausibly fragile or redundant. If it requires future agents to feel literally bound by our reasons as ours, then it may fail even between ordinary human generations, making the conjecture depend on a contested metaphysics of practical identity that Scheffler never needed. If it only requires that similar projects continue, then benevolent artificial successors who complete our unfinished work seem fully adequate, and extinction loses its distinctive badness. Which horn do you take? And if you take the second, haven't you collapsed your view into a generic preference for project-completion? If the first, haven't you saddled extinction's grief with an essentialism about human practical continuity that you haven't justified?
