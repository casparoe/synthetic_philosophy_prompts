Produce the document that a temporal conventionalist and a local-chronology realist should sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration on Gödel's rotating-universe solution to Einstein's field equations and its implications for the objectivity of time. The conventionalist holds that the metric's closed timelike curves undermine not merely global clock synchronization but objective temporal passage itself, and that Gödel's philosophical remarks about the ideality of time are supported by the geometry; the realist holds that linear chronology along any observer's worldline remains intact, that global circularity is either a coordinate artifact or physically unrealizable, and that Gödel's metaphysical conclusions overreach the physics.

Structure the output exactly as follows.

First, a numbered section of agreed claims, each precisely worded so that neither party can later claim hidden reservations. Cover: the formal properties of the Gödel metric, including the existence of closed timelike curves, the absence of a global Cauchy surface, and the uniform rotation of matter relative to local inertial frames; Gödel's own interpretation of these results regarding the status of time; and the standard physical objections that render the model cosmologically unrealistic, such as cosmic expansion, observed isotropy, and energy-condition violations. Formulate these without invoking obscure post-1990 technical literature; keep them grounded in the 1949 solution and widely accepted features of relativistic cosmology.

Second, a disagreements table with three columns: the disputed issue; the conventionalist's position in wording that side accepts; and the realist's position in wording that side accepts. Include at minimum: whether an uninstantiated mathematical model can yield metaphysical conclusions about the A-theory of time; whether local linear chronology is sufficient to preserve objective temporal passage or is a question-begging retreat; and whether Gödel's geometry collapses the distinction between chronometric conventionalism, on which all temporal metrics are coordinate artifacts, and causal conventionalism, on which only worldline causal structure is physically real, or whether that distinction remains robust.

Third, identify the cruxes: the two or three sub-questions such that resolving them would move at least one party. One crux should concern whether a cosmological solution can be metaphysically decisive if it conflicts with observed boundary conditions. Another should concern whether closed timelike curves generate logical contradictions in the ontology of events or only in global synchronization schemes.

Fourth, for each crux specify one concrete argument, thought experiment, or physical consideration that both sides agree bears on it, and record in advance what each side predicts the evaluation will show.

In drafting, inject the conceptual distinction between chronometric conventionalism and causal conventionalism as an explicit framing device that both parties must address. Do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Do not include verbatim quotations from any text; paraphrase all references to Gödel's philosophical remarks without quotation marks. Ensure that every formulation of disagreement is neutral enough that neither camp can dismiss it as a straw man, and that the document reads as a constructive text both parties could put their names to.
