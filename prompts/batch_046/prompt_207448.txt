I'm trying to determine what I actually think about suffering under a pan-sentient or panpsychist framework—specifically, which organizational and physical conditions turn minimal fundamental experience into ethically significant suffering. Here are my gut verdicts across cases, and I want to know whether any coherent principle captures them all, or whether I must abandon one or two.

First, a single electron in a ground state: if consciousness is truly fundamental, it may possess some bare experiential quality, but I do not think it suffers. There is no valence, no integrated dynamics, and no structure that could support harm. Second, a high-fidelity silicon simulation of a human brain undergoing acute pain: suffering is genuinely present. The substrate differs, but the functional architecture—integrated feedback loops, representational content about damage, unified information processing—seems to be what matters. Third, a thermostat maintaining room temperature: clearly no suffering, despite having feedback, deviation-correction, and a kind of goal-directed dynamics; the organization is too impoverished and there is no subject-boundary. Fourth, a biological human brain in an identical pain state: suffering is present, and I take it to be the same phenomenon in kind as in the simulation, though perhaps with a different qualitative texture.

Fifth, a crystal lattice at thermal equilibrium, with phonons vibrating in disordered patterns across countless constituent particles: despite being composed of entities that might each carry minimal experience, I think there is no collective suffering. The disorder and lack of integration seem to prevent the formation of a unified experiential subject. Sixth, two human brains temporarily linked via a direct cortical bridge, sharing sensory and affective states in real time: I think they constitute a single suffering subject. If one is injured, both suffer, but not as two separate individuals each experiencing half the harm. Seventh, a black hole actively accreting matter near its event horizon, with extreme tidal gradients, massive entropy production, and complex correlations in Hawking radiation: I am genuinely torn. There is enormous physical complexity and information dynamics, yet attributing suffering to it feels like a category error—there is no organism, no evolutionary history, and no clear boundary of a subject. I lean slightly toward denying ethically relevant suffering, but I feel the opposing pull strongly.

Is there a principle—perhaps invoking integrated information, functional valence-structure, subject-boundary conditions, or substrate-independent organizational thresholds—that can accommodate all seven verdicts? If not, which verdict should I drop: my substrate-independence in the simulation case, my denial of collective suffering in the crystal, or my ambivalence about the black hole? And what does the best available principle predict for a case I have not considered: a planet-scale atmospheric system with complex feedback loops, information processing in storm dynamics, and no biological components whatsoever?
