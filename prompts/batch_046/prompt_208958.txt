As a journalist who covers the philosophy of science and public reasoning, I have been examining the way advocates of cause prioritization—particularly those working with importance, tractability, and neglectedness—routinely invoke emergency-room triage as their governing analogy. The physician who bypasses a minor wound to stanch a hemorrhage is said to model the rational allocator who ignores emotionally accessible suffering to maximize expected impact. I want a rigorous audit of this analogy rather than a casual endorsement. Please provide an explicit correspondence table: what plays the role of the triage officer, the vital signs, the time constraint, the ethical duty not to abandon, and the institutional protocol? Then map the disanalogies that matter for the framework's intended use—for instance, whether neglectedness genuinely corresponds to an empty treatment bay or rather to a hidden queue shaped by structural neglect; whether the single-metric urgency of trauma medicine can be translated to long-horizon, probabilistic interventions without distortion; and whether the analogy quietly imports a consequentialist calculus that real triage ethics often reject in favor of procedural fairness. I also want you to address a less common extension I have been exploring in my reporting: the notion of intertemporal triage, in which future beneficiaries are treated as patients in a remote ward whose conditions have not yet manifested. Does this extension reveal a feature the original analogy suppresses, or does it collapse under the weight of epistemic distance and moral-status ambiguity? Finally, assess whether the conclusion that rational agents ought to be emotionally detached, quantitative allocators survives once these disanalogies and the intertemporal extension are fully on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying the normative load all along. If a better analogy exists for this kind of reasoning, propose it.
