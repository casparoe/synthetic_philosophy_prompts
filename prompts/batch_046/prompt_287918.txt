As a novelist long accustomed to calibrating the distance between a narrator and a character's consciousness—particularly through free indirect discourse—I have been drafting an essay that treats Adam Smith's account of sympathy and the impartial spectator as a form of distributed narrative technology rather than a strictly juridical mechanism. In my notes, I propose that the spectator functions analogously to an implied narrative consciousness within a community: sympathy operates like a reader's temporary inhabitation of another's mental state, maintaining evaluative distance without full identification. I further suggest that the internal figure Smith describes—the spectator within—acts less like a magistrate delivering verdicts and more like a focalizer that shifts its position according to the density and proximity of social relations, which might explain why moral judgment feels simultaneously personal and externally anchored. I worry, however, that importing concepts such as focalization or the implied author risks anachronistically imposing twentieth-century literary theory onto Smith's eighteenth-century moral psychology, and that my framing may conflate the phenomenology of reading fiction with the practical dynamics of moral approval.

Could you comment on these draft notes, addressing both their interpretive accuracy and their conceptual coherence? I am particularly interested in whether Smith's writings on sympathy genuinely accommodate a narratological parallel, whether the distinction between judgment-without-possession and the spectator's evaluative authority survives scrutiny, and whether this approach offers genuine illumination or merely dresses a familiar psychological account in aesthetic language. In your response, please separate what is broadly settled among scholars of Smith's moral philosophy from what remains genuinely contested—especially regarding the spectator's precise function, the relationship between sympathy and moral judgment, and whether the internal spectator should be understood as representational, regulatory, or relational—and please avoid presenting any contested interpretive position as if it were the consensus view.
