You direct a forensic perceptual-analysis unit that certifies visual-evidence reports for legal proceedings. Your examiners record what an observer sees under standard, magnified, and controlled-contrast conditions, but reports are often edited to substitute descriptions—"a crack in the varnish" for "what looks like a crack," "the pigment" for "the red region"—before submission. Write a reusable ten-minute procedure, grounded in disjunctivism about perception (the view that veridical seeing and a subjectively matching hallucination are not tokens of a shared mental kind, so substituting descriptions can alter the report's ontological commitment), that your editors can apply report by report. Structure it as ordered tests: first determine whether the original commits to a specific perceptual relation (seeing-that versus seeming-to-see); then check whether the proposed substitution preserves, collapses, or inverts that commitment; then assess whether the change shifts the evidentiary weight in a legally material way. Provide three worked cases—one safe substitution, one where the change favors the submitting party in a misleading way, and one degraded-viewing case where the procedure yields an answer you will dislike. Mark which steps are irreducible judgment calls, state the required sign-off for those, and specify exactly what the procedure cannot settle, especially regarding the argument from illusion's bearing on reports made under conditions that could induce misperception.
