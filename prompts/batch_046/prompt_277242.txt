I'm moderating a colloquium Q&A with a political theorist who defends a conceptual framework she calls "deliberative closure" as the defining feature of democratic backsliding. On her view, backsliding is not best captured by institutional deterioration metrics—such as those used by cross-national democracy indexes—but by a progressive, institutionally enforced narrowing of the range of public reasons that citizens can invoke without facing formal sanctions or structured informal penalties. She applies this framework to Hungary, Poland, Turkey, and the United States, arguing that Hungary and Poland exhibit closure primarily at the legislative and media-access levels; that Turkey combines closure with executive personalization, producing a distinct but related pathology; and that the United States, despite severe polarization, lacks comparable federal-level closure because institutional penalties for invoking contested reasons remain geographically and sectorally localized rather than nationally systemic. Draft eight to ten interview questions that move well beyond asking her to restate the framework. I want at least one question pressing the "who decides" problem—specifically, how to distinguish genuine deliberative closure from a situation in which a particular faction has merely lost democratic argument—together with a follow-up keyed to her likely reply that closure must be operationalized through observable penalty structures rather than through subjective perceptions of silencing. I want at least one question asking whether the framework risks becoming a partisan label, given that competing US factions each accuse the other of closing deliberation, with a follow-up anticipating her appeal to cross-ideological procedural benchmarks and asking how she avoids circularity in selecting those benchmarks. I want at least one comparative question on Hungary versus Turkey that presses whether executive personalization makes Turkish closure qualitatively different or only more severe, followed by a follow-up asking whether conceding a qualitative difference undermines the unity of her central concept. Finally, include a question about what empirical pattern would count against her claim that the US lacks federal-level closure—anticipating her likely move of citing state-level variation, and then asking whether that very variation is compatible with her insistence on a single national-level assessment.
