As a physicist who spent thirty years in precision measurement and only began studying philosophy in retirement, I have come to think that moral disagreement is structurally closer to systematic error than to taste. I am organizing a workshop for an advanced philosophy reading group—fourteen participants, one ninety-minute session—and I want them to experience, not merely discuss, the tension between German folk-moral rule-following and American folk-moral conscience, specifically through a distinction I will call normative visibility: the degree to which an outsider can reconstruct the reasoning behind a decision without access to the agent’s private mental state. The guiding idea is that the commitment to Ordnung is not authoritarian rigidity but a preservation of intersubjective transparency, while the appeal to Gewissen introduces an asymmetry between ex ante rule-acceptance and ex post exemption that erodes collective moral learning.

Design a structured in-class game with explicit rules, assigned roles, paper-token payoffs, and a debrief. The setting should be a concrete institutional task: distributing a fixed pool of resources among competing claims where every claim has merit, but a procedural protocol defines priority. In Round One, participants operate under strict procedural fidelity—Regulators apply rules, Practitioners execute them, Affected Parties receive allocations, and Observers document reasoning in shared view. The group earns institutional-trust tokens only when every decision is publicly reconstructible. In Round Two, the exception regime is introduced: any Practitioner may invoke private conscience to override the protocol, with no disclosure obligation and with private moral-satisfaction tokens awarded instead. Use the same sequence of cases in both rounds so the group can compare outcomes.

Specify: (1) the four roles and any rotation rules; (2) a timeline dividing the ninety minutes; (3) the token economy—how institutional-trust and moral-satisfaction points are gained, lost, or traded, and how they map onto coordination success or failure; (4) the specific case sequence—at least three scenarios—arranged so that Round Two produces not mere inefficiency but epistemic opacity, where Observers can no longer distinguish justified deviation from self-interest; (5) a debrief that maps each round onto normative visibility, onto the folk-moral contrast, and onto the claim that conscience-based exception undermines second-order agreement about who has standing to override; (6) an honest statement of what the activity cannot reproduce—such as the historical religious and civic formation of conscience in specific national contexts—and how the facilitator must supply that background verbally; (7) a prediction of what will predictably go wrong the first time the game is run, for instance participants collapsing the distinction into bureaucracy versus freedom, and how to correct it without destroying the experiential force.

Frame the exercise in the weeds: turn on the asymmetry between first-order moral disagreement and second-order disagreement about override authority, and arrange the incentives so participants feel that the apparent moral superiority of conscience collapses once moral disagreement is modeled as a coordination problem with asymmetric information rather than a failure of virtue. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citation request; do not ask participants to quote or reference specific texts. Write in a formal, polished register appropriate to a retired scientist addressing fellow scholars.
