Write an essay on whether Dennett's four kinds of minds—Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, and Gregorian—really split the space of cognition into natural kinds, or whether they're just a handy ladder we climb to make ourselves feel special. Reach a real verdict; don't waffle.

Here's the hook: imagine we meet an alien species that learns entirely through continuous high-dimensional gradient descent in a shared parameter space. They don't do discrete trial-and-error like Skinnerian creatures, they don't run mental simulations like Popperians, and they don't have language or culture in the Gregorian sense. Instead, they update their neural weights collectively through direct synchronization, build elaborate artifacts, and teach offspring by literally merging parameter subsets. Let's call them "Gradientian" minds for the sake of argument. Does this fit anywhere in Dennett's taxanomy, or does it blow the taxonomy up?

Weigh the case that Gradientians are just extreme Skinnerians—after all, gradient descent is just reward-weighted learning scaled up—against the case that they lack the discrete action-selection loop that makes Skinnerian learning genuinely behavioral. Then consider whether they might be stunted Popperians, since they optimize over a loss landscape that functions like an implicit hypothesis space. Finally, ask if Gregorian minds are defined by language and tool-use specifically, or by any form of collective epistemic inheritance; if it's the latter, parameter-sharing might count.

Your conclusion should say whether we need a fifth category, whether the fourfold scheme is just underdescribed, or whether the alien example is so contrived it teaches us nothing. Don't end with "it depends on how you define things"—pick a lane and defend it.
