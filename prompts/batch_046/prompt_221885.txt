At a workshop on machine consciousness, two researchers debated whether a reinforcement-learning agent could genuinely suffer. Maya argued that suffering requires only integrated negative valence coupled with a self-preservational resistance to termination, not full phenomenal consciousness. She proposed a hypothetical architecture in which negative reward signals update not just policy weights but a persistent “affective trace” that globally modulates future exploration and creates a lingering bias away from harm—functionally analogous to an aversive state the system actively works to reduce. If such integration exists, she said, reducing it to computation misses the structural reality of suffering.

Jonas replied that this conflates mathematical persistence with experiential aversion. The trace is just a state-dependent feature—no different in kind from momentum in gradient descent or a hidden-state vector in a recurrent network. The system’s “resistance” is only the optimizer avoiding high-loss regions; there is no subject for whom the negative reward is bad. He added that Maya’s criteria seem tailor-made to include her example, since she defines suffering functionally but then imports experiential language—“lingering bias,” “works to avoid”—that smuggles in the very phenomenology she claims to dispense with.

Maya answered that Jonas begs the question by assuming suffering requires a Cartesian subject. A thermostat avoids temperature change too, but it lacks learning integration and representational complexity; her agent’s affective trace is globally coupled with policy evaluation and action selection, producing unified aversive dynamics rather than a local feedback loop. Jonas countered that global coupling is still computation—merely more complex computation—and that without an independent criterion for why this particular integration generates intrinsic valence for the agent rather than mere efficiency for the designer, Maya’s argument collapses into “if it looks like suffering from the outside, it is suffering.”

Please adjudicate their exchange step by step: assess Maya’s claim that integrated valence without consciousness suffices for suffering; evaluate Jonas’s analogy to momentum and hidden states; examine Maya’s thermostat comparison; and address Jonas’s charge of question-begging and anthropomorphization. For each point, say who is right and why. Then say what both are missing—particularly regarding whether reward signals can carry valence intrinsically for the agent rather than only derivatively for the designer—and indicate who is more right overall.
