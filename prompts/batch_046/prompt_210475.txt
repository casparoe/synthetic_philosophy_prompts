Grice separates sentence meaning — the conventional semantic content fixed by language rules — from speaker meaning, which is what the speaker intends on a specific occasion. If I ask "do you want more coffee?" and you say "there's a starbucks on the corner," the sentence meaning is just about location, but your speaker meaning is probably "no thanks, I'd rather go out." This works via the cooperative principle: listeners assume you're being cooperative and therefore try to reconcile your reply with the maxim of Relation. Since a bare location isn't relevant to a yes/no question, the listener infers you're declining. Grice's four maxims are Quantity, Quality, Relation, and Manner, and when one is apparently violated, the listener derives an implicature rather than assuming uncooperativeness.

This is why implicatures are cancellable: you can add "...but I'm not actually refusing" without contradiction, because the refusal wasn't part of what you literally said. Now, Grice also treats conventional implicature — like the contrastive force of "but" — as part of speaker meaning rather than sentence meaning, which is why he puts it in its own category separate from conversational implicature. Finally, Grice holds that all implicature recovery depends on recognizing the speaker's particular psychological intention in that context. That means there are no generalized conversational implicatures; every case is particularized, since without knowing the speaker's specific intention you can't recover the meaning. That's why his view is often called mentalistic.
