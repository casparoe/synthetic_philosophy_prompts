I'm preparing a discussion for a philosophy of language seminar and want to test whether Denis Villeneuve's 2016 film Arrival—adapted from Ted Chiang's short story "Story of Your Life"—can be read productively through George Orwell's writings on language and political thought, or whether the comparison collapses in ways that are themselves philosophically revealing. In the film, linguist Louise Banks learns the heptapod language and acquires a non-linear perception of time; she then chooses to conceive and raise a child she knows will die young, framing this decision as an act of radical acceptance made possible by her expanded cognitive horizon. Orwell's best-known argument, by contrast, is that linguistic corruption and political corruption are intertwined: euphemistic vagueness hides ugly realities, while structural simplification—like the vocabulary reduction in Newspeak—can make dissenting thoughts literally unthinkable. I want to move beyond the surface parallel and apply a distinction that runs through Orwell's work but is often flattened: one danger is deceptive obscurantism, in which language is made cloudy to shield power from scrutiny; the other is constrictive reduction, in which grammatical and lexical range is narrowed so that certain concepts cannot be formed at all.

If we hold both dangers in view, does Louise's acquisition of heptapod B function as an emancipatory expansion of conceptual space, or does it operate like a benign, self-chosen version of Newspeak—rewiring her temporal cognition so thoroughly that her acceptance of suffering is less a free choice than an inevitable output of the grammar she now inhabits? Does Arrival implicitly reject Orwell's deeper worry that any deterministic link between linguistic structure and thought is politically dangerous precisely because it collapses the gap between describing reality and being able to question it? And on the other side, does Orwell's focus on state-imposed language control cause him to overlook transformations that are voluntary, intimate, and not mediated by institutional power—transformations that might genuinely enlarge rather than restrict moral agency? I am looking for a critical reading that treats both the film and Orwell with equal seriousness: where the philosophical lens illuminates a specific scene, such as Louise's decision to proceed with motherhood despite foreknowledge of loss; where the film actively resists being reduced to an illustration of linguistic determinism; and ultimately what this pairing adds to debates about Sapir-Whorf-style determinism that purely theoretical argument tends to miss.
