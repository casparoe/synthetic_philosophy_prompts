I was in a reding group on philosophy of law last week and we kept circling the Hart-Devlin debate without really resolving anything. One person kept summarizing Devlin as saying society has a shared moral code and the law should protect it like an immune system — if you tolerate too much private immorality the whole social fabric starts to unravel, even when no specific person gets hurt. But then another person said that's not quite Devlin, it's more like a specific kind of disintegration argument, and I started wondering if there's a tighter version that nobody named.

So I came up with this idea I'm calling "threshold moralism": the law should enforce a moral norm only when it's in a fragile equilibrium, where private violations wouldn't just be tolerated but would actually trigger a rapid collapse in compliance — kind of like a bank run but for norms. Compare this with Devlin's view. Is threshold moralism just Devlin with an extra fragility condition, or does the condition change the argument fundamentally? Specifically, Hart criticized Devlin for treating popular disgust as empirical evidence and smuggling in conservative bias; if threshold moralism requires actual proof that a norm is unstable, does that dodge Hart's objection, or is it still moralism with a thermometer? And where exactly does Hart's harm principle draw the line — can it cover indirect social harms that are predictable, or does harm have to be direct? I'm trying to figure out if threshold moralism is a real third position or just Devlin in a lab coat, and whether Hart's framework could even recognize the kind of harm it points to.
