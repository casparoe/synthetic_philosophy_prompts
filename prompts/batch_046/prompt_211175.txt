i was arguing with a friend last night about whether there's really a "special composition question" at all, and i ended up defending this weird hybrid view: call it framework-relative pluralism about parts and wholes. the idea is that whether some parts compose a genuine object isn't settled by mind-independent metaphysical facts but by which theoretical framework we're working in — physics treats planets as composed of atoms, ecology treats a wetland as composed of organisms and water cycles, and there's no single correct answer that applies across all domains. so universalism is wrong because it forces one compositional relation everywhere, and nihilism is wrong because it denies all of them; my view says there are many legitimate compositional relations, each tied to a different explanatory purpose. but i'm worried this just dissolves the problem by being vague or unfalsifiable, or that it hides some existantial commitment i haven't noticed. so what would actually count against it? not the usual "but then tables don't exist" gotcha — i'm thinking of serious defeaters: maybe empirical results that show we need one unified ontology of physical objects, formal arguments in mereology that collapse pluralism back into restricted universalism, or conceptual pressure showing that "framework-relative" can't apply to concrete physical objects without sliding into idealism. rank these from fatal to merely awkward. which ones would someone holding my view have to concede as damaging, and which could they brush off as missing the point? also, is there a hidden commitment i'm not seeing — maybe to some version of Carnapian tolerance or to anti-realism about scientific ontology — that makes this view stand or fall with debates i haven't signed up for?
