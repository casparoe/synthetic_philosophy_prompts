I am taking a graduate seminar on AI alignment and safety, and after a long discussion with my study group about whether “low-impact” agents can be designed before anyone solves value alignment, I found myself stuck in an argument that seems to prove the concept is incoherent. I want to check whether I am missing an exit, and I would prefer to keep the reasoning mostly intact if possible. Here is the chain as I have reconstructed it. (1) To call an outcome a “side effect” rather than an intended effect, we need a prior boundary that separates the causal pathway we designed the system to influence from the rest of the world we want left alone. (2) Drawing that boundary requires specifying which states or variables count as “background” that ought to be invariant, and that specification is nothing other than a partial or complete value judgment about what should be preserved. (3) Therefore, defining “avoid side effects” already presupposes a value specification—if not fully explicit, then at least implicit in the choice of invariant set. (4) A defender of low-impact design might answer that we can draw the boundary structurally, without invoking values: we simply trace the physical causal chain that connects the agent’s action to the reward or goal signal, and label everything outside that chain as a side effect. (5) But the causal graph we trace is never given by nature alone; it depends on which variables we include in our model, and the decision to include some variables and exclude others is guided by what we want to explain, predict, or protect—which is again a value-laden choice. (6) So the structural defense does not escape the need for value specification; it merely relocates it from the boundary-drawing stage to the model-selection stage. (7) From (3) and (6) together, it follows that “avoid side effects” is either undefined or arbitrarily defined unless we have already solved the full value-specification problem. (8) But the point of low-impact AI is precisely to behave safely in domains where value specification is incomplete or contested, so the concept is self-defeating. I would especially like to hold onto (4), because it feels like physics should give us an objective footing independent of ethics, but I also want to keep (8) from being true because it makes the entire research direction collapse.
