I'm moderating a podcast interview with a philosopher—call her Dr. Voss—who defends the strong impossibility thesis that literal self-deception is conceptually incoherent: it requires one and the same subject to fully believe p and fully believe not-p while being aware of both. I need eight to ten interview questions that go well beyond restating the view. For at least three, anticipate the standard reply she is likely to give and include a targeted follow-up that makes that reply do more work.

Specifically, include: (1) a question pressing whether her rejection of epistemic-partition models—treating the mind as containing access-restricted compartments that can hold contradictory contents without destroying personal unity—is driven by a normative claim about identity or an empirical claim about cognitive architecture, with a follow-up keyed to her likely answer that such partitions violate the transparency of first-person authority; (2) a question on strategic ignorance (deliberately avoiding evidence) asking whether she collapses this into self-deception or keeps them sharply separate, with a follow-up that asks how she accounts for the phenomenology of surprise when the ignored fact finally breaks through; (3) a question about whether self-deceptive emotional postures—behaving as if grieving while feeling nothing—can be explained without contradictory beliefs, and what her view implies for therapeutic practice if such postures are conceptually impossible rather than merely hidden; (4) a closing question asking what result from predictive-processing neuroscience—say, evidence that the brain routinely maintains incompatible generative models at different hierarchical levels—would count against her conceptual argument, and why she thinks conceptual incoherence survives empirical pluralism.

Keep the tone colloquial but precise, suitable for an audience that knows basic philosophy of mind but not the specialist literature. Do not ask for citations, reading lists, or biographical background.
