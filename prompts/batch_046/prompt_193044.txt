I've been having these long chats with my mates about whether there's really anything wrong with selling your kidney, or charging fifty quid for bottled water right after a flood, or even flogging your vote to the highest bidder when you're skint. One of them keeps saying it's all just supply and demand, another says it's gross but can't pin down why, and I'm stuck in the middle trying to work out what a proper theory about where markets should stop would actually need to cover before anyone can claim they've won the argument.

So here's what I'm after, and I want it all in continuous prose like an email — no bullet points, no bold, no headings, no tables. Lay out the full list of requirements such a theory must meet. It needs to handle kidney sales, emergency price gouging, and vote selling properly, but it can't just say they all feel wrong for the same reason because I reckon the reasons are different. It should keep corruption — where putting a price on something actually changes what that thing is — separate from coercion, where people only agree because they're desperate, without collapsng them together. There's also that notion of plural value, that some goods like citizenship or bodily health can be valued in ways that don't convert into money, and the theory needs to account for that rather than brushing it aside. Tell me which of these are hard, non-negotiable constraints and which are just prejudices from people who already hate markets. I especially want to know if some subset is jointly unsatisfiable — maybe protecting autonomy always lets in exploitation, or stopping exploitation always restricts freedom — and you should say so plainly if that's the case. Take a definite position on which single requirement does the most damage to the "everything should be priced" view, and whether you think the whole list can actually be met at once. Don't sit on the fence listing options; commit to what you think and explain why.
