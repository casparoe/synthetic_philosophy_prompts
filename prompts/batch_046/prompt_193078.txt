My intuitions about origin essentialism hold up in ordinary cases but collapse into tension once I push past them, and I want to know whether one principle can save the bulk of my verdicts or whether I have to surrender one.

Here are my case judgments, in order. If my father had contributed a different sperm, or my mother a different egg, the resulting child is not me but a sibling: the particular nuclear gametes seem essential to my origin. If those exact gametes form a zygote that splits at the two-cell stage, and only one half is implanted and develops, I do not think the unimplanted half is me — it is a twin that never came to exist, not an alternative version of the same person. But if both halves are implanted, I treat them as ordinary twins: neither is "more me" than the other.

Now cases that strain a simple gamete-criterion. In mitochondrial replacement therapy, the nuclear genome comes from my parents' sperm and egg, but the mitochondria come from a donor. I still think the child is me; foreign organelles do not break origin identity. Yet if scientists synthesize gametes carrying my parents' exact nuclear genomes years later, fertilize them, and implant the result, that child is not me — it is a late clone or sibling, despite perfect nuclear genetic identity. So genetic sameness is not sufficient.

What about divergence without genetic change? If the zygote formed from my actual gametes is cultured in vitro and a temperature fluctuation alters early epigenetic activation, yielding someone with the same genome but a markedly different temperament, I lean that this is still me, though less confidently than in the mitochondrial case. Likewise, if the embryo is implanted in a different surrogate with a different uterine environment, producing the same genome but different prenatal conditions, I think that is me.

Chimerism, however, breaks the pattern. Suppose two embryos — one formed from my parents' gametes, one from strangers' — fuse in utero. The resulting adult carries cells with both genomes. Is that fused adult me, partly me, or an entirely different individual? My intuition wavers: I resist saying I am the fused person, but I also resist saying I am only the subset of cells carrying my parents' DNA, since personal identity does not seem to fragment by tissue.

So my verdicts are: different nuclear gametes exclude identity; same nuclear gametes with donor mitochondria preserve it; identical genome with synthetic origin at a different time excludes it; identical genome with altered early development or different gestational environment preserves it; fusion with an independent embryo makes identity unclear.

Can these be unified by a principle about the causal-historical continuity of a specific developmental trajectory initiated by those gametes, where "trajectory" permits some environmental and epigenetic variation and spatiotemporal relocation but requires non-branching continuity and excludes both later replication and fusion with an independent trajectory? Or do I need two principles — one for genetic origin, one for spatiotemporal path — and must drop one verdict?

Please state the principle or principles precisely, identify which clause is motivated by which case, and apply them to the unimplanted twin half, the synthetic clone, and the chimeric fusion. Tell me honestly which of my judgments the principle cannot accommodate. Also predict the result for a case I have not listed: my parents conceive naturally, but a rare uterine anomaly causes the embryo to divide, with only one portion implanting and developing while the rest is absorbed — is the absorbed tissue "me," and does the answer depend on whether the division occurs at the two-cell stage versus the blastocyst stage?
