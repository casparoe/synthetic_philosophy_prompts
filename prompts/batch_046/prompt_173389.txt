I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, though I've been reading some philosophy of mind in my spare time. I've become interested in the family of views usually called higher-order theories of consciousness — the idea, roughly, that a mental state is conscious only if there is another mental state directed upon it. What fascinates me is not the basic claim but a structural problem that arises when you model it in layers: if every conscious state requires a higher-order observer, the stack either continues indefinitely or terminates at some level that is conscious without being observed. In distributed systems we face something analogous with monitoring loops — a service watches another service, but nothing watches the watcher — and I want a group of people to feel physically why terminating that loop at any particular point feels like an arbitrary design choice rather than a natural endpoint.

Please design a runnable workshop activity for about 14 participants, lasting roughly 90 minutes, suitable for an interdisciplinary reading group with no prior philosophy training required. The deliverable should be a structured simulation or role-play, plus a debrief. The rules, roles, and payoffs must be arranged so that participants build the observation hierarchy themselves: assign them to observe and report on one another's reports, letting the stack grow round by round. They should discover the regress through the mechanics of the game, not through a facilitator's explanation. At a forced-choice moment, require the group to place a single "ground-level" state that needs no further observer, and make them vote or negotiate on where that floor sits and what justifies its special status. They should come away seeing that any termination point is stipulative, that self-observation collapses the distinction they have been maintaining, and that the infinite stack is no more satisfying.

In the debrief, map each round onto parts of the theoretical problem — first-order states, higher-order observations, the regress, and the termination problem — and name the idea only after the mapping is complete. Be explicit about which feature of the philosophical debate the activity cannot reproduce, such as the qualitative feel of first-person experience, and state how the facilitator must supply that gap verbally. Also predict what will go wrong in practice — for instance, participants may propose that the bottom layer observes itself — and explain how to handle that move without breaking the simulation. Present the design in clear sections: setup and rules, round-by-round sequence, the forced-choice moment, and the debrief. Write the response in British English, and do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
