I am drafting a novel about a neuroscientist who undergoes gradual cerebral replacement by synthetic substrate, and in preparing the philosophical architecture of the plot I have tried to articulate my own commitments about personal identity and consciousness, partly informed by Dennett's brain-transplant scenario. At a recent writers' residency, a philosopher friend pressed me on whether these commitments actually cohere, and I realized the narrative logic of the book depends on resolving the tension rather than glossing it. Here is my profile: first, I take Dennett's "Where Am I?" to show that personal identity is anchored not in a specific biological brain but in the continuity of a narrative center of gravity; second, I hold that perfect informational transfer of that narrative to a non-biological substrate preserves identity, provided there is no branching; third, I maintain that if branching occurs—if two equally continuous narrative streams emerge—neither can claim to be the original person, since uniqueness is a condition of identity; fourth, I insist that consciousness is irreducibly first-personal, that there is genuinely something it is like to be the subject of this narrative, even if that subject is substrate-independent; and fifth, for the fiction, I treat consciousness as distributed across a network of synthetic nodes that collectively sustain one narrative thread, with no single node functioning as the brain.

Go through these commitments and tell me which pairs are in genuine tension and which only appear to be. For each real tension, assess its severity, propose the cheapest revision that restores consistency, and state what that revision costs the novel's conceptual framework. Identify the single change that would clean up the most conflicts at once. Do not lay out options neutrally; take a definite position and defend the specific adjustments you think are necessary. Please write in American English.
