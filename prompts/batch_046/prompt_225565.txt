I'm delivering the keynote address at the retirement symposium for Dr. Elena Voss, who spent thirty years writing biographies that deliberately refused both hagiography and sensationalist debunking. The room is split: half are historians and philosophers of history who know her work on Plutarch's Parallel Lives and the ethics of exemplarity, and half are family and former students who have never read Plutarch and do not share the disciplinary vocabulary. I need to honor her career without turning it into a saint's life or a clinical case study, and without pretending that her subjects were uncomplicated role models. The speech should take as its structural spine the tension between Plutarchan moral learning—where biography teaches virtue through emulation, offering great lives as patterns to imitate—and the modern critical insistence that such emulation is dangerous mythmaking. But rather than choosing sides, I want to articulate a third framing that captures Dr. Voss's actual method: biography as moral topography, not providing heroes to copy or villains to expose, but mapping the inseparable terrain of virtue and failure under specific historical constraints, so that the reader's task is neither worship nor contempt but a disciplined attention to how a life takes shape. This idea must do real work in the speech: it should explain to her colleagues why her refusal to sanitize subjects is not cynicism but a form of respect, and it should make clear to her family why she spent her life writing about flawed people without ever reducing them to their flaws. The address should be about five minutes, should include one concrete example drawn from her best-known biography, and should conclude that her greatest contribution was not revealing hidden greatness or hidden corruption, but preserving the complexity that both hagiography and debunking destroy.
