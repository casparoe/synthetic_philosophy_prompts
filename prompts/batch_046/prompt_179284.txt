For the past several weeks, since a close family member faced a treatment decision involving an open-ended sequence of potential outcomes with both catastrophic losses and substantial gains distributed across an indefinite timeline, I have been trying to understand whether formal decision theory offers any genuine guidance when expectation itself collapses rather than merely exploding. The clinical analog is inexact, but it has directed my attention to a structural feature of certain games that I find more conceptually troubling than the familiar St. Petersburg divergence.

Please argue that in decision problems structurally analogous to the Pasadena paradox—where expected utility is undefined not through monotonic divergence to infinity, but through the non-absolute convergence of opposing contribution series that yield an indeterminate form—rational agents ought to regard the choice situation as decisionally malformed and suspend preference ordering entirely, rather than applying dominance reasoning, arbitrary temporal truncation, or conditionalization on finite subgames. Your argument should distinguish this case, which I will call Type-II undefinedness, from Type-I undefinedness (the St. Petersburg-style unbounded growth), and should explain why treating both with the same remedial strategy—particularly dominance-based selection—commits a category error that obscures the specific normative failure involved. Further, defend the claim that horizon truncation does not resolve the paradox but substitutes a different decision problem with a different normative profile, and that suspension of judgment is therefore not a pragmatic concession but the only response consistent with the internal coherence conditions of expected utility theory itself.
