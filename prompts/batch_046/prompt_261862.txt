For my graduate seminar on Berkeley's immaterialism—fourteen students, ninety minutes—I would like you to design a structured classroom activity centered on the master argument, arranged so that participants run into the contradiction of conceiving an unperceived object before anyone names it for them. Rather than relying on the familiar tree-falling-in-forest framing, I want the exercise grounded in a somewhat niche scenario: an undeciphered clay tablet stored in a climate-controlled vault monitored solely by non-conscious sensors, with the vault scheduled for algorithmic incineration in fifty years so that, by design, no conscious being will ever form an idea of the tablet or its contents.

The activity should assign roles—materialist defenders, Berkeleyan critics, and neutral arbiters—establish rules that prevent the defenders from using perceptual or mental predicates unless they can be defined without reference to an idea or perceiver, and build in small stakes or forced collective choices so that the defenders must jointly construct a coherent description of the tablet during its unread period. Provide a clear timeline, specify the group dynamics, and include a debrief that maps specific moments onto steps in Berkeley's master argument, anticipates predictable failure modes such as appeals to causal continuity or algorithmic tracking, and frankly states which feature of Berkeley's ontology the simulation cannot reproduce—specifically, that existence just is actual perception by a spirit, not mere availability for observation, and that classroom language games can only police vocabulary rather than replicate ontological dependence. Please do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
