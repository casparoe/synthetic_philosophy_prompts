I just finished a long and somewhat heated argument with a colleague about whether invoking the separateness of persons objection against utilitarian reasoning is anything more than a rhetorical reflex in institutional ethics. We kept returning to the same impasse: the objection clearly says something about why it is wrong to justify imposing a burden on one individual solely by pointing to benefits that flow to others, yet nobody seems able to say what that claim requires of an actual decision procedure in practice. I serve as a senior review officer for a regional public-health consortium, and my board evaluates resource-allocation proposals—triage protocols, vaccination strategies, funding trade-offs between preventive and acute care—where aggregate welfare metrics are the default language but periodically feel like they treat patients as fungible containers for well-being rather than as distinct moral persons.

I need a reusable audit procedure the board can run consistently without philosophical expertise: an ordered sequence of diagnostic questions, with clear criteria for what counts as a sufficient answer at each stage, explicit stopping points where a proposal fails (indicating a separateness violation), explicit hand-off points where the remaining issue is one of values rather than institutional fact, and flagged zones where judgment cannot be eliminated despite procedural discipline. The procedure should be anchored in the conceptual core of the objection—namely, that justification cannot proceed by substituting one person's interests for another's within a single calculus, as if the boundaries between lives were permeable to welfare aggregation—while remaining operational. In particular, I want the audit to distinguish between what I will call direct aggregative substitution, where a specific burden on an identifiable person is explicitly weighed against gains to others in the same computation, and structural fungibility, where the institutional design renders persons replaceable without any explicit cross-personal comparison; my colleague insisted these demand different procedural treatments, but I am unsure whether the philosophy actually supports that bifurcation and want the procedure to either validate or collapse it. Apply the audit to three proposals: denying costly life-extending therapy to Patient A in order to fund preventive care for fifty Patients B; adopting a triage scoring system based exclusively on expected quality-adjusted life-years, with no procedural safeguard for those who bear the risk of misclassification; and deliberately exposing a small, identifiable cohort to predictable mild harm in order to secure herd immunity for a larger population. At least one application—preferably the case that will seem counterintuitive to board members trained in welfare economics—should yield a result that conflicts with the aggregate-optimal recommendation. Finally, specify exactly what the procedure cannot settle: which distinctions collapse once institutional scale and urgency are introduced, where the boundary between permissible burden-sharing and impermissible substitution depends on contested premises that no checklist can resolve, and the conditions under which the board should suspend the procedural audit and escalate to open ethical deliberation.
