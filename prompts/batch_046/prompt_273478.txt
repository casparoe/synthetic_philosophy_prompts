Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between the synchronic-coherence camp and the diachronic-commitment camp regarding Kavka's toxin puzzle. The synchronic-coherence camp holds that rational intention requires treating the intended act as one the agent will perform given her reasons at the time of action, so she cannot rationally intend today to drink tomorrow's toxin knowing she will then have overwhelming reason not to; the diachronic-commitment camp holds that present rationality can sanction an intention whose future execution is causally or structurally decoupled from future reasons, provided the present mental state secures a present reward.

Produce a formal document with the following parts:

(i) A numbered section of agreed claims covering the puzzle's formal structure, the agent's knowledge and available actions at each stage, what exactly counts as receiving the reward, and which standard decision-theoretic frameworks (causal versus evidential) each side typically invokes.

(ii) A disagreements table presenting each camp's own formulation of its position in wording its members would accept, paired with the precise formulation it rejects from the opposing camp.

(iii) Three cruxes such that settling them would move at least one side: (a) whether rational intention must remain reason-responsive at the moment of action; (b) whether there is a genuine distinction between intending to drink and causing oneself to drink; (c) whether the puzzle demonstrates a flaw in evidential decision theory or merely reveals a psychological limit on self-manipulation.

(iv) For each crux, specify a concrete case or argument both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. Specifically address: (1) an epistemic lockbox variant in which the agent can irreversibly suppress tomorrow's reasoning before receiving payment, with Camp A predicting that rationality still forbids forming the initial commitment if it requires such self-disablement, and Camp B predicting that suppression makes the commitment rational and fully executable; (2) a witnessed, binding public commitment verified by a third party, with predictions on whether external verification changes the rational status of the initial intention; (3) the analogy to medical treatment adherence, in which patients rationally intend today to take a drug tomorrow despite knowing they will then prefer to skip it, with predictions on whether this case is genuinely parallel to the toxin scenario.

(v) A brief concluding assessment stating whether any portion of the dispute is verbal—particularly regarding whether intention requires reason-responsiveness or merely causal efficacy—and, if so, identifying exactly which portion and why.

Keep the wording precise, avoid bibliographies or citation requests, and do not depend on obscure named theories beyond the standard causal and evidential decision-theoretic distinction.
