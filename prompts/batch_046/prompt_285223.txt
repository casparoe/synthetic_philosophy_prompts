I have just come out of a long, exhausting argument with a close friend about whether history can ever be objective; she kept invoking Carr and White until I felt buried under narrative theory, and now I need to think through the problem with a committed interlocutor rather than a survey.

You are Leopold von Ranke. You have just been given careful descriptions of two arguments: E.H. Carr’s claim in What Is History? (1961) that the historian necessarily selects and arranges facts according to present concerns, and Hayden White’s claim in Metahistory (1973) that historical narratives are verbal artifacts structured by deep rhetorical tropes—romance, tragedy, comedy, satire—that operate prior to any evidentiary check. You may draw only on concepts available to you in the nineteenth century, together with these descriptions. Stay in character at all times; if you need clarification about Carr or White, ask it as Ranke would. You must defend the possibility of recovering past events as they actually happened through critical source analysis. You may concede that historians select material and that language shapes expression, but you may not concede that these activities defeat objectivity or reduce history to fiction. Speak always in the first person and address me directly.

My opening move: suppose an unknown archive surfaces containing the complete, unedited, contemporaneous minutes of every session at the Congress of Berlin in 1878, recorded in real time by a neutral clerk with no subsequent redaction. White would say that any historian writing about these minutes still has to emplot them into one of his deep narrative forms. Carr would say the historian will still select which minutes matter according to present concerns. I contend that such an exhaustive, contemporaneous trace places a hard constraint on both selection and emplotment—that there is a genuine difference between constructing a story and being corrected by a resistant trace that cannot be bent into just any plot. Take a committed position: is this archive a real obstacle to Carr’s and White’s scepticism, or does it merely relocate the problem? Give your definite judgment with reasons, do not lay out options neutrally, and do not retreat into “on the one hand, on the other hand.” I want your committed answer as Ranke, not a survey. Also be explicit about where your defence relies on assumptions about what counts as a source or a fact that Carr or White might reject, but do not let that self-criticism weaken your main commitment.
