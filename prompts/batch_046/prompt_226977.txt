As a retired experimental physicist who has only recently turned to philosophy with any seriousness, I have spent the past year working through Marx’s writings on exploitation and alienation—principally the 1844 Economic and Philosophic Manuscripts and the first volume of Capital—without the mediating framework of professional Marx scholarship. I should like you to draft the joint statement that might emerge from a rigorous adversarial collaboration between two interpretive camps that have been arguing past one another on these topics.

The two camps are:

Camp A, the Value-Form Structuralists, who maintain that exploitation is rigorously co-extensive with the extraction of surplus value within specifically capitalist relations of production, and that alienation is therefore best understood as the empirically variable, subjective experience of separation from the means of production and from the product of one’s labour.

Camp B, the Normative Ontologists, who maintain that exploitation is fundamentally the asymmetric instrumentalisation of human labour-power for ends not reciprocally controlled by the labourer—a condition that can obtain outside capitalism—and that alienation is an objective deformation of human species-being (Gattungswesen), such that one may be alienated irrespective of subjective satisfaction.

Please produce a formal document comprising:

(i) A numbered section of agreed claims—statements about Marx’s texts, about the logic of capitalist production, and about the phenomenology of modern labour that both camps would endorse without reservation, phrased so that neither side reads its own defeat into them.

(ii) A disagreements table setting out, for each residual dispute, Camp A’s formulation in wording Camp A would accept, and Camp B’s formulation in wording Camp B would accept. The disagreements should be precise, not merely rhetorical; distinguish those that are genuinely substantive from those that dissolve upon clarification of terms.

(iii) A list of the cruxes—those sub-questions such that settling them in one direction would move at least one camp significantly closer to the other. I suspect three are central: whether surplus-value extraction is a necessary condition for exploitation; whether alienation requires conscious subjective distress or can be purely objective; and whether Marx’s notion of species-being denotes a biological-anthropological constant or a historically mutable normative ideal.

(iv) For each crux, specify one concrete case or argument that both camps agree bears upon it, together with each camp’s explicit prediction about what that case shows. To avoid rehearsing familiar textbook examples, use the following constructed scenarios: first, a fully automated cooperative commune in which no surplus value is extracted in monetary terms, yet an algorithmic planner assigns all inhabitants to repetitive maintenance tasks without democratic input; second, a worker-owned factory operating within market competition, in which the collective appropriates all surplus but retains extreme task fragmentation; and third, a comparison between high-autonomy pre-capitalist craft guilds and modern highly remunerated but rigidly scripted creative labour.

The document should contain no bibliography, no reading list, and no requests for citations; it should be written so that a philosophically informed reader without web access can assess the logic of the disagreement on its own terms. Write in polished British English, with the formality appropriate to a retired scientist who expects conceptual precision but who is not yet fluent in disciplinary jargon.
