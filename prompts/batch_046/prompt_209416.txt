I am writing a feature on whether recent cognitive science is making old philosophical puzzles obsolete, and I have been focusing on self-deception in light of predictive-processing frameworks. It seems to me that if belief formation is governed by hierarchical prediction-error minimization, then so-called self-deception—say, a manager who refuses to see that her division is failing—might not involve any contradiction in belief at all. Instead, there could be a high-level prior that assigns low precision to error signals carrying the bad news, so the information is registered subpersonally but never updates the personal-level belief. If that is correct, then the traditional philosophical problem—how one and the same agent can both know and not know the same proposition—simply dissolves, because the agent never actually holds the contradictory belief; she merely suppresses an error signal. But I worry I may be conflating subpersonal mechanisms with personal-level attitudes. Could you examine whether this predictive-processing account really eliminates the logical tension of self-deception, or whether it merely relocates the contradiction into the architecture of the mind? In particular, I want to know if the agent's behavior—her strategic avoidance of the reports, her emotional reactions when the topic is raised—still presupposes both the acceptance and the rejection of the same fact, and whether distinguishing precision-weighted suppression from outright denial is enough to save the coherence of self-deception.
