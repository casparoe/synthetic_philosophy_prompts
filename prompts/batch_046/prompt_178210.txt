After a long, unresolved argument with a close friend about what makes consent valid, I want to turn our disagreement into a formal research proposal. She argued that the standard triad—absence of deception, absence of coercion, and competence—is incomplete because it treats consent as a static mental event rather than as a dynamic authorization that unfolds over time. Specifically, she proposed an independent fourth condition she called temporal coherence: the consenter’s grasp of what they are authorizing must remain stable across the interval between giving consent and its execution, and any relevant change in circumstances that the consenter could not have reasonably anticipated invalidates the consent retroactively rather than merely prospectively. I replied that this collapses consent into contract law, renders routine medical and research consent practically impossible given inevitable minor changes in conditions, and that any legitimate demands here are already captured by a dynamic interpretation of deception and competence. We could not agree on whether temporal coherence is a genuinely distinct condition or a misapplication of contractual logic to consent.

Please write a short research proposal—approximately two pages, suitable for a dissertation prospectus or a small fellowship—addressing this precise question: Is temporal coherence an independent necessary condition for valid consent, or can all of its apparent normative demands be fully absorbed by the standard conditions of deception and competence interpreted dynamically?
