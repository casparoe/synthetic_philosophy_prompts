I've been working through discussions of indicative and counterfactual conditionals in the philosophy of language — particularly Stalnaker's similarity-based semantics for counterfactuals, Adams's probabilistic account of indicative conditionals, and the various unification projects that try to derive both from a common modal logic — and I keep returning to an asymmetry that standard treatments seem to gloss over. Take a rational agent who reasons, "If I accept the offer in Berlin, then I will learn German," using this indicative conditional to guide actual planning. The same agent can then ask the counterfactual, "If I had accepted the offer in Berlin, would I have learned German?" and the evaluation conditions appear to shift in a way that is not fully captured by saying that one updates the actual world and the other compares nearby worlds.

I want to suggest that indicative conditionals carry an implicit structural feature — call it an epistemic anchor — that binds the evaluation of the antecedent to the agent's present epistemic state in a manner that actively prevents the kind of world-shifting required for counterfactual assessment. This anchor is not merely a pragmatic feature of context; it functions as a constraint on how the conditional can be embedded in practical reasoning, which explains why indicative conditionals resist nesting inside counterfactual hypotheses without either collapsing into counterfactuals or losing their action-guiding force. I am asking you to argue that this anchoring view offers a better explanation of the indicative-counterfactual divide than the standard semantic bifurcation, especially with respect to cases where probabilistic and categorical indicative reasoning come apart. You should address the obvious objection that this is just a redescription of Stalnaker's modal-base parameter, and you should explain how the anchor operates differently when the conditional serves a decision-theoretic function versus a purely doxastic one. I am not looking for a literature survey or a list of references — I want to see whether the conceptual architecture holds up under pressure.
