I serve on my university's Committee on Predictive Governance and Evidence Standards, which oversees how institutional forecasting units may revise their public-facing models following ambiguous external audits. At Thursday's session we must issue a binding ruling in a live case.

The unit in question maintains an epidemiological-resource-allocation model composed of three structural modules: transmission dynamics, behavioral adaptation, and supply-chain logistics. Rather than issuing a binary verdict, the external auditors delivered a graded assessment mapped onto a partition of structural-adequacy classes: they assigned high confidence to the transmission module, moderate confidence to the behavioral module, and low confidence to the supply-chain module, without identifying specific internal errors or certifying any module as sound. In response, the unit revised its aggregate forecasts by redistributing institutional credence across these three classes—holding constant the internal conditional probabilities within each class—while retaining the overall modeling architecture intact. They characterize this revision as an application of Jeffrey conditionalization.

One faction of the committee accepts this characterization. They argue that when audit evidence does not determinately confirm or deny a specific structural hypothesis but instead shifts confidence across a partition, rational belief revision requires redistributing weights across that partition rather than forcing an artificial binary condition. They warn that mandating categorical conditioning—treating the audit as either a full validation or a total invalidation—would discard graded epistemic information and commit the institution to an implausible all-or-nothing epistemology.

The opposing faction rejects the revision. They contend that Jeffrey-style updating allows the unit to preserve its core framework despite serious localized failures, effectively sliding institutional commitments by redistributing rather than rebuilding. They argue that for institutional action—especially when forecasts inform public-resource allocation—evidence must cross categorical thresholds; ambiguous audits should trigger suspension, targeted rebuilding, or withdrawal of institutional endorsement, not calibrated internal redistribution. They further worry that endorsing this method creates a procedural loophole: any future audit with mixed results can be interpreted as a license for partial adjustment rather than a mandate for structural correction.

Please draft the written opinion I will circulate before Thursday's meeting. Recommend a concrete course of action—endorse the unit's Jeffrey-style revision as permissible, reject it and require binary conditioning or suspension protocols, or approve it only under specified procedural safeguards—with an explicit implementation or review timeline. The memo should explain, with sufficient precision for non-specialist committee members, what Jeffrey conditionalization demands (specifically, the redistribution of probabilities over a partition with fixed internal conditional probabilities, as distinct from Bayesian conditioning on a single evidential event), apply that distinction honestly to the audit case, and treat seriously the strongest objection raised by the faction that favors categorical evidentiary thresholds. The document should be suitable for formal circulation and should not include requests for citations, bibliographies, or reading lists.
