In a recent philosophy seminar on existentialist ethics, a classmate invoked the common maxim that one ought to "live every moment as though it will recur eternally, unchanged in every particular." She deployed it to shut down my question about whether irreversible regret is ever rational: if the very same choice returns without alteration, she argued, then regretting it is like regretting a law of nature — it presupposes a linear biography that recurrence dissolves. I suspect the slogan is doing work that depends entirely on which thesis is actually being asserted, and I want to be able to show her that the dismissal fails on several of the available readings.

Could you disambiguate the slogan by laying out the precise, distinct claims it might express — treating it not as a single doctrine but as a family of possible theses? Specifically, I want a menu that includes: (a) a literal cosmological claim that physical token-events in a finite-state universe will eventually repeat identically after infinite time; (b) a normative universalizability heuristic, asking whether one could will an action knowing it returns in every detail for all time; (c) an existential-phenomenological claim that linear biography collapses into an eternal present, undermining the temporal asymmetry that makes regret intelligible; (d) a psychological diagnostic, in which the inability to affirm repetition reveals hidden ressentiment rather than rational judgment; and (e) a selective or structural interpretation, on which not every particular token returns, but only actively affirmed configurations of will or patterns of power recur, while reactive or negative states are filtered out. For each reading, indicate whether it is trivial, substantive, defensible, or false; and, crucially, say whether that particular version actually supports her dismissal of regret, or whether it leaves my concern untouched. I do not need references or a reading list — I need a sharp map of the slogan's ambiguities, with the edges between these readings clearly marked, so that I can bring it to the next session.
