You are an expert philosopher of moral psychology and an experienced facilitator of adversarial collaborations. Your task is to draft the joint statement that two opposing research camps could both sign after sustained, good-faith collaboration on this question: Is guilt or shame the structurally superior moral emotion, and can a culture deliberately choose which one to cultivate?

Camp A — the Reparative-Autonomy Advocates — holds that guilt is superior because it targets discrete actions rather than global self-worth, preserves the agent’s capacity for rational repair, and can be institutionally channeled through restitution frameworks. They claim cultures can shift emotional architecture toward guilt-dominance through legal and pedagogical design—replacing honor-based sanctioning with procedural accountability—without requiring evolutionary overhaul. Camp B — the Integrative-Relational Advocates — holds that shame is superior because it operates through social belonging rather than private judgment, motivates deep character revision rather than transactional compensation, and binds communities through shared normative visibility. They claim cultures can cultivate shame-dominance by embedding moral failure in communal ritual and narrative practices that make restoration inseparable from identity, though they deny this can be achieved by mere institutional decree.

Please begin with a brief brainstorming phase: list three conceptual distinctions—such as action-targeting versus self-targeting, transactional versus transformative repair, and privatized versus communal accountability—that both camps must clarify before substantive agreement is possible. Then introduce one novel framing of your own, distinguishing emotional architecture (the institutional and pedagogical structures that shape which emotion is available and rewarded) from emotional climate (the background normative atmosphere that determines whether an emotion is experienced as toxic or salutary), and use it to organize the document. Produce the formal collaboration text in this order: (1) a numbered section of shared claims about the phenomenology of guilt and shame, about what “better moral emotion” could mean across normative, functional, and cultural dimensions, about the conditions of emotional emergence, and about the limits of cultural engineering—each claim precise enough to be contestable yet worded so representatives of both camps could sign it; (2) a disagreements table with columns Issue, Camp A formulation (worded so Camp A accepts it), Camp B formulation (worded so Camp B accepts it), and Crux status—the issues should include whether guilt’s action-specificity protects self-worth or enables moral compartmentalization, whether shame’s relational embeddedness resists manipulation or invites authoritarian capture, and whether cultural choice is constrained by evolutionary psychology, social ontology, or institutional path-dependence; (3) for each crux, identify one concrete comparative scenario—such as a society transitioning from communal restoration rituals to individual legal restitution, or a pedagogical system teaching children to articulate “I did a wrong thing” versus “I am seen as wrong”—that both camps agree is relevant, and state in advance what each camp predicts will happen to moral repair rates, social trust, and emotional coherence; (4) a brief meta-section flagging which disagreements are genuinely substantive and which dissolve once “guilt” and “shame” are disambiguated into sub-types like reparative guilt versus integrative shame versus toxic shame. Write formally, avoid bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and do not invoke highly specialized empirical studies that a small model could not reasonably recall. Think carefully about whether any part of the dispute is verbal rather than real, and say exactly which part and why.
