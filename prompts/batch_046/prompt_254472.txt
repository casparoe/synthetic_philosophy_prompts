I've been reading Plato's Laws for a seminar on political philosophy and I keep coming back to this weird argument in Book I and II where the Athenian Stranger says getting people drunk in a controlled setting isn't just okay but actually a civic tool — like a stress-test for the soul. He compares it to testing gymnasts with fear and pleasure to see if their charcter holds up. The idea is that wine reveals your true nature because it strips away the social masks you wear sober, so a well-run symposium becomes this kind of democratic audit where citizens expose whether they're actually courageous and temperate or just faking it. I find this kind of compelling but also a little suspicious.

So here's my position: Plato's defense of regulated drunkenness as a civic diagnostic is basically right — alcohol doesn't create false traits but dissolves performative ones, and a state could in principle use moderated symposia to map the moral topography of its citizens without coercion. What would actually count against it? I don't want obvious objections like "drinking is unhealthy" or "this sounds like surveillance." I want deeper defeaters: what empirical or conceptual results would show alcohol doesn't reveal but rather reconstructs the self? Could there be an argument — maybe from situationist psychology or narrative identity — showing the "drunken self" is just as constructed as the sober one, only with different social scripts? And what about the assumption there's a stable core character being tested? If continuity theories of personal identity or situationism in ethics are right, maybe there's no "true nature" to expose, just contextual responses.

Which of these would Plato's own framework concede, and which would he dismiss as missing the point about habit-formation? Also, does this view risk collapsing into unfalsifiability — if every behavior while drunk can be read as revealing hidden virtue or hidden vice, is the theory doing real work? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and tell me if I'm being unfair to Plato by treating the Laws argument as a literal policy proposal rather than a metaphor for philosophical dialectic. I was discussing this with my roommate last night and they said I was overreading the text, but I think there's something genuinely strange going on here.
