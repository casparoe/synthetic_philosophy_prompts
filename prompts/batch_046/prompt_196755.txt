I am a retired experimental physicist—previously in condensed-matter research—who has recently begun serious study in philosophy of science and normative ethics. Next month I am moderating a small invited colloquium for an audience of fellow scientists and engineers with minimal formal philosophy training. The guest is a philosopher who defends a specific, somewhat unconventional thesis about cross-cultural folk morality: he holds that the well-documented divergence between WEIRD and non-WEIRD moral intuitions is best interpreted not as evidence against moral objectivity, but as a form of “calibration noise” distributed around invariant moral coordinates constrained by shared ecological and social conditions. Rather than asking him to restate this thesis from scratch, I need eight to ten interview questions that press on its actual content, its vulnerabilities, and its implications.

Please include at least one question that challenges whether treating moral disagreement as measurement error commits a category mistake—specifically, whether normative claims can be analogized to instrument readings without losing their action-guiding force. For this, provide a follow-up keyed to his most likely reply, in which he invokes inter-laboratory calibration in physics to defend the analogy; then include a second-level follow-up that asks how he distinguishes calibration error in a physical constant from normative revision when the “instrument” is itself a cultural practice.

Please include at least one question on the domain-specific variance pattern: the observation that harm and fairness judgments show relatively low cross-cultural variance, while purity, loyalty, and authority show high variance. Ask whether his framework predicts that high-variance domains correspond to weaker ecological constraints or merely to weaker linguistic framing, and include the follow-up keyed to his probable reply that these domains have domain-specific calibration curves rather than shared invariant coordinates.

Please include at least one question on moral progress: if disagreement is noise around invariant coordinates, does moral reform consist in correcting local calibration errors rather than discovering previously unknown moral truths? Include the follow-up keyed to the answer that distinguishes coordinate correction from coordinate discovery, and then ask what empirical evidence—say, sustained cross-cultural convergence on a previously contested domain—would count as correction rather than discovery.

Please also ask him to specify an empirical result that would genuinely falsify his view rather than being absorbed as additional noise, and include the anticipated reply that only systematic, non-random divergence across all domains would do so, with a follow-up pressing whether that criterion renders the view unfalsifiable by design.

Finally, ask whether his framework requires a principled distinction between first-order moral disagreement and second-order disagreement about how to weight different moral domains, and whether that distinction can be maintained without collapsing into a form of moral relativism about weights. Provide one follow-up keyed to the answer that weights are themselves part of the coordinate system rather than external to it.

The questions should be formulated in language accessible to scientists without philosophical jargon, should avoid generic biographical inquiries, and should not request bibliographies, citations, or reading lists.
