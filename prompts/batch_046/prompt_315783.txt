I am in a philosophy-of-mind course discussing pain and affective consciousness. I have adopted the view that clinical cases of pain asymbolia—where subjects report nociceptive sensations without any unpleasantness—show that the sensory-discriminative features of pain (location, intensity, qualitative character) are conceptually and phenomenologically separable from its negative affect. I take this to undermine the claim that pain is essentially or necessarily bad: on my view, what is bad is not the pain-state itself but an associated evaluative response that can be absent. What would have to be true for this position to be wrong? I am not looking only for the familiar objection that asymbolics still avoid injury; I want to know which specific intuitions about phenomenal content, which empirical results regarding asymbolics' behavioral choices or verbal reports, which theoretical commitments about representation or value, and which analogies to other dissociable sensory-affective pairs would genuinely threaten it. Please rank these potential defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, noting which ones defenders of the essential-badness view would concede as damaging versus which they would reject. In addition, please distinguish what is settled in the clinical and philosophical literature on asymbolia from what is actively contested, and avoid presenting any contested interpretation as the consensus.
