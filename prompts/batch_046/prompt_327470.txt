Write a short story of roughly 900–1200 words that takes hinge epistemology — the view, central to Wittgenstein’s On Certainty, that some propositions (for example, that one has hands, that the earth existed long before one was born) do not function as ordinary beliefs supported by evidence, but as the fixed “hinges” around which doubt, inquiry, and justification turn — and embeds it in an invented, concrete scenario. To keep the story from collapsing into a familiar philosophical dialogue or allegory, use this niche framing: in the world of the story, hinge propositions have become technologically isolable and transferable, managed by licensed “hinge auditors” who can temporarily extract, store, or graft these background certainties for clinical, artistic, or bureaucratic purposes. Center the plot on one auditor who, during a routine procedure, discovers that her own foundational hinge — perhaps the certainty that she has never been inside this particular building before, or that her memories belong to her — has been silently replaced with someone else’s, not through any rational refutation but through administrative or physical displacement. Let the narrative show, rather than explain, that losing a hinge is not like being mistaken about a fact; it is like losing the ground that makes mistake and correction possible. Avoid any authorial introduction, summary of Wittgenstein’s philosophy outside the action, or explanatory afterword. Begin with the story itself, with no preface, no commentary, and no framing device that steps outside the fiction.
