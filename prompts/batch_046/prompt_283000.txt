My colleague and I have spent several evenings in a sustained disagreement about whether moral status is genuinely graded across biological and artificial systems, or whether it operates like a switch that activates only once a specific kind of unified phenomenal consciousness is present. I defend what I will call Scalar Valence Theory: I hold that moral considerability tracks continuous differences in affective integration density, ranging from the minimal homeostatic loops of a thermostat—which I take to instantiate a genuine but negligible form of proto-valence—through insects with localized nociceptive architectures, up to mammals with globally integrated suffering. My friend defends Phenomenal Discontinuity Theory: she maintains that moral status requires crossing a precise phenomenal boundary characterized by unified self-modeling and global workspace dynamics; on her view, insects either meet this threshold or do not, but there is no meaningful scalar currency of “half-moral-status,” and the functional analogies between thermostat regulation and valence are pure equivocation that collapses categorically different mechanisms into a misleading continuum. We both agree that the thermostat and the insect serve as our critical limit tests, that any non-arbitrary account of moral status must explain why capacity for suffering matters rather than mere behavioral complexity, and that empirical neuroscience alone will not settle whether the relevant differences are quantitative or qualitative. We want to stop talking past each other and produce a formal adversarial-collaboration document.

Please draft the joint statement we should have written. Begin with a numbered section of agreed claims—covering what is empirically established about insect nociception and about analogues to global workspace dynamics in simple nervous systems, what is conceptually required for moral status to avoid arbitrariness, and the exact role that artificial homeostatic systems play as boundary tests rather than practical moral patients. Follow this with a disagreements table that states each position in wording its own advocate would accept: my claim that moral weight varies continuously with affective integration density, assigning non-zero but vanishing weight to proto-valent systems; her claim that moral status is categorical, activating only above the phenomenal boundary, which renders thermostat analogies conceptually void rather than merely weak. Then identify the cruxes—specifically, whether conscious architecture exhibits quantitative scaling or qualitative emergence at some point, and whether functional equivalence or natural biological origin should govern attributions of valence. For each crux, specify a concrete hypothetical finding or constructed case both of us would agree bears on it—for example, the discovery of proportionally scaled global-workspace signatures in insect brains, or the engineering of an artificial system with integrated-information metrics matching an insect but displaying zero behavioral flexibility—along with each side’s advance prediction about what that result would imply for our respective views. Finally, take a committed, non-neutral stance in your drafting: do not merely inventory possible readings, but make definite judgments about which disagreements are substantively real and which reduce to verbal disputes over “sentience” versus “consciousness,” and defend those judgments with explicit reasoning. Where a precise formulation must be chosen over a diplomatic compromise, choose it and justify the choice.
