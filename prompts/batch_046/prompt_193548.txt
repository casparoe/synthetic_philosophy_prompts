okay so heres a position i've been chewing on: in the GEB framing, meaning lives in the outer message, not in the frame or the inner message. the frame is just the rules of interpretation, the inner message is pure syntax (like Gödel's sentence sitting there un-interpreted), and only when an outer observer maps synatax onto semantics does anything mean anything. so basically all semantic content is exogenous—there's no intrinsic meaning inside the system.

what would actually count against this? like, not just "well computers understand stuff" handwaving. i'm talking about specific things: if there's a formal result showing that a system can generate its own interpretation frame without an external observer, that'd hurt it. or if the frame itself turned out to carry semantic content that isn't reducible to syntax—maybe through some kind of pragmatic entailment baked into the rules. or if we found a case where the inner message clearly has meaning even when every possible outer observer is removed from the loop (maybe in some weird quantum-interpretation setup? i dunno).

i also want to know: is this position well-insulated? like, can its defenders just say "oh that case still needs an implicit observer" and retreat? what would the defenders themselves concede is a real defeater versus what critics claim? and does the view risk collapsing into standard intentionalism, or is there a genuine middle ground where frames do some semantic work but not all?

also—this might be niche—but what if we applied the frame/outer/inner distinction to something like large language models? the weights are the frame, the prompt is the outer message, the generated token is the inner message. does that analogy break the position, or does it just show that LLMs are pure syntax machines? i want an honest map of where this view is actually exposed.
