I'm preparing a feature on collective cognition for a science-ideas magazine, and two researchers I interviewed—Dr. Elena Vasquez, a philosopher of biology, and Dr. Marcus Okonkwo, a cognitive scientist—became locked in a dispute I cannot resolve from the transcript alone. Vasquez defends what she calls the "Ant Fugue" view: an ant colony solves pathfinding and resource-allocation problems that exceed any individual ant's capacity, with pheromonal signals weaving through the group like contrapuntal voices in a fugue, so that the colony itself realizes a minimal form of distributed consciousness. Okonkwo counters with a scenario he labels "Aunt Hillary"—an aging woman whose memory, decision-making, and emotional regulation are sustained by a tightly knit circle of relatives and embedded devices; we do not treat that circle as conscious, he insists, because Hillary's biological brain remains the experiential anchor, whereas the colony has no analogous anchor. Vasquez replies that Hillary's brain is merely one node in her network, and that split-brain patients already show human consciousness can survive without a single neural center, so the absence of a central ant-brain should not disqualify the colony. Okonkwo then argues that without a non-arbitrary boundary condition—something that distinguishes the system's self-model from its environment—the colony is just a complex mechanism, not a subject of experience; Hillary's skin and her first-person reports supply that boundary, while an anthill's perimeter is ecologically contingent. Vasquez's final move is to ask whether Okonkwo's boundary criterion would also deny consciousness to future humans with extensive neural prostheses or to certain cephalopods with distributed ganglia, implying that his standard is either parochial or self-undermining. For my article, please adjudicate point by point: on which specific claim is each researcher correct, what shared premise about whether consciousness requires a unitary point of view or only functional integration are both missing, and what further empirical or conceptual case—perhaps involving synthetic distributed systems or radically decentralized nervous systems—would actually force one view over the other?
