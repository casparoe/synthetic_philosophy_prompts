I am a doctoral student in comparative philosophy and philosophy of mind, designing a ninety-minute seminar for first-year philosophy graduate students who are fluent in analytic debates about personal identity—Parfit’s psychological continuity and reductionism, substance versus bundle theories, and the basic machinery of fission and fusion cases—but who have no prior training in Buddhist philosophy. I want to teach them the Theravada Abhidharma doctrine of anatta (no-self) in a way that makes clear it is not reducible to a Western bundle theory, but involves the stronger claim that there are no diachronic unity conditions whatsoever, coupled with the momentariness thesis (ksanika): that even the aggregates (skandhas) exist only for an instantaneous moment and are immediately replaced. To prevent them from assimilating this to familiar reductionism, I will inject a comparative framing drawn from contemporary cognitive science: I will present predictive-processing accounts of the self as a continuously updated generative model, then argue that anatta targets not merely the model’s representational content but the reification (samjña) of even these momentary updates into a persisting owner or narrative center.

Plan the explanatory arc. First, specify the exact order in which the conceptual components must be introduced and the dependency relations among them—what must be firmly in place before the predictive-processing comparison can do its philosophical work, and before the momentariness claim can be heard as a metaphysical thesis about existence rather than as a psychological observation about rapid change. Second, identify the single carrying example or thought experiment that should anchor the session: it must be concrete enough to survive without reliance on Sanskrit terminology or canonical texts, must clearly distinguish anatta from Parfit-style reductionism, and must fail in a specific, visible way if the student still smuggles in a hidden integrative function or diachronic bridge. Third, map the misconceptions these students will predictably import from their analytic training—particularly the conflation of anatta with bundle theory, the interpretation of momentariness as mere rapid succession rather than instantaneous existence and replacement, and the assumption that denying a self entails denying moral agency or accountability—and specify the precise point in the sequence where each misconception should be defused rather than merely mentioned. Fourth, propose a rigorous check for understanding that would demonstrate they have grasped the distinctiveness of the Buddhist position: it should require them to articulate why anatta differs from both substance dualism and Parfit’s reductionism without falling back on vague appeals to “Eastern mysticism” or “holism.” Fifth, state explicitly what I should refuse to explain at this level—whether the full Abhidharma taxonomy of mental factors (cetasikas), the complete twelve-linked chain of dependent origination (pratītyasamutpāda), or specific canonical formulations—and indicate how I should openly say so in the session. Do not provide a bibliography, reading list, or citations.
