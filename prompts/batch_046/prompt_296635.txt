I am a retired experimental physicist who, since leaving the laboratory, has been working through philosophy of science and metaphysics in a local adult-education circle. For our next meeting—twelve participants, ninety minutes, in a seminar room with movable tables—I want to stage an activity that makes the non-identity of an artifact and its constituent matter feel unavoidable before anyone names the problem. Please design a structured simulation, not a lecture, built around a novel twist on the classic case: in addition to a traditional marble statue that is gradually repaired with replacement stone, participants must also track a second object, a bio-engineered sculpture composed of a synthetic tissue matrix that continuously replaces its own cells to maintain form, so that after a simulated century none of the original biological substrate remains, yet the shape and artistic function are preserved intact. The rules, roles, or incentives should be arranged so that participants themselves discover that they are tracking two different entities—the enduring artwork and the ever-changing material—before the facilitator introduces the terminology of constitution versus identity. Include a timeline with rounds or stages, specify any materials or cards needed, describe the debrief that maps participants' decisions onto the metaphysical distinction, predict where the activity will break down or generate confusion, and be explicit about which feature of the material-constitution debate—such as the modal argument that the statue and the clay could not be identical because one could survive reshaping that destroys the other—the exercise cannot reproduce and must therefore be supplied by me afterwards. Keep the design self-contained: do not ask participants to read specific texts in advance, and do not require citations or bibliographic recommendations.
