I'm giving a talk next thursday at our dept aesthetics workshop defending the idea that the special value we assign to original artworks isn't about authenticty or causal history in the usual sense, but about what I'm calling causal legibility — the property that an object's present material form lets a viewer trace specific intentional actions back to the artist without mediation. I argue that a perfect forgery made with identical materials and techniques actually lacks this not because it wasn't touched by the artist, but because its material history is opaque to that tracing, even when we know it's fake. But I also claim that heavily restored originals, like paintings where sixty percent is inpainting, are in the same boat — right causal origin but lost legibility — so if we still value them more than forgeries, we're just being metaphysical about origin, not aesthetic.

The audience is six analytic philosophers of art: three hardcore formalists who think all value is in perceptual and structural properties, one institutionalist who thinks art-world conferral solves everything, and a grad student in cognitive aesthetics obsessed with empirical studies on authenticity detection. There's also an art historian who always asks about actual museum practice. I need the objections they'd actually raise, not generic ones from the literature — ordered by how damaging they'd be, phrased in their vocab, with a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is softest. What should I concede upfront? How do I answer the formalist charge that I'm smuggling in a non-perceptual property? How does the institutionalist reframe the restored-original versus forgery comparison? And please include the one question I'm praying nobody asks — probably something about digital reproductions or AI-generated forgeries, but phrased exactly like this crowd would ask it. I can sketch my argument more if it helps, but the core claim is that causal legibility is an aesthetic property, not just epistemic, and forgeries fail aesthetically because they break the viewer-object relation in a specific way.
