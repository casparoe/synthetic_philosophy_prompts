I'm studying how communities remember events that the official records say didn't happen, or vice versa. There's one pattern that doesn't quite fit the usual terms like counter-memory or mnemonic conflict. A community insists on remembering a specific detail — say, that a local factory closed on a Tuesday — but every archive, newspaper, and government record says it closed on Thursday. The Tuesday version isn't politically charged; it's not about heroism or victimhood. It's just a stubborn local fact that refuses to align with the paper trail, and people use it in casual conversation without treating it as resistance. Meanwhile historians correct them, and the correction doesn't stick becuase the Tuesday version is embedded in other memories (who was there, what they ate for lunch, etc.). Is there a name for this kind of low-stakes, structurally embedded divergence between collective memory and documentary record? Not the big ideological battles, but the small persistent misalignments that don't serve any obvious social function? If there is no term, can you coin one that separates it from ordinary false memory and from politically motivated revisionism, and tell me what its edges are — for instance, does it require the misalignment to be harmless, or could it apply to small harmful errors too?
