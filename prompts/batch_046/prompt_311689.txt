I take the view that practices such as dueling and chattel slavery ended not because moral arguments finally prevailed, but because the honor economies sustaining them collapsed once dominant groups faced unsustainable costs — with moral philosophy largely providing post-hoc rationalization rather than causal force. I extend this Williams-Appiah picture with a further claim: that these honor codes become epistemically brittle when subaltern groups can systematically expose the gap between a practice's status-claims and its actual risk distribution, accelerating withdrawal by elites who can no longer maintain credible commitment to the ritual. What would count decisively against this account, and what would merely be awkward? I want the defeaters ranked: historical cases where institutional abolition clearly preceded any structural shift in honor or economic interest; comparative patterns showing normative change tracking argument diffusion rather than interest realignment; and whether the view has been insulated so that any moral rhetoric can be dismissed as rationalization — if so, is that insulation a virtue or a vice? Say also what defenders of the position should themselves concede as counting against them, not only what critics allege.
