Examination question: Compare Christopher Browning’s explanation of Reserve Police Battalion 101’s participation in mass shootings with Daniel Goldhagen’s explanation of ordinary Germans’ involvement in the Holocaust. Focus specifically on how each account weighs pre-existing ideology against situational and structural pressure. Then apply both frameworks to this hypothetical case: a unit composed of middle-aged postal clerks and railway accountants, drawn from regions with no documented history of militant ethnic nationalism, is ordered to oversee the forced relocation of a minority population they have previously treated with bureaucratic indifference rather than active hostility. Which mechanisms—peer dynamics, career incentives, authority obedience, deep cultural belief, or other factors—would Browning’s framework predict as the primary drivers of compliance? Which mechanisms would Goldhagen’s framework require? Be specific about where the predictions diverge, and assess whether one framework can accommodate the other without conceptual distortion.

Student answer: Browning’s Ordinary Men studies Reserve Police Battalion 101, a unit of middle-aged, working-class reservists from Hamburg who were not ideologically vetted. He shows that these men participated in mass shootings largely because of direct orders, peer pressure, the desire not to appear weak before comrades, and concern for their careers and social standing. Browning argues that ideology played essentially no role; the perpetrators were ordinary people placed in an extraordinary situation where refusal was technically possible but psychologically and socially costly. They were motivated by conformity and self-preservation rather than by belief.

Goldhagen’s Hitler’s Willing Executioners takes the opposite view. He argues that ordinary Germans across regions and social classes shared a uniformly intense, culturally specific form of eliminationist antisemitism. For Goldhagen, situational factors like peer pressure or authority are secondary, almost irrelevant, because German culture had already made ordinary people into willing executioners. They killed eagerly because they believed Jews deserved destruction.

Applying both to the postal clerks and railway accountants: Browning’s framework predicts compliance driven by authority structures, peer dynamics within the unit, and the bureaucratic normalization of violence. Even without prior hatred, these men would likely participate because refusal carries professional and social costs. Goldhagen’s framework, however, predicts that they would only comply if they already possessed the same deep, culturally rooted eliminationist drive. Since the clerks have shown only mild indifference, Goldhagen’s model suggests they would resist, perform the task reluctantly and inefficiently, or require coercion—lacking the enthusiastic hatred that makes executioners effective.

The two frameworks are therefore best understood as complementary. Browning explains how circumstances push ordinary people into violence, while Goldhagen explains why belief pulls some people eagerly into it. In the hypothetical case, both might operate together: situational pressure creates baseline compliance, but only those with dormant eliminationist beliefs become truly willing and efficient participants. Thus, the postal clerks might obey orders under pressure, yet only a hidden subset with suppressed hatred would carry out the relocation with the zeal Goldhagen describes. This synthesis shows that situation and culture are not rivals but partners in explaining perpetrator behavior.
