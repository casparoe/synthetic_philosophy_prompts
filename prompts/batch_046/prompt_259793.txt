Consider the institutional design rule, present in several crisis-standards protocols for scarce-resource allocation, that permits clinicians and decision-support systems to withhold or withdraw life-sustaining intervention—thereby allowing a patient's death—while maintaining an absolute institutional prohibition on computing or administering lethal interventions, even when prognosis, suffering, and net resource outcomes would be identical. Within the same framework, withdrawal is documented under "resource reallocation" and reviewed by clinical ethics boards, whereas active termination falls under a separate legal audit category with different consent requirements and liability structures, despite the two pathways being functionally indistinguishable in outcome and patient experience.

What philosophical commitments about the distinction between doing and allowing, and between killing and letting die, does this institutional design presuppose? Does it require that the moral difference lies in the causal structure of the agent's contribution, in the normative status of intentions versus foreseen effects, in institutional accountability, or in some other feature? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, and say whether the same framework could consistently license both pathways, prohibit both, or is philosophically underdetermined among these options. Furthermore, address whether the practice presupposes a coherent metaphysics of action—one that remains stable when the "agent" is a distributed system of clinicians, algorithms, and institutional protocols rather than a single human actor.
