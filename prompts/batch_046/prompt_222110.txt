As a doctoral student working on epistemic dependence and the erosion of understanding in AI-assisted scholarship, I am analyzing an emerging institutional norm: graduate philosophy programs that allow students to rely on LLM-generated synthetic "conceptual maps"—integrated summaries of debates such as scientific realism or phenomenal consciousness—as primary study material for qualifying exams, rather than treating them as auxiliary aids. What philosophical commitments about understanding and justification does this practice presuppose? In particular, does it implicitly endorse a distributed-cognition view on which understanding can reside partly in an external synthetic artifact, or does it still require that the student possess an integrated mental model? Can it be reconciled with virtue-epistemological demands for intellectual autonomy, and with social-epistemological conditions that justified belief requires tracking the reliability of one's sources? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely fits it, and specify what changes to exam design or training norms would be required if we replaced that view with one that treats such synthetic outputs as strictly non-justificatory prostheses.
