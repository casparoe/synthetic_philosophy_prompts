I'm auditing the consistency of my advisor's judgments for a philosophy of science paper, and I need to extrapolate his position on a borderline case he hasn't discussed. Over the past semester, Dr. K. M. Reeves has delivered clear verdicts on whether various research programs count as genuinely scientific under a falsificationist framework, though he has never spelled out the exact criterion he applies. He ruled that Freudian psychoanalysis is pseudoscientific because its interpretive apparatus allows virtually any human behavior to be reconciled with the theory after the fact; that Darwinian evolutionary biology is scientific because it risks specific quantitative predictions about morphological distributions in the fossil record; that a hypothetical "morphic-field" biology explaining every anomalous development by adjusting an unobservable field strength is not science; that contemporary climate modeling is scientific despite imperfect forecasts, because its core physical mechanisms are tested against independent paleoclimate data and the models are revised when they fail; and that behavioral-genetics studies linking particular polymorphisms to complex cognitive traits are scientific only if they publish null results and do not retroactively redefine phenotypes to protect the association. He also said that multiverse cosmology invoking eternal inflation, taken as an empirical claim about our particular universe rather than as pure mathematics, fails the demarcation test because it can accommodate any observed value of the cosmological constant.

The new proposal comes from a cognitive neuroscience group studying consciousness. They claim that conscious awareness arises from "integrated information gradients" computed across laminar cortical columns. Their model generates a precise quantitative prediction: under general anesthesia, the gradient should collapse below a threshold of 0.3 within ninety seconds in ninety-five percent of subjects. The study is designed to risk clear empirical failure—the researchers have agreed to abandon the framework if forty percent of subjects maintain gradients above 0.5—but the model also contains a free "cortical scaling parameter" that can be adjusted post-hoc for individual neuroanatomy without changing the qualitative prediction. They have already invoked this parameter twice to explain away outliers in whom the gradient remained elevated. Would Reeves classify this program as scientific? Which of his earlier rulings is the closest analog—the climate-modeling case, where imperfection is tolerated but mechanisms are independently tested; the behavioral-genetics case, where post-hoc redefinition is forbidden; or the morphic-field case, where an adjustable unobservable saves the theory? If his precedents genuinely underdetermine the answer, what principle is he adopting by ruling yes versus ruling no? And what further hypothetical case—say, an identical gradient model but with the scaling parameter fixed in advance by an independent neuroanatomical assay—would best distinguish the competing rules that fit his track record?
