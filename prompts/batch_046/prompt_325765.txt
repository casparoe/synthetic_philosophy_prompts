I have recently been asked to present at an interdisciplinary colloquium on the historiography of perpetrator motivation, and the invitation has taken on personal urgency: my current project on bureaucratic violence in authoritarian institutions requires me to take a precise stand on whether situational and ideological explanations compete or cooperate. I intend to argue that Christopher Browning's analysis of Reserve Police Battalion 101 and Daniel Goldhagen's account of widespread German antisemitism are not the binary alternatives they are often treated as; instead, situational mechanisms—peer conformity, role fragmentation, and incremental commitment—operate as necessary enabling conditions that only yield sustained mass violence when they intersect with a culturally available framework of dehumanization that lowers the psychological cost of participation. The audience will include social historians committed to Browning's situationalism, cultural historians aligned with Goldhagen's intentionalism, and a political theorist who believes both overlook the structural career incentives embedded in SS bureaucracy. Prepare me for the objections: list them in the order they are likely to arise, phrased in the vocabulary of each respective subgroup, provide a concise reply for each, note explicitly where my reply is weakest, and identify the one question I most hope is not asked—namely, whether my model predicts that removing the antisemitic cultural framework while preserving all situational pressures intact would still produce comparable participation rates, and why I regard that counterfactual as either empirically tractable or conceptually malformed.
