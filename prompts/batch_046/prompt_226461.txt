Expected-value reasoning in moral decision-making is often criticized for generating fanaticism—the willingness to bear arbitrarily large costs or risks for arbitrarily small probabilities of arbitrarily large payoffs, as illustrated by Pascal’s Mugging and by longtermist scenarios involving astronomical future populations. The standard responses invoke risk aversion or bounded utility functions. Please argue, instead, that fanaticism is conceptually incoherent under a person-affecting restriction on moral obligation, and that the error lies not in the shape of the utility curve but in a failure of indexical connection. Specifically: defend the claim that an agent’s “ought” requires some relation of narrative continuity or practical identity between the decision-maker and the affected parties, and that this relation necessarily breaks down when outcomes are distributed across populations so vast—say, 10^40 potential lives—that they can no longer be incorporated into any coherent first-personal standpoint. From this, conclude that aggregating value across such scales violates a condition for moral agency itself, not merely prudence. Address the objection that this collapses into general skepticism about longtermism or population ethics, and explain why it targets specifically fanatical trade-offs rather than ordinary large-scale altruism.
