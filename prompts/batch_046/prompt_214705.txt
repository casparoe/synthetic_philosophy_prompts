I serve as a senior ontology auditor for an intergovernmental certification body that reviews institutional proposals—treaties, regulatory mechanisms, and digital governance frameworks—to determine whether they constitute fundamental normative structures or grounded derivative instruments. This classification determines eligibility for expedited legal review and for funding reserved for genuinely foundational innovation, so the stakes are high and the decisions are frequent: I must complete an audit in roughly thirty minutes, without reconstructing metaphysical debates about well-foundedness or irreflexivity, and I must sign off on a determination that a panel of lawyers and policymakers will treat as authoritative.

Please design a reusable audit procedure, expressed as an ordered checklist or flowchart in words, that operationalizes the metaphysics of grounding and fundamentality for this institutional context. The procedure should distinguish full grounding (the grounded entity could not exist without its ground, and its nature is fixed by it) from partial grounding (it might exist independently, but its specific character depends on the ground), and it must include explicit tests that separate grounding relations from mere causal dependence, conceptual reducibility, and supervenience without reduction. It needs to handle grounding chains—where a proposal claims fundamentality but actually rests on prior treaties, customary norms, or technological infrastructures—and it must flag, at each step, whether the determination is rule-governed or requires an unavoidable judgment call about metaphysical dependency versus legal or historical derivation.

The deliverable should be usable by auditors with philosophical literacy but no time for first principles. Apply the procedure to three proposals: (1) a proposed global digital-identity registry that asserts it establishes a new, fundamental right to digital personhood rather than deriving from existing privacy and citizenship norms; (2) a carbon-border adjustment mechanism presented primarily as an implementation of current trade law, but which also claims to ground novel obligations for non-member states; and (3) a voluntary cross-border arbitration protocol that insists it is fully grounded in longstanding customary dispute-resolution practices and therefore requires no state authorization. For each case, run the steps, identify where the classification is clear, where it stalls because metaphysical and legal dependency are conflated, and—crucially—where the procedure yields a result the proposing party will resist. Specifically, case (1) should come out as grounded rather than fundamental, despite the registry’s framing.

Finally, state explicitly what the procedure cannot do. Identify at least two categories of proposals for which the audit will misfire or return an indeterminate result, and explain whether the failure stems from limitations in the metaphysics of grounding itself or from the practical need to map institutional reality onto a binary of fundamental versus grounded. The answer should not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and should avoid treating any technical term—such as well-foundedness, grounding pluralism, or fundamentality—as if it were an obscure specialist coinage rather than part of the standard framework being operationalized.
