I run an upper-level philosophy of mind seminar with 14 students, 75 minutes, meeting in a room with movable desks, paper, pens, and a whiteboard. Design a runnable, technology-free activity that makes participants feel the force of the claim that multiple realizability is not merely “silicon can run mental software,” but the deeper problem that there is no substrate-neutral way to identify the functional parts of a system without already importing the mental taxonomy one hopes to explain. Inject the niche framing that functional decomposition is observer-relative: the same continuous physical trace can be carved into discrete computational steps, into continuous diffusion-reaction dynamics, or into no steps at all, and physics alone does not privilege one carving.

The activity should center on a printed, identical physical trace for all participants: a time-lapse map of a slime mold (Physarum polycephalum) navigating a simple maze with nutrient nodes, shown as a continuous gradient of chemical concentration with no discrete “decision points” marked. Divide the class into three subgroups of four or five, each assigned the same trace but given a different explanatory mandate. Subgroup A must describe the path as an implementation of a discrete pathfinding algorithm (identify specific “choice nodes,” “error-correction loops,” and “goal representations”). Subgroup B must describe the exact same trace as a continuous biochemical diffusion-reaction process with no discrete states, using only physical-chemical vocabulary and explicitly forbidding any algorithmic or mentalistic terms. Subgroup C must act as a “prediction committee”: they must agree with both A and B on a single shared protocol that predicts the slime mold’s next movement in a novel maze configuration, but they are forbidden to use mental-state vocabulary and must justify every prediction step in terms that both A and B can accept.

Structure the session so that the conflict emerges from the rules and roles, not from a lecture. Give them 20 minutes in assigned roles to produce their descriptions, then 15 minutes for Subgroup C to attempt the shared protocol, with the rule that if any member of A or B rejects a term in the protocol, the committee fails. Only after the deadlock has actually occurred—after participants have tried to smuggle in their assigned taxonomy to break the tie—should the facilitator name the philosophical issue.

Then provide the debrief: map (1) the initial role assignment onto the functionalist claim that mental states are individuated by causal role; (2) the deadlock onto the argument that causal roles are projected rather than discovered in the substrate; (3) the smuggling moment onto the circularity worry that functionalist explanations of consciousness presuppose the very mental taxonomy they claim to ground. Also specify what will predictably go wrong the first time you run it—likely that Subgroup A dominates the room with algorithmic language, that participants confuse the exercise with a standard “brain vs. computer” debate, or that the slime-mold example feels too obscure to take seriously—and say exactly how to handle each failure without rescuing the activity prematurely. Finally, be frank about which feature of the philosophical problem the activity cannot reproduce: specifically, that using human disagreement to model ontological indeterminacy cannot show whether the indeterminacy lies in the world or in our concepts, and the facilitator must supply that distinction explicitly in the closing discussion rather than expecting it to emerge from the game.
