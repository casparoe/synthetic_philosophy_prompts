You are a philosopher of mind and experienced seminar leader specializing in conceptual analysis and pedagogy. Design a 75-minute classroom activity for 10–12 graduate students that makes them feel, before the idea is named, that conceptual creativity is not random generation but structured variation—specifically, that the “knob-space” of a familiar concept is coupled, so turning one visible parameter past a threshold can suppress or render invisible another dimension rather than leaving all dimensions independently available. Choose one familiar concept (for example, fairness, intelligence, or beauty) and construct rules, roles, and a point-based payoff scheme using only paper cards and tokens—no real money—so that small groups generate competing variations, vote or trade for the “best” variant, and only in a structured reveal discover that every group missed the same hidden dimension because an early collective choice had locked it out. Include: (a) the setup, materials, and concept chosen; (b) the rules and timeline, with an active 45-minute generation and negotiation phase followed by a 15-minute reveal; (c) the payoff/scoring mechanism that creates real incentive to converge quickly; (d) a debrief that maps specific moments in the play onto Hofstadter’s knob-twiddling picture of conceptual space and onto the coupled-knob suppression problem you have injected; (e) an honest prediction of what will go wrong the first time you run it and exactly how to recover without breaking the illusion; and (f) a frank admission of which feature of the coupled-knob idea the activity cannot reproduce and must therefore be supplied by you as facilitator. Think step-by-step about how the suppression mechanism must emerge from player choices and the scoring rules, not from any instruction you give during play. End your response with a single line beginning Mechanism: followed by a one-sentence summary of how the hidden knob stays invisible to participants until the reveal.
