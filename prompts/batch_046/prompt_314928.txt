Draft the joint statement that two opposing research camps could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration on the dollar auction and escalation of commitment.

Camp A comprises scholars who interpret the auction as a clear instance of irrational sunk-cost escalation: bidders continue investing not because of strategic value, but because they are psychologically anchored to recover prior losses, producing outcomes analogous to loss-chasing in behavioural economics. They predict that if bidders are deprived of any visible record of their accumulated expenditure, escalation should collapse.

Camp B comprises scholars who interpret the same behaviour as rational within a strategic or self-signalling framework: withdrawal reveals weakness or low private valuation to an observing audience (or to the bidder's own future self), so continued bidding is an equilibrium response to reputation costs rather than a cognitive error. They predict that escalation should persist in anonymous conditions provided that self-signalling or implicit audience inference remains intact.

Your statement should include:

- A numbered section of agreed claims. These should cover the formal structure of the dollar auction, the empirical regularity that total payments often exceed the prize value, the conditions that typically terminate escalation, and any points of methodological agreement (for instance, about which experimental variations are genuinely diagnostic).

- A disagreements table. Each row should present one precise point of divergence, phrased in wording that the respective camp would accept for its own position. Key disagreements should include: whether prior expenditure exerts causal influence on the decision to continue; whether an audience or self-signalling mechanism is necessary to reproduce observed escalation patterns; and whether the phenomenon is best classified as a failure of rationality or as an optimal response to an implicit information structure.

- The cruxes. Identify at least two sub-questions such that resolving them would move at least one camp. One crux should address whether the dynamics of the dollar auction map meaningfully onto the giving-up density framework from optimal foraging theory—where an agent continues to exploit a patch while expected returns exceed departure costs, even when prior investment is irrecoverable—and whether this ecological analogy undermines the claim that escalation is cognitively irrational. Another crux should concern whether escalation rates differ systematically between one-shot anonymous settings and settings with even minimal social visibility.

- For each crux, state a concrete case, experimental design, or piece of evidence that both camps agree would bear on it, and record each camp's explicit prediction about the result. For instance, both might agree that a double-blind, single-round auction with hidden bid histories and no audience is a valid test; Camp A should predict withdrawal near the rational value threshold, whereas Camp B should predict continued escalation if internal self-signalling remains operative.

- A brief, neutral assessment of whether any part of the disagreement is merely verbal—for example, if irrational is being used in a purely descriptive sense versus a normative sense—and, if so, specify exactly which claims become aligned once the ambiguity is resolved.

Please write the response in British English. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or requests for citations. Avoid relying on obscure specialist theories that are not broadly covered in standard philosophical and economic training; you may invoke familiar frameworks such as sunk-cost reasoning, war-of-attrition models, or optimal-foraging analogies, but explain any technical term briefly in your own words.
