hey, quick question about a weird pattern in how biopics and historical fiction treat dead people. there's this unwritten industry norm: you can invent a dead person's private inner life—secret fears, invented arguments with a spouse, made-up moments of doubt—as long as you don't alter the occurence or details of documented public events. and then there's the "life rights" thing, where if the dead person has living descendants within two generations, studios often seek family approval for the script, but for older figures they just go wild with the private invention.

what philosophical commitments does that double standard presuppose? does it assume harm to the dead is only about corrupting the historical record or damaging legacy, not about violating some kind of posthumous dignity? and does the split between inventable private self and protected public record presuppose a particular theory of personal identity—like, that our public actions constitute us but our inner lives don't?

also, is the generational cutoff consistent? does it presuppose that descendants inherit representational authority, or that moral standing decays like a half-life? and can all this be squared with the view that dead people have no interests whatsoever?

reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the practice actually requires from what just fits it, and say what a dramatization practice built on genuine respect for the dead would have to change—would we need "interiority disclaimers," or a blanket ban on invented private scenes?
