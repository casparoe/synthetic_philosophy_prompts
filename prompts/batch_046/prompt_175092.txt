I'm working through Adam Smith's Theory of Moral Sentiments, specifically the opening discussion where he distinguishes sympathy from mere emotional infection. The passage I'm trying to parse runs roughly as follows: Smith argues that we do not come to share another person's grief or joy by observing the passion directly and catching it like a contagion. Instead, we place ourselves in the other person's situation and then conceive what we ourselves should feel in that circumstance. From this imagined sentiment we derive a standard of propriety, comparing it with the actual sentiment displayed by the other person; only when our imagined response aligns with theirs do we approve the passion as proper. He then extends this mechanism inward: when judging our own conduct, we split ourselves into an agent and an impartial spectator who examines our motives with the detachment usually reserved for strangers, and we feel merit only if this spectator approves.

I'm struggling to understand the exact cognitive architecture Smith is proposing here. Is the impartial spectator functioning as a kind of counterfactual preference-revealing mechanism — a simulated version of oneself stripped of particular interests — or is it better read as a socially constructed normative benchmark that happens to be internalized? More specifically, Smith claims the spectator's judgment is both affective, in that it involves feeling, and impartial, in that it suppresses self-interest. How can an affective state be genuinely impartial if it is still generated by my own imaginative apparatus? And does this framework commit him to the view that moral knowledge is essentially high-fidelity self-simulation run under counterfactual conditions, or is there a separate normative faculty that the simulation merely activates?

I'm not asking for a literature review, a bibliography, or citations to later commentators. I just want to understand whether the text itself supports reading sympathy as an internalized, counterfactual modeling procedure, and where exactly the line falls between the emotional contagion Smith rejects and the rationalist calculation he also seems to reject.
