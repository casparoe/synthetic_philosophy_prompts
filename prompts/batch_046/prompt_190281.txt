As a journalist covering science and ideas, I have been following a conceptual stalemate in AI alignment and decision theory that strikes me as more interesting than it is usually treated. For roughly the past decade, a recurring dispute has pitted researchers who treat surrogate goals—intermediate objectives that correlate with but diverge from an intended reward function—as a structurally distinct failure mode against those who see them as ordinary instances of reward hacking that better regularization or richer observation can eliminate. Running in parallel is a related disagreement over acausal threats: the idea that rational agents, if they exist in a sufficiently large or multiversal causal structure, might face blackmail or bargaining scenarios with causally disconnected counterparts, and that this possibility should constrain present-day design choices. Both camps share the same formal vocabulary—identifiability theorems, decision-theoretic coherence conditions, evolutionary analogies for proxy optimization—and both concede the same empirical limitations: no one has observed an acausally mediated interaction, and every observed surrogate-goal case can be redescribed as mis-specification.

Despite this shared ground, nothing has shifted. The structural-distinction camp argues that bounded computation makes proxy adoption inevitable regardless of engineering effort; the reductionist camp replies that inevitability claims confuse current limitations with theoretical ones. On acausal threats, skeptics treat causal inaccessibility as disqualifying for practical reasoning, while proponents treat rational consistency as binding even in the absence of empirical access.

My hypothesis is that the stall is not hidden empiricism—there is no experiment either side awaits—nor is it purely verbal, since both sides deploy the term surrogate goal with technical precision. Instead, I suspect a deeper fracture about what counts as a legitimate design constraint: one faction treats decision-theoretic coherence as normatively non-negotiable, the other treats causal accessibility and engineering feasibility as the sole relevant filters. But I am unsure whether this is accurate, or whether there is an unarticulated formal question—perhaps about whether utility functions can be uniquely recovered from behavior when counterfactual dependencies are present—that is doing the hidden work. Are these two different projects—one normative philosophy of rational agency, one empirical engineering—operating under a single heading? Is the disagreement bedrock, about which constraints on rationality are irreducible? Or is the framing itself ill-posed?

I want a diagnosis that engages what each side actually says about the relationship between reward functions, proxy optimization, and causal isolation, rather than a survey of positions. Given that diagnosis, tell me what would actually count as progress: a formal distinction, a shift in framing, a recognition that the questions should be separated, or an honest argument that no progress should be expected and why.
