As a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, I am trying to think clearly about a governance design problem that feels less like an engineering trade-off and more like a question about justice. I maintain an open-source contribution framework that automatically restricts maintainer privileges when a contributor accumulates a defined number of minor policy violations. Each violation—an incomplete changelog, a missed review comment, an unannotated dependency update—is treated as a fungible token; context, severity, and intent are deliberately excluded from the scoring logic. Once the threshold is crossed, the revocation is irreversible and executed without human review, on the principle that predictability is the only credible deterrent across a distributed team.

Last quarter, a maintainer who has contributed core modules for over five years crossed that threshold. The violations were genuine, but they clustered during a two-month period when he was the primary caregiver for a parent in hospice. The mistakes were careless rather than malicious, caused no actual damage to users or the repository, and were corrected immediately upon discovery. Under the rules, he was permanently suspended.

Some colleagues argue that the suspension is unfair because it ignores the moral weight of the particular acts: he did not deserve to lose years of accumulated trust over errors that were essentially circumstantial, and a just sanction should look backward at culpability, proportionality, and the specific circumstances that shaped his conduct. Others respond that the purpose of the mechanism is not to settle a moral account with this individual but to preserve the institutional conditions that keep hundreds of contributors diligent; allowing an exception based on inferred hardship or good character would reintroduce the very arbitrariness the automation was designed to eliminate, undermining the forward-looking protective function of the rule.

I can see the force of both positions, but I lack the conceptual vocabulary to map the disagreement precisely. Could you help me identify the underlying philosophical structure—specifically, whether the fairness of an institutional punishment is determined primarily by what the punished party deserved in light of the particular offense and its context, or primarily by the effects of enforcing the rule uniformly on the behavior of the community? Furthermore, could you advise how one might design a governance mechanism that respects the intuition that sanctions should be proportionate to the moral character of the conduct, without collapsing back into the kind of discretionary, case-by-case judgment that makes automated governance unreliable? I am not looking for generic workplace mediation, but for a principled way to think about whether an algorithmic rule can ever be both consistently enforced and genuinely just.
