In the course of drafting a novel in which a physician must allocate a scarce antidote between one intimate acquaintance and five strangers, I have found myself returning to the long-standing philosophical dispute over whether the numbers should count in rescue cases—the debate crystallized by John Taurek’s argument that, faced with a choice between saving one life or five, one ought not to prefer the greater number but rather to distribute one’s concern equally, perhaps by lot. For decades, critics have responded that this neglects the multiplicity of distinct claims held by each of the five, or that it confuses the weight of a reason with the number of its bearers, while defenders maintain that adding lives does not compound the moral urgency of saving them. Despite these well-rehearsed moves, the literature seems to me to circle without convergence: neither camp denies the equal intrinsic value of each life, neither disputes the badness of death, and yet the disagreement persists as if anchored in something deeper than a miscalculation of aggregate welfare. My working hypothesis is that the impasse is not genuinely about aggregation at all, but about whether moral reasons under conditions of scarcity are comparable across persons in the way economic goods are—that is, whether a reason to save one person can be stacked against a reason to save another without committing a category error between atomic obligations and scalable values. Yet I remain unsure whether this diagnosis captures the real fracture, or whether the sides are instead answering different questions—one about the formal logic of comparative judgment, the other about the phenomenology of choice—under the single heading of “counting numbers.” I want an honest structural diagnosis rather than a verdict: is this disagreement verbal, substantive, or rooted in bedrock intuitions that neither side can argue for? Does it conceal a hidden formal question about the transitivity or commensurability of rescue obligations, or is it a case of normative ethics and moral psychology using the same vocabulary for different projects? And given your diagnosis, what would actually constitute progress—a refined distinction between scalar and vector reasons, a reconceptualization of the rescue scenario that dissolves the framing, or an acknowledgment that no further argument should be expected? Please do not survey the literature or offer a bibliography; I am asking for an analysis of the disagreement’s architecture, supported by what each side actually claims, together with an assessment of whether anything short of a shift in foundational commitments could move it.
