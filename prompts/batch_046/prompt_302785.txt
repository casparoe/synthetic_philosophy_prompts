I'm in this course on collective responsability and we started talking about the Fischer controversy - like Fritz Fischer's claim that Germany actually planned WWI on purpose, not just slid into it. That made me think about the Versailles Treaty with its 'war guilt' clause that made Germany pay reperations for the whole conflict.

There's this practice in international treaty-writing where you stick blame for a war onto an entire nation-state, as if 'Germany' is one agent that both caused the war and owes money for it. So what philosophical commitments does that practice actually presuppose? Does it need nations to have moral agency and intentions like individual people, or can it work with some thinner institutional story? And I keep noticing this slippage: the treaty talks about causing the war (causal), being guilty (moral), and paying reparations (legal/financial), but I'm not sure these all need the same theory behind them. If we replaced whatever view is doing the work with a purely distributive model - where only the specific decision-makers are blamed and costs get assigned by some other rule - which parts of the treaty practice would fall apart and which could survive? Is this consistent with how we treat corporations, or does it require something stronger? I feel like I'm mixing history and philosophy but I want to know if the practice is actually coherent, or if it's quietly smuggling in different accounts of blame without noticing.
