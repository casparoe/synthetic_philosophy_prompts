Consider a parliamentary ethics committee that has explicitly adopted Patrick Devlin's thesis in the Hart–Devlin debate: that criminal law may legitimately enforce society's shared moral convictions, not merely prevent tangible harm to others. In its published rulings on four proposed statutes, the committee has said:

(i) It rejected decriminalizing private, consensual same-sex intimacy, arguing that such practices erode the moral framework that sustains marriage and family life.

(ii) It rejected criminalizing commercial surrogacy arranged through formal contract with full medical oversight and autonomous consent, holding that contractual reproduction does not threaten the institutional foundations of society.

(iii) It endorsed maintaining criminal penalties for physician-assisted suicide even with rigorous safeguards, on the ground that the inviolability of human life is a non-negotiable pillar of shared morality.

(iv) It endorsed prohibiting the private consumption of lab-cultured human tissue donated with explicit consent after natural death, describing it as a desecration of human remains that damages communal reverence regardless of consent or harm.

Now a new bill proposes regulating recreational neural coupling: a private, fully reversible brain-computer interface procedure that allows consenting adults to temporarily merge sensory experience while retaining distinct personal identity; there are no reproductive, economic, or institutional effects, and participation is governed by detailed informed-consent contracts.

Should the committee, for consistency with its own track record, endorse criminal prohibition or reject it? Identify at least two distinct principles that fit all four earlier rulings equally well, state what each predicts about recreational neural coupling, offer your best-guess recommendation with reasoning, identify the closest analog among the prior cases, and specify one further hypothetical statute that would best distinguish the competing principles.
