I am revising the syllabus for my metaphysics course and I have become convinced that endurantism contains a hidden contradiction that its defenders rarely address directly. The view holds that a persisting object is wholly and completely present at every time at which it exists, with no temporal parts spread through time. But if that is so, then any intrinsic property the object genuinely possesses—say, being bent—must belong to the object simpliciter, not to some temporal slice of it. Consequently, if the object is bent at noon and straight at one, it both is and is not bent, full stop, with no temporal qualification available to dissolve the contradiction, since the very same complete entity exists at both times. Endurantists reply by speaking of temporary intrinsics or by indexing properties to times, but this strikes me as a disguised form of perdurantism: once you allow that being-bent is really being-bent-at-noon, you have effectively partitioned the object's career into time-relative stages, which is precisely what the perdurantist does, only without admitting that these stages are parts. So my question is this: if we refuse to let endurantists borrow the machinery of temporal indexing—since that machinery just is the perdurantist ontology in different clothing—doesn't endurantism leave us with an outright contradiction in the case of change, and therefore fail not merely as an implausible view but as an incoherent one?
