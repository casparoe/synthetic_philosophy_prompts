Write a sestina—six six-line stanzas with rotating end-words, followed by a three-line envoi that gathers all six—on the philosophical question of whether emotions are judgments, perceptions, or raw bodily feelings. The six end-words for the first stanza, in order, are: judgment, tremor, world, belief, flesh, and sight; rotate them through the remaining stanzas according to the standard sestina pattern. Do not resolve the debate; let the form do the structural work. The first stanza should present the judgmental view: an emotion is an evaluative belief directed at the world. The second stanza should introduce the problem of recalcitrance—someone can judge that no danger is present, yet the body still trembles—forcing the vocabulary of belief and judgment to share space with flesh and tremor. The middle stanzas should rotate the theories so that each is forced to borrow the others’ terms: let the perceptual view claim that we see danger in the world as we see color, carrying tremor and belief; let the feeling view reduce world-directedness to a pattern of bodily awareness, carrying sight and judgment. By the envoi, all six words should appear together in suspension, implying that any adequate account must hold judgment, perception, and feeling simultaneously rather than reducing one to another. The poem should be philosophically precise enough that a reader familiar with cognitivist, perceptual, and feeling theories of emotion recognizes the positions, but it must contain no proper names, no citations, and no references to specific texts or authors. Write in blank verse or tightly controlled free verse, approximately thirty-nine lines, with rhythmic discipline appropriate to the form’s repetitions.
