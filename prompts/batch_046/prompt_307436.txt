In the debate over whether artifacts have politics — the long-running dispute between those who hold that technological objects such as reactors, algorithms, or infrastructure embed specific power relations by design, and those who hold that any political effect is entirely mediated by social context, institutional framing, and interpretive practice — progress seems to have stalled despite broad agreement on the empirical history of particular technologies. Both camps now routinely accept that social forces shape design and that artifacts produce differential impacts; yet they continue to talk past each other when one side claims that a given system requires hierarchical control and the other replies that it only requires it under contingent organizational arrangements. My hypothesis is that the stall is not primarily empirical but ontological and methodological: one side treats political properties as intrinsic, design-locked features analogous to functional capacities, while the other treats them as extrinsic, relational properties that depend on networks of users, regulators, and meanings. Under this reading, the disagreement may be partly verbal — both sides might accept the same case studies yet disagree about whether to classify them as political artifacts — but there may also be a hidden dispute about what counts as data, for instance whether failed or abandoned technologies should weigh equally with stabilized ones, and whether normative assessments of justice are being smuggled into descriptive claims about determination. Please diagnose the nature of this impasse: is it verbal, substantive about the ontology of affordances and properties, or a case of two research programs — one normative and one descriptive — operating under a single heading? Ground your diagnosis in what each side actually commits to rather than in labels. Then specify what would count as progress: a precise conceptual distinction between intrinsic and relational properties applied to artifacts, a formal framework for modeling artifact-property relations, a revised standard of evidence that resolves which cases are decisive, or an argument that the core question is ill-posed because it conflates causal determination with normative evaluation? In your answer, separate what is settled among specialists — for example, that technology and society are co-constitutive — from what remains genuinely contested, and do not present any one contested interpretation, such as the intrinsic-properties reading, as the consensus view.
