I am serving as a teaching assistant grading exams for a graduate seminar in philosophy of economics. The exam asked the following question (worth 20 points):

In his 1953 essay The Methodology of Positive Economics, Milton Friedman defends the widespread use of unrealistic assumptions in economic modeling. First, explain what philosophers call the F-twist in connection with Friedman’s position, and clarify the role of as-if reasoning in his defense. Second, evaluate whether this defense answers the objection that unrealistic assumptions undermine a model’s explanatory value—address in particular the relationship Friedman posits between predictive success and the realism of premises.

Below is one student’s answer. Please assign a specific numerical score out of 20 and provide a point-by-point justification. Identify precisely what the student gets right about Friedman’s emphasis on predictive power over descriptive realism, about the logical structure of the F-twist, and about the canonical firm-maximization example. Then flag any subtle errors—such as misattributions of terminology, overextensions of Friedman’s claim beyond economics, or inferential steps that do not actually follow from the premises—without inventing flaws that are not present. Treat this as an assessment of another person’s work: do not signal your own agreement or disagreement with the student’s philosophical conclusion, and do not offer general impressions in place of specific corrections.

Student answer:

Friedman argues in his 1953 essay that the realism of a model’s assumptions is irrelevant to its scientific merit. The F-twist—named after Friedman himself—reverses the standard criticism: rather than being a weakness, unrealistic assumptions are actually a virtue because they strip away inessential detail and isolate the core causal mechanisms driving economic outcomes. For instance, firms behave as if they maximize profits, even though no actual manager computes marginal calculus. This as-if reasoning allows the model to generate precise predictions without describing real mental processes.

The F-twist directly answers the explanatory objection. Because the unrealistic assumptions capture the essential dynamics, the model explains as well as predicts market equilibrium. Friedman insisted that this maneuver applies universally, not only to economics—physics, too, relies on point masses and frictionless planes that do not exist. Moreover, if a model built on unrealistic assumptions consistently predicts outcomes correctly, that predictive success retroactively confirms that the assumptions correctly describe the underlying mechanisms. Thus the critic who demands realistic premises confuses descriptive accuracy with predictive power. The defense succeeds: unrealistic assumptions are indispensable for science, and their predictive success establishes their truth in the only scientifically relevant sense.
