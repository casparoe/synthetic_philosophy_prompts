Two colleagues in a philosophy of mind study group recently argued about the distinction between the hard problem of consciousness and what is sometimes called the meta-problem of consciousness — the question of why we think there is an explanatory gap between physical processes and subjective experience in the first place. The first colleague, Mara, claimed that resolving the meta-problem is essentially a job for cognitive neuroscience and psychology: once we map the specific neural and functional mechanisms — she referred to them collectively as the phenomenal-judgment module — that generate reports of an inexplicable qualitative residue, the mystery dissolves, because we can then understand those reports as outputs of an introspective architecture rather than as veridical detections of some ontological rupture. The second colleague, Jonah, replied that this mistakes explaining the causal origin of a belief for explaining its epistemic justification, and that in other domains, such as evolutionary explanations of religious belief, giving a mechanistic account of belief-formation often undermines rather than supports the belief’s claim to truth. He argued that consciousness is asymmetrical in this respect: if the phenomenal-judgment module is genuinely tracking real phenomenal properties, then explaining its operation actually presupposes that those properties exist, which means the meta-problem cannot be solved without first taking a substantive stand on the hard problem itself. Mara answered that Jonah is quietly importing phenomenal realism, since an illusionist can solve the meta-problem by showing the module is systematically misfiring, while a realist can solve it by showing the module is well-calibrated to its target — so in either metaphysical scenario the psychological explanation stands independently. Jonah then countered that this proves there is no unified meta-problem at all, but rather two distinct problems — one for realists and one for illusionists — and that framing the issue as a single meta-problem therefore assumes a false unity that collapses once we make our metaphysical commitments explicit.
