I want to read the novel through the philosophical debate over whether the Enlightenment was a source of progress or of domination, particularly as articulated by critics who treat instrumental reason as inseparable from control over both nature and the human subject. Where does this idea illuminate specific scenes — for instance, Victor’s refusal to grant the Creature a name or a fixed social place, the Creature’s self-education through texts such as *Paradise Lost*, or his demand for a female companion — and where does the novel resist being reduced to an allegory of domination? Does Shelley’s portrayal of the Creature’s moral reasoning and linguistic capacity suggest that Enlightenment capacities can be detached from dominating structures, or does the narrative ultimately vindicate the critique? I would like a reading that takes both the philosophical debate and the novel seriously in both directions, that does not distort either into mere illustration, and that concludes with what the work adds to the conceptual argument that abstract reasoning alone cannot provide.
