I'm working through the metaphysics of dispositions for a philosophy of science seminar, and I have built an argument that seems to prove far too much—specifically, that there are no dispositions at all—but I cannot locate the precise slip. Here are the steps as I have them:

(1) To genuinely possess a disposition is to be such that, in the appropriate stimulus conditions, one would manifest the characteristic behavior.
(2) Yet for any system—whether a crystal of sugar or a trained neural network—we can specify interfering conditions (masks, finks, or adversarial prompt injections) that prevent that manifestation even when the stimulus conditions fully obtain.
(3) So if the system genuinely possessed the disposition, it should still manifest in the presence of these interferences; since it does not, it never genuinely possessed the disposition to begin with.
(4) This reasoning applies universally: solubility can be masked by encapsulation, a model's reasoning capacity can be masked by a specific input framing, and so on.
(5) Therefore, no system ever genuinely possesses any disposition; there are only actual performances and the counterfactual regularities we project onto them.
(6) But this eliminates the distinction between a genuinely soluble substance and one that merely happens to dissolve in nearby worlds, and between a model with a stable capability and one that merely produces correct outputs in training distributions.
(7) Since that distinction is indispensable both in chemistry and in AI safety evaluation, premise (1) must be false.

I suspect the error lies between (2) and (3)—perhaps I am conflating the presence of a disposition with its unimpeded exercise—but I am unsure how to repair (1) without turning dispositions into mysterious, unobservable powers. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? In particular, does saving dispositions require accepting that they can exist in a permanently masked or finked state, and if so, how does that affect our ability to attribute or test for them in black-box systems?

Please separate what is settled about the logic of finks and masks—for instance, that they defeat simple conditional analyses of dispositions—from what is genuinely contested among metaphysicians and philosophers of science, such as whether a powers ontology, a revised conditional analysis, or some other response is correct. Do not present any one of these contested responses as the consensus view.
