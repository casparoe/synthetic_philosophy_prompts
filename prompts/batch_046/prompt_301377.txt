Exam question: In Galileo’s 1615 letter to the Grand Duchess Christina (commonly called the Letter to the Grand Duchess Christina), he confronts the tension between Copernican astronomy and passages such as Joshua 10:12–13. Some scholars describe his hermeneutic as an epistemic partition: the view that science and Scripture occupy non-overlapping semantic domains, so that biblical descriptions of nature carry no assessable physical content. Others read him as defending only an institutional separation: ecclesiastical authority does not extend to natural philosophy, yet Scripture may still contain physically determinate descriptions that science can evaluate. Analyze Galileo’s treatment of the Joshua miracle in light of this distinction. Does he deny that the passage asserts anything physically determinable (the epistemic-partition reading), or does he allow that a literal reading makes a physically determinate claim but insist that such a claim is not binding because Church authority is restricted (the institutional-separation reading)? In your answer, address whether Galileo’s appeal to Augustine and to the principle that demonstrated truths should guide exegesis supports one reading over the other, and consider whether his position permits science to correct scriptural interpretation in principle, or merely renders particular literal interpretations implausible.

Student answer: Galileo clearly adopts the institutional-separation position rather than the epistemic-partition one. He holds that the Bible does speak about the physical world—Joshua’s command that the Sun stand still presupposes a geocentric cosmology—but its purpose is salvation, not scientific instruction. Because the Church lacks astronomical competence, theologians should not impose a literal interpretation once natural philosophers have demonstrated its falsehood. Thus Galileo allows that the literal sense of Joshua is physically false—the Sun does not actually halt in space—but this does not damage Scripture’s authority, since the Holy Spirit accommodated the wording to the capacity of common people. This is not the same as claiming that the Bible is semantically void about nature; rather, its authority is confined to faith and morals. Galileo invokes Augustine’s principle that if reason demonstrates something about nature, we ought to revise our interpretation of Scripture rather than reject the demonstration. From this he derives an asymmetrical rule: demonstrated science can directly correct biblical exegesis, but theologians have no reciprocal right to veto scientific conclusions. His view is therefore not a modern non-overlapping-magisteria model like Gould’s, because the domains do intersect, with science holding priority in physical matters. His analysis of Joshua supports this: he shows that a literal geocentric reading generates physical contradictions—if the Sun stops while the other planets continue in their orbits, the cosmic order collapses—so the passage must be read non-literally under the guidance of Copernican astronomy. Consequently, Galileo permits direct correction of interpretation by natural knowledge whenever the two appear to conflict.
