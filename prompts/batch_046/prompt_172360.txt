Half the members want to make it optional because people have kids now and weird jobs, but the old guard keeps pulling out Chesterton's fence — don't tear down a fence until you know why it was put up — and tying it to Burkean ideas about inherited wisdom being smarter than individual reason. They use it to say we shouldn't mess with the shift until we understand what social function it serves, and that feels like it's carrying the whole argument by itself.

Can you audit the analogy properly? I want the correspondence table — the fence is the Sunday shift, the builder is maybe the founding members in '78, the field is the coop? — then the places it breaks. Also, fences stay put if you leave them alone, but if we keep the shift and nobody shows up, that's not stability, that's rot — so the leave-it-alone conclusion doesn't map cleanly. And what does Burke's partnership across generations actually look like here? Our tradition is only forty years old and mostly bureacratic inertia, not some ancient social contract.

I need to know whether the fence image is actually defending the shift, or whether it's just smuggling in a Burkean conclusion about tradition that doesn't apply because our institution is young and the rule might just be leftover paperwork. Where does the analogy break in ways that matter specifically for reform debates? What gets imported about the value of the whole versus the parts? And if the fence analogy collapses, is there a better one for institutional change — maybe immune systems or language evolution? Give me the mapping, the disanalogies that survive the audit, and whether the conservative warning still stands once the fence is gone.
