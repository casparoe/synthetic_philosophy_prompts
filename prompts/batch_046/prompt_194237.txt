I am drafting an essay that reads Marx's fourfold division of alienation—alienation from the product, from the productive act, from species-being, and from other workers—not as a catalogue of psychological injuries but as manifestations of a single structural failure I am calling teleological misalignment. By this I mean a systematic decoupling of an agent's proximate instrumental goals from the constitutive ends that would make those goals rationally intelligible as parts of a coherent practical identity.

My working notes are as follows.

First, alienation from the product and from the act of production are usually treated as separate: the worker does not own the object produced, and the labor process itself is externally directed. I want to argue that both are symptoms of a deeper misalignment between the worker's immediate reason for acting (wage acquisition) and the kind of activity that could justify itself to the worker as an expression of developed capacity. The worker's instrumental reasoning is intact—she calculates efficiently—but it is structurally isolated from her practical identity.

Second, alienation from species-being is the crux, but I find Marx's essentialist language about "human nature" as productive self-realization both obscure and vulnerable to modern critiques. I propose replacing it with a thinner, non-essentialist concept: developmental plasticity, understood as the capacity to acquire, integrate, and exercise new skills in ways that reshape one's practical self-understanding. Alienation then consists not in failing to realize a fixed essence, but in being confined to repetitive, non-integrative labor that blocks this plasticity.

Third, I want to maintain a strict separation between exploitation and alienation. Exploitation, on my reading, is the distributive mechanism—surplus extraction through the wage relation—while alienation is the structural-phenomenological result of the labor process being organized in a way that severs instrumental success from practical development. They are causally linked in capitalism, but conceptually independent: one could imagine non-exploitative but highly alienating labor regimes, and perhaps exploitative regimes where alienation is minimized.

Fourth, I worry that this framework smuggles in assumptions from modern philosophy of action and cognitive psychology—particularly the idea that practical rationality requires integration between means and constitutive ends. I am unsure whether Marx's text supports such a normative loading, or whether I am projecting onto it.

I would appreciate comments on the following. Is the teleological-misalignment reading textually defensible, or does it distort Marx's materialist commitments by importing an overly rationalist vocabulary? Does replacing species-being with developmental plasticity preserve the critical force of the argument, or does it collapse Marx's critique into a liberal concern with human-capital formation? Finally, is the conceptual separation of exploitation and alienation illuminating, or does it artificially divide what Marx intended as an inseparable unity of economic extraction and lived degradation? I am especially interested in whether any part of this reconstruction depends on claims that are either trivially true or clearly false once separated from Marx's original dialectical framing.
