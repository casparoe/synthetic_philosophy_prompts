I have been working through some introductory metaethics and legal philosophy with a small study group, and we keep encountering a recurring argumentative move that none of us can place precisely within the standard taxonomy of reasons. The pattern involves appeals to what we have taken to calling, provisionally, orphan norms—statements that something is required, valuable, or rationally demanded, yet there appears to be no being for whom it is required, valuable, or rationally demanded. We have noticed it in environmental ethics, where someone asserts that a particular biome ought to persist even in a counterfactual scenario with no sentient observers at all; in philosophy of mathematics, where it is said that there is reason to accept a true theorem irrespective of whether any mind exists to appreciate it; and in jurisprudence, where scholars occasionally claim that a legal system possesses internal reasons to maintain coherence even if every citizen and official remains entirely unaware of the contradiction. In each instance, the speaker seems to invoke a genuinely normative consideration—something that genuinely counts in favor—rather than merely describing a causal regularity or expressing an aesthetic preference. At the same time, the consideration is not agent-neutral in the familiar sense, because agent-neutral reasons still apply to agents, only impartially; here, there does not seem to be any agent, individual or collective, to whom the reason applies.

We have considered whether this is simply a loose way of speaking about collective goods, about objective values independent of valuers, or about institutional reasons that belong to an organized body, but in our examples there is no collective agent present, and treating the claim as pure value-talk seems to miss the specifically normative, action-guiding force being asserted. Does this configuration have an established philosophical name—perhaps relating to discussions of free-floating rationales, or to debates about normative realism in the absence of reasoners? If there is a settled literature, how exactly does the concept differ from standard agent-neutral reasons, from institutional reasons belonging to a collective entity, and from mere descriptions of what is causally or structurally stable? If the concept remains unsettled or only partially mapped, how should we delineate its boundaries: what distinguishes a genuine orphan norm from a metaphorical extension of personal reasons, from an error that confuses being good for something with being good that something occurs, and from a disguised claim about the interests of possible future agents? We are not looking for a bare label; we are trying to understand exactly what the phenomenon is, where its edges lie, and which neighboring concepts it threatens to collapse into.
