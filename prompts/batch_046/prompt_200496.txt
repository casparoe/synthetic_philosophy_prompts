I'm in a course on collective responsibility and we've been fighting about whether Fischer's thesis actually pins anything on modern Germany, or if it just blames dead people. (1) Fischer showed the Kaiserreich's leadership in 1914 wasn't dragged into war; they pursued it deliberately via the blank check, the September Program, and the conscious risk of continental escalation. So the outbreak wasn't an accident for which no one is blamworthy.

(2) Call this the institutional-continuity principle: if collective agent C is responsible for X, and C's legal-bureaucratic identity survives into successor S without S explicitly repudiating the specific decision behind X, then S inherits C's responsibility.

(3) The Federal Republic never issued a formal repudiaton of the 1914 decision to escalate; it repudiated Nazism, but left the Kaiserreich's war choice untouched in its constitutional self-understanding.

(4) So modern Germany inherits blame for 1914.

(5) But apply (2) consistently: the French Third Republic's general-staff traditions flowed into later armies, and no postwar government ever formally repudiated Plan XVII's role in escalation. So modern France inherits a slice.

(6) In fact every European state with bureaucratic roots in 1914-era ministries inherits guilt, unless they passed a specific act of repudiation — which none did.

(7) That makes collective blame for WWI so distributed that singling out Germany, which is what Fischer is usually read as doing, becomes arbitrary.

(8) So either (2) is false, or Fischer's specific indictment collapses into a universal indictment of European institutional memory.
