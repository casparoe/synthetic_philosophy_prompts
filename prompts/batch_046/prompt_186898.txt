I am a doctoral student in social epistemology designing a diagnostic handout for a graduate seminar on peer disagreement. The standard literature frames the debate as a binary between conciliationism and steadfastness, but I want an instrument that maps a respondent’s actual commitments by running them through concrete cases rather than asking them to self-identify with a label.

Please construct a branching taxonomy—presented as a decision tree with internal nodes as splitting claims and terminal nodes as positions—that classifies views on epistemic peer disagreement according to the following specifications.

Root case: Two climate scientists with identical credentials and access to the same raw dataset disagree about whether a particular feedback loop dominates in a given model; each assigns different weights to structural uncertainty, and neither can identify a localized error in the other’s reasoning. From this root, build branches that ask:

(1) Does the bare fact of peer dissent provide direct first-order evidence against one’s original belief, or only higher-order evidence about one’s own reasoning reliability?

(2) Is it permissible to retain one’s belief without articulating an independent asymmetry (e.g., a hidden prior, a differential weighting of model types), or must retention always be backed by a further reason?

(3) Should the appropriate epistemic response vary by domain—so that disagreement about formal proof, empirical forecasting, and aesthetic judgment trigger different norms—or should one principle govern all domains?

(4) Is there a numerical or confidence threshold (e.g., three dissenting peers, or peer confidence exceeding one’s own) that mechanically forces belief revision, or is any threshold merely heuristic?

(5) When retention is permitted, is the retained belief held with the same confidence, reduced confidence, or suspended judgment about the peer’s rationality?

The leaves must include at least: strong conciliationism (always split the difference); moderate conciliationism (revision required only in high-uncertainty or high-stakes domains); steadfastness with articulated reasons; steadfastness as default retention; and agnostic suspension of judgment on the first-order claim. Include at least one unnamed combination—such as a view that treats disagreement as direct first-order evidence in empirical domains but as purely higher-order in formal domains—and provide one sentence diagnosing why this combination might be unstable or unexplored.

For each leaf, state in one sentence its most awkward practical commitment in a real-world setting (e.g., jury deliberation, peer review, or clinical diagnosis). Flag any pair of positions that the tree’s questions cannot separate, and specify what additional case distinction would be needed to split them.

Keep the tree to no more than seven internal nodes and ten leaves, suitable for a seminar handout. Every question must be answerable without prior knowledge of technical labels; do not presuppose familiarity with specific named views in the literature, and do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citation requests. The instrument should reveal whether a respondent’s intuitions fall within named positions or into underexplored logical space, particularly around domain-sensitive forms of steadfastness.
