Since becoming a parent six months ago, I have found the standard existential-risk account of human extinction—quantifying the lost well-being of all future generations—intellectually correct but existentially hollow. I keep circling back to Scheffler's afterlife conjecture: the idea that the point of many present commitments—raising a child, writing, building institutions—depends on an implicit assumption that humanity will continue, not merely that future people will be happy. Among philosophers and risk researchers I talk with, the debate about why extinction is genuinely bad beyond the immediate deaths has stalled for years. One camp treats Scheffler's point as a psychological quirk about motivation; another treats it as a deep structural feature of practical reason, where present value is transitive, anchored to an inheriting future rather than self-contained. My working theory is that the stall is not empirical but categorical: the sides differ about whether "badness" here must be reducible to states of sentient beings, or whether it can include the collapse of the narrative conditions that make current projects intelligible as projects. Is that right? I want a committed diagnosis, not a survey: is this disagreement verbal, about standards of explanation, about hidden metaphysical commitments to presentism in value theory, or about something else?
