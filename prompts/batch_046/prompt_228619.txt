I'm working through Skolem's paradox for a philosophy of mathematics seminar, and I've sketched an argument that now feels suspicious because its conclusion seems to dissolve cardinal arithmetic altogether. (1) By the downward Löwenheim-Skolem theorem, ZFC has a countable model M. (2) Inside M there is a set R that M takes to be the real numbers, and M proves that R is uncountable. (3) From the external viewpoint, M itself is countable, so R — being contained in M — is also countable, and there exists a bijection between R and ω in the ambient set theory. (4) If mathematical existence and cardinality are absolute — if a set genuinely is or is not uncountable regardless of the model in which we evaluate it — then R both is and is not uncountable, which is contradictory. (5) To avoid contradiction, either cardinal properties are model-relative (R is uncountable-in-M but countable-outside-M with no conflict), or we must deny that ZFC correctly captures mathematical reality. (6) The model-relativity option, however, seems to collapse all substantive distinctions: if "uncountability" is only an internal predicate with no external grip, then every cardinal claim becomes a claim about how a particular model is arranged rather than about the size of any actual collection. (7) Consequently, every consistent first-order theory describes structures that are "really" countable from some outside view, so the distinction between ℵ₀ and ℵ₁ becomes purely notational and cardinal arithmetic loses objective content. (8) This proves far more than Skolem's paradox requires; it eliminates all non-trivial size distinctions in mathematics. Where does the argument slip? I would prefer to keep (3) and (5) if possible, but I am willing to weaken (4) or reject the move from (6) to (7). Is the error the assumption in (4) that absolute cardinality is required, or is there a hidden mistake in (3) regarding whether the external bijection is available inside the relevant meta-theory?
