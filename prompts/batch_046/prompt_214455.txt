I'm running a 90-minute grad seminar on Dewey's experimentalism—about 10 students, mostly familiar with Democracy and Education but not with The Public and Its Problems. I want a structured simulation that lets them feel why Dewey thinks democracy is a mode of social inquiry rather than preference aggregation, and specifically why he thinks a "public" only comes into being when people discover shared indirect consequences through communication. I want them to experience the failure mode where voting happens before those hidden connections surface, so they end up with a decision that feels democratically legitimate but lacks what Dewey calls warranted assertibility—the idea that the outcome is only justified by the quality of the inquiry process, not by matching anyone's prior opinions.

Design the activity: rules, roles, materials, and a payoff scheme that doesn't need real money. They should recieve hidden cards representing indirect consequences that only activate if shared in discussion, plus a mechanism to either vote immediately or enter an inquiry phase first. The rules should be arranged so they run into the coordination failure themselves—probably they split into subgroups with partial information and have to decide on a communal resource allocation—before anyone names Dewey's distinction between crowd and public.

Then a debrief that maps specific moments in the simulation onto parts of Dewey's view, says what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (I assume they'll vote too quickly?), and is frank about which feature of Dewey's account—maybe the long-term historical development of social intelligence—the game can't reproduce and I'll have to supply in lecture. Don't lecture them up front, and don't say the phrase warranted assertibility until afterwards. Also avoid giving them a reading list; they should come in cold except for basic Dewey familiarity.
