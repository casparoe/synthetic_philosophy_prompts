I am a doctoral student in political philosophy working on the phenomenology of collective judgment, and I have been discussing with colleagues how to teach Simone Weil's critique of political parties without reducing it to the banal observation that partisanship biases reasoning. Weil's argument — developed especially in her posthumous essay on the abolition of parties — is not merely that parties produce poor outcomes, but that they structurally suppress what she calls attention (l'attention): the disciplined, receptive orientation toward justice that requires the suspension of one's own will-to-power. The difficulty is that this sounds like moralising unless students feel how a collective identity can colonise their own cognitive processes before they have consciously endorsed anything.

Please design a runnable classroom activity or structured deliberation for a workshop of about ten graduate students, lasting roughly ninety minutes, that makes participants experience this colonisation from the inside. The setting should be a seminar room with no prior lecture; participants should not hear Weil's name or the phrase political party until the debrief. Design the rules, roles, or payoffs so that the group naturally fractures into two factions with assigned colours or symbols, and each faction must defend a sequence of policy positions that become progressively more distant from what the members privately believe, because loyalty to the faction's strategic success is structurally rewarded. The idea should emerge from the dynamics: by the end, participants should have noticed that they have stopped asking Is this just? and started asking Will this help us win? — and that this shift felt like a natural adaptation rather than a conscious betrayal. Include a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto Weil's distinction between the gravity of collective passion and the grace of attentive judgment, indicates what will predictably go wrong (for instance, one faction dominating the room or participants breaking character), and is frank about which dimension of Weil's argument — her specifically religious or mystical framing of attention — the exercise cannot reproduce and must be supplied by the facilitator. Please provide your answer in British English, and avoid bibliographies, reading lists, or direct quotations from Weil's texts; paraphrase her concepts in your own wording.
