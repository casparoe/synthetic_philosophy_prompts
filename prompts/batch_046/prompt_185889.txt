I am preparing the interview guide for a podcast Q&A with the author of a recent monograph that extends Karl Jaspers’ fourfold taxonomy of guilt—criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical—to intergenerational collective responsibility for systemic ecological extraction and colonial-era resource depletion. The subject has become personally urgent: I recently served on a citizens’ deliberative panel assessing whether present-day residents of my country bear any meaningful form of guilt for long-past environmental harm done in the nation’s name, and I found that Jaspers’ categories, designed for the immediate aftermath of totalitarian crime, strain badly when applied to diffuse structural damage spread across centuries and mediated by institutions rather than individual intent.

I need a sequence of interview questions that does not rehearse the taxonomy from scratch but presses exactly where the scholarly literature presses—anticipating the standard reply that political guilt is collective yet non-distributive across citizens; that metaphysical guilt risks collapsing into universal human solidarity and therefore offers no practical guidance for policy or reparation; and that extending these categories to ecological harm moralizes history without yielding clear agents. Each question should set up or include a direct follow-up that makes the usual deflection progressively harder to maintain. Build toward a final question asking what specific conceptual argument or piece of empirical-historical evidence could actually move the author to abandon the extension of one of these four categories to intergenerational ecological harm. Keep the focus tightly on the content of the position; avoid generic biographical or methodological openings, and do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
