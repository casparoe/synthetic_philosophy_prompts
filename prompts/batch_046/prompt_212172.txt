I am preparing a conceptual case study for my seminar on punishment and social morality, and I would like a rigorous philosophical analysis of the following situation, which my students debated at length after class over coffee. A volunteer-run community archive—let us call it the Meridian Collective—has maintained a shared workspace and public reading room for nearly fifteen years. One of its founding members, now largely retired from administrative duties, recently had a decade-old public statement resurface in which he expressed measured skepticism about a particular social reform that the collective now treats as a non-negotiable commitment. The statement violated no formal rule in force at the time, and the member has not repeated it within any collective forum; indeed, he contributed substantially to the archive's recent digitization project and remains financially supportive. Nevertheless, several current members propose that he be removed from the honorary advisory board and that his name be struck from the founding plaque—not because they anticipate any practical harm, since he holds no operational authority, but because his continued symbolic presence, they argue, undermines the moral climate the collective wishes to cultivate. Opponents regard this as an unjustified sanction for a past expression that poses no present danger and predates the collective's current normative framework.

What I seek is not institutional policy or legal counsel, but a precise conceptual diagnosis. Is the proposed removal properly classified as punishment, and if so, does its justification turn on backward-looking desert, forward-looking deterrence, or on what might be called a constitutive or expressive function—specifically, that a community partly defines its moral identity through the figures it honors? I am particularly interested in whether one can defend a principled distinction between sanctions that aim to correct future conduct and sanctions that aim solely to shape communal moral identity, and whether such a distinction can justify excluding someone who poses no operational risk and whose offense occurred before the collective adopted its present values. Please treat the practical details as merely the occasion for the philosophical problem, lay out the competing interpretations with their respective commitments, and avoid invoking specific legal precedents or bibliographic references.
