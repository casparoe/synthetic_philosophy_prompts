After a long argument with a friend about whether two people can literally share an intention rather than merely coordinate, I have reconstructed our exchange into a chain that seems to prove collective intentionality impossible, and I cannot locate the slip. Here it is, with each step marked:

(1) In a genuine joint action—say, two people carrying a sofa up stairs together—there is a collective intention that they carry it, and this intention is not reducible to the sum of two separate individual intentions.

(2) For either participant A to contribute to this collective intention, A must possess an individual mental state—call it a we-intention—whose content is essentially "we carry the sofa."

(3) The state of affairs denoted by "we carry the sofa" includes both A and B as agents of the carrying, and therefore includes both A's and B's corresponding we-intentions as constituent parts of that very state.

(4) So the content of A's we-intention includes B's we-intention, and symmetrically B's includes A's.

(5) An intentional state can have determinately fixed content only if all entities and states mentioned within that content are already available to fix its reference; it cannot depend for its own determinacy on a mental state that is not yet formed.

(6) Therefore A's we-intention can exist only if B's already exists, and B's only if A's already exists—an impossible circular dependency in formation.

(7) One might break the circle by saying that A's we-intention refers only to B's bodily movements, not to B's mental state.

(8) But then A's mental state is merely an individual intention to coordinate one's own movements with B's; it makes no essential reference to a shared agent "we," and premise (1) collapses—joint action becomes nothing more than parallel individual action.

(9) If the reasoning holds for pairs, it holds a fortiori for larger groups: a corporation, a legislature, or a democratic assembly can possess no irreducible collective intention, only an aggregation of individual ones.

(10) Yet we routinely distinguish a mob deliberately pushing a car from two strangers who happen to push in the same direction, and we hold groups responsible in ways that seem to presuppose a unitary agent rather than a coincidence of parallel aims.

(11) So either we accept that all collective agency is an illusion, or one of the steps above fails. I would prefer to keep (1) and reject (8)—there really is something it is like to act together that exceeds parallel coordination—but I want to know precisely what I am paying. Which premise or inference should be surrendered? Is there an equivocation between including a mental state in one's intentional content and causally depending on it for one's own formation? Or does saving joint action require us to allow mental states with genuinely circular or interdependent contents, and if so, what does that cost our broader theory of mind?
