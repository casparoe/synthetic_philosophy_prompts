I'm not an academic by training, but I've been working through a puzzle about knowledge and its consequences on my own, and I'd like to pressure-test my intuition with a structured estimate. The idea I've encountered is Robert Nozick's tracking account: you genuinely know something only when your belief tracks the truth—specifically, if what you believed turned out to be false in a nearby possible world, you wouldn't still hold that belief, and if it remained true under slightly different circumstances, you still would. The well-known tension with this view is epistemic closure. If I know I'm not a brain in a vat, and I know that not being a brain in a vat entails I'm sitting at a desk, then closure says I should know I'm sitting at a desk. Yet under tracking conditions, in the nearest worlds where I'm not sitting at a desk I am precisely a brain in a vat being fed the experience, so my belief that I'm sitting doesn't seem to satisfy the sensitivity condition. Hence Nozick denies closure for this kind of case.

What I'm trying to figure out is whether closure might survive in a more restricted domain that I haven't seen discussed in standard treatments—let's call it historical-testimony closure. Suppose I know, through a chain of reliable testimony and records, that a specific document was signed in 1776, and I also know that this entails the document actually existed at that time. Here the "nearby worlds" where the entailment's conclusion fails seem different in kind: they aren't worlds where I'm radically deceived about my own experience, but worlds where archives were forged, witnesses lied, or copies were misdated. So the modal distance of skeptical error and the modal distance of historical error might come apart.
