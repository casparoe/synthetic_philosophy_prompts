I retired from marine biolgy about four years ago and since then I've been trying to keep my brain working by sitting in on philosophy classes at the local community college. Last Tuesday I got into a long arguement with two other students there -- let's call them Priya and David -- about ubuntu and whether it actually flips the separateness-of-persons objection inside out. We were working with a made-up case: an elderly man in a village clinic refuses dialisis because he feels he's a burden to everyone. Here's the back and forth.

(1) Priya said that in ubuntu a person is a person through other people, so the old man isn't some separate atom making an isolated choice. His personhood is literally constituted by his family and community, which means letting them decide for him isn't paternalism -- it's just recognizing who he actually is. (2) David said that's wrong because ubuntu doesn't erase agency, it just situates it inside relationships. If you override his refusal you've destroyed the reciporcity that makes those relationships moral in the first place; you've turned him into a node rather than a person-in-relation. (3) Priya answered with this idea she named reverse separateness: in a relational ontology, treating someone as fully separate and self-owning is itself the harm, so the usual Western worry about merging people doesn't apply here. Insisting on his isolated autonomy actually does violence to his real identity. (4) David came back saying she was just smuggling hierarchy back in. Elder care in ubuntu is reciprocal, not one-directional, and if reverse separateness lets the community absorb an elder's refusal then it isn't reversing the objection at all -- it's relabeling coercion as ontology.

I couldn't figure out who was right about what. Can you adjudicate move by move? Rate each of the four moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right with a brief reason. Then tell me who's more right overall, and point out what both of them are missing -- maybe something about how actual ubuntu communities handle refusal in practice? I don't need a reading list or citations, just the conceptual breakdown. Sorry if my wording's rough, I'm still getting used to the jargon.
