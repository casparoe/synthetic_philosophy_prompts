I've been thinking Simone Weil was right that parties are machines for manufacturing collective passion, but I want to push it further in a specific direction: maybe the real damage isn't emotional or moral but epistemic, in the sense that parties don't just suppress dissent, they restructure the cost of attention so that keeping an independant view becomes cognitively expensive in a hidden way. It's not social punishment, it's more like an organizational sunk-cost fallacy applied to belief—once you've bought into the framng, the mental labor of stepping outside feels wasteful rather than dangerous. So my position is that party affiliation systematically degrades the kind of attention Weil valued, not mainly through fear or loyalty, but through a kind of invisible cognitive triage. What would actually count against this? I want the evidence and arguments that should move me, sorted by how decisive they'd be: formal models of group cognition that show parties preserving heterodox attention, organizational-psychology studies that show the opposite of my sunk-cost mechanism, historical cases where party structures actively protected dissent, and maybe conceptual arguments that "attention" can't be structured by institutions in the first place. Include things that have arguably already happened. Also, be honest about whether this view has been qualified into unfalsifiability—like if every instance of conformity gets read as attention-restructuring and every counterexample gets dismissed as "not a real party"—and if it has, is that fatal or just a framing device? I want to know what defenders of this view would themselves concede counts against it, not just what critics say. And if it turns out well-insulated, say so, and say whether that's okay for a claim of this kind. Personally I'd find empirical organizational results more convincing than historical anecdotes, so weight those higher if you can.
