Consider the scale argument applied to invertebrate sentience: enormous populations multiplied by a low, uncertain probability of consciousness yield potentially massive moral stakes, yet the argument quietly assumes that moral weight can be assigned as a scalar. Suppose we reject that binary framing and instead treat sentience as graded and architecture-dependent—specifically, as varying with whether an organism exhibits something like global integrative capacity versus only localized nociceptive circuits without binding. Given that injection, which open conceptual questions should be prioritized? First, does moral consideration scale continuously with integrative complexity, or does it collapse into discrete thresholds that would break the scale argument's linear multiplication? Second, can the probabilistic reasoning survive if we replace a single "probability of sentience" with a vector of architecture-specific capacities, each carrying its own uncertainty and moral weight? Third, how should the empirical tractability of invertebrate neuroscience constrain normative allocation of concern—should we weight questions by how likely they are to resolve within a research career, or by how radically they would shift priorities if settled? Defend an explicit ordering: identify which questions are upstream of the others, which can be advanced without speculative metaphysics of consciousness, and what resolution over the next several years would most change how a rationalist framework weighs invertebrate welfare against other moral priorities. This is not a request for a literature survey; I want a defended agenda that stays legible to someone who accepts the scale argument's framing but suspects the underlying ontology of mind is more complicated than a yes-no toggle.
