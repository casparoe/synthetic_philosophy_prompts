I've been discussing with colleagues in a philosophy of science reading group whether Darwin's account of natural selection and Turing's formalization of computation share a deeper structural feature that standard treatments tend to keep separate: both generate outcomes that appear to require foresight, design, or comprehension—adaptive biological complexity in one case, apparently intelligent behavior in the other—through mechanisms that themselves possess none of these capacities. We have been describing this as a strange inversion of reasoning, since it reverses the traditional philosophical assumption that genuine competence must be grounded in some prior understanding possessed by the agent or system in question. I am trying to determine the most important implications of this inversion for our conception of scientific explanation, particularly whether mechanistic accounts in evolutionary biology and theoretical computer science can remain fully adequate when the explaining mechanism lacks any representational, intentional, or normative grasp of what it produces.

Specifically, does this inversion undermine the classical demand that explanations be graspable by rational agents in virtue of the mechanism's own intrinsic understanding, or does it instead show that explanation is essentially relational—dependent on an interpreter rather than on any feature internal to the causal process itself? And if the latter, what follows for attempts to account for the emergence of biological function or for normative success in reasoning without smuggling in teleological or mentalistic commitments that the underlying mechanisms do not actually possess? I want a direct conceptual analysis of how far this inversion can be pushed before it collapses into either an eliminativism about understanding or an implicit anthropomorphism of purely mechanical processes; I am not asking for a bibliography, citation list, or historical survey of primary texts.
