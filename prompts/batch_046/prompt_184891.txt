For a high-school philosophy workshop of about 16 students running 75 minutes, design a role-play with small real stakes that lets participants feel why promises bind even when the promisee is unknown and no social sanction is possible. Each student privately writes a one-week personal commitment, seals it in an envelope that is randomly redistributed, and then receives another student’s anonymous commitment; they must choose whether to honor it despite never learning the author’s identity, bearing a minor tangible cost (giving up a favorite snack for one day) if they break it and gaining nothing if they keep it. Arrange the rules so that students first experience the pull to honor the paper, then discover they can defect without detection, and only afterwards discuss what made the commitment feel binding or empty. Provide a timeline, the rules and any roles, a debrief that maps those moments onto the distinction between expectation-based, autonomy-based, and intertemporal-authority accounts of promissory obligation, and an honest note about which feature—especially the continuity of personal identity that lets a past self bind a future self—the activity cannot reproduce and must be supplied by the facilitator. In your response, clearly separate what is settled about promises from what is genuinely contested among philosophers who work on the topic, and do not present any contested view as the consensus.
