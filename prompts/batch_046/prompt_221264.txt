I have been discussing with a friend those major life changes that people describe as altering the very fabric of who they are—shifts so profound that, in her view, you cannot genuinely grasp what they are like until you have undergone them yourself. She has no philosophical training, yet her judgments are remarkably consistent, and I want to understand what implicit rule she is applying rather than whether she is correct. She firmly counts becoming a parent for the first time and gradually losing one's sight in adulthood as examples of this kind of change, because in both cases the world is experienced through a radically different structure of feeling and attention. At the same time, she denies that learning a difficult foreign language as an adult, or adopting vegetarianism after confronting factory farming, belongs in the same category, since these strike her as demanding choices or skills rather than transformations of the experiential frame itself. She also includes gender-affirming medical transition, explaining that the embodied sense of self shifts in a manner outsiders cannot access, but she explicitly excludes taking a powerful cognitive enhancer that permanently doubles working memory, calling that an upgrade rather than a change in what it is like to be you.

If her pattern is coherent, I would like to know what she would say about two further cases and, more importantly, what competing principles could explain her track record. The first case is a patient with severe epilepsy who elects to have their corpus callosum surgically divided and afterward reports experiencing two independent streams of conscious awareness; the second is a grieving individual who chooses a targeted neurosurgical intervention that permanently removes all emotional memories of a deceased partner in order to end debilitating grief. Would she regard the split-brain patient as having undergone the transformative change, or would the division of awareness seem to her like a technical alteration that leaves the core self intact? Would she see the memory-erasure procedure as transformative in the same sense as blindness or parenthood, or as a therapeutic removal of content that does not restructure the self? Please reconstruct at least two general rules that fit her earlier verdicts—perhaps one focused on irreversible restructuring of first-person phenomenology and another on the epistemic inaccessibility of new values—predict her answers for both new cases under each rule, state which rule you think she actually follows, and tell me what single additional scenario would best distinguish between them.
