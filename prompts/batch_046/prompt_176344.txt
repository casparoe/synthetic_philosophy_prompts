I just finished a long, somewhat circular argument with a friend about whether standard expected-utility reasoning can survive once we take seriously the risk that our own utility assignments are distorted—for instance, by adaptive preferences or by extreme outlier cases that function like a utility-monster scenario. I am now drafting the final exam for my graduate seminar in normative decision theory, and I need one essay question that forces students to work with a framework I am calling distortion-weighted expected moral utility. In this framework, the moral value of an act is computed as the sum across outcomes of [probability × utility × (1 − d)], where d is an epistemic-risk coefficient between 0 and 1 that scales with how vulnerable the underlying utility assignment is to systematic distortion. The exam question should ask students to defend or reject the claim that whenever d is appreciably greater than zero, the framework collapses into status-quo conservatism, and it should require them to address whether d itself can be calibrated without circular reliance on the very utility judgments it is meant to correct. The question must be answerable in 90 minutes, must not be answerable by reciting standard expected-utility theory, and must require a definite verdict—not a non-committal survey. Please write the exam question itself, a model answer of approximately 650 words that reaches a clear conclusion and justifies it, and a marking scheme totaling 20 points. The scheme should separate an A-level answer from a B-level answer by identifying the specific argumentative moves each tier must contain—especially the move that distinguishes epistemic risk from moral risk, and the move that either answers or concedes the circularity objection. Do not include any request for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; evaluation should depend entirely on conceptual precision and argumentative structure. Write in American English and keep the tone formal and polished.
