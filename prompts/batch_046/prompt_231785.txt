I am a software engineer without any formal training in philosophy, though I have been reading philosophy of science in my spare time—mostly because my day-to-day work involves maintaining classification taxonomies that must remain stable even as user behavior evolves in response to them. I keep encountering what Ian Hacking described as looping effects in psychiatric classification: once individuals are assigned a diagnostic label, they often alter their self-conception or behavior in ways that change the very category that was applied, which in turn can shift how clinicians apply the label in the future. In the literature I have read, this observation seems to have produced a long-standing impasse between two camps. One group—call them the dynamics realists—treats looping as a form of feedback no different in principle from self-correcting predictions in economics or ecology; they argue that psychiatric categories can still be objective if we model them as dynamic systems whose boundaries are constantly updated, and that failure to stabilize is an empirical problem, not an ontological defeat. The opposing group—call them the kind essentialists—maintains that looping demonstrates psychiatric categories are partly constituted by social response, which makes them fundamentally unlike chemical or biological kinds; on their view, the presence of looping means we should treat diagnoses as pragmatic instruments shaped by institutional practice rather than as discoveries about mind-independent disorders.

What frustrates me is that both sides routinely cite the same historical shifts—such as the changing symptom profiles of certain personality disorders across successive editions of diagnostic manuals—and both agree that patient behavior changes post-diagnosis, yet neither camp appears to have moved in decades. In my reading group, we have begun to suspect that a hidden distinction might be doing unstated work. Some loopings seem thin: the underlying mechanism remains constant, but its expression or reporting changes once the label is applied. Others seem thick: the classification itself participates in constructing the underlying mechanism, so that without the label the phenomenon would not exist in the same form. If this distinction is coherent, then perhaps the dynamics realists are asking whether taxonomy can achieve predictive stability despite thin looping, while the essentialists are asking whether thick looping makes ontological realism impossible—two different questions running under the single heading of whether psychiatric categories are real. I want to know whether this stall is actually a verbal disagreement about the word objective or natural kind, with one side requiring strict mind-independence and the other allowing mind-dependence so long as there is reliable predictive structure; whether it is a substantive disagreement about hidden empirical predictions, such as whether any psychiatric category can eventually stabilize; whether it turns on different standards of evidence, with one camp demanding mechanistic explanations and the other accepting functional patterns; or whether it ultimately rests on bedrock intuitions about what counts as a genuine discovery versus a useful fiction, intuitions that neither side can argue for from neutral ground. Given your diagnosis, what exactly would count as progress—would it be a formal model that distinguishes thin from thick looping and tracks stability conditions, an empirical study measuring whether specific categories converge over time, a conceptual clarification that splits the debate into separate questions, or is there reason to think no further movement should be expected and why?
