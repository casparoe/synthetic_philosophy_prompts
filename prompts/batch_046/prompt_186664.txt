I have been thinking on my own about the rule-following problem often associated with Kripke's reading of Wittgenstein — specifically, the idea that if a rule does not contain instructions for every possible future application, there is no determinable fact about whether someone is following it correctly. To make this less abstract, I imagined a small, close-knit community that decides whether a newcomer is 'trustworthy' based on unspoken behavioral cues rather than any explicit code. In this group, elder members and newer members regularly disagree about borderline cases, and there is no written standard or external authority to settle the dispute. I am helping to design an assessment for an upper-level undergraduate philosophy seminar in which students have read selections from Wittgenstein's Philosophical Investigations but are not expected to have studied Kripke's monograph in depth. Please construct one essay question that a student could answer in 75 minutes. The question must not be answerable by simply reciting the skeptical paradox and the standard 'community agreement' reply; instead, it should require the student to locate precisely where the skeptical argument gains traction when the rule in question is normative and socially stratified, and to evaluate whether appealing to community practice genuinely resolves the disagreement or merely conceals it behind status differences. Along with the question, provide a model answer of approximately 500 words, and a marking scheme built around four essential moves that distinguish a strong answer from a weak one. The scheme should assign specific marks to each move (totalling 20), and for each move include a brief note on the characteristic error that causes candidates to lose credit there. Please avoid asking for citations, bibliographies, or references to specific scholarly debates that depend on knowledge of particular articles or books beyond the course readings mentioned.
