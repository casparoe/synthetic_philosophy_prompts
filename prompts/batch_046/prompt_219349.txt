I am a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, history, or anthropology. I have been asked to deliver the opening remarks—approximately ten minutes—at an interdisciplinary workshop titled "Synthetic Memory and the Architecture of Long-Term Explanation." The audience is divided evenly between humanities scholars familiar with macro-historical narratives and their critics—specifically the kinds of large-scale explanatory frameworks associated with Harari, Diamond, and the counter-arguments advanced by Graeber and Wengrow—and AI researchers and engineers who think in terms of training data, feature extraction, and predictive modeling. I want the speech to treat this division not as a conflict to resolve but as a structural feature that should shape what I can and cannot say.

Please write this address so that it does real philosophical work rather than serving as decorative framing. I want to propose—without presenting it as literal science—a conceptual analogy: that grand historical narratives function like lossy compression algorithms applied to the human past. In this framing, Harari's account of a cognitive revolution can be read as a feature-extraction layer that collapses millennia into a single transformation; Diamond's geographic determinism risks overfitting local conditions into a universal linear model; and Graeber and Wengrow's critique can be understood as an insistence on ensemble methods that preserve local variance and contradictory evidence rather than smoothing it away. The analogy must structure the argument, not ornament it.

Crucially, I want to introduce and defend an unconventional evaluative criterion for such narratives: not predictive accuracy over the past, but what I will call "reversibility"—the capacity of a compressed historical account to be decompressed back into granular, contradictory source material without catastrophic loss of the contradictions themselves. The speech should use this criterion to explain why I, as someone who builds compression systems professionally, am skeptical of any single grand narrative, and why I believe the most responsible use of macro-history is to keep the source contradictions visibly present in the compressed form.

The address should be formal, accessible to both halves of the room without condescension, and should avoid bibliographic references, citations, reading lists, or appeals to scholarly authority I do not possess. It should acknowledge explicitly that the compression metaphor is an engineer's heuristic, not an established historical method. Please make sure the speech can stand without web access: it should rely on widely recognized conceptual positions rather than obscure texts, and should not ask the audience to verify specific page numbers, publication dates, or verbatim quotations.
