I have recently retired from a career in experimental physics and, having taken up philosophy in earnest, now convene a small monthly discussion circle for fellow retired scientists and engineers who share my late-blooming interest in conceptual questions. Next month we shall examine the boundary between manipulation and legitimate persuasion, and I wish to construct a teaching plan rather than deliver a lecture. Rather than framing the distinction through the familiar transparency-versus-deception axis, I intend to introduce it through the notion of epistemic reversibility—whether the influenced party retains the capacity to reconstruct the reasoning pathway independently once the influence is withdrawn—and to carry the idea with an analogy drawn from experimental design: the difference between openly varying an input parameter to observe a system's natural response and covertly recalibrating the measurement apparatus so that the observer's inference is directed without awareness. I am concerned, however, that this analogy may smuggle in mechanistic assumptions about causation that distort the moral-psychological domain, so I need careful sequencing.

Could you plan the order in which the components must be introduced, specifying what must come first—perhaps the concept of directed influence as such—before the reversibility criterion can be layered in, and explaining precisely why that dependency holds? For the carrying example, state exactly what it must possess and what it must lack: it should involve intentional direction of belief or practical choice without physical coercion, yet it must avoid cases that turn solely on hidden information, since I want to head off the misconception that manipulation is merely persuasion with incomplete disclosure. Identify the misconceptions this audience—accustomed to thinking in terms of signal integrity, feedback loops, and asymmetric information—will predictably form, such as conflating manipulation with noise or with any informational asymmetry, and indicate precisely where in the sequence each should be anticipated and corrected. Provide one check question I can pose at the end to determine whether they have grasped that the moral distinction lies in the structure of the influence pathway rather than in the speaker's intent or the hearer's rationality. Also advise what should be deliberately omitted at this level—perhaps the literature on autonomy and behavioural nudging—and state so explicitly. Please write your response in British English.
