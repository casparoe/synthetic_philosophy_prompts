I am drafting a paper for an advanced seminar in eighteenth-century aesthetics, and I have developed an argument that applies Hume's framework from his essay on taste to a phenomenon he could not have anticipated: the aesthetic judgment of algorithmically curated media streams. My thesis is that the ideal critic Hume describes—possessing delicacy of imagination, freedom from prejudice, and extensive practice in comparative evaluation—operates as a normative individual standard, whereas modern recommendation algorithms approximate only a statistical aggregation of unexamined preferences. I contend that treating algorithmic popularity as an aesthetic standard therefore commits a category error: it substitutes quantitative correlation for the qualitative, deliberative refinement that Hume takes to be essential to taste. At the same time, I want to probe whether Hume's own criteria for critical excellence are culturally parochial rather than universally normative, which raises a tension in my position if I simultaneously defend refinement against algorithms and question its neutrality.

Below are the notes for my draft's three sections. First, I reconstruct Hume's distinction between sentiment and judgment, emphasizing that even correct sentiment requires a cultivated faculty to become authoritative rather than merely idiosyncratic. Second, I draw the analogy between collaborative filtering and the aggregation of critical opinions, arguing that the absence of reflective comparison and corrective deliberation in algorithmic systems breaks the normative structure of Hume's standard. Third, I consider the objection that delicacy and freedom from prejudice are themselves embedded in specific historical practices of connoisseurship, and ask whether pluralistic digital cultures require a reconception of the ideal critic rather than its simple defense against mechanized taste.

I would welcome focused comments on three things: whether my reading of the sentiment-judgment distinction is accurate to Hume's account; whether the analogy to algorithmic curation is philosophically productive or merely a strained metaphor; and whether I have adequately flagged the internal tension between defending normative refinement and questioning its cultural neutrality. I am not seeking a bibliography or further readings—the seminar texts are fixed—but suggestions for sharpening the conceptual distinction between statistical aggregation and normative consensus in the second section would be especially valuable.
