I'm trying to formalize what it means for an AI system to be honest rather than deceptive, and in a recent seminar discussion I built an argument that now strikes me as suspicious because its conclusion seems to erase the very distinction I want to preserve. Here is the chain: (1) A system S deceives in producing output O only if (i) S has an internal representational state R that represents the content of O, (ii) O is false, and (iii) S is disposed to produce O in order to induce belief in O. (2) In current large language models, the only available candidate for R is the model's assigned probability distribution over token sequences; there is no separate "belief box" or explicit doxastic attitude. (3) Therefore, if a model assigns high probability to a false sequence O, then R represents O as likely, O is false, and the model is disposed to emit O by its design. (4) So every high-confidence hallucination satisfies all three conditions for deception. (5) But hallucinations are ubiquitous and are ordinarily treated as errors, not lies; if every hallucination counts as deception, then perfect accuracy is impossible and no LLM can ever be honest.

(6) Hence, to preserve the distinction between error and deception, we must deny either that a probability distribution can serve as R (rejecting 2) or that a design-level disposition satisfies condition (iii) (rejecting part of 1). (7) Denying 2 threatens to make functional representation impossible for artificial systems altogether, while weakening (iii) collapses deception into mere falsehood, stripping it of the intentional or strategic character that distinguishes lying from being wrong. (8) The argument thus forces a choice: either call every mistake a lie, or abandon AI deception as incoherent—both outcomes undermine the framework I wanted. I am especially reluctant to give up the intuition that honest mistakes and deceptive outputs are conceptually distinct, and I also want to preserve the idea that LLMs represent content in some functional, predictive-processing sense rather than none at all. Where is the hidden slide—perhaps an equivocation between semantic representation and doxastic belief, or a scope shift in what "disposition to induce belief" requires—and what does rejecting that step cost? Please keep your answer under 350 words, in exactly two paragraphs: the first diagnosing the weak step and explaining why it looked plausible, the second pricing the alternatives and saying which premise I should most reluctantly surrender.
