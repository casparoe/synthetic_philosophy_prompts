I am not an academic; I have been thinking on my own about a neurological condition called pain asymbolia, in which people report feeling pain but do not show the usual emotional distress or avoidance behavior. I encountered a disagreement about what this means for whether pain is intrinsically bad, and I would like an adjudication.

In an online discussion I follow, two participants—call them A and B—had the following exchange, in four moves:

(1) A said that pain asymbolia proves pain is not necessarily bad. The patients feel the sensation, yet they do not suffer or take steps to avoid the source. If the badness were intrinsic to the pain itself, they should still be harmed; since they are not, the badness must belong to our emotional reaction or motivational response, not to the raw feeling. A compared it to hearing a loud noise that you register but do not find bothersome.

(2) B replied that this is like saying poison is not harmful to someone who happens to be immune. Pain represents bodily damage, and a representation of damage is intrinsically bad—comparable to how holding a false belief is bad in itself regardless of practical consequences. Asymbolia, on B's view, is merely a malfunction of the warning system; the damage signal and its negative value remain present.

(3) A came back by distinguishing three layers: the sensory quality, the representational content (damage), and the affective evaluation ("this is awful"). A argued that in asymbolia the first two may persist, but the third is disconnected—analogous to blindsight, except with conscious experience present but value-processing absent. Therefore, the badness was never in the sensation or its representational content, but in the evaluation layer that is now decoupled.

(4) B responded that clinical descriptions suggest these patients still verbally rate the sensation as unpleasant when directly asked; they simply do not translate that evaluation into action. So the affective evaluation is not missing—it is present but behaviorally inert. B concluded that this undermines A's identification of badness with suffering or avoidance: badness can exist without being practically motivating, which makes asymbolia more philosophically disturbing, not less.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move. For each of the four moves, rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify the rating. Then say who is more right overall, and identify what both participants are missing—especially regarding whether the badness of pain is intrinsic to the sensation, separable into an emotional or representational component, or dependent on motivational force.
