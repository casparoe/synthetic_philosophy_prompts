I've been arguing with my flatmate for ages about whether Gen Z, Millennials and Boomers are actual real kinds of people, or just marketing labels that sociologists borrowed without thinkin too hard. He keeps saying it's all age-period-cohort confussion — if you control for economic conditions and tech access, the differences between Boomers and Gen Z vanish into statistical noise, and all you've got is people getting older in particular historical windows. I keep pointing out that Mannheim's idea of generational location and those cyclical Strauss-Howe theories seem to capture something real about shared formative trauma, like growing up with algorithmic feeds during puberty rather than dial-up. But we've been going round this for months and neither of us has moved an inch, and I see the same deadlock in every sociology thread I read.

My working theory is that we're not actually disagreeing about the data at all — it's that he's treating generations as predictive variables in a regression model, and I'm treating them as ontologically real social entities with emergent causal powers, almost like Durkheimian collective representations but for cohorts. But I'm not sure if that's right, or if there's a hidden empirical question about whether cohort effects are robust enough to survive multivariate controls once you factor in class and geography. Is this a verbal dispute about what generation means, a methodological fight about which statistical models count as legitimate evidence, or a bedrock disagreement about whether social kinds need to be carved at the joints like biological natural kinds? And honestly, is there anything — a new longitudinal study, a sharper conceptual distinction between cohort and life-stage effects, or maybe just abandoning the ontology question for a pragmatist framing — that could actually break the stalemate, or are we stuck with incompatible intuitions about what makes a group real?
