Write a one-semester research proposal for a philosophy-of-mind term paper of approximately six thousand words. The precise question is whether Dennett's two-bitser thought experiment genuinely undermines Searle's distinction between original and derived intentionality, or whether it succeeds only by fixing an observer-relative functional grain that, if applied consistently to biological brains, collapses the very distinction it aims to defend. Note that the existing literature tends to treat the two-bitser either as a decisive functionalist counterexample or as an obvious category mistake, but neither camp addresses whether the granularity of functional description—what counts as one state versus two, and which transitions are semantically loaded—is itself doing the hidden philosophical work. Your proposal should state the question with that framing, explain why standard replies leave it open, and describe your method: you will analyze the two-bitser at three distinct grains of functional resolution and ask at which grains, if any, the system exhibits original rather than derived aboutness, relying on conceptual analysis rather than empirical neuroscience. Clarify what a successful result would look like—identifying a non-arbitrary, non-circular criterion for selecting the correct grain that preserves Searle's distinction without begging the question against Dennett—and what failure would look like, for instance demonstrating that grain selection is always pragmatically motivated and therefore that original intentionality is relative to an interpretive stance rather than an intrinsic property. Give an honest assessment of scope: the project avoids empirical literature, which keeps it feasible for a single semester, but you must ensure the conceptual analysis of functional grain does not collapse into mere stipulation. Do not include a bibliography, literature review by citation, or reading list; you may refer to positions and arguments generally and add citations yourself. Present the proposal in continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as if writing a formal letter to a committee.
