I serve on the Ontology and Classification Committee of the National Digital Heritage Archive. We must issue a binding decision on whether artifacts whose manifest sensory properties belong to category A but whose causal-genetic origin belongs to category B should be filed under A, under B, or in a newly created hybrid class. The immediate case is an AI-assisted reconstruction of a 14th-century celadon vessel: its surface morphology and glaze chemistry match the original ceramic kind, but its substrate includes polymer composites and its form was inferred by a generative model trained on fragmentary data. Curatorial staff are split between filing it with ceramics (by phenomenological kind) and with synthetic reconstructions (by production history). Please draft the committee's decision memo: it must recommend one concrete classification rule with explicit implementation criteria and a 90-day pilot timeline, and it must treat the opposing view—that separating appearance from origin collapses the very concept of an artifact—seriously rather than dismissing it. In the reasoning, draw on the conceptual resources of the School of Names, particularly the white horse dialogue's separation of form-color from kind-membership and the Mingjia analysis of similarity (tong) versus strict identity (yi), to show whether hybrid objects force a new taxonomic logic or can be accommodated within existing categories without contradiction. Lead with the bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
