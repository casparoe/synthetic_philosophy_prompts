In my own words, the passage asks us to imagine a machine constructed large enough to think, feel, and perceive—like a mill enlarged so that we could walk inside it. Within it we would observe only parts pushing against other parts, and we would never encounter anything that explains perception. Leibniz concludes from this that perception is not to be found in composite bodies or mechanisms, but only in simple substances.

My friend insisted that if one takes Leibniz's doctrine of pre-established harmony seriously and applies it to the Chinese Room, then the room is not genuinely one unified system manipulating symbols, but rather an infinite aggregate of simple monadic sub-routines, each running its own internal program in perfect synchronization with the others. On her reading, Searle's intuition that the system as a whole might understand Chinese is metaphysically undermined before it begins, because there is no compound entity there capable of bearing understanding—only harmonized simple substances. I am unsure whether this monadic dissolution of system-level syntax saves the Chinese Room by blocking the intuition pump, or instead destroys it by denying the very premise that there is a single system to evaluate.

Please help me understand precisely what Leibniz means by the claim that parts of a machine cannot yield perception, and whether my friend's application of pre-established harmony to dissolve the room into non-interacting monads actually undermines Searle's argument or merely changes the subject.
