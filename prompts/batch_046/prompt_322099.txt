I am composing a sequence of formal poems for a novel concerned with the ethics of representation, and I need one on the paradox of fiction—the difficulty of explaining genuine emotional response to characters and events we know to be non-existent. Write me a villanelle of exactly nineteen lines: five tercets followed by a quatrain, with rhyme scheme ABA in each tercet, and two refrains (call them A1 and A2) that serve as the final line of successive tercets, alternating, then appearing together as the final two lines of the quatrain. The first refrain must state the absence of existential belief; the second must confess an involuntary emotional response—grief, fear, pity—without justification. Let the first three tercets unfold like the three classical conditions for emotional engagement: in the first, the belief that the object exists, negated; in the second, the belief that danger or harm is present, negated; in the third, the affective response itself, affirmed despite the first two failures. In the remaining tercets, let the refrains return unaltered, so that the form performs the stubbornness of the contradiction rather than resolving it. In the concluding quatrain, place both refrains side by side without synthesis, since that is precisely where the paradox lives. Write it in a voice that is intimate but precise, as though a novelist were addressing a character who has just realized she is made of language and is trying to explain why she still made the reader weep. Do not invoke named theories, cite authors, or offer a philosophical resolution; let the architecture of repetition and convergence do all the work.
