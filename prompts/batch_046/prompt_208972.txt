You are a professional philosopher of law with expertise in twentieth-century British jurisprudence. Before delivering your verdict, briefly recall the core structure of the Hart–Devlin debate on legal moralism, then work through the exchange below carefully and step by step.

At dinner, Leo and Mara argued about whether Devlin’s defense of legal moralism survives Hart’s critique. Leo spoke first:

(1) Leo said Devlin’s point is not merely that people feel disgust at immorality, but that a shared moral framework is a necessary condition for social cohesion—analogous to the way a shared language is necessary for communication. Without legal reinforcement of that framework, society risks disintegration into a mere aggregation of individuals lacking any common bond.

(2) Mara replied that this is exactly the move Hart attacked. Hart showed that societies survive deep moral disagreement—religious pluralism, for instance—without falling apart. She said Leo is treating emotional reactions such as indignation and intolerance as if they were empirical evidence of social harm, which is precisely the confusion Hart exposed.

(3) Leo countered that Mara misreads Hart’s rebuttal. Hart only demonstrated that there is no empirical proof that relaxing moral laws causes disintegration; he never addressed Devlin’s conceptual claim that if morality becomes entirely private, the very idea of a society with shared values collapses. Leo added a more specific point: Devlin’s analogy between serious immorality and treason is not about physical damage but about betrayal of the social bond itself—and Hart never explains how a liberal society can justify its own “thin” shared morality without smuggling in the same kind of moral enforcement Devlin advocates, just at a lower intensity.

(4) Mara said Leo is now attributing to Devlin a much weaker, almost Hart-compatible thesis. Devlin explicitly argued that any significant deviation from shared morality weakens the social fabric, not merely that some minimal core must exist. If Leo retreats to a “thin shared morality” that tolerates widespread private deviation, then he has either misrepresented Devlin’s strong claim or conceded that Devlin’s position collapses into Hart’s notion of critical morality. She added that Leo’s treason analogy fails because treason threatens the state’s existence, whereas private immorality—by Devlin’s own admission—often goes undetected and society continues regardless.

They asked me to referee. Please adjudicate move by move: for each of the four moves, rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify your rating, distinguishing conceptual claims from empirical ones. Then say who is more right overall, identify what each side is missing, and answer whether Leo’s “thin shared morality” interpretation genuinely rescues Devlin or merely re-describes Hart’s position in different language.
