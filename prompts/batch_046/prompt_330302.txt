I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, but I've been thinking about why I keep abandoning side projects despite knowing exactly which ones align with my long-term goals. Last week, two colleagues—Maya, who consults on behavioral modeling, and David, a systems architect—got into a sustained argument over lunch about whether weakness of will is even a coherent phenomenon once you take hyperbolic discounting seriously. I mostly listened and want a rigorous breakdown of where each of them actually stands.

Maya opened by saying that akrasia is essentially a labeling error. Once intertemporal choice is modeled with hyperbolic rather than exponential curves, preference reversal near the present isn't irrational; it's the predictable result of a steeper discount rate at shorter distances. The planner on Sunday and the abandoner on Tuesday aren't two selves in conflict—they're the same agent applying consistent weights from different temporal positions. Calling that weakness of will, she said, is like calling gravity a moral failure. David granted that hyperbolic curves describe the pattern accurately, but argued that akrasia specifically involves judgment-sensitive failure: you don't merely update your preferences, you act against your own all-things-considered judgment at the moment of action. He introduced the idea that in genuine weakness of will, the present self treats the prior planner's reasoning not just as distant in time but as epistemically discounted—like receiving advice from a trusted source whose current perspective you deem irrelevant because "they aren't here now." Maya replied that this is just poetic redescription of the same mathematical weighting. If the present self assigns lower value to future rewards, the discount factor does all the explanatory work; inventing a separate "epistemic" layer is redundant and unfalsifiable. David then pointed to commitment devices: if the problem were purely temporal weighting, then binding yourself with an immediate penalty should eliminate failure, yet people still break commitments even when costs are instant—suggesting the present self actively repudiates the planner's authority rather than merely weighting outcomes differently. Maya answered that immediate penalties can themselves be hyperbolically discounted when framed as "future losses caused by present action," and that David's "authority repudiation" adds no predictive power beyond adjusting the curve's parameters.

Can you adjudicate this exchange point by point? Specifically: is Maya right that hyperbolic discounting dissolves akrasia into consistent preference reversal, or is David right that there remains a judgment-sensitive failure that the curve alone doesn't capture? Evaluate David's claim about epistemic discounting of the prior self—does it identify something structurally distinct from temporal weighting, or is it indeed redundant? Then assess the commitment-device argument: does the fact that people still fail under immediate penalties support David's view that the present self repudiates planning authority, or can Maya's extended discounting explanation fully absorb those cases? Finally, tell me what both of them are missing—particularly whether treating the self as either fully unified across time or fully fragmented into competing agents misses some intermediate account of how long-term projects actually get sustained. I don't need references or reading lists; I just want to know whose reasoning holds up at each step and where the conceptual gap actually is.
