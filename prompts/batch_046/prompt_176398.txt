In rationalist and effective-altruist forums, the argument over whether individual actions—skipping meat, reducing personal carbon footprints, or voting—carry moral weight in large-scale collective harms has settled into a familiar stalemate. One camp runs the expected-value arithmetic: your emissions or consumption are marginal, supply-side replaceability swamps your omission, and the counterfactual impact is diluted across billions of agents and systemic drivers. The opposing camp insists that moral reasons need not be causal in this aggregative sense, citing expressive consistency, virtue-based obligations, and the epistemic possibility that you might be closer to a decisive pivot than the base rate suggests. Despite broad agreement on the empirical scale of climate change, factory farming, and electoral mechanics, and despite shared fluency with counterfactual reasoning, neither side has moved appreciably in years.

My working hypothesis is that the stall is not about the arithmetic but about an unacknowledged disagreement over the shape of the harm function—what I will call linear-aggregation inefficacy versus threshold-trigger inefficacy. Under the linear model, harm is continuous and your contribution is statistically redundant; under the threshold model, harm is discontinuous and your action might push a system across a critical boundary, but you lack the information to know whether you are the pivot. I suspect the debate persists because one side implicitly treats all collective harms as linear and therefore dismisses individual action as optimization noise, while the other side treats them as potentially discontinuous and therefore preserves a non-redundant space for individual moral significance—yet both sides frame their dispute as being about morality rather than about the formal properties of collective causation.

I want a diagnosis of the disagreement itself, not another adjudication of whether I should vote or eat the burger. Is this a hidden empirical question about which harm curves are actually continuous versus discontinuous? A verbal disagreement about what “making a difference” means—causal contribution, statistical correlation, or symbolic participation? Or a substantive divide over whether moral reasons must track expected causal efficacy, with one side treating non-causal reasons as rationalizations and the other treating them as foundational? Are the sides answering different questions—one about optimization under linear aggregation, one about integrity under radical uncertainty—under the single heading of “individual responsibility”? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress: a formal model distinguishing continuous from discontinuous collective action, a conceptual separation of causal from expressive reasons, or should we expect no resolution because the impasse rests on bedrock intuitions about personal agency that argument cannot reach?
