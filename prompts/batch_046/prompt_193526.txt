I am working through a problem set for my philosophy of rational choice course and have built an argument that seems to prove far too much about decision theory in general. Here it is:

(1) In the St. Petersburg game, the expected monetary payoff is infinite, yet no rational agent should pay an infinite entry fee.

(2) The standard resolution introduces a concave utility function so that expected utility is finite.

(3) If concavity is justified by the psychological fact that real agents experience diminishing marginal utility, then it applies not only to St. Petersburg but to every decision problem.

(4) In particular, it applies to any gamble with an unbounded potential payoff, no matter how remote the probability.

(5) So a rational agent should reject any gamble with unbounded upside if the cost is bounded but nonzero, because concavity will eventually drive expected utility below the price.

(6) But this means rational agents must reject many apparently reasonable long-shot bets—say, a one-dollar ticket with a one-in-a-billion chance of an arbitrarily large reward—whose expected utility under linear weighting is clearly positive.

(7) Therefore either the concavity resolution of St. Petersburg is wrong, or standard decision theory forbids rational acceptance of long-shot gambles that both intuition and practice allow.

(8) Since forbidding all such long-shot bets is absurd, the concavity resolution must fail, leaving the paradox unsolved.

Which step should I reject? I would prefer to keep (2) and (3), since the psychological justification for concavity seems hard to abandon selectively. But if I keep them, I seem stuck with (7). Where exactly does the reasoning slip—perhaps in how (3) applies concavity globally, or in how (5) treats bounded costs? And what does giving up the problematic step cost for the broader theory of rational choice?
