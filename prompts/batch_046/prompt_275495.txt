I'm working through my methodological commitments for a paper on historical practice and want a consistency audit before I finalize the framework. I hold the following: (a) explanatory fidelity demands what I call hermeneutic closure—interpreting past actions strictly within the normative and conceptual resources available to the agents themselves, since importing later frameworks corrupts causal and interpretive accuracy; (b) all historical writing is an unavoidable present-tense intervention, because archive selection, narrative framing, and the identification of significant causal chains are shaped by the historian's own political and moral situation, which makes perfect hermeneutic closure impossible in practice; (c) presentism—applying contemporary ethical standards to past societies—is a category error that treats historiography as a moral tribunal rather than an explanatory enterprise; (d) historians bear an obligation of reparative framing: they must actively deploy present-day concepts of structural injustice to identify, name, and foreground harms that were rendered invisible within the agents' own conceptual schemes, especially where archival silence was institutionally engineered; and (e) there is a strict methodological partition between explanatory charity (understanding agents on their own terms) and normative assessment (evaluating them by ours), and preserving this boundary protects history from both presentist distortion and moral abdication.

Which of these commitments are genuinely in tension, which pairs only appear incompatible but can be reconciled, and what is the least costly revision that restores coherence? Please rank the tensions by severity, specify what each revision costs me conceptually, and do not treat all conflicts as equally damaging—some may dissolve once the distinction between framing, explanation, and evaluation is properly drawn.
