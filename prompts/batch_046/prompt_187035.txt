In my philosophy of history seminar we are working with the tension between collective memory and archival history—specifically Halbwachs’s claim that memory reconstructs the past to serve present social identity, and Assmann’s distinction between living communicative memory (within three generations) and institutionalized cultural memory. Analyze the 1995 Smithsonian National Air and Space Museum Enola Gay controversy through that lens. The curators planned an exhibit using declassified strategic documents, casualty estimates, and Japanese perspectives to contextualize the atomic bombings; veteran groups, members of Congress, and parts of the public treated this as an assault on the communicative memory of the bombings as a necessary, sacrificial act that saved American lives, and the full exhibit was canceled and replaced with a minimal display. Map the episode precisely: who counts as the mnemonic community versus the archival community, what each side treated as authoritative evidence versus belonging, whether Assmann’s framework predicts that communicative memory should dominate a public museum, and whether the curators’ own framing was itself a present-serving reconstruction rather than pure archival neutrality. Say where the theory explains the outcome, where it is silent—especially on who has legitimate standing to decide in a publicly funded institution—and what the episode reveals about whether collective memory should ever yield to the historical record.
