I do not have any formal background in philosophy or decision theory, but I have spent considerable time thinking about cooperation, reasoning, and what it means to be the same person across different choices. I would like to present a set of notes for an essay I am trying to develop, and I ask for your comments on them.

The starting point is a pairing of two puzzles I have read about. The first is the Platonia dilemma, in which two agents who share identical reasoning processes face the same binary choice and, recognizing that their answers must match, converge on a single strategy—often cooperation—without any communication. The second is the Luring Lottery, in which a large group is invited to submit entries, with the rule that if exactly one person enters they win everything, but if more than one enters everyone receives nothing; this creates a tension between free-riding and collective failure.

My own addition is a twist I have not seen discussed. Rather than treating the agents as discrete individuals, imagine them as points along a continuous spectrum of my own future states—each separated by slight differences in mood, fatigue, or context—all facing the same choice. Each point knows that the others share my present reasoning, but they also know that I, the present self, will observe the aggregate pattern of their choices and update my confidence in my own reasoning accordingly. If the spectrum largely cooperates, I will treat my reasoning as validated; if it largely defects, I will treat it as suspect and perhaps override it in the future. This introduces a feedback loop: each future state is not merely deciding whether to cooperate or defect, but is implicitly voting on whether the reasoning they all share deserves to be trusted.

From this, I draw three implications I think are important:
First, superrationality may not be a static agreement but a dynamic process of self-observation, where the outcome of reasoning changes the conditions under which that reasoning operates.
Second, the boundary between self and copy becomes unstable, because my present self can reward or punish the reasoning process that produced the spectrum's behavior, turning a decision problem into a problem of self-governance.
Third, the Luring Lottery's classic temptation to free-ride is transformed: the real risk is not that others will enter, but that I might manipulate my own future beliefs about rationality to justify a defection I want to make now.

Please comment on these notes. Are they faithful to the underlying concepts I have named? Is the twist coherent, or does it collapse into something already well known? Do you find the implications interesting, or do they miss something obvious? I ask you to say how confident you are in your assessment and to single out the specific point about which you are least sure.
