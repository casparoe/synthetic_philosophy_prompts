A colleague presented the following account of Nelson Goodman’s new riddle of induction in our philosophy of science reading group: Goodman demonstrates that Hume’s problem of induction cannot be solved rationally by introducing the predicate grue—defined as green before some future time t and blue thereafter. Since emeralds observed up to now are both green and grue, induction offers equal support for predicting they will remain green and for predicting they will turn blue after t. Goodman resolves this by appealing to linguistic entrenchment: we are entitled to project green but not grue because the former is syntactically simpler and historically entrenched in our vocabulary, whereas the latter is an artificially gerrymandered predicate. Therefore, induction is not rationally justified by correspondence to nature; it is a conventional practice governed by the structure of our language, and Goodman’s account ultimately makes scientific lawhood a matter of linguistic habit rather than objective similarity.

I suspect this reading conflates Goodman’s descriptive account of projectibility with a normative solution, and it may overstate his conventionalism, but I am not certain where the line falls.
