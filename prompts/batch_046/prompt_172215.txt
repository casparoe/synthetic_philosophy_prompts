I sit on my university's Committee on Professional Conduct and Institutional Obligations, and we must circulate a written opinion before next month's faculty senate vote on a disciplinary case that has divided us evenly. A senior lecturer in philosophy was suspended without pay after she refused to sign the administration's new 'Institutional Loyalty and Transparency Pledge,' which requires faculty to report student gatherings discussing designated 'politically sensitive' topics to an internal oversight office. She does not deny violating her contract's direct-compliance clause; instead, she argues that political obligation to institutional rules is indirect—grounded not in administrative fiat but in what members of the institution would authorize under conditions of idealized deliberation in which no one knows whether they will be the reporter, the reported student, the administrator enforcing discipline, or the faculty member punished for refusal. Behind this 'institutional veil of ignorance,' she claims, rational deliberators would reject a suppression rule that lacks public justification, so the pledge lacks normative force even if it is legally valid. The administration responds that indirect normativity governs basic social structures, not particular employment policies, and that allowing every employee to nullify rules through hypothetical deliberation destroys institutional authority.

Please draft the committee's decision memo—recommend whether we uphold the suspension, overturn it outright, or overturn it with conditions (for instance, reinstating her but requiring a formal dissent filing rather than non-compliance). The memo should present a clear bottom line up front, followed by reasoning the senate can act on. It must treat the lecturer's indirect-normativity defense seriously: assess whether idealized deliberation can genuinely authorize institutional civil disobedience, and if so, where the boundary lies between justified refusal and mere insubordination. Crucially, include an honest engagement with the strongest consideration on the losing side—if you uphold the suspension, explain why the philosophical defense fails without simply assuming contract law trumps morality; if you overturn it, address the institutional-risk objection without dismissing it. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the reasoning should stand conceptually on its own. At the very end, state the recommendation in fixed form: Verdict: [Upheld / Overturned / Overturned with conditions].
