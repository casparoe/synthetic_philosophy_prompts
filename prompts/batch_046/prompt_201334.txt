As a journalist covering the philosophy of decision-making, I am preparing a feature on Douglas Hofstadter's superrationality experiments—specifically the Platonia dilemma, in which twenty isolated individuals must each independently choose, with some probability, to send a single telegram so that exactly one arrives and secures a shared prize, and the related Luring Lottery, which introduces a probabilistic reward structure that appears to complicate the convergence logic Platonia demands. The conventional reading holds that truly rational, symmetric agents should recognize their reasoning processes as identical and therefore coordinate on matching strategies—randomizing at one-in-twenty in Platonia, and adopting whatever calibrated mixed strategy the lottery's lure requires.

Yet a sharper philosophical objection has emerged: this reading may smuggle in a questionable metaphysics of agent-identity, treating my reasoning and yours as interchangeable nodes within a single computation, while ignoring the indexical asymmetry that distinguishes a centered, first-person decision problem from an abstract logical puzzle. Defenders reply that causal independence does not entail logical independence, and that Hofstadter's point was precisely that rationality can operate non-causally in symmetric settings.

Write an essay examining whether the Platonia dilemma and the Luring Lottery genuinely test a single, unified capacity for superrational reasoning, or whether they expose an internal tension—one case apparently requiring convergence among identical minds, the other requiring a calibrated divergence that resists simple coordination. Weigh the argument that superrationality depends on an illicit collapse of indexical perspectives against the counter-argument that symmetric reasoning legitimately produces identical outputs without metaphysical confusion. Reach a definite verdict: either defend that superrationality survives as a coherent framework (perhaps by distinguishing coordination conditions from lure conditions), or argue that the two experiments jointly undermine the coherence of treating symmetric reasoning as identity of choice. Give your reasons fully, and do not retreat to an agnostic or purely descriptive conclusion. If your position requires distinguishing what these experiments strictly presuppose from what they merely accommodate, make that distinction explicit.
