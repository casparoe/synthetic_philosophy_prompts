I'm working through a problem for a seminar on personal identity and practical reason, and I keep returning to a tension in how we justify advance directives for progressive dementia. I want to lay out an argument that seems to show that the authority of the earlier self collapses not just in dementia cases but everywhere, and then ask where the collapse is stopped.

Consider this chain. (1) An advance directive issued at t1 binds the patient at t2 only if the authoring self has normative authority over the affected self. (2) That authority cannot rest merely on numerical identity, since psychological continuity between t1 and t2 is severely disrupted in advanced dementia; it must rest on some substantive advantage—say, that the earlier self had a more stable or authentic conception of the patient's good. (3) But if the earlier self's authority depends on having a better conception of the patient's good, then it is contingent on an epistemic claim: the pre-dementia self knows what is best for the post-dementia self. (4) In dementia, however, the post-dementia self possesses direct phenomenological access to its own present welfare—its pains, pleasures, and preferences—while the earlier self had only speculative access; so the epistemic advantage actually runs in the opposite direction. (5) Therefore the directive should be overridden by the present preferences of the affected self. (6) The same reasoning applies whenever a future self acquires direct experiential knowledge that the planning self lacked: a young adult who signs a lifelong career contract, a citizen who ratifies a constitutional clause before experiencing its effects, or even a past generation that legislated for conditions it never lived through. (7) So either we must allow present selves to void virtually all long-term commitments made without direct experiential knowledge, or we must deny that epistemic privilege determines authority—meaning we need a different justification for advance directives, one that also applies to contracts, constitutions, and career vows. (8) But the conclusion in (6) is clearly too broad; we do not think every experienced self may unilaterally dissolve past commitments. Where does this argument slip? Is the error in treating experiential access as automatically conferring normative authority, in overgeneralizing from dementia to other domains, or in the initial assumption that authority requires an epistemic advantage at all? And if we save advance directives by rejecting the epistemic premise, do we pay a price elsewhere—perhaps by making the authority of the earlier self seem arbitrary or paternalistic?
