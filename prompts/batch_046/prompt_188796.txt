I have recently begun directing a small research group in philosophy of science, and the question of how I should assess my own methodological judgments—relative to the judgments of my peers—has become personally urgent. In discussions of forecasting and rationality, a common analogy compares the inside view of one's own reasoning to calibrating a scale while standing on it: you feel every fluctuation in the argument's weight, but because you are part of the load, you cannot read your own bias from the dial; only an outside observer, comparing you to a reference class, can correct the measurement. This is used to defend strong epistemic modesty—the claim that whenever you find yourself reasoning well, you should systematically discount that assessment in favor of the base-rate failure of similar thinkers.

I want an audit of this analogy. First, lay out the correspondence table: what plays the role of the scale, the weights being measured, the calibration standard, the act of stepping off, and the "load" contributed by the observer? Second, identify where the mapping breaks, particularly with respect to reflexivity—reasoning about reasoning changes the reasoning being assessed—and with respect to the possibility that the observer's contribution to the load is not noise but structurally necessary information. Third, distinguish the disanalogies that genuinely undermine the case for deference to the outside view from those that are merely decorative. Fourth, state whether the conclusion—systematic discounting of inside assessment—survives intact, or whether it requires a weaker formulation. Finally, if the scale analogy collapses in important respects, suggest a better one that preserves both the need for external calibration and the fact that the observer is constitutively part of the system being measured.
