I'm putting together the reusable evaluation rubric that a small neuroscience grant panel will apply every quarter to screen proposals studying subjective visual experience, and I want it grounded in Dennett's distinction between a Cartesian theater architecture and a multiple drafts model — not as philosophical ornament, but as a practical filter. The reviewers are working scientists with no formal philosophy training and about fifteen minutes per proposal, so I need a word-based decision tree: a sequence of tests they can run on the study's design, its choice of dependent measures, its treatment of temporal ordering in reports, and its handling of conflicting or non-canonical responses. The rubric should flag proposals that implicitly treat consciousness as having a single canonical screening point where experience becomes unified and available to a central observer, and it should do so without requiring reviewers to read primary philosophical texts. I also need the hard cases marked explicitly — for example, when a backward-masking study interprets a missing report as direct evidence of absent experience rather than as absence of fame in the brain, or when a continuous flash suppression protocol assumes that only one draft can be real at a given time — and I want clear annotations showing where judgment is unavoidable versus where the rule applies mechanically.

Please construct this as a reusable procedure with the role and stakes fixed: the reviewer must sign off, the decision affects funding up to fifty thousand dollars, and there is no appeal beyond the rubric's output. Apply the procedure to three hypothetical proposals: first, a binocular rivalry study that collects a single binary report per trial; second, a rapid serial visual presentation task that gathers graded confidence ratings across multiple time windows and allows subjects to register competing impressions; and third, a study that includes a delayed-revision condition in which subjects are invited to update their initial perceptual report after a short interval, modeled on revisionist accounts of introspective access. For each case, show exactly what the rubric outputs, highlight the architectural assumption it detects or clears, and include one instance where the rubric yields an answer the proposal authors likely did not intend — specifically, flagging a hidden Cartesian commitment in a study that presents itself as purely operationalist. Finally, state precisely what the rubric cannot do: it should not claim to settle whether phenomenal consciousness exists independently of reportability, and it should acknowledge that some design choices remain underdetermined by the theater-versus-drafts distinction alone. This was sparked by a thread I read on a rationalist forum arguing that most methodological checklists in consciousness science miss the architectural level entirely and treat first-person reports as transparent data; I'm trying to translate that critique into something a busy reviewer can actually use without guessing at citations.
