For a piece I am preparing on consciousness and narrative form, I would like a philosophical reading of Franz Kafka’s The Metamorphosis through the lens of epiphenomenalism, with particular attention to the paradox of phenomenal judgment. The paradox runs as follows: if phenomenal consciousness lacks causal efficacy, then judgments about consciousness—whether first-person reports or third-person attributions—are physically produced without being guided by the conscious states they concern, leaving their reliability unexplained. Kafka’s novella dramatizes this asymmetry structurally: the reader receives direct, narratively unmediated access to Gregor Samsa’s phenomenal life through limited narration, while the characters around him must infer his inner state from behavior and sound alone, eventually concluding that the creature in the room is no longer Gregor at all. Examine the family scenes in which they debate whether he understands language, and Gregor’s own attempts to assess whether he remains fully human inside. Does the narrative suggest that phenomenal judgment can be reliable despite causal isolation, or does it expose the skepticism that follows when consciousness leaves no behavioral footprint? Please take both the philosophical argument and the novella’s narrative technique seriously: show where the text resists reduction to an illustration of epiphenomenalism, and identify what the story adds to the debate about phenomenal testimony that abstract philosophy of mind tends to miss.
