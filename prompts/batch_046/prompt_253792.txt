I was discussing with a colleague whether the standard tripartite division—Middle Ages, Renaissance, Scientific Revolution—is something we discover in the historical record or something we impose upon it, and I realized our disagreement might be distributed across several independent judgments rather than resting on a single binary. I want you to treat this as a forecasting exercise: what is your actual credence, on a scale where 0 means pure retrospective invention and 1 means objectively real macro-historical kinds, that these three labels track genuine discontinuities in institutional and intellectual practice rather than being largely pedagogical constructions shaped by later historiography? My own intuition hovers near 0.30, but I am mainly interested in where your reasoning diverges from that baseline.

Please decompose your answer across four dimensions, each with a rough probability and a brief line of justification, then combine them into an aggregate number. First, assess whether "the Middle Ages" names a coherent cluster of practices that would survive a bottom-up clustering of primary sources without prior period labels—consider whether the coherence is mainly ecclesiastical, linguistic, or economic, and whether it fragments once we stop treating 500–1500 as a unit. Second, estimate the likelihood that the transition to the Renaissance represents a discontinuous break in modes of authority and textual practice, as opposed to a gradual diffusion of humanist methods through existing university, court, and chancellery structures. Third, evaluate the "Scientific Revolution" specifically: what is your credence that there was a unified transformation in the epistemic status of experiment and mathematical description, rather than a set of locally contingent innovations—astronomical, anatomical, mechanical—that only appear unified from the vantage of later textbooks? Fourth, weigh the meta-historical factor: to what extent does the survival of these categories depend on pedagogical utility rather than explanatory power, and would a continuous-gradient model with locally named turning points actually lose predictive or explanatory capacity?
