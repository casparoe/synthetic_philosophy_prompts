Here are my judgments about whether various "why X and not Y?" questions in economic and technological history are genuinely well posed, and I want to know whether a coherent principle unifies them or whether I must abandon one.

Asking why Britain experienced sustained per-capita industrial growth after 1750 rather than France strikes me as well posed: both societies shared comparable legal frameworks, market integration, scientific cultures, and Atlantic access, so the contrast can isolate specific institutional or resource differences without collapsing the background.

Asking why Britain and not China had achieved mechanized factory production by 1800 feels badly posed. The baseline conditions—property-rights regimes, state fiscal capacity, demographic patterns, labor-market structures, and the organization of knowledge—had diverged for centuries in ways so deep that the comparison seems to presuppose a shared causal pathway that never actually existed. It is closer to asking why a tree grew rather than why a stone did not.

Asking why coal-powered steam engines were refined in Cornwall and the Midlands rather than in coal-rich Shanxi seems well posed: both regions possessed accessible coal seams, mining expertise, and localized demand for drainage or transport, so holding those background factors roughly constant allows the contrast to probe the role of patent institutions, capital markets, or repair cultures.

Asking why modern economic growth began in eighteenth-century Britain rather than in second-century Rome strikes me as ill posed for a different reason. The technological, epistemic, and energetic baselines are separated by so much temporal distance that no stable counterfactual about "Rome with British conditions" can be constructed without anachronism.

But I keep wavering on this case: asking why Britain developed a parliamentary-capitalist order rather than the bureaucratic-absolutist form that prevailed in Qing China. On one hand, both were large agrarian empires facing comparable fiscal-military pressures and commercial expansion in the early modern period, which makes the contrast tempting. On the other hand, framing the comparison as "Britain versus China on development" risks smuggling in teleological assumptions about what "development" meant, and the institutional baselines may still be too heterogeneous to support a clean counterfactual. I go back and forth.

Is there a single principle—perhaps concerning the counterfactual stability of background conditions, the independence of the causal mechanisms being isolated, or the granularity appropriate to the comparison class—that delivers all four verdicts? If so, state it precisely, indicate which clause governs each case, and tell me what it predicts for a contrast I have not listed: why Britain moved toward centralized factory production rather than retaining the putting-out system that dominated in parts of Flanders and the Low Countries. If no principle saves every verdict, identify which one I should overturn, explain why that abandonment is less costly than complicating the principle, and show what the repaired principle then says about the Britain-China case.
