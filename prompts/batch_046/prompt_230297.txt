During a recent discussion with colleagues about whether sustained economic growth and social stability can ever serve as moral substitutes for democratic accountability, I found myself drawing analogies from distributed systems engineering—my own field—rather than from political theory, since I have no formal background in philosophy. The conversation touched on regimes that maintain high prosperity and order without competitive elections, with China and Singapore occasionally raised as reference points, though I am less interested in empirical country studies than in the underlying normative architecture. I want to defend a somewhat unconventional position: that prosperity and order can justify authoritarian rule, but only if we replace standard consent-based frameworks with what I call adaptive legitimacy, defined as a regime’s demonstrated capacity for rapid, harm-minimizing policy correction analogous to a self-healing system’s error-recovery protocol.

Please construct a rigorous argument for the claim that an authoritarian system is morally justified in lacking electoral mechanisms if, and only if, its institutional error-correction velocity—measured by the speed and accuracy with which it revises harmful policies—exceeds the predicted deliberative latency of an equivalent democratic process for the same domain, and if the resulting prosperity and order are distributed according to a modified difference principle that privileges the worst-off through stability rather than through voting rights. Your argument should specifically address the autonomy-based objection that reducing citizens to monitored nodes within a governance feedback loop eliminates their status as moral agents, and should explain why rapid error correction should be treated not merely as an instrumental good but as a constitutive element of political legitimacy under this model. Keep the reasoning at a conceptual level, avoid citing named philosophers or historical texts by name, do not provide empirical case studies, and rely on first-principles reasoning that integrates the systems-engineering analogy with normative evaluation.
