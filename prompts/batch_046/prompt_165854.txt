I have recently begun assisting with a citizenship-education curriculum for adult immigrants, and I have become personally unsettled by the tension between the official narrative—which presents national identity as rooted in continuous, ancient tradition—and what I now understand from Hobsbawm and Anderson about invented traditions and imagined communities. I will lead one ninety-minute discussion session with these same learners, all of whom have just completed naturalization and are invested in defending the legitimacy of their adopted nation. I am not asking for a summary of the theories; I need a teaching plan. In what precise order should I introduce invented tradition and imagined community, given that each illuminates the other but they risk collapsing into a single idea? For the carrying example, avoid the standard cases—the Scottish kilt, Bastille Day rituals—that this audience may already have encountered in popular media; instead propose a concrete scenario that simultaneously demonstrates ritual repetition and the mental co-presence of strangers, and explain why it suits this particular group. What misconceptions will arise specifically among people who want to defend national dignity—for instance, conflating "imagined" with "fabricated," or treating the recognition of recent invention as an insult rather than an analysis—and at which point in the sequence should each be anticipated and defused? Include one brief exercise or final question that tests whether participants understand that a community can be both historically recent and genuinely binding, rather than merely recalling terminology. Finally, what portion of the theoretical apparatus—perhaps the full typology of traditions or Anderson's print-capitalism thesis—should I explicitly set aside for this session and acknowledge as beyond scope?
