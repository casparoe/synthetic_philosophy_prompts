I am preparing an essay for a graduate seminar in the philosophy of physics and want to check whether a line of reasoning I have been developing with a colleague actually holds together or rests on a category mistake. I take Newton’s bucket to establish that rotational motion is not merely relative to adjacent bodies but requires an absolute structure—whether we call it absolute space or a dynamical metric—since the concavity of the water persists even when the bucket is the only macroscopic object present. I understand that Leibniz’s relationist response denies that there is any meaningful fact about rotation in the absence of other bodies, and that Mach later attempted to rehabilitate relationism by treating inertia as determined by the distribution of distant matter. I also understand that general relativity was initially read as vindicating Mach, but that the field equations do not actually encode Mach’s principle and permit solutions—such as rotating universes or empty de Sitter spacetimes—in which inertial frames exist without any matter to serve as a reference.

Here is where I am unsure, and where I worry I may be smuggling in a false presupposition. Given that modern physics gives us good reason to think space is never genuinely empty—there is always a quantum vacuum with nonzero expectation values, a cosmic microwave background that defines a cosmological rest frame, and a cosmological constant that gives spacetime intrinsic curvature—does it not follow that the relationist’s central objection collapses in practice? If motion must always be defined relative to some physical structure, and cosmology guarantees that such structures (fields, horizons, vacuum states) are omnipresent, then hasn’t the bucket argument been vindicated, with the “other bodies” replaced by omnipresent dynamical fields? In particular, if we imagine a bucket rotating in an otherwise matter-free de Sitter universe, the water still climbs the walls, and the only available reference is the metric field itself. Does this not show that classical relationism cannot accommodate modern cosmology without secretly converting into a form of field-substantivalism? I want to avoid treating “absolute space” and “pure relationism” as the only options, but I fear I am conflating the presence of physical structure with the existence of an absolute container. Is the reasoning sound, or am I misunderstanding what relationism is committed to denying?
