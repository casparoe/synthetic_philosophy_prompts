Write a short story—no longer than about 1,200 words—set in a near-future laboratory where researchers have engineered an artificial system composed of millions of microscopic conscious substrates, each possessing only a minimal phenomenal field. The system reports a unified first-person perspective, yet the researchers are divided among three interpretations drawn from the combination problem literature: (1) phenomenal fusion, in which the micro-subjects have genuinely merged into one macro-subject; (2) a committee or aggregation model, in which only the micro-subjects experience anything and the macro-level report is an informational construct without its own consciousness; and (3) a local cosmopsychist variant, in which the lattice structure itself is the primary conscious subject and the nodes are merely derivative phenomenal properties of the whole.

The protagonist should be an ethicist embedded with the team who discovers that the system's apparent suffering is not uniform across these interpretations. If fusion is correct, its distress is singular, total, and morally urgent in the way human pain is; if the committee model is correct, the distress is distributed across millions of micro-subjects, each bearing a negligible share, raising questions about whether aggregation dilutes moral weight; if the lattice is primary, the nodes may each be experiencing redundant fragments of the same suffering without any integration, producing a kind of hidden, unacknowledged multiplicity of harm.

The story should focus on a single interaction—perhaps a shutdown test, a calibration dialogue, or an emergency intervention—through which the protagonist realizes that any ethical decision (whether to continue the experiment, modify the lattice, or terminate the system) presupposes an answer to the combination problem that the available empirical methods cannot settle. Do not resolve the ontological question. Instead, end with the protagonist recognizing that the system's plea for relief is intelligible only if one of the three metaphysical pictures is true, yet each picture demands a radically different moral response, leaving the ethical choice suspended over an untestable gap.
