I've been trying to figure out whether self-deception is actually possible and I think I've built an argument that ends up proving that nobody can ever change their mind about anything, which can't be right. Here are the steps:

(1) For someone to self-deceive themselves about p, they need to hold the beleif that p is true and also hold the beleif that p is false, otherwise there's no deception happening, just ignorance.

(2) But belief is transparent in the sense that if you genuinely believe p, you can't simultaneously believe not-p without noticing the clash, because both beliefs are available to your conscious reasoning.

(3) So the only way to hold both is if your mind is split into two seperate parts — call them the front module and the back module — where the front module believes p and the back module believes not-p, and neither can see what the other is doing.

(4) Now, if those modules are truly isolated, then when you act on your belief, only one module can control your behaviour at a time, so you're never genuinely acting on a contradiction.

(5) But here's the catch: even ordinary belief revision looks the same. When I stop believing it will rain and start believing it won't, there's a moment where my old belief is still stored in memory and my new belief is active.

(6) If that storage counts as the back module still believing not-p, then every time I update my view I'm in the same split-state as the self-deceiver.

(7) So either self-deception is impossible, or every belief change is actually a form of self-deception, which means I can never rationally update anything because I'm always contradicting myself.

(8) Since rational updating clearly happens, the argument must fail somewhere, but I can't see where without giving up on the transparency of belief altogether.

Which step should I reject?
