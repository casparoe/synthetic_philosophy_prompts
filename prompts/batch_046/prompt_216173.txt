My friend and I just finished a long argument about why so many governments offer tax breaks, cash subsidies, and expanded parental leave specifically tied to the number of children one has. She insists the practice is purely economic—future labor supply and tax bases—but I think it carries hidden philosophical baggage. By treating each additional birth as a policy win rather than a neutral private choice, these institutions seem to presuppose that bringing new people into existence is an unqualified contribution to social welfare, which sounds like total utilitarianism. Yet they stop short of framing childbearing as a collective duty or allowing trade-offs that sacrifice existing citizens' interests for purely potential ones, which sits awkwardly with strict totalism. So what implicit view of population ethics—total versus average versus person-affecting—does pronatalism actually commit to? Does it also presuppose a particular stance on the non-identity problem or on whether merely possible people have moral standing? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely fits it well, note where it is philosophically underdetermined, and say what concrete policy feature would have to change if we replaced its underlying view with a replaceability-sensitive or asymmetry-respecting framework.
