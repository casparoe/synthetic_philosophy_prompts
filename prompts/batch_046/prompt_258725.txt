I serve on an interdisciplinary review panel that approves predictive models submitted for regional policy—climate adaptation, epidemiological forecasting, fisheries management—by external contractors. We review roughly one proposal per session, must sign off within thirty minutes using only the documentation the modeler provides, and our approvals carry regulatory weight: an unconditional pass allows immediate implementation, a conditional pass mandates specific sensitivity analyses, and a rejection sends the proposal back for structural revision. Every submission contains unrealistic assumptions—continuous variables for discrete populations, perfect rationality, homogeneous mixing, linear response curves, absence of feedback loops. The modelers invariably invoke Friedman’s principle that unrealistic assumptions are permissible if the model produces accurate predictions. I have noticed, however, a recurring defensive maneuver—call it the F-twist—in which a modeler protects an unrealistic assumption not by independently validating predictive accuracy, but by redefining the prediction target so that it is tautologically satisfied by the very structure the assumption imposes.

I need a reusable decision procedure my panel can run in ordered steps, without requiring specialized domain knowledge beyond the proposal’s own text. Structure it as a sequence of tests with clear stopping rules: the conditions for an unconditional approval, for a conditional approval with required checks, and for a rejection; and the precise point at which the decision must be handed over to human judgment because it concerns values—acceptable risk, distributive fairness, or precaution—rather than predictive validity. The procedure must classify each unrealistic assumption as predictively inert in the domain of interest, load-bearing but empirically anchored, or structurally distorting—meaning it alters the causal mechanism that the policy intervention is meant to influence. It must include a specific diagnostic for the F-twist. For each test, state what counts as a sufficient answer given only the proposal’s claims, and explicitly flag which steps remain judgment calls dressed in procedural form. Be honest about misfire modes: specify at least one class of models the procedure will wrongly approve and one it will wrongly reject.

Then apply the full procedure to three proposals. First, a fisheries management model that treats fish biomass as continuously divisible, ignoring discrete cohort structure, and defends the assumption by citing accurate aggregate catch predictions over the past decade. Second, a regional housing-price forecast that assumes all buyers are rational optimizers with perfect information and homogeneous preferences, defending the assumption through strong out-of-sample predictive accuracy in stable markets. Third—the case I expect the procedure to handle in a way that makes me uncomfortable—a public-health intervention model for respiratory disease transmission that assumes homogeneous social mixing across the population. The model’s infection-curve predictions have matched observed data in three prior outbreaks, yet the homogeneous-mixing assumption eliminates superspreading dynamics, which means the model cannot distinguish between targeted closures and broad restrictions, even though that distinction is exactly what the policy decision requires. Run every step, show where the procedure stops, state the verdict it delivers, and say clearly whether accepting that verdict requires the panel to suppress an intuition that should not be suppressed.

Finally, list three structural limitations of the procedure—things it cannot do regardless of time or data, such as detecting hidden assumptions not disclosed in the proposal, substituting for ethical judgment about risk distribution, or validating predictions in genuinely novel regimes outside the model’s calibration range—and explain why each is irremediable rather than merely impractical.
