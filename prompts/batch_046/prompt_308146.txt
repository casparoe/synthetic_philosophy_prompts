As a novelist who has spent years reconstructing lives from fragmentary archives, I want to articulate the ethical framework I implicitly work within when I dramatize real people who can no longer answer back. Before I defend any particular stance, I need the full specification. What are the desiderata—phenomena, distinctions, and consistency conditions—that any adequate theory of our obligations to the dead in historical fiction and biopics must satisfy?

Specifically, map out: (1) the cases it must handle without contradiction, including at minimum—dramatizing a figure who left explicit posthumous instructions about representation; dramatizing someone whose historical record was deliberately erased, leaving only hostile accounts; and dramatizing a private individual whose descendants are still living and actively contest the narrative; (2) the distinctions it must preserve—such as between harming a posthumous reputation and distorting collective historical memory, between obligations owed to the dead themselves and obligations owed to living audiences or survivors, and between artistic invention that illuminates and invention that merely sensationalizes—together with the reason each distinction is non-negotiable; (3) the results from law, moral psychology, and historiography it cannot flatly contradict—for instance, that dead persons lack sentience but may retain legible interests in legacy, or that narrative representations often become the dominant mnemonic image of a life (a phenomenon I will call mnemonic supersession)—and why each consistency check matters; (4) which desiderata are genuinely binding and which are merely preferences of particular camps, such as absolute fidelity to the archive versus radical artistic autonomy; and (5) whether any subset of these requirements is jointly unsatisfiable—for example, whether honoring a dead person's explicit wish for obscurity can ever be reconciled with a public interest in knowing their story, and if that tension is resolvable or a permanent antinomy.

Finally, without constructing a bibliography or reading list, briefly assess how three broad positions—strict archival fidelity, unrestricted artistic license, and a consent-based or relational ethics—fare against this list, flagging for each which desiderata they violate and which they satisfy. I am writing this for my own craft, not for an academic citation chain, so I need conceptual precision rather than scholarly apparatus, and I want to know which single requirement does the most work in excluding the popular theories.
