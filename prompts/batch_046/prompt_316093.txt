Having retired from experimental physics only recently and begun studying moral philosophy, I have approached Rousseau's letter to d'Alembert on the theater with the conceptual habits of someone accustomed to thinking about instruments and systematic error. Rousseau describes theatrical spectacle as substituting artificial sentiments for direct civic engagement, which I initially read as an epistemic diagnosis: the stage functions like an improperly calibrated detector, introducing distortion into the spectator's moral perception of virtue and vice. If that interpretation is correct, then the defect should lie in representational inaccuracy rather than in representation as such, and a rigorously unmediated or documentary form of theater ought to escape his condemnation. Yet Rousseau praises the public festival—in which citizens are simultaneously actors and audience without intermediaries—and condemns even plays of the highest moral content. This suggests that the corruption lies not in misrepresentation but in mediation itself. But then I face a difficulty: if mediation per se corrupts, then language, legal codes, and scientific notation, all of which interpose symbolic structures between us and immediate reality, should be equally suspect. Since Rousseau plainly accepts language and law, there must be a principled distinction between benign and corrupting mediation. Is the distinction that theatrical representation severs the actor from the audience in a way that undermines the general will, whereas language and law are collective self-representations? Or does his argument rest on a hidden empirical premise—perhaps that spectacle weakens civic courage through a specific psychological mechanism that reading statutes does not—which my scientific background makes me want to isolate and test? I am trying to locate precisely where the analogy between theatrical representation and instrumental error collapses, and whether that collapse exposes a misunderstanding on my part or a genuine tension in Rousseau's account of symbolic form and political virtue.
