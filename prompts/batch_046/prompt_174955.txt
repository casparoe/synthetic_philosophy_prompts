Specify the full list of desiderata that a normative theory of social attitudes toward individual achievement must meet if it is to adjudicate, without collapsing into either pole, between the American Dream's celebratory individualism and the egalitarian leveling dynamics captured by Jantelagen and the tall poppy syndrome. For each requirement, state the motivating case or distinction it protects—such as earned versus structurally advantaged success, private satisfaction versus public prominence, suppression of arrogance versus suppression of excellence, and equality of opportunity versus equality of outcome—together with the sociological or psychological constraint it must respect (e.g., that high-trust societies tolerate greater visibility of disparity, that cutting down high achievers correlates with both reduced status anxiety and reduced innovation, and that recognition economies depend on credible signaling). Mark which desiderata are non-negotiable across contexts and which are culturally contingent preferences of particular camps. Then inject and evaluate a novel institutional mechanism—call it "calibrated prominence," in which an individual's public recognition is algorithmically scaled in inverse proportion to the community's measured equity gap—specifying for each desideratum whether this mechanism satisfies, violates, or transforms it. Finally, identify whether any subset of these requirements is jointly unsatisfiable given that recognition and leveling are structurally opposed, and state which single desideratum does the most work in excluding the two dominant folk theories: pure meritocratic celebration and pure egalitarian concealment. A structured list is acceptable; the weight should be on the criteria and their tensions rather than on a final scorecard.
