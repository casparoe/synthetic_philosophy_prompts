I'm working on a small side project about AI alignment — nothing fancy, just trying to wrap my head around these 'impact measures' people talk about. The basic idea is you want an agent to do its task without messing up the world more than necessary: don't knock over vases, don't rearrange the office just to grab a coffee, that kind of thing. So people propose formal rules like comparing the world-state after action to a baseline and penalizing big divergences, or locking actions inside some referance class of variables. At first glance it looks totally value-neutral, just 'don't cause side effects.'

But every time I dig into the details it collapses. You have to pick the basline and the variables — atoms? human welfare indicators? the exact position of every coffee cup? Make it too broad and literally breathing gets penalized; make it too narrow and you've basically hard-coded what counts as important, which is exactly the value specification you claimed to avoid. I noticed the same pattern in older robotics papers on minimal-intervention planning, where there's always a hidden preference smuggled into the choice of 'normal' conditions.

So does this specific collapse have a name? I'm not asking for the general 'alignment is hard' problem or Goodhart's law, but the particular failure mode where a technical constraint on side effects pretends to be value-free but actually hides its value commitments inside the baseline or referance set. If there's no established term, what would you call it, and where are its edges — is it different from the statistical problem of defining a 'normal' range, or is it the same beast wearing different clothes? Also, does it show up outside AI, like in environmental policy where people talk about 'keeping ecosystems intact' without saying intact relative to what?
