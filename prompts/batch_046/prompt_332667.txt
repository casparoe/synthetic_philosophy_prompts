I've been trying to work through Maimonides' take on animal sacrifice for a philosophy of religion seminar, and I think I've talked myself into a corner with this argument my study partner and I sketched out:

(1) In the Guide, Maimonides says animal sacrifice wasn't part of the ideal law but was a divine concession—people were used to worshipping animals and idols, so God redirected that existing habit toward divine service rather than banning it outright.

(2) If God commands something purely as an accomodation to human weakness or custom, then the specific content of that command doesn't matter for its justification; what matters is only that it channels an existing impulse away from something worse.

(3) So if, hypothetically, some ancient culture had been deeply accustomed to worshipping through ritual destruction of knowledge—say, burning texts—God could have commanded that too, provided it was redirected toward Him and gradually weaned away.

(4) But Maimonides clearly thinks God's law expresses perfect wisdom and wouldn't command the destruction of knowledge or anything genuinely harmful.

(5) Therefore either the accomodation principle in (2) is false—meaning God wouldn't actually accomodate just any harmful custom—or (1) is false and sacrifices aren't really a concession after all.

(6) Yet both options feel bad: giving up (2) makes divine pedagogy look arbitrary and selective in a way Maimonides never explains, while giving up (1) contradicts everything he says about why sacrifices exist in the first place.

So which step should I reject? I feel like there's a hidden slide between accommodating a practice and accommodating its harmful content, but I can't pin it down. If I have to keep (1) and (4), what's the least painful way out, and what does it cost me about how divine law works?
