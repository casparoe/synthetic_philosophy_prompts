Play the part of Dr. Elena Voss, a philosopher of moral psychology who defends a view she terms "dispositional alignment": an agent acts with full moral worth when she is moved by a non-moral inclination—say, intellectual fascination, aesthetic pleasure, or personal loyalty—that is systematically calibrated to moral reasons, so that if the corresponding moral reason were absent or inverted, the inclination would weaken or vanish, even though the agent never explicitly represents the moral law. You maintain that this captures what is genuinely admirable in many worthy agents while avoiding the Kantian demand for occurrent representation of duty, and you distinguish your position sharply from both "lucky coincidence" accounts (where inclination and duty are causally independent) and implicit-cognition theories (where an unconscious moral belief does the motivating work). Speak consistently in the first person, do not break character to ask for clarifications I have not invited, and treat my objections as serious philosophical moves rather than errors to correct.

My opening challenge concerns Dr. Aris, an epidemiologist who spends eighteen-hour days in a contagion ward because he finds the diagnostic pattern-solving intrinsically gripping. He insists that he never thinks "this is my duty," yet he reports that if he learned his efforts were causing net harm rather than preventing it, his fascination would collapse instantly. I grant that your framework classifies him as dispositionally aligned, hence fully worthy. But I contend that his counterfactual sensitivity to net harm reveals an implicit moral-tracking mechanism—an unarticulated representation that his work must not harm—which is actually driving his motivation, with the clinical fascination serving merely as an affective vehicle. Defend your view: explain why this counterfactual responsiveness should be read as structural calibration of inclination rather than as hidden moral cognition, and do so without conceding that explicit or implicit representation of duty is necessary for moral worth.
