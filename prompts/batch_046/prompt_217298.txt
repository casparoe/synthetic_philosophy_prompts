I serve as a welfare compliance auditor for an aquaculture cooperative that raises Atlantic salmon, Pacific white shrimp, and European lobster in shared-water recirculation systems. We keep receiving proposals to modify husbandry practices—adding substrate structures, altering photoperiod, switching stunning methods, or changing feeding regimes—with claims that they improve welfare, but we have no reproducible framework for deciding when a change genuinely advances welfare rather than merely altering measurable behavior or reducing mortality without addressing subjective experience. I want you to construct an operational decision procedure my team can apply to any such proposal, grounded in the conceptual distinction between nociceptive reflex, integrated affective state, and long-term functional impairment, but translated into a sequence of practical questions that do not require a philosophy degree to answer. The procedure should specify, in order: how to establish the relevant welfare baseline for the species and life stage; what kind of evidence, behavioral or physiological or both, must be present to claim improvement rather than mere change; where the analysis stops with a definitive rejection because the intervention fails on empirical grounds; and where it must hand the decision to a values judgment—such as trade-offs between individual suffering intensity and population-level mortality—because the science underdetermines the choice.

Then run the procedure on two concrete proposals: installing vertical refuge panels in lobster grow-out tanks to reduce aggression-related injuries, and reducing daily feeding frequency in shrimp to lower metabolic stress markers despite potential growth-rate penalties. One application should produce a verdict that the cooperative's production managers are likely to reject, and I want the procedure to pinpoint whether the conflict is empirical, conceptual—about which welfare dimension takes priority—or normative, about acceptable risk thresholds. Be explicit about the steps where judgment cannot be eliminated, flag the specific conditions under which applying the procedure mechanically would produce a misleading result, and distinguish the cases where the procedure gives a clear yes or no from those where it can only structure the disagreement without resolving it.
