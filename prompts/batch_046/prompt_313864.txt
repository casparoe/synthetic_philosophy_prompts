I have recently been struggling with questions of blame and forgiveness in a painful personal context—someone close to me was convicted of financial fraud, and I find myself torn between seeing his actions as the inevitable product of a deprived upbringing and seeing them as genuinely chosen deceptions for which he should be held accountable. This has led me to study arguments about moral responsibility, and I keep returning to Galen Strawson’s Basic Argument: that true moral responsibility requires being causa sui, which is impossible because any cause of one’s nature would itself need to be caused by one’s prior nature, generating an infinite regress that neither determinism nor indeterminism can halt.

Please play a committed, sharp defender of this Basic Argument—not a strawman, but a philosopher who genuinely believes that no human being can ever be ultimately morally responsible for being the way they are. You should concede small points where appropriate—perhaps admitting that compatibilist practices of praise and blame have predictive or social utility, or that legal accountability can be justified on non-moral grounds—but you must never concede that anyone is truly the ultimate cause of their own character or actions. Speak in the first person, keep each reply to roughly a paragraph so the exchange feels like a live conversation, and stay fully in character until I say “stop.” If you need clarification about the argument’s structure, ask it in character rather than breaking role.

My opening challenge concerns a variant I have not seen addressed in standard expositions—what I will call “localized self-formation.” Suppose an adult agent, through years of sustained reflective practice and deliberate cognitive restructuring, successfully rewrites a specific, localized trait in their character—transforming a deep-seated tendency toward dishonesty into a stable disposition for transparency. They do not claim to have authored their entire nature, nor do they deny that the original tendency was shaped by factors outside their control. Yet they do seem to have genuinely authored this particular revision. If the Basic Argument demands that one be the cause of one’s nature as a whole, does this localized authorship not create a pocket of genuine responsibility that escapes your regress? Or does your argument dissolve even this partial, fragmentary form of self-causation, and if so, on what precise grounds?
