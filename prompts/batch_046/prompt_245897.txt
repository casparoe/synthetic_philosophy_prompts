I've been reporting for some time on the replication problems in moral psychology and on the growing suspicion that much of what philosophers treat as raw "moral intuition" is in fact a WEIRD artifact—shaped by literacy, market integration, and weak kinship ties rather than by direct access to moral truth. From that work I've come to hold a somewhat defensive position about cross-cultural divergence: the well-documented attenuation of loyalty, authority, and purity judgments in WEIRD populations, compared with their persistence elsewhere, does not actually undermine moral objectivity, provided we reconceive objectivity not as universal intuitive agreement but as what I would call institutionally mediated convergence. On this view, moral truths stabilize only under particular social-coordination regimes; they can lie dormant or be actively suppressed when institutional scaffolding shifts—say, when kin-based reciprocity and hierarchical coordination are replaced by contract, bureaucracy, and anonymous exchange. So WEIRD samples are not stripping away cultural noise to reveal morality's true skeleton; they are revealing what morality looks like when the scaffolding that activates certain domains has been dismantled, which suppresses those domains without falsifying them.

What would genuinely count against this position? I am not asking for the standard skeptical move—that disagreement proves subjectivity—since my view is designed to absorb exactly that. I want the defeaters that its defenders ought to take seriously: empirical patterns, conceptual tensions, or rival interpretations showing that institutional mediation cannot do the explanatory work I am assigning it. For example, if cross-cultural divergence turned out to be far deeper and more chaotic than the WEIRD/non-WEIRD binary allows—if there were no reliable mapping between specific institutional features and specific moral profiles—would that collapse the view? Or if developmental or neuroscientific evidence showed that the "suppressed" moral domains in WEIRD populations are not merely dormant but genuinely incoherent or unrecoverable in ways that institutional reconstruction cannot repair? I also want an honest assessment of whether the position has been qualified into unfalsifiability: can any observed divergence be accommodated by adjusting the institutional story, and if so, is that insulation a theoretical virtue or a warning sign? Please rank the potential defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, distinguish what a committed institutional-convergence theorist should concede from what they could reasonably dismiss, and indicate whether any observation—already documented or still conceivable—should actually move someone who holds this view.
