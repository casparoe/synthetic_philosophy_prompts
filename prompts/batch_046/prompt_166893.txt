As a retired experimental physicist who has only recently begun systematic study in political philosophy, I find myself returning repeatedly to Hannah Arendt's account of deception in modern governance—specifically her argument, developed in direct response to the 1971 Pentagon Papers revelations, that bureaucratic lying is not merely instrumental concealment directed at enemies or publics, but a systematic defactualization in which policy-makers come to inhabit a fictional reality sustained by quantitative abstractions and internal image-management. What strikes me as an unexamined corollary—one I want to propose not as an established doctrine but as a conceptual extension—is that if the metrics and strategic models used to justify a policy are themselves generated within a deceptive framework, then the deception does not merely mislead outsiders; it recursively corrupts the evidentiary base available to decision-makers themselves, producing a closed epistemic loop that Arendt describes but never fully formalizes.

The historical episode is well known: Daniel Ellsberg leaked classified Department of Defense studies demonstrating that successive U.S. administrations had systematically misrepresented to Congress and the public both the scale and the prognosis of American involvement in Vietnam, even as internal analyses—many produced by RAND Corporation analysts and defense intellectuals—recorded far more pessimistic assessments. Applying Arendt's framework, I ask you to examine what the principal actors actually faced. In what respects were their falsehoods traditional statecraft—targeted deceptions for strategic or domestic political ends—and in what respects did they constitute the specifically modern, bureaucratic form of defactualization that Arendt emphasizes? How well does the proposed extension—call it recursive data corruption, in which body counts, bombing statistics, and strategic assessments become artifacts of the very narrative they are meant to support—explain the conduct of figures such as Robert McNamara and the problem-solvers she criticizes? Where does her theory illuminate structural features that standard historical narratives overlook, and where does it misdescribe the institutional dynamics or remain silent?

Finally, I want the analysis to run in the opposite direction, testing the theory against the episode. The fact that an insider could access, reproduce, and eventually release these documents suggests a certain fragility in the closed loop—does this undermine Arendt's pessimism about the self-sustaining nature of bureaucratic fiction, or can it be accommodated as an exceptional breach that confirms the rule? Does the subsequent public exposure and congressional reaction demonstrate that factual truth retains a disruptive power Arendt underestimated, or does it instead show how easily revealed falsehoods are absorbed into revised narratives without genuine correction? I am not seeking a bibliographic survey or a list of secondary readings; I want a disciplined, bidirectional case study that applies both Arendt's original claims and my proposed extension to the salient facts of the Pentagon Papers crisis, clearly marking where the theory illuminates, where it strains, and where it falls silent.
