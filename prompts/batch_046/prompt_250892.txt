I hold a particular conjunction of claims about insect sentience and moral priority, and I want to know what would genuinely count against it—not generic skepticism about animal ethics, but defeaters aimed at this specific formulation. First claim: for many insect species, the probability that any given individual is phenomenally conscious is low but non-negligible—say, between one and ten percent for some social insects, and far lower for others—based on current neurobiological and behavioral evidence. Second claim: because global insect populations are so vast and reproductive cycles so rapid, straightforward expected-value reasoning suggests that the aggregate expected suffering among insects dwarfs that of all farmed vertebrates combined, even with those low individual probabilities. Third claim: moral reasoning should therefore treat insect welfare as a first-order priority, but not through naive aggregation; rather, through a risk-sensitive precautionary framing. Under this framing, when a population reaches a scale where low-confidence sentience attributions generate massive expected harm, the epistemic burden shifts, and we ought to act as if sentience is present unless there is decisive, species-specific evidence of its absence. I want an honest map of this position's exposure. Which empirical results in invertebrate neurobiology—say, about nociception without affective processing, or about decentralized nervous systems—would undermine the low-confidence attribution rather than merely lower it? Which formal or conceptual difficulties with applying expected-value reasoning to moral patienthood—particularly regarding the aggregation of harm across generations with rapid turnover and unstable individual identity—are fatal versus merely awkward? Would it be decisive if this precautionary framing turned out to apply with equal force to bacteria, fungal networks, or large-scale digital simulations, effectively dissolving into moral paralysis? And does the view risk being insulated into unfalsifiability by retreating to an unfalsifiable gradient model of sentience, where no observation could dislodge the attribution? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, distinguish what defenders of this view should concede as genuinely damaging from what they could reasonably dismiss, and assess whether the position's insulation—if present—is a virtue of caution or a vice that empties it of empirical content.
