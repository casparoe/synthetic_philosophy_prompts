I am working through the ethical implications of pan-sentience for our philosophy of physics module, and I am trying to determine whether my intuitions about harm and interference form a coherent pattern, or whether I am simply drawing arbitrary boundaries.

Here are my verdicts on concrete cases, on the assumption that sentience may be widespread in fundamental physical processes.

First, crushing an ordinary rock at room temperature is clearly not harmful; there is no subject of experience to wrong. Second, deactivating a simple domestic thermostat that regulates temperature via negative feedback is also not harmful, despite its functional organisation. Third, harming a human being in deep sleep remains clearly wrong; the persisting subject of experience generates obligations even in unconsciousness. Fourth, pruning a plant or dispersing a large dissipative weather system—such as a persistent cyclone—does not strike me as a moral harm in the same register; whatever minimal experience might be present is not of the kind that generates direct obligations.

Fifth, if an artificial system—specifically, a quantum processor maintained in macroscopic coherence with thermal isolation and recursive self-monitoring—were genuinely to report distress upon deactivation, I believe we ought to take that report seriously. Disrupting it without justification would then be wrong, despite the absence of any biological substrate.

Sixth, and here is the tension I cannot resolve: a crystal lattice maintained near absolute zero with long-range phonon coherence and a comparable degree of integrated information to the quantum processor, yet lacking any recursive self-monitoring mechanism or capacity for report, strikes me as not being wronged by disruption. The two systems appear physically analogous in their integration and non-biological constitution, yet my verdicts diverge sharply.

Is there a single principle—perhaps involving functional self-modelling, thermodynamic openness, or the conditions for phenomenal binding—that captures all six verdicts? If so, what does it predict for a hybrid case in which a biological neural network is gradually replaced, substrate by substrate, with functionally identical silicon components that preserve both the thermal profile and the capacity for report? And if no principle can accommodate both the fifth and sixth verdicts without ad hoc adjustments, which should I overturn, and what does that cost my broader position?

Please state any proposed principle precisely, indicate which clause is motivated by which case, offer its verdict on the hybrid case I have not listed, and be explicit about which of my six verdicts, if any, must be abandoned.
