I'm giving the opening remarks at an interdisciplinary workshop on colonial legacies and museum restitution, co-hosted by a museum studies program and a cultural heritage law center. The audience is evenly divided: half are curators and art historians who work with Benin Bronzes and Parthenon Marbles collections, and half are philosophers and legal theorists who approach restitution mainly through frameworks of distributive justice and corrective duty. I want the speech structured around a conceptual distinction I'm developing in my own work—the difference between restitution as the correction of wrongful possession and restitution as what I call "ontological reclassification," meaning the shift of an object from being an artifact embedded in a universal, taxonomic museum narrative to being a living element within a specific communal history whose meaning is not fully translatable. This distinction should do real structural work: it should prevent me from framing return as a simple reversal of theft that restores the status quo ante, and it should also prevent me from endorsing the custodial argument that preservation requires continued universal access. I need to welcome both practitioners and theorists without collapsing the tension, to reference the Benin Bronzes and the Parthenon Marbles concretely—not as illustrations but as cases where the object's ontological status is actively contested—and to conclude by framing the day's discussions rather than delivering a verdict. Please write a polished ten-minute address for this mixed audience, avoiding bibliographic references, reading lists, or citations.
