I am preparing notes for an essay in a jurisprudence course that examines whether the Hart-Fuller debate can be productively extended to interpretive problems neither author explicitly considered—specifically, the stabilization of legal meaning in administrative systems where interpretive rules are continuously updated through automated processes rather than by human legislators or judges. I want to test whether this framing genuinely extends the debate or merely applies it anachronistically.

My draft argument proceeds in three stages. First, I claim that Hart’s distinction between the core and penumbra of legal rules depends on a shared internal point of view among human officials; in an automated system lacking such shared practical attitudes, the penumbra does not merely expand but becomes structurally unstable, because there is no social practice to anchor the rule of recognition. Second, I propose that Fuller’s well-known canons of legality—particularly the requirements that rules not demand the impossible and that official action remain congruent with declared rules—can operate as procedural constraints on interpretation even in the absence of human legislative intent, suggesting that Fuller’s inner morality provides functional stability where Hart’s sociology of practice fails. Third, I argue that this does not vindicate Fuller’s natural law thesis in its traditional moral form, since these procedural constraints carry no substantive ethical content, but it does reveal that Hart’s legal positivism relies more heavily on a sociology of human legal practice than his separation thesis admits.

I would welcome critical comments on several points. Does my characterization of the internal point of view as essentially sociological misrepresent Hart’s more normative account? Can Fuller’s canons genuinely apply without a human interpreter whose actions might be congruent or incongruent with rules, or does the automated case simply fail to meet the conditions for Fuller’s framework? And does my conclusion—that we have uncovered a sociological dependency rather than a moral one—actually avoid collapsing back into Fuller’s position, or does it implicitly concede that law necessarily embeds a minimal morality of procedural rationality? I am especially interested in whether there are hidden premises in my second stage that assume precisely what needs to be proved. I am not requesting reading lists, citations, or bibliographic guidance; I am working directly from Hart’s principal work on legal positivism and Fuller’s principal statement of the inner morality of law, along with the standard seminar literature. Suggestions for sharpening the logical structure, identifying distortions in my translation of either author, or assessing whether the automated-interpretation case is genuinely distinct from ordinary judicial discretion in the penumbra would be particularly valuable.
