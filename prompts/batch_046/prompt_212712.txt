I have been trying to formulate a precise position on museum restitution, and I want to stress-test how exposed it really is. The view—call it the custodial-continuity thesis—holds that a present community’s claim to the physical return of an artifact is morally compelling only when there exists an unbroken, institutionally recognized chain of custodial practice linking that community to the original creators, and that the strength of the claim scales with the integrity of that chain rather than with the severity of the original dispossession. Applied to familiar cases, this framing seems to complicate standard narratives: the Benin Bronzes, for instance, involve not merely a broken chain but a structural replacement of the Oba’s court institutions by colonial and post-colonial governance, which might weaken rather than strengthen the continuity claim; while arguments for the Parthenon Marbles might gain or lose force not from modern Greek state sovereignty per se, but from whether one can demonstrate unbroken ritual or civic custodial ties to the Acropolis site itself, independent of state continuity.

What I need is a careful map of what genuinely counts against this thesis—not just the obvious corrective-justice appeal to historical theft, but which specific intuitions about collective persistence over time, which analogies from property doctrine (adverse possession, escheat, or trust failure), which empirical considerations about cultural transmission, and which formal considerations about group identity it stands or falls with. Which of these defeaters would a committed custodial-continuity theorist concede as genuinely damaging, and which could they absorb by refining what counts as institutional recognition? Please rank the vulnerabilities from fatal to merely awkward, and say whether insulating the thesis against them risks collapsing it into a conventional cultural-heritage argument, or whether that insulation comes at an acceptable conceptual cost—for example, accepting counterintuitive results about diaspora communities or about artifacts that have been held in universal museums for generations. I am especially interested in whether there is any result in the philosophy of collective identity or institutional persistence that would decisively undermine the idea that custodial chains can be reconstructed after structural rupture, and whether such a result would be one that defenders of the thesis should treat as a refutation or merely as a boundary condition.
