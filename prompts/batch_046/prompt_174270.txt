As a journalist covering the history of science, I have been tracking a recurring maneuver in recent biographical scholarship that sits awkwardly with standard discussions of presentism. The historian explicitly declines to judge a past figure—say, a seventeenth-century natural philosopher—by contemporary moral standards, thereby claiming to have escaped presentism. Yet the narrative is organized around a modern analytical framework, such as systemic extraction or epistemic asymmetry, that would have been conceptually unavailable to the subject; the figure is then praised or faulted for anticipating or missing this framework. Moral presentism is avoided, but conceptual presentism is smuggled in under the guise of neutral analysis. Does this specific double-move have an established name in historiography, and if not, how should it be carved off from ordinary presentism, from Whig history, and from the unavoidable use of retrospective analytical vocabulary? I am less interested in a bare label than in the boundary conditions: what exactly makes this a failure rather than a necessary feature of historical explanation?
