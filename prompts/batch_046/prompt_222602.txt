Here is an argument I have been using in a seminar on reliabilism and now find suspicious because the conclusion makes scientific knowledge look too cheap. (1) If an instrument is reliable, then checking its internal consistency across repeated measurements can yield knowledge that it is functioning properly, without presupposing its reliability in advance. (2) If the instrument is unreliable, the consistency check will reveal anomalies, so the researchers can come to know its failure on the same internal basis. (3) So a research group can bootstrap justified beliefs about its own instruments' reliability using only data those instruments produce. (4) By parallel reasoning, the group can bootstrap knowledge of its theoretical framework's adequacy: if the framework generates predictions that match the instrument readings, and mismatches are explained away by auxiliary adjustments rather than core revision, then internal coherence licenses belief in the framework. (5) Therefore a fully self-contained laboratory—one that never compares results with other institutions, never relies on historical continuity with prior science, and never appeals to external authority—can generate complete scientific knowledge. (6) But that is false: isolation and circularity are precisely what make pseudoscience possible. Where does the argument slip? Is the error in (1), in treating instrument reliability and theoretical adequacy as analogous in (4), or in assuming that avoiding external checks is compatible with knowledge? If you reject (1), say whether any self-calibrating process can avoid presupposing some prior reliability, and whether that revives the bootstrapping problem for easy knowledge.
