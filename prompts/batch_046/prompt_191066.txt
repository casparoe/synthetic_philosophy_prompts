I am a final-year undergraduate taking a course on comparative political morality and the sociology of achievement. Please act as an expert in political philosophy and normative sociology, with particular familiarity with meritocratic ideology, Scandinavian social norms, and the sociology of status. Before you begin writing, please think carefully and start with brief brainstorming: identify the central conceptual tensions, the empirical claims that might be contested, and the normative premises each side relies on. Then write a polished, formal essay in British English.

The controversy is this: liberal meritocracies tend to celebrate exceptional success through public recognition, media amplification, and the cultural narrative of the American Dream, whereas Scandinavian societies have historically been shaped by Jantelagen — a normative framework that discourages individuals from presenting themselves as superior to others — and many competitive societies exhibit a tall poppy syndrome that cuts down highly visible achievers. Some argue that open celebration is essential for desert, motivation, and democratic freedom; others argue that suppressing visible exceptionalism is necessary to protect equality of status, prevent positional arms races, and maintain social trust.

Your essay should weigh these opposing positions, but it must also go further: analyse whether the conflict is genuinely between freedom and equality, or whether it reflects a deeper tension between two conceptions of merit itself — what we might call performative meritocracy, where success functions as a public spectacle that generates status externalities and symbolic accumulation, and substantive meritocracy, where success is evaluated primarily by contribution to collective welfare without such accumulation. You should reach and justify a clear conclusion about whether a just society ought to institutionalise mechanisms that limit the visibility of success, or whether such mechanisms inevitably violate legitimate claims of desert and expressive freedom. Do not end on a non-committal note; defend a specific verdict, even if qualified by conditions.
