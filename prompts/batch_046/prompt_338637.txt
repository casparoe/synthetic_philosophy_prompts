Please write a dialogue between two equally competent philosophers — call them Dr. Aris and Dr. Bex — debating whether cross-cultural variation in folk morality, particularly the documented differences between WEIRD and non-WEIRD populations, undermines the case for moral objectivity. The setting is an advanced graduate seminar in moral philosophy and psychology; the tone should be rigorous but conversational, with genuine back-and-forth rather than parallel monologues.

Both interlocutors should be familiar with the domain-specificity thesis in moral psychology — the view that moral cognition comprises distinct, ecologically calibrated mechanisms (care, fairness, loyalty, authority, purity, and so on) rather than a single rational faculty — and should treat it as a live constraint on normative theory, not as a settled empirical footnote. Dr. Aris should defend a form of domain-sensitive objectivism: she should argue that cross-cultural differences represent different weightings of genuinely universal moral values, and that WEIRD philosophers' emphasis on harm and fairness is not an outlier bias but a more reflective calibration of those universal domains. Dr. Bex should defend a constructivist alternative: he should argue that the domain-specific structure itself shows moral cognition to be an adaptive toolkit shaped by local social ecologies, and that the WEIRD/non-WEIRD divide reveals Western normative ethics to be a parochial extension of statistically atypical intuitions.

The dialogue must address three specific conceptual cruxes in turn. First, whether domain-specificity undermines the universalist's appeal to shared human reason, or whether universal values can be reconstructed at the level of underlying mechanisms despite surface variation in weighting. Second, whether the statistical atypicality of WEIRD moral judgments — especially the relative suppression of loyalty, authority, and purity concerns — gives non-WEIRD moral ecologies any epistemic privilege in normative ethics, or whether philosophical reflection can correct for demographic skew without collapsing into relativism. Third, whether the method of reflective equilibrium survives the discovery that the intuitions of trained ethicists are outliers within their own cultures; if not, what normative method should replace it?

Neither character should concede defeat. The exchange should conclude with the central disagreement sharpened: specifically, they should disagree about whether empirical moral psychology provides boundary conditions for normative ethics or instead dissolves the distinction between descriptive and normative inquiry. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations; do not invoke obscure theoretical frameworks that depend on knowledge of highly specific recent literature. Instead, ground the exchange in concepts that are philosophically substantive but generally accessible to someone with training in ethics and psychology.
