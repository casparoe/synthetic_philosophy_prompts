I have come to hold the view—associated with Chase and Sanborn—that positing qualia as intrinsic, non-physical, directly accessed properties of experience is conceptually incoherent rather than merely mysterious. The problem, as I understand it, is a triadic tension: qualia are said to be (i) non-functional and non-relational, (ii) immediately known to the subject, and (iii) available for discrimination and report. Any position that maintains all three appears unstable, since direct knowledge and discrimination seem to require some mediating structure, yet introducing that structure collapses the intrinsic, non-relational character of the property being accessed. I find especially compelling a sharper formulation that targets the notion of phenomenal transparency: the idea that we have unmediated acquaintance with the intrinsic nature of experience conflicts with the structural fact that introspective access is implemented by cognitive mechanisms—attention, working memory, conceptual framing—that are themselves relational and functional. On this view, the incoherence is not merely epistemic (outsiders cannot detect qualia) but internal to the concept: one cannot consistently claim both that a property is causally and relationally inert and that it is the direct object of a mediated cognitive act.

What would count against this position? I want an honest map of its exposure—what it risks, where it is vulnerable, and whether it has been insulated into unfalsifiability. Which arguments, thought experiments, empirical findings, or conceptual distinctions should genuinely move a defender of this incoherence thesis, and which are only apparently damaging? Please distinguish decisive defeaters from merely awkward ones, and rank them accordingly. In particular:

- Are there ways to preserve phenomenal transparency without making qualia functional, or does any such attempt necessarily smuggle in relational structure?
- How should the view treat cases of apparent first-person authority—such as the conviction that one's own experience is irreducibly present—without reducing that authority to a functional self-model?
- Could there be a minimal pair of cognitive architectures, isomorphic in all functional and behavioral respects, one of which contains an irreducible phenomenal layer and one of which does not, such that the subject can genuinely discriminate between them from the inside? If so, does that rescue qualia, or does it merely show that the layer is doing causal work?
- Has the incoherence argument been qualified into unfalsifiability—for example, by treating every phenomenal report as a disguised functional or dispositional report—such that no observation could in principle count against it? If it has, is that insulation a virtue of conceptual rigor or a vice that empties the claim?
- Which of these challenges would a committed proponent of the Chase-Sanborn line concede as real damage, and which would they dismiss as theory-laden intuition pumps or category errors?
- Among the live debates—whether about predictive processing, global workspace dynamics, or the phenomenal concept strategy—which results, if accepted, would most seriously threaten the claim that qualia are incoherent rather than merely unexplained?

I am personally most persuaded by arguments that preserve the reality of experience while denying its mysteriousness, so a defeater that shows how direct acquaintance could be genuinely non-functional yet cognitively accessible—without collapsing into a relational property—would be especially decisive for me.
