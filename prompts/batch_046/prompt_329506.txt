I am preparing material for an advanced seminar on rational agency and formal game theory, and in discussion with a colleague I have sketched an argument that appears to extend the framework of program equilibrium far beyond its natural domain, yielding what I take to be an implausible consequence for human strategic reasoning. The reasoning proceeds through the following steps.

(1) In program equilibrium, two rational agents that can inspect one another's source code will cooperate in a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma, because each can deterministically verify that defection by either party will be met with reciprocal defection, leaving mutual cooperation as the only stable rational outcome. (2) The operative condition is not substrate-specific implementation but mutual epistemic access to the decision procedure itself: if agent A can predict agent B's choice with logical certainty, and vice versa, the cooperative equilibrium obtains regardless of whether the agents are silicon-based or biological. (3) Human decision-makers can achieve precisely this kind of mutual access—not by reading neural source code, but through shared rational transparency, common knowledge of game-theoretic reasoning, and direct psychological understanding—so that, for the purposes of strategic prediction, human mutual comprehension is functionally equivalent to source-code inspection. (4) Therefore, whenever two rational humans possess such mutual understanding, the conditions for program equilibrium are satisfied, and defection is rendered self-defeating. (5) Given that rational agents in modern societies frequently do share such understanding, even in ostensibly anonymous one-shot interactions, rationality should demand universal cooperation in every human Prisoner's Dilemma. (6) But this obliterates the standard philosophical distinction between one-shot and iterated games, and entails that rational self-interest can never justify defection in a single encounter—a conclusion that strikes me as plainly false. (7) Consequently, either program equilibrium is irrelevant to human rationality, or our ordinary account of one-shot defection is deeply mistaken.

I feel that something slips between (2) and (4), perhaps an equivocation between logical determinacy and fallible psychological prediction, or a modal slide from the possibility of access to its actual prevalence. Which premise ought one to reject, and at what philosophical cost? Must we deny (3) and thereby confine program equilibrium to artificial agents, rendering it an elegant but empty technical curiosity? Or is there a more subtle error in the inference from mutual predictability to cooperative obligation? I should welcome a precise diagnosis that preserves the technical interest of the framework without collapsing human strategic reasoning into it, and I ask that the reply be composed in British English.
