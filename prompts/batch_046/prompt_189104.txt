I'm putting together my philosophical profile for a small reading group on minds and symbols, and I'd like someone to have a proper go at it before I present. Here's where I stand, more or less.

First, I think Hofstadter's 'I' is basically right: it's a strange loop, a symbol inside the system that points back at the system itself. I don't think there's anything mystical going on; it's just a stable pattern that gets its grip because it refers to its own source. Second, I don't think every feedback loop counts. For a loop to be an 'I', I think it needs a kind of symbolic mirror — not just self-reference, but a recursive mapping where one representational layer tracks errors in another, and that meta-layer is itself part of what's being represented. That's why a thermostat doesn't have an 'I': its feedback is causal but not representational in that layered, recursive way.

Third, I'm a straight physicalist. The loop is just neurons firing, or silicon gates flipping — nothing extra. I think that once you understand the mechanics of the symbolic mirror, the so-called hard problem is mostly a scale illusion. The what-it's-like aspect is just the loop describing its own state from the inside, not a seperate phenomenon that needs explaining. Fourth, substrate independence follows pretty naturally: if you rebuilt the exact same symbolic-mirror architecture in silicon, preserving all the recursive mappings, you'd have a new 'I' with its own first-person perspective. Fifth, but I don't think that copy is me. If you scan my brain and run it on a server, that's a branching, not a continuation; my identity is tied to this particular causal chain, not just the abstract pattern. Sixth, I think phenomenal concepts are special — they explain why the loop feels mysterious to itself — but they don't point to any non-physical property; they're just a particular kind of self-referential concept with no external correlate.
