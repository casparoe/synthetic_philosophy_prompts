Take the 2016 Brexit referedum and the three years of parliamentary gridlock that followed, and analyze it through Machiavelli's idea that fortune is like a river you can't stop but can build dikes against, and that virtù means adapting fast when the waters break through. I want the analysis to run both ways. First, apply the lens: was Cameron's call for the vote a failure of virtù (he didn't adapt to the risk he created) or was he just crushed by fortuna (the populist wave)? Once the vote happened, did May and then Johnson show virtù in how they handled the chaos, or were they just riding different currents? Second, let the case test the theory: Machiavelli writes about one prince facing fortune, but here you've got a whole institution — Parliment — trying to respond. Does spreading the decision across so many actors dilute virtù to the point where nobody can actually exercise it? And there's this other twist I'm not sure has a name: the Leave campaign framed Brexit as "taking back control," which looks like building a dike against global economic fortune. So was that collective virtù, or just another form of luck wearing a patriotic mask? Tell me whether Brexit confirms Machiavelli's view that boldness is the only answer to fortune, or whether it reveals that in modern democracies fortune is too distributed for any single actor to master, and that the theory needs an update it never anticipated. Keep it grounded in the actual politics — who had real options, what was actually at stake, and where Machiavelli is silent.
