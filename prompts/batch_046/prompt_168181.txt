At a recent colloquium on historical sociology, two scholars—Dr. Aris Thorne and Dr. Lena Vasquez—debated whether "collapse" is a useful concept for describing the transformations seen in the Late Bronze Age, the Classic Maya lowlands, and pre-contact Rapa Nui. I was sitting in and could not tell who was carrying the argument. Their exchange went like this:

(1) Thorne said that "collapse" is largely a modern, state-centric projection. In the Bronze Age, palace economies dissolved but village-level agriculture and local exchange persisted; literacy disappeared mainly in administrative contexts, not in social life generally. The Maya "collapse" was essentially a demographic shift away from unsustainable lowland urbanism toward the northern peninsula and highlands, where Maya culture continued with different political forms. And on Easter Island, recent work suggests deforestation was driven partly by introduced rats and climatic variability, after which the society reorganized into smaller-scale, sustainable social forms rather than disappearing. So in all three cases, he argued, "collapse" is a misleading moral narrative that treats decentralization as failure.

(2) Vasquez replied that Thorne is defining collapse away. If "collapse" just means "everyone died," then indeed no society ever collapses, but that renders the term useless. She argued that what matters is the rapid loss of interregional integration, specialized craft production, monumental architecture, and shared symbolic systems. The Bronze Age saw the breakdown of the palace economies and writing systems that had linked the eastern Mediterranean; the Classic Maya saw the abandonment of divine kingship, the cessation of monument carving, and the fragmentation of political networks in the southern lowlands. These are not neutral "reconfigurations"—they represent a severe contraction in organizational complexity, regardless of whether villagers kept farming.

(3) Thorne countered that Vasquez smuggles in a normative assumption: that organizational complexity is inherently valuable and that its loss is a tragedy. He suggested that the post-classic Maya and post-moai Rapa Nui societies may have been more egalitarian and ecologically sustainable. Calling their transformations "collapse" imposes a teleology of progress that privileges centralized, extractive states. The real question, he said, is not whether complexity dropped, but whether the new social forms were adaptive responses to changed conditions—responses that a collapse narrative pathologizes.

(4) Vasquez said this romanticizes deprivation. She pointed out that archaeological evidence in the southern Maya lowlands and on Rapa Nui shows increased skeletal markers of nutritional stress and violence after the respective transitions. Even if the old palace or moai-building systems were hierarchical, they also supported long-distance exchange, specialized knowledge, and large-scale cooperation that disappeared. The fact that some people survived in smaller groups does not negate that they lost capabilities—writing, trade networks, engineering knowledge—that took centuries to rebuild, if they were rebuilt at all. She concluded that "mere change" obscures real downward mobility.

(5) Thorne responded that Vasquez is conflating descriptive and evaluative claims. He agreed that nutritional stress and violence increased in specific regions and periods, but insisted that labeling the entire transformation a "collapse" still privileges a particular scale of analysis—the imperial or state scale—while ignoring local resilience. He proposed that we should talk about "phase transitions in network density" rather than collapse, recognizing that societies can shift between centralized and decentralized modes without either being a fall from grace.

(6) Vasquez replied that "phase transition in network density" is just jargon that avoids the phenomenological reality: for the people living through it, the end of familiar institutions, the cessation of public monument construction, and the loss of interregional connections were experienced as catastrophic ruptures, not neutral physics. She argued that if our conceptual vocabulary refuses to distinguish between gradual evolution and rapid, destructive simplification, then we lose the ability to study vulnerability, resilience, and recovery in any meaningful way.

Then say who is more right overall, what important point both are missing—particularly regarding whether "collapse" requires a normative baseline or can be defined purely structurally—and whether introducing a scale-sensitive typology (rather than a binary of collapse versus change) resolves their dispute or merely relocates it.
