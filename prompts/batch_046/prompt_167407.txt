I am taking a seminar on medieval philosophy of religion and have been working through Maimonides' treatment of animal sacrifice in the Guide for the Perplexed. Rather than accepting the standard historical-concession reading—where sacrifice is tolerated only as a remnant of pagan habits acquired in Egypt—I want to defend a more structural interpretation: the sacrificial system functions like a therapeutic taper. It retains the form of corporeal worship but progressively redirects the practitioner's attachment away from material gratification and toward non-corporeal intellectual devotion, with ritual violence serving not as an intrinsic good but as a pedagogically necessary transitional scaffold. What would genuinely count against this interpretation? Please rank the defeaters, from decisive textual or philosophical contradictions within Maimonides' own metaphysics and ethics to merely awkward conceptual objections—especially the worry that the taper analogy imports an anachronistic therapeutic framework he could not have held. I would also like to know whether defenders of the conventional concession account could accommodate the taper structure without abandoning their core claim, and whether the analogy survives once those disanalogies are made explicit.
