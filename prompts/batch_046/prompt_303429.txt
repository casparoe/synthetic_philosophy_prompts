The debate between virtue ethicists and situationist critics in moral psychology has been at an impasse for roughly three decades: every standard move—distinguishing competence from performance, appealing to local rather than global traits, or charging situationists with a category mistake—is familiar, and neither camp seems to concede ground. I am trying to diagnose whether this is a genuine substantive disagreement about the structure of moral agency, a verbal dispute over what 'character' must mean, or two projects—one normative and aspirational, one explanatory and predictive—running under a single heading.

Concretely, virtue ethicists in an Aristotelian register hold that moral development aims at stable, cross-situational dispositions—virtues and vices—that integrate perception, affect, and action, and that these dispositions both explain behavior and serve as standards of evaluation. Situationist critics, drawing on classic social-psychological findings about obedience, bystander intervention, and personality variability, argue that observed behavior tracks situational features far more reliably than it tracks putative global traits, and that the empirical evidence for high cross-situational consistency in moral domains is weak. Virtue-theoretic replies typically invoke one or more of the following: virtues are rare and require appropriate conditions for expression; traits can be fragmented or local rather than global without losing normative force; or the psychological literature measures the wrong kind of consistency, such as behavioral frequency rather than rational coherence.

My working hypothesis is that the stalemate is not primarily about empirical data—both sides largely accept the same experimental results—but about the normative-explanatory double duty that the concept of virtue is expected to perform. Virtue ethics asks trait-language to do aspirational and integrative work: it specifies what a flourishing human life looks like and presupposes that agents can become the kind of people for whom right action flows naturally. Situationism asks the same language to do predictive and causal work: it demands that trait-ascriptions track robust behavioral regularities across contexts. If this is right, the disagreement may be partly verbal—'character' means one thing in a normative anthropology and another in a behavioral science—but partly substantive, because each side insists that its use should govern moral education and institutional design.

What I want is a diagnosis supported by what each side actually says, not a verdict about who is right. Specifically: Is there a hidden formal or empirical question doing the work—perhaps about the conditions under which a disposition counts as the same across contexts? Are the sides answering different questions—one about how we should cultivate agents, the other about how we should predict behavior—while treating them as competing answers to a single question? Or is the impasse a sign that the underlying concept is ill-posed, requiring not reconciliation but replacement?

Finally, what would count as progress on your diagnosis? Would it be a conceptual distinction—say, between integrative dispositions and context-indexed competencies—that allows both sides to keep what they value? An empirical result that distinguishes global consistency from local coherence in a way both accept? A formal model of trait-behavior relations that fixes the conditions of cross-situational identity? Or is the correct conclusion that no progress should be expected, because the questions belong to incommensurable frameworks? I am especially interested in whether injecting a pluralist ontology of dispositions—distinguishing higher-order habits of situation-selection from first-order behavioral regularities—resolves anything or merely relocates the dispute.
