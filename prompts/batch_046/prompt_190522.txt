Consider the institutional architecture shared by adversarial legal arbitration and civil adjudication: parties present mutually exclusive narratives of fact and liability; the tribunal must issue a single binding determination—claim upheld or dismissed, liable or not liable; dissenting opinions may be published but carry no enforceable institutional weight; and a judgment that merely lists competing partial truths without selecting a winner is treated as a procedural failure rather than a valid resolution. At the same time, interdisciplinary assessment bodies—such as those producing integrated risk or climate reports—aggregate heterogeneous models into one consensus range for policymakers, relegating methodological disagreement and conditional qualifications to technical annexes that do not shape the binding recommendation. What unified or divergent theories of truth, knowledge, and legitimate conflict resolution do these practices presuppose?

Reconstruct the implicit epistemology: do they commit to the view that every dispute admits of one substantially correct account, that partial perspectives are only instrumental steps toward a singular verdict, or that practical tractability demands a false unity? Distinguish what these institutions strictly require from what they merely tolerate, and assess whether the presupposed view is coherent on its own terms and whether it can accommodate genuine, irreducible disagreement.

Then contrast this framework with Jain anekāntavāda, the doctrine that reality is complex and multi-aspected, that no unconditional statement exhausts the truth, and that valid knowledge claims must be qualified—expressed through syādvāda and nayavāda with the conditional marker syāt, indicating that a claim holds only in some respect and from a particular standpoint. Is the adversarial-singular model compatible with many-sidedness, or does it implicitly rule out the Jain position that competing narratives can all be partially true and that a legitimate resolution might take the form of a qualified, multi-perspective synthesis rather than a winner-take-all judgment?

Finally, specify what would have to change in courtroom or arbitration procedure—regarding the authority and form of dissent, the criteria for a successful award, the standing of conditional or perspectival claims, and the role of synthesis versus selection—if the institution were rebuilt on anekāntavādin commitments rather than on the implicit epistemology you reconstructed. Be precise about where human judgment remains unavoidable and where the redesign eliminates false precision without collapsing into indecision.
