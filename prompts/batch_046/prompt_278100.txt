hey, i'm putting together a sorting quiz for my pragmatics seminar and i'm a bit stuck on the taxonomy part. i want students to figure out where they actually stand on directness vs indirectness, using dutch bluntness and japanese indirectness as the two poles, plus whether white lies are ever okay. but i don't want the usual "cultural relativist vs universalist" tree everyone can just lift from an encyclopedia.

build me a decision tree (or flowchart, whatever works) with exactly six leaves, each a named position. start from this concrete case: a colleague asks "does this presentation look okay?" right before going on stage, and it's actually pretty rough. from there branch on specific commitments. the branch questions should be about actual beliefs, not about what the view is called — phrase them so a student can answer yes/no without knowing which leaf it points to.

here's the framing i want baked in: treat "politeness" not as one thing but split it into "surface-level social lubrication" vs "deep respect for the listener's autonomy." meanwhile treat dutch directness not as rudeness but as "cognitive transparency" — giving the other person unfiltered info so they can decide. the tree should ask whether you think these two goods (transparency and deep respect) can both be satisfied, or whether they trade off.

the six leaves must include:
1. a view saying white lies are always wrong because they violate cognitive transparency, even if they preserve surface lubrication.
2. a view saying indirectness is the only real respect, and directness is just laziness or masked aggression.
3. a view saying dutch directness and japanese indirectness are both okay but in different "registers" of interaction.
4. a view saying white lies are fine if they protect deep autonomy by preventing unnecessary emotional harm.
5. one combo that pairs directness with permissibility of white lies — flag this one as possibly incoherent or unstable.
6. fill in the logical gap that's left (maybe a view that cares about surface lubrication but rejects deep respect? or whatever actually belongs).

also flag any pair of leaves your tree can't really seperate with the questions you've used. and note any empty branch — like if someone answers yes to everything or no to everything — and say whether that combo is incoherent, ugly, or just unexplored. keep it casual, audience is grad students, and don't ask for bibliographies or reading lists.
