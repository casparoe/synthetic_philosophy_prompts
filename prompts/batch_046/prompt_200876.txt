I cover philosophy and the history of ideas for a science and culture publication, and I am currently preparing a feature on how mid-twentieth-century moral theology is taught in contemporary undergraduate ethics seminars. During my reporting, a university instructor shared with me an anonymized exam exchange from an advanced course on action theory and the ethics of war, and I need a rigorous, point-by-point assessment of the student response for my piece. The exam asked the following: "In her protest against Oxford University's decision to award Harry Truman an honorary doctorate in 1956, G.E.M. Anscombe argued that Truman was a murderer. Reconstruct her argument, paying particular attention to her distinction between direct and indirect killing and her use of the principle of double effect, and then explain how she handles the objection that her view would also condemn killing enemy soldiers who are asleep."

The student wrote: "Anscombe published her pamphlet Mr. Truman's Degree to oppose Oxford honoring Truman in 1956. She holds that Truman is a murderer because he deliberately caused the deaths of thousands of innocent civilians in Hiroshima and Nagasaki. She distinguishes direct killing, in which an innocent person's death is intended either as an end or as a means, from indirect killing, in which such death is a foreseen but unintended side effect of a legitimate act. Drawing on Aquinas's principle of double effect, she allows that an action producing bad effects can be permissible if the harm is not intended and is proportionate to the good achieved. In the case of the atomic bombs, however, the civilian casualties failed the proportionality condition: the destruction was so vast relative to the strategic goal of forcing Japan's unconditional surrender that it could not count as a mere side effect. She also considers the objection that her absolutism implies it is murder to kill sleeping soldiers; she accepts this implication and argues that it reveals her position is consistently applied, since even combatants who pose no immediate threat fall under the prohibition on killing the innocent. Consequently, she concludes that Truman's authorization was direct murder, and any attempt to excuse it through double effect collapses once the scale of harm is properly weighed."

Please grade this answer as though you were the course instructor or exam grader. Assign a specific point score or letter grade, and provide precise comments that identify exactly what the student gets right, where a subtle conflation or misattributed step slips in, and whether the reasoning actually follows. Lead your response with the bottom line—your assigned grade and a one-sentence overall verdict—followed immediately by the reasoning and specific justifications. Do not include any preamble, and do not restate the exam question.
