You serve on the clinical ethics committee of a large teaching hospital whose charter recognizes both legally valid advance directives and culturally specific family-deliberation practices, though it never specifies how the two should interact when they conflict. A 42-year-old patient, referred to in records only as M., suffered catastrophic hypoxic brain injury eight months ago and now remains in a minimally conscious state with no meaningful prospect of recovery. Two independent neurologists have certified that continued mechanical ventilation is non-beneficial. Before her injury, M. executed an explicit advance directive refusing prolonged ventilation in irreversible conditions, and the attending team proposes withdrawal within seventy-two hours, framing the case as a straightforward exercise in respecting prior autonomous choice.

M.’s family and designated community representatives reject that framing entirely. They do not contest the medical prognosis, but they invoke an Ubuntu-informed relational conception of personhood: they hold that M.’s moral standing is constituted through her embeddedness in an ongoing network of relationships and communal recognition rather than through an isolated, pre-injury expression of individual preference. From this view, unilateral withdrawal by clinical staff does not honor M.’s autonomy but severs the relational fabric that sustains her personhood; they request a structured six-month extension involving regular family-led presence, community-mediated review sessions every four weeks, and a prohibition on unilateral termination during that period. They explicitly deny that they are acting as conventional proxies for an incompetent patient; instead, they claim participatory authority as co-constituents of the person whose fate is being decided.

Your committee’s policy permits “reasonable cultural accommodation” in end-of-life decision-making but defines it only in procedural terms, leaving substantive conflicts unresolved. The committee is split: some members treat the advance directive as dispositive regardless of philosophical anthropology, while others worry that enforcing it imposes a liberal, individualist ontology of the person that the family’s position legitimately contests.

Draft the committee’s formal decision memo—structured for circulation before Thursday’s vote and not exceeding the length appropriate for institutional action. Recommend one concrete course of action with a precise time frame: for example, approve withdrawal with conditions, deny withdrawal and institute the requested mediation protocol, or approve withdrawal only after a defined period of structured dialogue. Your reasoning must engage the family’s relational-personhood argument seriously on its own conceptual terms rather than reducing it to proxy consent or emotional resistance, and it must include an honest treatment of the strongest consideration on the opposing side—specifically, whether accommodating a relational ontology undermines the legal and moral authority of advance directives or improperly subjects an individual’s previously expressed wishes to communal veto. Do not include requests for external bibliographies, expert lists, or citations; the memo should stand on internal reasoning, the institutional values at stake, and the concrete realities of the case. Write in a formal, polished register appropriate to an institutional body.
