I've been trying to pin down my view on moral worth for a seminar paper and I keep going in circles. I was talking with my roommate about whether doing teh right thing for teh wrong reason is ever praisworthy, and I think I've landed somewhere near Kant but with some tweaks that probably don't fit together. Anyway here's roughly where I'm at:

(1) I think moral worth only attaches when you act from the right reason, and specifically when you recogntion the moral fact as giving you a binding reason — not just symapthy or habit or social pressure. (2) I also think moral luck shouldn't affect worth at all: if two people both help a stranger out of pure recognition of duty, but one had to overcome trauma and the other was having an easy day, they're equally worthy. (3) But I also want to say that counterfactual robustness matters — if you'd have walked past the stranger whenever it was slightly inconvenient, then even your actual good act lacks full worth, it's kind of shallow. (4) At the same time, I think people raised in super supportive moral communities can still be fully worthy, even if they've never actually faced serious temptation, as long as they genuinely grasp the reason. (5) And I don't buy that symapthy can be part of the right kind of reason; emotional response might align with duty but it can't substitute for rational recogntion.

So do these hang together? I'm especially worried about (3) and (4) — if counterfactual robustness is required for full worth, doesn't that mean people who never faced hard situations are automatically less worthy, which sounds like moral luck sneaking back in? And does (3) conflict with (2) in a deeper way? Also, is there a hidden tension between (1) and rejecting symapthy entirely, like maybe recognizing duty without any affective engagement is actually a kind of moral fetishism? I want an honest audit — rank the tensions by severity, say which ones are just apparent, and tell me the least painful revision if I have to drop something.
