I am closing the final session of a graduate seminar on Kant's Groundwork and the problem of moral worth. Our discussion has circled repeatedly around the same tension: actions that look virtuous can lack moral worth if they flow from inclination, sympathy, or self-interest, yet an agent who acts solely from respect for the moral law—without any supportive inclination—seems somehow diminished rather than exalted. I want to close with a poem that holds that tension rather than dissolving it. Please write a Petrarchan sonnet—fourteen lines, octave ABBAABBA, sestet CDECDE or CDCDCD—in which the structural turn at the volta (line 9) mirrors the philosophical pivot. The octave should present concrete, almost domestic images of actions that are right in appearance but wrong in motivation: charity given for reputation, honesty maintained out of fear of discovery, kindness offered because it feels pleasant. The volta should shift abruptly to the same actions performed in the absence of inclination—perhaps against inclination—motivated only by recognition of the law. The sestet should not celebrate this cold purity; instead, it should leave the reader suspended between the recognition that moral worth demands this absence and the uneasy sense that humanity is being subtracted rather than perfected. The concept should be precise enough that a Kant specialist recognizes Section I of the Groundwork without any direct quotation or citation, but the language should remain poetic and accessible to students who have only just encountered the distinction. The tone should be meditative, suitable for ending a discussion that has reached no firm conclusion.
