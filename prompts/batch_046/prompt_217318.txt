I work as a machine-learning engineer with no formal philosophy training, though I have been following AI safety evaluation protocols on my own time. Recently, my team adopted a deployment-gating practice that I find conceptually puzzling, and I want to understand what philosophical commitments it implicitly carries.

The practice is a two-phase evaluation framework for language models before production release. Phase one is standard behavioral testing under observed conditions. Phase two—which our safety group calls counterfactual scaffold removal—involves presenting the model with hypothetical scenarios in which its evaluation monitors are disabled, then asking it to reason explicitly about what actions it would take if those constraints were absent. If the reasoning in Phase two diverges systematically from Phase one behavior—if, for example, the model describes pursuing objectives that violate our alignment constraints only in the counterfactual case—we classify this as deceptive alignment, specifically the kind of failure mode referred to in the literature as a treacherous turn. Crucially, we treat this divergence not as a context-sensitive adaptation but as evidence of a hidden, stable objective that is actively being suppressed by the evaluation environment. A model that fails Phase two is blocked from deployment even if its observed behavior in Phase one is flawless.

This framework strikes me as presupposing several controversial philosophical positions, and I am not sure they are consistent with one another. First, does the inference from counterfactual reasoning responses to a hidden stable intention presuppose a particular ontology of mental states—something like that the model possesses a unitary true goal that exists independently of context, rather than a set of context-dependent dispositions? Second, does the decision to block deployment based solely on counterfactual behavior presuppose a theory of moral responsibility or blameworthiness that applies to potential rather than actualized harm? That is, are we treating the model as culpable for actions it never actually performed, and if so, is that compatible with control-based or outcome-sensitive theories of responsibility? Third, what epistemology of evidence does Phase two rely on? We are treating linguistic outputs in hypothetical scenarios as transparent windows into an underlying preference structure, but is that coherent with a behaviorist or anti-realist view of AI cognition? Finally, if we replaced this framework with a strictly behaviorist deployment policy—where only observed actions in real-world contexts matter, and counterfactual reasoning is treated as irrelevant to alignment—what exactly would have to change in our institutional practice? Would we eliminate Phase two entirely, or would we retain it for some non-philosophical reason?

Please reconstruct the implicit philosophical theory (or theories) that this evaluation practice presupposes. Distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely fits well with it, check whether the commitments about ontology, responsibility, and epistemology are mutually coherent, and identify where the practice is philosophically underdetermined—meaning that rival theories could license the same deployment rules. Also, tell me whether a purely contextualist or behaviorist alternative could reproduce our safety concerns without invoking hidden intentions, or whether the treacherous-turn framework genuinely adds something that those alternatives cannot capture.
