I am currently taking a graduate seminar on moral philosophy, and we have spent several weeks on supererogation—acts that are praiseworthy yet not obligatory. In our discussions I have kept returning to a pattern I do not see named in the standard literature, though it feels distinct from the usual examples of heroic rescue or charitable donation. The pattern runs like this: an individual in a professional or communal role repeatedly performs labor that clearly exceeds any formal or informal requirement—mentoring colleagues after hours, covering shifts without compensation, or maintaining shared resources without being asked. At first this is recognized as exceptional and voluntary. But because the behavior is consistent and reliable, others gradually restructure their expectations around it; the extra labor becomes a background condition of the group’s functioning. Eventually, if the agent withdraws, they face not mere disappointment but a kind of localized blame or resentment, as though they have broken an implicit contract—despite the fact that no agreement was ever made, no institutional rule changed, and the act remains, in the abstract, beyond duty. The expectation seems to attach specifically to that agent rather than generalizing to everyone in the role.

What interests me is whether this mechanism—where an agent’s own sustained supererogation generates a de facto, agent-specific obligation without institutionalization or explicit consent—has an established philosophical name. I am aware of nearby concepts: moral licensing, where prior good deeds excuse later lapses; role creep, where organizational expectations expand informally; and tacit promissory obligations arising from reliance. Yet none of these seem to capture the particular moral geometry here: the obligation is created not by external demand but by the agent’s own history of excellence, and it binds them specifically rather than applying to anyone who occupies the position. Does this configuration have a recognized term in discussions of supererogation, promissory ethics, or moral expectation? If so, how is it precisely distinguished from a tacit promise formed through reasonable reliance? If not, I would like a coined term with a rigorous definition, including its boundary conditions—specifically, whether the agent can dissolve the obligation unilaterally and whether the mechanism requires actual reliance by others or merely predictable repetition.
