I am preparing a single seminar session for a first-year philosophy graduate reading group that has just worked through Dennett’s essay on the intentional strategy and is now arguing over whether large language models can be said to hold beliefs in the intentional-stance sense. The students arrive divided between those who treat predictive success as literal proof of belief and those who think the absence of biological representation rules out attribution entirely; neither camp sees the stance as a methodological posture adopted for predictive leverage and suspendable when it fails. I want them to understand belief attribution as an interpretive strategy rather than an ontological discovery, using a specific LLM interaction pattern—not Dennett’s original chess-computer or thermostat cases—as the carrying example.

Plan the session’s explanatory arc: identify the conceptual distinction between predictive posture and metaphysical commitment that must be in place before the AI case is introduced; specify the one concrete interaction pattern (for example, a sustained role-play in which the model consistently asserts a demonstrably false claim that serves a conversational goal) that must carry the idea, stating exactly what predictive and failure conditions it must display and what it must lack—no conscious access, no transparent designed representational architecture, and no biological substrate; map the three misconceptions they will likely raise in sequence—the stochastic-parrot reduction, the slide from successful prediction to literal belief, and the dismissal of the stance as merely fictional—indicating at which point in the arc each should be absorbed by redirection rather than direct refutation; design the check for understanding that reveals whether they now treat belief attribution as a strategic choice rather than a factual mistake about machine minds; and explicitly say which technical details about transformer architectures or training dynamics should be left out at this philosophical level. Deliver this teaching plan as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
