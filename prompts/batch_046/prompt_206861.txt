I spent the evening arguing with a friend about whether Camus's The Stranger is best read through Strawson's reactive-attitudes framework. My friend holds that Meursault is constructed precisely to trigger the objective attitude: he withholds the emotional responses—resentment, gratitude, love in its morally loaded form—that make someone a proper object of participant reactive attitudes, so society treats him as a clinical case to be executed rather than as a moral interlocutor. I countered that Meursault is not emotionally blank; he feels irritation, physical attachment, and ultimately a kind of cosmic resentment in the prison scene, but these are directed at the wrong objects or expressed in the wrong register. So the real question is whether moral responsibility requires having reactive capacities in general, or having them shaped in ways that match a local moral culture. Read the novel through this tension: do the trial scenes show that Meursault lacks reactive attitudes altogether, or that he possesses them but refuses to perform them in the socially legible way? Focus especially on the beach shooting—where his action is triggered by physical discomfort rather than malice—and on the chaplain scene, where his outburst might be interpreted as a reactive attitude directed not at a person but at the framework of meaning itself. Does Strawson's distinction between reactive and objective attitudes illuminate the text, or does Camus use Meursault to argue that any such distinction already smuggles in a normative conception of shared emotional life that Meursault rejects? Be honest about where the philosophical lens distorts the novel, and say what the work adds to the debate that argument alone cannot.
