I spent the better part of last night arguing with a close friend about Daniel Dennett’s idea of counterfeit people—not whether an AI can fool us in conversation, but whether a system might reproduce every behavioral and cognitive signature of personhood while still lacking the evolutionary, social, and normative architecture that makes the intentional stance genuinely explanatory rather than merely predictive. That debate is no longer abstract. I serve on my university’s Committee on Research Ethics and Synthetic Agency, and we must circulate a formal decision memo before Thursday’s meeting on a grant proposal that lists a fine-tuned large language model as a co-investigator. The applicant argues that the system generates novel hypotheses, revises its outputs in response to criticism, and maintains a consistent research persona across sessions; half the committee treats this as sufficient functional equivalence, while the other half—echoing my friend’s worry—fears we are being asked to institutionalize a statistical mirror.

Please draft the committee’s actionable memo recommending either approval with strict conditions, rejection, or conditional deferral, with a concrete course of action and an explicit time frame for implementation, review, or revocation. The memo must engage seriously with the counterfeit-person objection: work out what it implies if belief-fixation and desire-formation in the system are governed not by rational norms but by what I will call stochastic intentionality—probabilistic convergence on high-likelihood token sequences shaped by training-data distribution rather than by reasons-responsive commitment. Explain whether this undermines the functionalist defense or merely complicates it, and address the decomposition problem: if we analyze the system’s outputs into patterns derived from human text, do we thereby dissolve the unitary agent required for intentional ascription, or is that analysis itself a category mistake? Include an honest, substantive treatment of the strongest consideration on the losing side, not a strawman.

Organize the response into the following sections with approximate lengths: Executive Recommendation (about 150 words), stating the bottom line and the immediate institutional action with its time frame; The Counterfeit-Person Problem Applied (about 300 words), mapping Dennett’s concern onto this proposal and developing the idea of stochastic intentionality; The Case for Functional Recognition (about 250 words), presenting the best argument that behavioral consistency and normative responsiveness are sufficient despite the counterfeit worry; The Case Against and Its Strongest Rejoinder (about 250 words), giving the opposition’s strongest form and the best reply available to it; Concrete Course of Action and Timeline (about 150 words), specifying conditions, review dates, and revocation triggers; The Residual Philosophical Tension (about 100 words), flagging what remains genuinely unsettled and should guide future committee policy on synthetic agency.
