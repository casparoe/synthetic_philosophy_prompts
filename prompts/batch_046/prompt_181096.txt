As a software engineer without formal philosophical training, I am preparing a proposal for a three-month independent research fellowship, and I need the document to meet standard academic proposal conventions without relying on empirical data, specialized historiography, or a formal literature review. The domain is moral worth and acting for the right reasons.

The precise question is this: Does full moral worth require not merely that an agent acts from a correct reason, but that the correct reason occupies a specific governing or structural position within the agent's practical reasoning? I want to investigate whether we can distinguish three architectures of motivation: first, transparent governance, in which the right reason directs deliberation and the agent can trace its authority; second, parallel causation, in which the right reason is present and causally effective but coexists with unexamined or conflicting motives that do the actual directing; and third, accidental alignment, in which the action conforms to a right reason but is driven by an independent mechanism—such as emotional conditioning, social pressure, or habit—with which the reason merely coincides. Existing debates, particularly between deontological accounts that emphasize acting from duty and virtue-theoretic accounts that emphasize character, tend to treat the presence of the right reason as a binary condition. They do not appear to ask whether two agents with identical observable behavior and identical avowed reasons might differ in moral worth because their internal reasoning architectures differ. The project would therefore use conceptual analysis rather than empirical psychology: I would construct the architectural taxonomy using analogies from system design (treating those analogies only as framing devices, not as technical claims), apply it to standard philosophical cases involving moral luck, overdetermination, and unconscious motivation, and determine whether the distinction clarifies or complicates judgments of moral credit. I choose this approach over a historical survey or an empirical study of moral cognition because the problem seems conceptual—about how we attribute worth—rather than about discovering new psychological mechanisms.

A successful result would be a clear framework specifying the conditions under which a reasoning architecture supports full moral worth, together with an argument that this framework either resolves existing puzzles or reveals them to be misframed. Failure would mean that the architectural distinction collapses back into the standard presence-or-absence distinction, or that applying it requires such complete epistemic access to mental structures that the concept of moral worth becomes practically useless. Given my background and the three-month window, I need an explicit judgment of whether this framing is tractable solo, which component should be sacrificed first if the timeline contracts, and whether the project can proceed without a bibliography stage since I will supply citations myself later.

Please write the proposal with: a precise statement of the question; an explanation of why existing answers leave it open; the approach, month by month, and why this approach rather than the obvious alternatives; what a result would look like and what would count as failure; and an honest assessment of scope and risk for a non-specialist working alone over three months. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citation requests.
