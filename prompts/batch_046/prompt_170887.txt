In a philosophy of mathematics discussion I am following, a participant keeps returning to an analogy: mathematics is like chess. They say that just as chess pieces (bishops, pawns) have no reality outside the game, mathematical objects (numbers, sets, functions) have no reality outside our axiomatic practice. The rules of chess are invented conventions, and so are the axioms of ZF, type theory, or intuitionistic logic. Proving a theorem is like making a legal move: the necessity is internal to the system, not a discovery about an independent world. They push the comparison further: just as chess has legitimate variants (Fischer random chess, shogi, chess960) that change rules without being “wrong,” mathematics has alternative foundations — NF, constructive mathematics, ZF with or without the Axiom of Choice, category-theoretic structuralism — and none is the “true” one. From this they conclude that platonism is a confusion: mathematicians are not explorers discovering a pre-existing continent of abstract objects, but players agreeing on rules, and the feeling of objectivity is merely intersubjective agreement, not correspondence to an abstract realm.
