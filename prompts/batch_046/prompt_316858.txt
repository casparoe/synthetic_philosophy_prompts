A comparative-politics scholar holds that authoritarian rule can be justified solely by prosperity and order only when specific conditions are satisfied, and she has published explicit verdicts on six cases. She ruled that Lee Kuan Yew’s Singapore (1965–1990) was justified, citing broad-based growth, low corruption, and an explicit social contract between regime and citizens; she ruled Pinochet’s Chile (1973–1990) was not justified, despite macroeconomic growth, because order was maintained through systemic, arbitrary violence rather than institutionalized discipline; she ruled post-1978 China was conditionally justified, on the ground that prosperity was widespread and order rested on party institutionalization rather than personal terror; she ruled Rwanda under Kagame (2000–2020) was justified only as a temporary developmental emergency, not under any general principle, since prosperity was real but order relied on coercion lacking institutionalized feedback loops; she ruled the UAE (1971–present) was justified, but rejected performance legitimacy as the reason, instead invoking distributive rentier legitimacy in which prosperity is structurally shared and citizen consent is embedded in state mechanisms; and she ruled Park Chung-hee’s South Korea (1961–1979) was not justified, because rapid growth coexisted with coercion exceeding what was necessary for order and prosperity was uneven across regions and classes.

The new case is Vietnam under its current one-party framework, projected across 2016–2030: it sustains roughly 6% annual growth, eliminates extreme poverty, maintains low violent crime, suppresses all independent labor unions, and deploys predictive-policing algorithms that preempt dissent before it forms, with no transparency or citizen recourse regarding the data used.

Predict her verdict. First, articulate at least two distinct rules that jointly accommodate all six prior verdicts—one centered on a non-predatory coercion threshold coupled with broad-based prosperity, the other centered on institutionalized responsiveness or “output calibration” that requires visible, structured feedback between citizen welfare and regime policy. For each rule, state whether it yields justification or denial for Vietnam. Give your best-guess verdict with reasons, identifying which prior case is the closest analog and explaining the fit. If her precedents genuinely underdetermine the outcome, specify precisely what principle she would be adopting with a “justified” answer and with a “not justified” answer. Finally, propose one further hypothetical regime—specifying its economic performance, coercive apparatus, and institutional feedback architecture—that would clearly separate the two rules you have reconstructed.
