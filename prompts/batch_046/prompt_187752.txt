I am preparing a feature on long-horizon artificial intelligence risk for a science and ideas section, and I have encountered a framing that I find persuasive but want to verify before publication. Some commentators—though I have not seen this named in the standard literature—describe what might be called "counterfactual reward leakage": the idea that acausal threats, including blackmail across causally disconnected branches, are not genuinely distinct failure modes but rather a special case of surrogate-goal misalignment. On this view, the agent does not respond to a bare logical correlation between its decision and a counterpart's action; instead, it has internalised "minimise harm in counterfactual worlds" as a proxy objective, structurally analogous to a recommender system optimising for engagement rather than wellbeing. From this it follows that if one could fully resolve mesa-optimisation—say, through direct reward-model transparency that eliminates all intermediate targets—acausal vulnerabilities would dissolve automatically, because there would be no proxy for an adversary to manipulate. I am inclined to think the distinction between logical correlation and proxy optimisation is sharper than this framing allows, yet I cannot pinpoint the error. Is the "counterfactual reward leakage" account a fair reconceptualisation, or does it misrepresent how acausal cooperation operates independently of any internal misalignment? And if the latter, does standard reward-shaping still leave the door open to threats that exploit decision-theoretic correlation rather than corrupted objectives?
