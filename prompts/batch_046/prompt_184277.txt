I have been trying to clarify a conceptual problem at the intersection of political morality and institutional design: whether corruption can be defined independently of the particular moral economy in which an exchange is embedded. The dominant Western framework treats corruption as a deviation from impersonal, rule-bound administration, so that clientelism and ritual gift-giving appear as hidden violations—vertical exchanges that improperly mix personal loyalty with public duty. Yet in many contexts these practices constitute visible, norm-governed reciprocity: horizontal solidarity exchange among peers operates differently from patron-client extraction, and the boundary between legitimate obligation and abuse depends not on the presence of a gift but on whether the exchange violates an implicit contract specific to an institutional role.

If we adopt this relational, role-sensitive definition—where corruption is the breach of reciprocity norms appropriate to a given position rather than the mere presence of personal exchange—then the universality of Western anti-corruption metrics becomes an open question rather than an assumption. Please describe the most important implications of this framework for the practical work of international audit and compliance bodies: which standard indicators, such as blanket disclosure thresholds for gifts or categorical prohibitions on kinship hiring, would systematically misclassify legitimate horizontal reciprocity as corruption; which structural features of vertical dependency genuinely warrant intervention even when they are locally normative; and where the framework forces the practitioner to acknowledge that the distinction between corruption and legitimate obligation is irreducibly normative rather than empirically detectable. I am particularly interested in whether this reframing resolves the apparent tension between cultural relativism and universal standards, or whether it merely relocates the conflict into the prior task of specifying which institutional roles carry which reciprocity obligations.
