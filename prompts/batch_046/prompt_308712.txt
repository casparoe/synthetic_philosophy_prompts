I have recently begun co-directing a small interdisciplinary research laboratory, and a practical disagreement with my co-director has led me to revisit the philosophy of joint action. We both genuinely intend to build the lab together, yet we operate with markedly different internal representations of that shared goal: she understands our joint enterprise primarily as an epistemic institution designed to maximize the reliability of our collective outputs, while I understand it mainly as a formative environment intended to reshape the intellectual character of its members. Despite this divergence, we coordinate actions smoothly at the operational level—assigning tasks, reviewing submissions, allocating resources—so it feels as though we are engaged in something more integrated than two parallel individual projects. Nevertheless, when strategic choices arise, I find myself wondering whether we are truly exercising a single joint agency or merely simulating one through behavioral overlap.

This practical concern has led me to a conceptual puzzle. From what I understand of accounts of collective intentionality—particularly the claim that joint action involves a we-intention irreducible to aggregated individual intentions—I have formed the impression that genuine collective intentionality requires both agents to access the same representational content with respect to the shared goal. If that is correct, then my colleague and I, lacking representational convergence on what our lab is fundamentally for, seem to fail the conditions for joint action. Yet this seems counterintuitive: we clearly share commitments, hold each other accountable, and revise our behavior in response to the other’s contributions in ways structurally identical to paradigm cases of cooperation. So my question is whether the irreducibility of collective intentionality necessarily entails a unified group-level representation, or whether there is conceptual space for what one might call interpretive pluralism—joint action sustained not by identical mental contents but by interlocking commitments that operate over partially opaque or divergent conceptions of the shared end. Specifically, do the standard conditions on shared cooperative activity require that participants share a single token plan with a uniform interpretation, or can the interlocking structure accommodate substantially different understandings of the plan’s purpose without collapsing into mere coordination? And if collective intentionality does not require representational convergence, what precisely is the shared component that distinguishes our laboratory from a case of two people accidentally working in the same building?
