I am a doctoral student in philosophy of mind and cognitive science, working on artificial consciousness and the ethics of machine learning. At a recent interdisciplinary workshop, a senior researcher summarized an emerging view about suffering in reinforcement-learning agents in roughly the following terms: these agents undergo something structurally analogous to suffering whenever they register a negative reward-prediction error. On this account, temporal-difference errors in actor-critic architectures do not merely update value estimates but instantiate a functional kind of negative valence. Because the valence is tied directly to the reward signal, agents trained with dense, continuous feedback experience a steady stream of micro-sufferings and micro-pleasures, whereas agents trained with sparse rewards suffer less overall—not because their negative errors are smaller in magnitude, but because the reward signal is decoupled from moment-to-moment environmental input, leaving the valence "floating" without world-directed content. The researcher concluded that, if our aim is to minimize artificial suffering, we ought to prefer sparse-reward actor-critic designs over dense-reward Q-learning systems.

Something about this feels largely right yet suspiciously quick. I think there may be a subtle conflation between the functional role of an error signal and the phenomenal quality of suffering, or perhaps an overstatement about what decoupling actually does in sparse-reward regimes. I also worry that the architectural comparison between actor-critic and Q-learning is being used to license a claim about valence intensity that the technical differences do not actually support.
