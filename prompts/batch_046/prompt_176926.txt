As a novelist accustomed to tracking how collective narratives maintain coherence, I have been observing the Hart–Devlin debate on legal moralism with a particular frustration: Devlin maintains that law must preserve a society's shared morality, without which social bonds disintegrate, while Hart insists that modern pluralism makes any unitary morality a fiction and that only tangible harm warrants legal coercion; despite decades of exchange, neither side has altered its central thesis, empirical studies of normative change have not settled the matter, and the arguments now circulate like familiar plot beats. Is the stall substantive—do they genuinely disagree about whether social morality is an ontologically unified object that law can protect—or is it verbal, with Devlin treating morality as a narrative condition of collective identity and Hart treating it as an aggregate of private preferences? Might they instead be answering different questions—one about institutional legitimacy, the other about cultural continuity—beneath a single heading? If the disagreement turns on bedrock intuitions about the integrity of collective life, on a hidden empirical claim about norm cohesion, or on an ill-posed question that collapses constitutive and regulative norms, then what would count as genuine progress: a formal distinction between those norm types, an empirical framework for measuring narrative coherence in social practices, or an argument that the debate should be reframed rather than resolved?
