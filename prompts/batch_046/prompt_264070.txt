Here is an argument I have developed that starts from Derek Parfit's hitchhiker scenario but ends in what I take to be an absurd conclusion, and I cannot locate the precise slip.

(1) In the hitchhiker case, it is rational to cultivate a disposition to pay the driver after reaching town, even though at the moment of payment there is no further causal benefit to doing so, because being predictably the type who pays is what secures the ride in the first place.

(2) The underlying structure is not causal decision theory but a selection argument: your present choice of disposition reveals your type, and types with certain properties are rewarded or punished regardless of whether your current act causes those properties.

(3) If this structure is valid, it applies wherever (a) your type is somewhat observable to others, (b) being a certain type yields a benefit, and (c) you can influence your type by adopting a policy or deliberately disabling a capacity.

(4) Now apply the structure to belief formation. Suppose you belong to a community in which holding belief B (for concreteness: that a particular well-established scientific consensus is mistaken) is socially and materially rewarded, and your reasoning patterns are somewhat transparent to others.

(5) By direct parallel, you should rationally cultivate a belief-forming policy that produces B regardless of the evidence you later encounter, because being the type who reliably holds B secures the benefit.

(6) Since belief is not under direct voluntary control in the way action is, cultivating such a policy requires rationally disabling your capacity for evidence-sensitive updating in this domain—making yourself locally epistemically irrational.

(7) Therefore rationality can require agents to render themselves systematically unable to respond correctly to epistemic reasons, whenever there is a reward for being a predictable believer.

(8) But this generalizes without limit: for any domain where belief-types are observable and rewarded, rational self-interest demands epistemic self-sabotage. That seems wrong—rationality ought not to systematically mandate false belief or the disabling of reasoning.

(9) Yet premises (1) through (6) each appear defensible on their face. If I must preserve something, I would most want to keep (1) and (3).
