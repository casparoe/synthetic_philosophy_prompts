hey, i'm a phd student in philosophy of mind and i've been trying to use HCH and factored cognition as an idealization for how we actually deliberate about practical stuff. i built this argument for a seminar and now it feels like it proves collaborative reasoning is literally impossible, which is obviously wrong, but i can't find the hole.

(1) In HCH, you split a hard problem into subproblems and have different people/agents solve them in relative isolation.
(2) For HCH to be an idealization of real deliberation, solving a subproblem in isolation has to yield the same normative result as solving it embedded in the full context.
(3) But framing/dependency effects in practical reasoning mean the "right" answer to a subquestion (e.g., "how much should i save for retirement?") depend on the overall life-plan it's embedded in.
(4) So real deliberation violates the independance condition in (2).
(5) Therefore HCH doesn't model deliberation; it replaces it with something structurally different.
(6) By the same token, factored cognition can't apply to practical reasoning unless we can factor problems without altering their normative content.
(7) But we clearly delegate practical reasoning all the time -- tax acountants, therapists, career coaches -- and it usually works fine.
(8) So either framing effects are illusory for practical reasoning (giving up 3), or there's a hidden coherence mechanism that lets us factor without loss.
(9) If there's a coherence mechanism, specifying it requires solving the original problem first, so HCH collapses back into single-agent reasoning.
(10) That proves too much: if HCH collapses, then no division of cognitive labor is possible, but we see it everywhere.

i'd really like to keep (3) and (7). is the mistake in (9) -- maybe there's a difference between specifying the mechanism and running it? or am i smuggling an equivocation between causal and normative independence between (4) and (6)? what's the cost of whichever fix is right?
