I have adopted a particular solution to the heterogeneity problem for hedonism—call it the uniform affective core view. It holds that pleasures are wildly heterogeneous in their intentional objects, causal origins, and sensory accompaniments, but they share a single, modality-independent phenomenal property: a bare, non-representational positive affective valence or “pro-attitude” that is invariant across gustatory, intellectual, malicious, and aesthetic cases. This core is not revealed by introspecting isolated episodes, but by comparative “hedonic calibration”: a disciplined exercise in ranking dissimilar pleasures, where subjects report an invariant directional “pull” that remains constant despite differences in content. The view claims this core explains normative aggregation onto one scale, survives the experience-machine objection (because the valence is intrinsic, not derived from representational accuracy), and predicts that damage to affective appraisal systems—not to sensory cortices—should flatten pleasure discrimination across domains.

Please provide a map of what would genuinely count against this position. Structure your response as follows:

1. The position in brief (about 150 words): restate the view precisely, emphasizing the calibration claim, the non-representational core, and the neural-prediction commitment.

2. Decisive defeaters (about 300 words): which observations, thought experiments, or patterns of phenomenological report would actually kill the uniform core—especially cases where cross-modal ranking systematically fails, where appraisal-system damage leaves certain pleasures intact, or where the supposed “pull” dissolves under careful comparison. Be specific about the evidential pattern that would be fatal rather than merely inconvenient.

3. Merely awkward evidence (about 250 words): anomalies the view can absorb only by adding qualifications—e.g., contextual modulation of the core, domain-specific “translation rules” for calibration, or graded rather than binary presence of the valence. Identify the theoretical costs of each absorption.

4. Defenders’ concessions versus dismissals (about 250 words): distinguish what a committed uniform-core hedonist would themselves concede as genuinely counting against them (not only what critics allege) from what they would reject—say, confusions between the core and its intentional clothing, or between failure of introspective access and absence of the property.

5. Ranked exposure (about 200 words): rank the defeaters from fatal to serious to awkward to cosmetic. Note which risks the view actually runs given its current commitments, and which it has already been insulated against.

6. Verdict on insulation (about 150 words): assess whether the view risks being qualified into unfalsifiability—whether defenders can always retreat to a thinner core or deny calibration conditions—and whether that insulation is an intellectual virtue or a vice.

Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Work from conceptual analysis and the internal commitments of the view itself.
