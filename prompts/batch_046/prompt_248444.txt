For my population-ethics seminar, I want to stress-test two live positions against a framing that breaks the usual “isolated lives” setup. Write a formal dialogue between a total-utilitarian advocate and a critical-level prioritarian—both equally sharp, neither a strawman—debating the following variant: Policy A creates a very large cohort of people whose lives are barely above the “worth living” threshold, but embedded in dense relational networks where welfare is partly constituted by social goods; Policy B creates a smaller cohort of flourishing individuals in near-isolation. The totalist should argue that scalable relational goods push the aggregate value of A past B without violating plausible axioms; the prioritarian should counter that relational welfare is not fungible with intrinsic welfare across the critical threshold, and that indexing the threshold to social density collapses the view into an untenable contextualism. Have them respond to each other’s actual points on three issues: whether relational goods are additive in the same currency as intrinsic well-being; whether the critical level should shift with social density; and whether the standard repugnant conclusion simply re-emerges once relational benefits are subject to diminishing returns at scale. Treat the positions as theoretical options rather than exegesis—no author names, no citations, no bibliography—and keep the tone that of a student preparing to present.
