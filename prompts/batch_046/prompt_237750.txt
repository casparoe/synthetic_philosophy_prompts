After a long, frustrating argument with a friend about whether the hard problem of consciousness is a genuine metaphysical puzzle or just a glitch in how we think about our own minds, I want to compare two accounts of what Chalmers calls the meta-problem. The first is the phenomenal concepts strategy: we possess direct, non-descriptive concepts of experience that make physicalism seem necessarily incomplete, regardless of the actual ontology. The second is an idea my friend kept pressing — call it the introspective calibration-error view — which holds that the explanatory-gap intuition arises not from special concepts but from a structural mismatch: phenomenal states have continuous, non-propositional dynamics, but introspection forces them into discrete, categorical reports, producing an illusory sense of unexplainability analogous to aliasing in signal processing. Compare these two approaches to dissolving the meta-problem. Which offers the more complete explanation of why consciousness seems so resistant to physicalist explanation? In your answer, specifically address how each treats the intuition of phenomenal transparency — the sense that in experiencing we are directly acquainted with an intrinsic, non-relational nature of the state — and whether either avoids implicitly presupposing the very dualism it claims to dissolve.
