Thought experiments in physics—such as Galileo’s inclined-plane argument against Aristotelian dynamics, or Einstein’s accelerating elevator—are frequently said to yield substantive knowledge about nature despite involving no laboratory measurement. Explain how this is possible, carefully distinguishing the epistemic contribution of a thought experiment from (i) a purely mathematical derivation, and (ii) direct empirical observation. Student’s answer:
“Thought experiments work by stripping away real-world complications so that pure reason can reveal the underlying structure of nature. Because there is no instrument error or observational noise, the result possesses the same modal certainty as a mathematical proof: if the premises hold, the physical conclusion must necessarily follow. For instance, Einstein’s elevator argument was used to derive the gravitational field equations directly from the equivalence principle, demonstrating that general relativity follows logically from that principle alone. Galileo’s argument similarly proves that all bodies fall at the same rate, because tying together bodies of different weights produces a contradiction—hence the conclusion is logically unavoidable and requires no empirical check. The sole function of actual observation is to verify whether the ideal conditions happen to obtain in the world; the knowledge itself is produced entirely by reason operating upon symmetric idealisations.”
