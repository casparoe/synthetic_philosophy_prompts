I work as a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I want a consistency audit on a set of views I have developed about welfare design, especially around workfare, conditionality, and the concept of an undeserving poor. I have tried to frame these issues as system-design problems rather than moral ones, but I am unsure whether the commitments actually cohere. Here is where I stand:

(1) The category undeserving poor is conceptually malformed for welfare policy. Economic need is a state variable, not a moral scorecard; attaching eligibility conditions based on moral biography is a category error analogous to judging a process by the intent of its inputs rather than by the behavior of its outputs.

(2) Workfare is ethically permissible if it is reconceived as an observability mechanism—similar to telemetry in a distributed system—rather than as moral testing. The state needs feedback signals (employment status, training participation, reported barriers) to detect misallocation and correct resource flows over time.

(3) Benefit fraud should be modeled as adversarial noise in the allocation pipeline. The correct engineering response is to harden verification layers and improve system robustness, not to expand a moral taxonomy of undeserving recipients.

(4) Unconditional cash transfers are inferior to conditional programs not because recipients fail to earn support, but because removing all conditions eliminates the observability channel. Without that channel, the system cannot learn where structural failures require rerouting resources.

(5) When a recipient fails a workfare condition due to structural barriers—unreliable transit, unpaid care obligations, or missing documentation—the correct system response is escalation: more support, not termination. The failure indicates a missing environmental dependency, not an agent fault.

Please audit these for joint tenability. Specifically: which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only appear to conflict but dissolve under closer inspection, and what is the least costly revision in each case? Rank the tensions by severity. Also, tell me whether there is a single revision—dropping or weakening one commitment—that cleans up the most conflicts, and precisely what I would have to sacrifice to make it. I am particularly concerned that my defense of conditionality in (2) and (4) might collapse back into the moral-testing framework I reject in (1), and that my escalation rule in (5) might undermine the learning function I claim for conditionality in (4).
