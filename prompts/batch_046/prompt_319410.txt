In debates over liberal democracy and religious citizenship, defenders of a Rawlsian public-reason requirement often compare it to navigation in a multilingual port: travelers may steer by private star maps at home, but shared coordination in the harbor requires everyone to read from a common nautical chart. The analogy is meant to show that asking citizens to justify coercive laws with secular, publicly accessible reasons is purely procedural—no one denies the truth of the private star maps, only their suitability for collective steering—so the burden is neutral rather than exclusionary. Audit this analogy in full. First, provide an explicit correspondence table mapping travelers, private star maps, the common chart, the port authority, and the “at home” exception onto citizens, comprehensive religious doctrines, public reasons, the liberal state, and private or associational life. Second, isolate the disanalogies that actually matter for the defense: does the common chart possess genuine epistemic authority, or is it merely conventional? Does the analogy quietly presuppose that religious reasons are parochial while secular ones are universal? And does it obscure the fact that the “shared” chart is itself drawn from one particular navigational tradition—secular liberalism—rather than being tradition-independent? Third, determine whether the normative conclusion—that public reason is an impartial filter rather than a substantive burden on religious citizens—survives once these breakdowns are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying the conclusion all along. If the mapping collapses, suggest a more accurate analogy and say what it changes. Write in American English, with formal precision, and do not provide bibliographies or reading lists.
