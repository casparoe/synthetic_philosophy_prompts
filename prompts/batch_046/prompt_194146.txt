The philosophical debate about dirty hands in politics—whether political actors can be morally required to perform acts that remain personally wrongful, and whether such situations represent genuine moral tragedies, resolvable conflicts of duty, or conceptual confusions about role morality—has remained structurally unchanged for decades. The same canonical scenarios recur (ordering deception to prevent catastrophe, sanctioning coercion to preserve institutional stability), and the standard responses have become predictable: one camp insists on an irreducible moral remainder, another dissolves the problem into ordinary weighing of reasons, and a third denies that roles can generate genuinely distinct obligations. Yet the disagreement shows no signs of resolution.
