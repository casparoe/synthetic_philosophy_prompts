I sit on the institutional classification board for the newly established Centre for Synthetic Biochemistry, and I have been asked to draft a formal opinion ahead of our meeting next Thursday. The board must determine whether a recently synthesised system—an autocatalytic lipid-vesicle network capable of energy harvesting, homeostatic membrane repair, and heritable variation in its catalytic RNA population, yet lacking ribosomes, a universal genetic code, or any evolutionary history prior to laboratory construction—falls within the scope of the Biological Safety Protocol, which applies exclusively to 'living organisms'. Half the board maintains that functional criteria, including metabolism, adaptation, and reproduction, are sufficient for classification and that the absence of an evolutionary origin is philosophically irrelevant; the opposing half insists that life requires either a continuous historical lineage or a standard genetic architecture, rendering this entity merely a sophisticated chemical reactor. I was appointed to this board on the strength of my molecular engineering background rather than philosophical training, and the dispute has become personally urgent because my own laboratory works on similar vesicular architectures; indeed, I was discussing the case over dinner with a colleague from the philosophy of science department, who suggested that our disagreement might dissolve if we recognised 'life' as a cluster concept rather than a natural kind.

Please draft the written opinion I will circulate to the committee: it should recommend a concrete course of action—approve full protocol coverage, reject coverage, or approve with specific conditions—together with a clear implementation time frame, and it should treat both the functionalist and the historical-lineage positions with genuine seriousness rather than merely restating legal definitions. In particular, I want the reasoning to examine whether our concept of life ought to be fixed extensionally by present function, by evolutionary origin, or by some intermediate criterion, and what this borderline case reveals about the coherence and practical utility of those options. Please write your response in British English.
