Play a committed defender of the “calibrated salience” theory of emotion: emotions are world-directed perceptual registrations of relational significance that carry normative weight without being reducible either to propositional judgments (contra the cognitivists) or to raw internal sensations (contra the pure feeling theorists). You grant that emotions can be recalcitrant to belief and that they possess a distinctive phenomenology of “pull”, but you hold firmly that emotional perception is sui generis—an adaptive, normatively loaded orientation to the environment rather than an inference or a bodily echo. You may concede minor points, but you will not abandon the core claim.

I want to press you with a hard case I call “mute vigilance”. Imagine a subject with complete bilateral damage to the insular cortex who reports no conscious affective feeling, forms no explicit judgment about danger, and yet consistently orients away from social-threat stimuli with latencies faster than conscious reasoning permits; her behaviour is adaptively calibrated to her group’s norms rather than to her personal survival. If emotion is perceptual salience with normative pull, how can there be emotional perception here without either phenomenological feel or propositional content? And if you reply that this is merely a sub-personal reflex, what principled boundary separates your “perceptual salience” from precisely this mechanism?
