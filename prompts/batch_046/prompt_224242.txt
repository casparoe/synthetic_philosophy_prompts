I'm trying to work out what I actually think about moral compromise — like when two value systems have to live together and both bend a bit. I keep hitting cases where my gut says yes to one deal and no to another that looks structurally identical, so I want to know if there's a principle behind it or if I'm just being inconsistent.

Here are my verdicts:

1. A vegan chef who hates meat agrees to serve one meat dish on Friday nights, because without it the resturant closes and she can't fund her plant-based R&D kitchen. I think that's okay — it's a bounded, structural compromise where both sides adjust practice.

2. The same chef writes a sponsored article praising "ethical beef" for a meat brand; the fee goes to an animal shelter. I think that's not okay, even though more animals might be saved. Here she hasn't changed her practice, she's just lent her values for cash. The contamination feels one-directional.

3. Two neighbours — one wants silence after 8pm, the other music until midnight — agree on 10pm with shared soundproofing costs. Fine, classic gains-from-trade.

4. A pacifist pays income tax knowing part funds the military, but she uses healthcare and roads funded by the same pot. I'm unsure — is this a compromise she entered, or just living inside a system? I go back and forth on whether it belongs in the same box.

5. A feminist collective takes sponsorship from a cosmetics firm with sexist ads to fund a legal defence fund. They don't change their message. I think this is closer to case 2 than case 1, but maybe less bad? I genuinely can't settle it.

6. A secular school lets a religious group use its hall after hours in exchange for the group maintaining the building's garden. I think that's fine — transparent, bounded, neither side has to betray their core commitments in public.

So is there a principle that captures this — maybe something about structural vs transactional compromise, or whether you have to perform the other value? Which of my verdicts does it have to drop? And what does it predict for a case I haven't decided: a university with a strict free-speech code allowing a controversial speaker only in a sealed room with no recording, in exchange for the speaker's group endowing a scholarship?

Please seperate what's settled from what's genuinely contested among people who actually study moral compromise and value pluralism — don't present a contested view as the consensus. Write in British English, please.
