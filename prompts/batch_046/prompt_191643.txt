I am presently drafting a historical novel set in Prague during the first years of the seventeenth century, and in preparing the inner life of my protagonist — a court astronomer who moves between imperial patronage and private doubt — I have been working slowly through Johannes Kepler’s 1601 treatise De fundamentis astrologiae certioribus. I find myself caught on a particular passage that seems to sit at an odd angle to everything else I understand about his cosmology, and I should be most grateful for a careful unpacking.

In this part of the text, Kepler argues that celestial bodies do not influence the sublunary world by mechanical impact or by any corporeal emission traversing the ether, for the distances are too vast and the spheres are not physically contiguous with Earth. Instead, he proposes that each planet emits what he terms species: an immaterial, geometric configuration corresponding to the angles and aspects it forms with other bodies. These species are not received by physical matter in the manner of heat or light, but are taken up by the anima terrae — the Earth’s own vital principle — in a fashion analogous to the way a mirror receives an image without being touched by the object it reflects. Thus, when Mars stands in quadrature to Saturn, the Earth’s soul registers a particular geometric tension that manifests in disturbances of weather, in the humours of living bodies, or in the collective temper of populations, all without any material chain of causation running through the heavens.

I should like to understand three things about this account. First, is Kepler advancing a mere metaphor — the mirror and the receptive soul — or is he committing to a genuine ontological entity, the anima terrae, that possesses a kind of perceptual faculty directed at geometric form? Second, how does this model relate to his own astronomical mechanics, particularly the magnetic and rotational forces he invokes in the Astronomia nova to explain planetary motion: does the immateriality of astrological influence protect it from contradiction with his physical cosmology, or does it expose an unresolved tension in his thought? Third, given that he distinguishes sharply between natural astrology, which explains physical effects, and judicial astrology, which predicts individual destinies, does this geometric-reception model actually provide a foundation for both, or does it inadvertently undermine judicial predictions by reducing them to generic, physically mediated responses of the Earth’s soul? I am not looking for bibliographic directions, but rather for a conceptual dissection that might help me render my character’s intellectual predicament with accuracy.
