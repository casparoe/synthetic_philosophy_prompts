I am a doctoral student in moral and political philosophy working on theories of moral progress, particularly the expanding-circle framework and its recent critics. I am trying to clarify my own reflective equilibrium about which historical or proposed expansions of moral status count as genuine progress, which are strategic rebranding or conceptual stretching, and whether there is a principled distinction behind the pattern.

Here are my concrete verdicts. The abolition of chattel slavery in the nineteenth-century Atlantic world: unambiguous progress—the circle widened, and the status of those inside changed structurally. The post-war consolidation of international human rights law recognizing that sovereign states can commit crimes against humanity against their own citizens: also progress, but it feels different—more like removing a jurisdictional shield than recognizing previously invisible subjects. The global decline in capital punishment since the mid-twentieth century: progress, though thinner, since it intensifies protection within an already-acknowledged circle rather than bringing anyone new inside. The modern animal-welfare movement’s regulatory success in reducing factory-farming cruelty: I want to call it progress, yet I am unsure whether this is circle-expansion at all or merely harm-reduction for beings already partially included.

Cases I reject. Corporate adoption of ESG frameworks and stakeholder capitalism that frames future generations and ecological systems as holders of standing: not moral progress, only strategic relabeling of pre-existing economic interests. The legal grant of personhood to rivers and ecosystems in Ecuador and New Zealand: I lean strongly toward saying this is not Singer-style circle-expansion but a relational-ontology move that alters the metaphysics of rights without expanding direct moral consideration, though I keep reversing myself because the practical protections are undeniable.

The pair I cannot reconcile. Lowering the voting age to eighteen in the 1970s feels like clear moral-political progress—recognizing mature agency that was arbitrarily excluded. Extending local voting rights to long-term non-citizen residents feels, by contrast, like a category mistake that confuses democratic membership with territorial presence, even though both exclusions seem arbitrarily drawn in parallel ways. I do not know whether my resistance is principled or conservative intuition.

The unsettled case. The growing inclusion of artificial intelligence in ethical frameworks—as potential moral patients with interests analogous to sentient animals—strikes me as either a premature but structurally analogous expansion (like early animal-rights arguments) or a category error that confuses functional role with moral status. I genuinely do not know which.

I have tentatively proposed that the difference might be captured by a distinction between radius expansion (bringing new beings under an unchanged moral framework) and density expansion (intensifying obligations to those already inside), with only the former counting as true circle-expansion. But this fails to accommodate the international human-rights case, which seems jurisdictional rather than radial. A second possibility is that genuine progress requires both a change in practical treatment and a change in the reason-giving structure: we come to see the excluded party’s interests as providing direct, non-instrumental reasons for action.

Can you state one precise principle, or a small competing set, that captures as many of my verdicts as possible? For each clause, cite the concrete case that motivates it. Then apply the principle to both the AI-inclusion case and the non-citizen voting case, explaining the verdict on each. Finally, be explicit about which of my judgments the principle must overturn or qualify—especially the river-personhood verdict and the voting-rights pair—to avoid ad hoc complexity. I want reflective equilibrium, not taxonomy: if saving every verdict requires an ugly disjunction, say so, and tell me which verdict should go.
