I'm preparing a ninety-minute discussion session for a local humanities reading group—educated adults with no background in classics or moral philosophy—on the claim that biography is not merely entertainment or commemoration but a structured form of moral education, and that different biographical traditions do radically different pedagogical work. I want the session to turn on a comparison between Plutarch's comparative biographies—his paired Greek and Roman lives—and Christian hagiography: where Plutarch pairs figures across cultures to show virtue and vice as intertwined in ordinary human complexity, hagiography tends to isolate a single excellence by removing contradiction, failure, and mundane particularity.

The underlying idea I need them to grasp is that moral learning from lives requires what I am calling normative friction—the reader must be able to recognize a life as excellent without being invited to replicate it wholesale, and must retain enough distance to disagree with specific choices while still accepting the figure as a calibration point for judgment. Could you design the explanatory plan rather than the explanation itself? I need the dependency order: which concept must be secured before the Plutarch-versus-hagiography contrast can appear as a structural difference in how biography teaches, rather than just a difference in tone or historical honesty. I need one carrying example—a specific paired comparison or a specific hagiographic narrative—and a precise account of what formal features it must display for the pedagogical point to become visible to non-specialists.

I also need the misconception map: the audience will likely arrive thinking that biography teaches by supplying imitable role models, that hagiography is merely propaganda or dishonest history, and that Plutarch is essentially comparing ancient resumes; I need to know exactly where in the sequence to defuse each so that none derails the arc. Finally, I need a closing check that reveals whether they have understood the idea of moral calibration through narrative distance rather than simply feeling inspired, and I need to know which elements of contemporary virtue-ethics discussions of exemplarity should be openly set aside at this level rather than imported into a ninety-minute session for general readers.
