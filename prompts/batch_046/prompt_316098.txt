Analyze the 1976 U.S. national swine-flu immunization program through the lens of the separateness-of-persons objection to utilitarian reasoning, but do not stay with the textbook version of that objection. Instead, construct and apply a distinction I will call risk-pooling versus harm-shifting. The premises are these: in 1976, fearing a 1918-style pandemic, the federal government vaccinated roughly forty-five million Americans; the pandemic never arrived; and the program caused an estimated several hundred cases of Guillain-Barré syndrome, with a smaller number of deaths, among the vaccinated. The standard separateness objection—roughly, that utilitarianism treats persons as interchangeable containers of welfare and therefore licenses sacrificing one for the aggregate—clearly applies if the program was harm-shifting: that is, if a definite, identifiable subgroup was made to suffer so that the majority could be protected. But apply the same lens if the structure was risk-pooling: each participant faced an independent, low-probability risk of harm in exchange for an independent, low-probability benefit of protection, with no ex ante identification of who would bear the cost.

Your task is a disciplined two-way mapping. First, say which moral structure actually describes the 1976 decision. Did the government ask citizens to enter a collective lottery in which anyone might draw the short straw, or did it effectively conscript a small, retrospectively identifiable group into bearing a definite harm for the definite safety of the rest? Second, ask what the separateness objection actually targets. Does it condemn risk-pooling just as sharply as harm-shifting, or does it depend on there being a specific victim whose welfare is being traded away? Third, consider the retrospective turn: because the pandemic never materialized, the benefit to the vaccinated turned out to be zero, while the harm to the GBS victims remained real. Does this ex post knowledge convert what was risk-pooling into harm-shifting? If so, does the moral evaluation of the program depend on outcomes that the decision-makers could not have known, and does that show that the separateness objection needs a supplementary theory of risk, time, and moral luck to do any work? Finally, say what this episode reveals about the objection itself—whether it is strengthened by the case, rendered silent, or shown to require distinctions it has not yet developed. Be precise about who the moral agents were, what options they faced, and where the theory runs out of resources rather than forcing it to speak.
