I am defending, in next week's philosophy of science seminar, the claim that a rigorously Darwinian model of cultural transmission predicts the competitive dominance of structurally simple but epistemically parasitic scientific analogies over more accurate but cognitively costly representations, and that scientific institutions therefore operate less like neutral transmission channels and more like adaptive immune systems that actively suppress such analogies. The audience comprises evolutionary biologists committed to gene-culture coevolution models and philosophers of science skeptical of strong memetics; both groups are technically literate but hostile to reifying cultural units as replicators with independent fitness interests. Prepare my Q&A defense sheet: list the eight objections I will actually receive, ordered from most to least damaging, phrased exactly as members of these two subgroups would put them, with for each a concise reply suitable for a two-minute response, an explicit note on where that reply is weakest or concedes too much, and an indication of whether the objection comes primarily from the biologists or from the philosophers. State upfront the single concession I should make about the limits of applying biological selection logic to intentional belief revision. Finally, identify the one question I am hoping no one asks, explain why answering it honestly would undermine the core thesis, and sketch the minimal response I should have ready if it is raised.
