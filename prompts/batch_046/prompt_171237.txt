Question: Explain the 'wrong kind of reasons' problem in moral philosophy. In your answer, distinguish it from Williams's internal-reasons thesis, and discuss whether Scanlon's contractualism avoids giving the wrong kind of reason for acting morally. Conclude by assessing whether the problem can be dissolved if we adopt a 'dual-source' view of practical reasons (where moral and prudential reasons operate on separate registers).

Student answer:
The wrong kind of reasons problem arises when we justify moral behaviour by pointing to benefits for the agent — like avoiding punishment or gaining social approval — because those aren't the reasons morality actually cares about. So if I tell you 'be honest because it makes you feel better', I've given you the wrong kind of reason. This is different from Bernard Williams's internal-reasons thesis, which says that for something to be a reason for me it must connect to something I already care about. Williams actually makes the wrong-kind problem worse, since if I don't already care about morality then no moral reason can reach me, which means any attempt to justify morality from outside is automatically the wrong kind. Scanlon's contractualism avoids this because it shows that moral reasons are internal to the practice of justifiability: we have reason to act morally because we wouldn't want to live in a society where everyone ignored the principle. This gives us a prudential-style reason, but since it's embedded in the contractualist framework, it counts as the right kind. Meanwhile, the dual-source view dissolves the problem altogether by putting moral and prudential reasons on separate registers that never interact; since they speak different languages, asking for a prudential justification of morality is just a category mistake. So Scanlon solves it structurally, while the dual-source view makes it vanish by separation.
