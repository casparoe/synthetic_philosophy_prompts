Consider the decision to launch the Space Shuttle Challenger on 28 January 1986, despite warnings from engineers at Morton Thiokol regarding O-ring resilience at low temperatures. The standard account describes a breakdown in the flow of technical information up through NASA management layers, with concerns being overridden at successive levels.

Analyse this episode through the lens of the regress problem in epistemology—specifically the dispute between foundationalism, coherentism, and infinitism about the structure of justification. Your analysis should run in both directions.

First, map the epistemic architecture of the decision onto each of the three positions. If we treat the belief that launch is safe as the target belief requiring justification, what, on a foundationalist reading, serves as the basic beliefs? Are they direct engineering measurements, prior successful launches, or managerial authority? Where does the regress stop, and does the episode expose that the putative foundations were themselves unjustified? On a coherentist reading, how do the various pieces of evidence—the temperature data, the history of O-ring erosion, the managerial risk assessments, the institutional culture—mutually support or undermine one another? Does the coherence of the belief-set actually obtain, or is there an illusion of coherence created by hierarchical filtering? On an infinitist reading, is there a non-arbitrary infinite chain of justification, perhaps through successive deferrals of authority from engineer to manager to administrator? Or does the episode show that the chain was not genuinely infinite but arbitrarily terminated?

Second, examine what the episode reveals about each theory. Does the Challenger case show that foundationalism fails in institutional contexts because the foundations are always mediated by social structures that lack basic status? Does it support coherentism by showing that justification was a property of the whole network rather than any single belief? Or does it vindicate infinitism by demonstrating that justification is genuinely open-ended, with each level deferring to the next?

Third, and more specifically, consider whether the episode forces us to recognise a distinctively social or distributed form of the regress—where the justifying beliefs are not all within a single mind but distributed across agents in a hierarchy. Can standard individualist versions of foundationalism, coherentism, or infinitism accommodate this? If not, does the case suggest that we need a hybrid account, or does it show that one of the three positions is more adaptable to collective epistemology than the others?

Please be disciplined about the mapping: identify precisely who plays the role of the believer, what counts as the evidence at each stage, and where each theory is silent rather than forcing it to speak. Conclude by saying which position, if any, best explains both the structure of the justification that failed and the nature of the failure itself.
