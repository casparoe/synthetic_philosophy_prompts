As a retired physiologist who has only recently begun working through normative ethics, I am trying to formulate my own position on suffering and well-being before presenting it in an informal reading group. I want an audit of whether the following commitments hang together, since I am not yet fluent in the literature and do not wish to rely on citation-based authority.

My commitments are these:

(a) I endorse a lexical-threshold form of negative utilitarianism: there is a region of extreme suffering such that no quantity of pleasure, however large, can morally outweigh its elimination; the priority is absolute, not merely weighted.

(b) I reject the idea that pleasure and pain are points on a single psychophysical scale. Drawing on my background in sensory physiology, I treat them as orthogonal dimensions—more like temperature and osmotic pressure than like positive and negative values on one axis.

(c) Even so, I believe practical rationality can construct a context-sensitive "exchange rate" between them for decision-making, using revealed-preference methods under conditions of risk, without reducing either to the other.

(d) I take the lexical threshold itself to be dynamically calibrated, not biologically fixed: it functions like an allostatic set-point that shifts with expectation, prior exposure, and interoceptive prediction error, so that what counts as "extreme" is partly frame-dependent.

(e) Once suffering is reliably below that calibrated threshold, the residual moral task is to maximize aggregate well-being, which I understand as requiring integration of both dimensions into a unified welfare calculus.

I can see potential friction between (a) and (d)—if the boundary is fluid, the "lexical" character seems to dissolve into a steep but continuous gradient—but I am unsure whether that tension is real or merely verbal. I also worry that (b) and (e) conflict, since integrating orthogonal dimensions into one calculus sounds like the very reductionism I reject in (b). Meanwhile, (c) might appear incompatible with (b), though I suspect that appearance is illusory: pragmatic comparability need not entail ontological commensurability, just as engineers trade temperature and pressure via a cost function without claiming they share a unit.

Please rank the tensions by severity, distinguishing those that are genuinely destructive to the set from those that dissolve on inspection. For each real tension, indicate the least costly revision—whether it requires abandoning lexical priority, abandoning orthogonality, treating the exchange rate as purely instrumental, or reconceiving the post-threshold calculus as multi-criteria rather than unified. Do not treat all conflicts as equally bad; I want to know which commitments are load-bearing and which are decorative. I am not asking for a literature survey, only for a consistency audit from first principles.
