I'm writing a feature on how medieval arguments are being reinterpreted in contemporary philosophy of mind. For it, I've adopted a specific reading of Avicenna's flying man thought experiment: rather than proving substantial dualism, I take it to defend phenomenal minimalism—the claim that first-person self-reference is irreducible to bodily representation, without committing to an immaterial substance. What would genuinely count against this reading? I want defeaters sorted by decisiveness: themes in Avicenna's Kitāb al-Shifā' and al-Ishārāt that resist a non-substantialist interpretation; modern findings on proprioception and interoception that suggest bodily self-modeling can replicate the argument's conditions; and logical critiques of the counterfactual suspension of sensation, for instance that it confuses absence of representational content with presence of non-physical reference. For each, indicate whether a defender of phenomenal minimalism could absorb it by qualification, and whether such qualifications risk rendering the view unfalsifiable. Be frank about which feature of Avicenna's actual metaphysical commitments this interpretation cannot reproduce and must therefore be supplied by me as the interpreter.
