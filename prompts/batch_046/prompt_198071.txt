I am a doctoral student in philosophy working on the epistemology of logic, and I have been returning repeatedly to Carroll's dialogue between Achilles and the Tortoise, in which the Tortoise forces Achilles into an infinite regress by demanding that every inference rule used in a deduction be added as an explicit premise. In seminars and in the literature, the same family of responses keeps circulating without apparent resolution: some philosophers treat the paradox as demonstrating that deduction cannot justify itself without vicious regress; others dismiss it as a category mistake between using a rule and mentioning it, or between premises in an object-language and rules in a meta-language; and still others read it as evidence that rule-following is ultimately a practical or psychological capacity rather than a propositional one. Despite well over a century of commentary, the disagreement feels genuinely stalled—not for lack of new footnotes, but because the same moves are replayed and no one concedes ground. My working hypothesis is that the dispute is not, at bottom, about formal logic or about the architecture of proof systems, but about what kind of thing "accepting an inference rule" is: is it possessing a propositional belief, exercising a practical ability, occupying a normative status within a social practice, or something else altogether? If that is correct, then the sides may be answering different questions—about ontology, about psychology, about explanation—while using the single heading "the justification of deduction."

I want a diagnosis rather than a survey of interpretations or a verdict on Carroll's argument itself. Specifically: is this disagreement verbal, turning on ambiguous uses of "accept" or "justify"; is it substantive but hidden, perhaps about the ontology of norms or the structure of formal languages; or is it a disagreement about standards of explanation—about what counts as solving the puzzle? Do the opposing sides differ about empirical matters (for instance, about cognitive psychology or the behavior of formal systems), about which imagined cases count as data, or about starting intuitions that neither side can independently argue for? Is there a concealed formal question doing the work—say, about the object-language versus meta-language distinction in modern proof theory—or is the impasse a sign that the original question is ill-posed? I also want an honest assessment of whether progress is possible: could a conceptual distinction—for example, between constitutive and regulative norms of reasoning—dissolve the stalemate; could a result in cognitive science about implicit rule-following settle it; could a theorem about formalization expose one side; or should we conclude that no single answer is available and change the question? Please ground the diagnosis in what the sides actually say, rank the potential defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and indicate where judgment cannot be eliminated. Write the response in American English.
