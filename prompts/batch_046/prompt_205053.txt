As a novelist, I am presently drafting a work in which the central drama is not romantic but ontological: a couple must decide whether to conceive knowing that any child they bear carries a substantial risk of inheriting a degenerative neurological condition. I want to embed within this narrative a philosophical disagreement that feels intellectually alive rather than pedagogical. To that end, I have been reflecting on the procreation asymmetry—the puzzling intuition that creating a being who will suffer is morally problematic, whereas failing to create a being who would flourish carries no comparable moral weight. Yet I do not wish my characters to rehearse familiar formulations.

Instead, I seek an example of a conceptual issue internal to the asymmetry itself, one that becomes acute when we consider not actual children but counterfactual ones—those who inhabit the narrative only as roads not taken. Specifically, I am interested in the tension that arises if we claim that a merely potential person can be harmed by being brought into suffering, yet cannot be symmetrically benefited by being brought into joy, for this seems to require that non-existence be a state with respect to which harm is intelligible, but not a state with respect to which benefit is intelligible. Could you articulate such a conceptual issue with philosophical precision? I need it rendered clearly enough to serve as the subtext for a dialogue between the couple and a physician-philosopher, without any reliance on bibliographic references or citations, since I am writing away from my library and require only the conceptual architecture.
