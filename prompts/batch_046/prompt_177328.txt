I spent the better part of an evening arguing with a close friend about the socialist calculation debate, and I need a conceptual adjudication rather than a scorecard. The dispute turned on whether modern computation resolves the classical objections of Mises and Hayek. Whenever I pressed the point that market prices encode dispersed, often tacit local knowledge that no central node can fully observe—so that the obstacle to socialist planning is epistemic, not merely technological—my friend would fall back on the same line: “prices are just compressed signals, and signals can be digitized and centralized.” He used this to dismiss both Mises’s argument about the impossibility of rational economic calculation without market exchange and Hayek’s argument about the use of knowledge in society, insisting that convex-optimization routines, large-scale forecasting models, and algorithmic price discovery only make the dismissal more obvious. If a planner can run the algorithm, he said, the planner already possesses what the market possessed.

I am not asking for a bibliography or a history of the debate; I want the slogan itself dissected. Could you lay out the distinct precise theses that “prices are just compressed signals, and signals can be digitized and centralized” might actually be expressing, arranged from the trivial to the radical? For each reading, indicate whether it is true, whether it is defensible, and—most importantly—whether it actually engages Hayek’s local-knowledge objection and Mises’s calculation problem, or whether it instead changes the subject. In particular, please distinguish at least the following possibilities: (i) a purely logistical claim that price data can be recorded, transmitted, and stored by a central authority; (ii) an epistemic claim that all knowledge embodied in prices is explicit, codifiable, and therefore available to a planner; (iii) a procedural claim that an algorithmic process (such as a Walrasian tâtonnement executed in software, or an iterative gradient-descent approximation of equilibrium) can reproduce not just the static outcomes but the real-time discovery dynamics of decentralized exchange; and (iv) any stronger thesis that collapses the distinction between the epistemic role of prices in markets and the informational role of data in computation. Also address whether importing modern analogies—machine-learning forecasting, convex optimization, distributed computing—alters the philosophical force of the slogan, or whether they merely update the machinery without touching the underlying argument about dispersed knowledge and entrepreneurial discovery. Present your analysis as a menu of readings with verdicts, so I can see which ones my friend probably meant and which ones, if any, actually earn him the conclusion he wanted.
