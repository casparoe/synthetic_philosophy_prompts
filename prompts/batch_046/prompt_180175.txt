Consider the institutional architecture of environmental governance in many states: emergency disaster funds are released within hours based on real-time casualty counts and infrastructure damage, while watershed-restoration budgets are appropriated in decadal cycles tied to geological erosion rates; climate-adaptation grants require annual deliverables and quantifiable outputs, but heritage-preservation statutes protect buildings on century-scale timelines with no intermediate metrics. The same legislative body treats a flash flood as an event demanding immediate individual-level response, yet treats soil salinization as a background condition not requiring intervention until it crosses an arbitrary decadal threshold. What philosophy of historical causation—what implicit ontology of events, structures, and temporal scale—does this bifurcated practice presuppose? Does it commit to Braudel's distinction between the rapid time of events and the slow time of geographical structures, or is it compatible with a uniform causal model that simply applies different discount rates? Reconstruct the implicit theory: does the practice treat long-duration processes as causally inert until they manifest as discrete events, or does it assign them a different kind of agency altogether? Say whether the parts of the practice are philosophically consistent, whether they could be licensed by rival views of historical explanation, and what a governance framework that genuinely integrated slow-structure causation with event-level response would have to change about funding rhythms, accountability mechanisms, and the unit of moral concern.
