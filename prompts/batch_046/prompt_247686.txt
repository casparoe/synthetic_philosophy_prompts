I'm trying to work out what I actually think about whether physical brain size is morally relevant, by testing my gut reactions to concrete rescue and triage cases. Here are my verdicts; I'd like to know whether there's a coherent principle behind them, or whether I have to drop one.

Case 1: Two strangers need a single life-saving intervention. One has benign macrocephaly—brain volume roughly twice the human mean—but cognitive and emotional capacities are within the normal human range. The other is neurologically typical. Verdict: brain size gives me no reason to choose either; it's a moral tie.

Case 2: I must choose between saving a typical adult human and an adult sperm whale. The whale's brain is roughly six times larger by mass. Verdict: I save the human. The whale's larger brain doesn't outweigh the human's claim.

Case 3: I must choose between saving a newborn human with severe microcephaly—brain volume well below the species mean, but with the normal developmental trajectory of human consciousness—and an adult domestic pig with a brain of comparable absolute size. Verdict: I save the infant. Absolute brain volume doesn't bridge the species gap.

Case 4: I must choose between saving a typical adult human and a hypothetical being whose brain contains fifty times as many neurons, with cognition scaled proportionally: it suffers and flourishes with an intensity and complexity far beyond human experience, and its mental life is continuous with ours rather than alien. Verdict: I feel pulled to save the being. At this extreme scale, brain-related capacity seems to carry independent weight.

Case 5: A human's brain is gradually replaced, neuron for neuron, with synthetic analogues, ending in a refrigerator-sized substrate with identical functional organization. Verdict: moral status is unchanged; the physical volume of the substrate is irrelevant.

Case 6: Two patients need a scarce treatment. One is in a persistent vegetative state with an intact, large brain; the other is conscious with an average-sized brain. Verdict: I treat the conscious patient. The presence of consciousness, not tissue volume, decides.

Case 7: One hundred humans pool cognition via direct, continuous neural interfaces, producing a single unified conscious system with the combined neural substrate of one hundred brains. I can save only this collective or a separate, individual human. Verdict: I think I should save the collective, which again treats total integrated neural capacity as morally weighty.

Is there a principle—perhaps about the scale of integrated conscious welfare-potential—that accommodates Cases 1, 2, 3, 5, and 6, while also explaining Case 4 and Case 7? Or do Cases 4 and 7 show that I'm smuggling in a simple "larger brain matters more" rule that I explicitly reject in Cases 1–3? Which of my verdicts should I overturn, and what does the repaired principle predict for a case I haven't listed: a human-level artificial intelligence running on a distributed server farm whose total transistor count is equivalent to one human brain's synapse count, facing destruction?
