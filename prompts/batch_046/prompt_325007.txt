I'm currently in a philosophy of science seminar that has been working through the relationship between formal learning theory and classical epistemology. This week we discussed Wolpert's no-free-lunch results for supervised learning, and the professor emphasized that they formalize a version of Hume's problem of induction: because any two learning algorithms have identical average performance over the uniform distribution of all possible target functions, successful generalization requires a prior that restricts the hypothesis space, yet that prior itself stands in need of justification.

The discussion left me with the impression that the real difficulty is not that induction fails outright, but that every inductive success is parasitic on biases we haven't independently earned. For a study-group icebreaker, I'd like a joke that turns precisely on this structure rather than offering a generic quip about machines being unable to learn. The punchline should depend on the listener recognizing that averaging over all possible worlds makes every pattern equally likely, so that expecting the sun to rise tomorrow is only rational if we have smuggled in an unjustified prior — and that this is structurally the same problem the NFL theorems expose in algorithmic learning. The joke should be intelligible to someone with that background and should not depend on references to specific paper titles, page numbers, or bibliographic details. Could you write one?
