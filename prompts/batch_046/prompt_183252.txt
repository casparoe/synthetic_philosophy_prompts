In my high school philosophy of mind seminar, we have been examining the color phi phenomenon and the larger problem of how conscious experience assigns temporal order to its contents. Our discussion centered on Daniel Dennett's well-known distinction between Orwellian and Stalinesque models of postdictive consciousness: on the Orwellian account, an earlier phenomenal state is literally rewritten or altered after a subsequent stimulus arrives, as though the brain went back and edited the record; on the Stalinesque account, no earlier state is changed, but the system constructs a false narrative that assigns fabricated timestamps to intact experiences, producing an illusion of continuous motion without retroactive content-change. Despite decades of discussion in the literature, the debate seems to have stalled at exactly the same juncture — both sides accept the same psychophysical data on apparent motion and the neural delays involved in reportability, yet they continue to disagree about whether the subject genuinely experienced a green flash in the middle of the trajectory or merely reports having done so.

What I find puzzling is that neither camp seems willing to address a possibility that strikes me as conceptually prior: that phenomenal consciousness might possess an intrinsic temporal thickness, so that there is no atomistic instant of experience that could be either revised or mislabeled in the first place. If the specious present is genuinely extended rather than punctate, then both the Orwellian and Stalinesque models may share a false presupposition — namely, that conscious states are discrete temporal atoms that must be arranged into a sequence either veridically or deceptively. I want to ask whether this stalemate is therefore a hidden empirical disagreement about neural dynamics that awaits a specific experimental signature, a verbal dispute about what counts as a revision of experience, or a bedrock clash about whether temporal properties are intrinsic to phenomenal states themselves. And if the impasse really does trace back to this unexamined assumption about temporal atomism, what kind of progress should we expect — a conceptual distinction that dissolves the framing, an empirical finding about the duration of conscious integration, or simply an admission that the question is ill-posed?
