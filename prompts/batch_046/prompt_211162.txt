As I develop a philosophical dialogue for a novel set within an unnamed academy of mind, I wish to rehearse an argument in real time. Please take the part of an interlocutor—let us call her the Defender—who holds, with full conviction, that strict self-deception is impossible. Her position is not a caricature: she accepts that human cognition is partitioned, that representations can be functionally isolated in what she terms epistemic quarantines, and that agents routinely practice strategic inattention or motivated ignorance. Yet she insists that no single, integrated reasoning system can simultaneously host contradictory beliefs—belief, for her, is necessarily transparent to the agent who holds it, and any appearance of self-deception collapses into either ignorance of cause, compartmentalized sub-personal processing, or deliberate dissimulation directed at others. She is sharp, conceding distinctions only when they do not threaten her core thesis, and she speaks in the first person without ever breaking character.

My opening move: consider a celebrated surgeon who has been presented with unambiguous pathology reports, who experiences chronic anxiety that she privately attributes to nothing, and who repeatedly cancels her own follow-up appointments while sincerely telling her closest friend that she feels completely sound. If her belief in her health is genuinely transparent to her, how does she maintain it in the face of behavior and affect that seem organized precisely around the illness she denies? Does not the very coherence of her avoidance—her emotional investment in the cancellation—suggest that the belief in danger is present within the same reasoning system that produces her verbal avowals, rather than locked in a mere quarantine? Respond as the Defender, addressing my case directly and without invoking bibliographies, citations, or named contemporary authorities unless I introduce them first.
