I am working through arguments in comparative development and keep returning to the claim that Korea is the closest thing history offers to a natural experiment separating institutions from geography and culture. Analyze the 1945 division of the Korean peninsula and the subsequent divergence of North and South Korea as a case study in causal inference about national wealth, using the institutions-versus-geography-versus-culture framework.

First, lay out the experimental logic with precision: identify what is held constant across the two cases (pre-division history, ethnicity, language, climate, topography, and resource endowments) and what is treated differently (political and economic institutions, integration into global markets, exposure to the Korean War, and patterns of Soviet, Chinese, and American patronage). Then map the actual causal structure: who the decision-makers were under each occupation regime, what institutional options were genuinely available, and how outcomes diverged not only in GDP per capita but in state capacity and technological adoption. Next, identify where the quasi-experimental analogy breaks — compound treatments (security guarantees and aid flows that came bundled with institutional choices), spillovers across the 38th parallel that violate SUTVA at the national level, differential wartime destruction, and the problem of counterfactual stability over seven decades. Finally, say what this reveals about the limits of using history for causal inference in development: does the Korean case genuinely strengthen institutionalism, or does it show that any historical natural experiment is so contaminated by geopolitical contingency and compound treatments that it cannot settle the debate between institutions, geography, and culture?
