I was reading about that absent-minded driver puzzle — you know, the one where you can't tell if you're at exit 1 or exit 2 because the scenary is identical — and I tried to build a general argument from it, but now it feels like it proves way too much. Here's the chain I wrote down:

(1) In the driver problem, you reach an intersection and can't distinguish exit 1 (keep going) from exit 2 (get off) because your information set covers both nodes.

(2) Rationality requires maximizing expected utility over your information set, so you have to merge the nodes and choose a mixed strategy with some probablity p of exiting.

(3) Generalize: whenever an agent is in two physically possible situations that are subjectively indistinguishable, rational choice demands that same merged-strategy treatment.

(4) But in ordinary life I'm constantly in subjectively indistinguishable internal states — am I genuinely tired or just unmotivated? Did I lock the door or only imagine it? Is this headache serious or just dehydration?

(5) So by (3), every time I face an internal uncertainty with two possible explanations, I should randomize my response instead of committing to the best guess.

(6) That means rationality requires flipping coins over whether to see a doctor, stop working, or apologize, which is clearly absurd.

(7) Therefore (3) is false, and the driver result tells us nothing about rational choice in general.

But I don't want to just chuck the driver analysis, since it seems correct for that specific loop. Where exactly does the slide happen — is it an equivocation between (2) and (3), or is (4) smuggling in something that doesn't belong? I'd rather keep (2) than (5) if I have to choose one, but I want to know what I'm paying for it.
