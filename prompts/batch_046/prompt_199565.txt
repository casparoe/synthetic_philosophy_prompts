I'm a PhD student in philosophy of physics working on the ontology of spacetime, and I'm trying to sharpen an argument I've been developing against relational accounts of rotation. The argument starts with Newton's bucket, but I extend it with a hypothetical case—call it Universe I—that I think pushes the Machian reply into an incoherent position. I'm worried, though, that I've smuggled in a premise that looks innocent but actually proves too much, because if I'm right then not just Machian relationism but every relational theory of space collapses.

Here is the argument, step by step:

(1) Relationism holds that all spatial and kinematic facts supervene on relations among material bodies; there is no spacetime structure independent of matter.
(2) Newton's bucket shows that uniform rotation of water relative to the bucket produces concavity, an effect not explained by bucket-water relative motion alone. The canonical Machian response is that rotation is relative to the distant stars and the global matter distribution.
(3) Now construct Universe I: a region locally identical to the bucket experiment, but globally empty—no distant stars, no cosmic background, only the bucket, the water, and their local gravitational fields.
(4) In Universe I, the water is set into rotation. The local hydrodynamic equations include a Coriolis term tied to an angular-velocity vector. On the standard interpretation, this vector is defined with respect to a local inertial frame.
(5) Hence, even in Universe I, the water should develop concavity; the local dynamics are unchanged.
(6) But the relationist, committed to (1) and (2), has no distant bodies in Universe I to serve as the relata of rotation. Therefore the relationist must deny that there is any fact of the matter about whether the water is rotating, and must predict flat water.
(7) So relationism gives the wrong empirical prediction for Universe I. This shows that rotational inertia does not depend on distant matter, and that substantivalism about spacetime (or at least about inertial structure) is correct.
(8) The argument generalizes: in special and general relativity, rotational and linear acceleration are not categorically distinct—both represent deviation from geodesic motion. So if rotational inertia is intrinsic to local dynamics, linear inertia must be as well.
(9) Consequently, in an otherwise empty universe, a lone particle undergoing linear acceleration would still experience inertial resistance. But with no other bodies present, the relationist lacks any relata to ground that resistance.
(10) Thus the reasoning in (3)–(7) does not merely refute Machian relationism about rotation; it refutes every relational account of space by showing that isolated bodies could not possess dynamical properties at all—an absurd consequence.

My intuition is that the slide occurs somewhere between (4) and (8), perhaps in the assumption that the Coriolis term in (4) is defined absolutely rather than relationally, or perhaps in the generalization in (8). But I'm not entirely sure which step is the real culprit, and I want to understand the cost of rejecting each option.

Could you diagnose which step I should reject? Explain why that step seemed plausible, and then price the alternatives: what does a Machian relationist, a Leibnizian relationist, a Newtonian substantivalist, and a dynamical relationist (who holds that spacetime geometry is codified in the laws of motion rather than existing as an independent substrate) each have to pay to reject that step? In particular, I'm inclined to keep (5) and (7) if I can, but I'm open to giving either up if the cost is lower.

Please also say how confident you are in your diagnosis, and single out the specific point about which you are least certain—especially regarding whether the dynamical relationist can consistently deny (8) without also undermining the local law structure that makes (5) seem compelling.
