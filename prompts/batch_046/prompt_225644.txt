University curricula, museum accreditation criteria, and national examination syllabi routinely divide Western history into three successive blocks — the Middle Ages, the Renaissance, and the Scientific Revolution — assigning them canonical boundaries (commonly c. 500–1400, c. 1400–1600, and 1543–1687). Professional historians have long argued that these are retrospective constructions, yet the tripartite scheme remains firmly embedded in institutional practice. What philosophical theory of historical time and change does that practice presuppose? Must it assume that epochs possess essential natures and sharp discontinuities, or is it compatible with a fully gradualist, anti-essentialist historiography? Note, furthermore, that the same institutions typically present the Renaissance as a regionally diffused movement (originating in Italy, then spreading northward) while treating the Scientific Revolution as a synchronised, continent-wide rupture defined by publication dates. What implicit theory of cultural versus scientific change renders that double standard coherent, if any? Reconstruct the commitments the practice strictly requires, distinguish them from what it merely accommodates on pedagogical grounds, and describe what a viable, non-periodised curriculum or museum layout would have to redesign or abandon.
