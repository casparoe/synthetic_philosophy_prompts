After a rather exhausting argument last night with an old friend about whether being healthy is purely a biological fact or whether it inevitably smuggles in values, I realised I need something practical for my actual work. I sit on a regional clinical ethics and resource-allocation board that convenes every six weeks to advise whether newly identified conditions — biomarker clusters, behavioural profiles, or genetic variants — should be formally recognised as diseases for screening eligibility and funding. The proposals are increasing, and we have no reusable framework.

Please construct a decision procedure — an ordered sequence of questions with clear stopping rules — that any board member could run on a proposal without philosophical training. The procedure should preserve the core tension between naturalism (roughly, statistical deviation from species-typical biological functioning or harmful dysfunction) and normativism (roughly, impairment of an individual's ability to pursue vital goals or achieve well-being). It must specify: what counts as a sufficient answer at each stage; where the procedure terminates with a clear recommendation or refusal; where it must hand the decision to human judgement because the question is irreducibly one of values rather than facts; and where it is likely to misfire or yield an answer the board will dislike.

Crucially, build in a designated stage for what we have started calling "functionally ambiguous polymorphisms" — states that are statistically abnormal relative to the general population yet confer clear functional advantages within specific occupational or social niches, and where affected individuals report high subjective well-being despite objective biological deviation. The procedure should neither automatically dismiss these nor automatically classify them as healthy, but must flag the conflict between population-level biostatistics and niche-relative functioning for explicit resolution.

Apply the procedure to three cases: (1) a proposed screening programme for "pre-symptomatic metabolic dysregulation" in asymptomatic young adults, where biomarkers are abnormal but no subjective impairment exists and future risk is merely probabilistic; (2) "chronic social disengagement syndrome", where individuals show objective social-cognitive deficits and reduced reproductive fitness markers but consistently report high life satisfaction and reject the label of illness; and (3) a politically pressured proposal to classify severe occupational burnout as a disease, where there is clear biological dysregulation in cortisol and sleep architecture but also strong arguments that the dysfunction is primarily environmentally caused and socially defined. For one of these, the procedure should yield a recommendation that conflicts with what the board is likely to want, and you should explain exactly where the value-judgement hand-off occurs.

Finally, state explicitly what the procedure cannot do — for instance, whether it can resolve disputes about the "proper function" of mental states, or whether it eliminates all normative commitments — and present the whole answer in British English.
