As a novelist, I am working on a piece of philosophical fiction and need a short story—no more than eight hundred words—centered on a precisely framed variant of Molyneux's problem. The premise: a woman blind from birth, an accomplished ceramicist who has known every contour of her vessels solely through touch, receives an experimental cortical implant that restores sight not gradually but in a single calibrated flash. Immediately upon awakening, she is shown one of her own vases placed among three works by strangers. The narrative tension should emerge from an ambiguity I want left unresolved: during her blindness she wore a proprietary haptic-mapping glove that translated tactile data into geometric coordinates she could mentally visualize, so that when she now sees the vase, neither she nor the reader can determine whether her recognition is genuine cross-modal perception or a reconstructed inference mediated by years of prosthetic translation. Do not resolve this with an explicit philosophical verdict; instead, let the recognition scene—her hand reaching out, her eyes hesitating over the glaze—carry the entire weight of the question. Keep the tone restrained, sensory, and interior, with no bibliographic references, citations, or didactic explanations of the underlying debate. I want the concept embedded in the physical action and the uncertainty of her own judgment.
