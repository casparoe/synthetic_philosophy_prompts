Write an opening address—approximately 600 to 800 words—for the first session of an interdisciplinary workshop titled “Non-Human Forces in Historical Explanation.” The audience is deliberately mixed: professional historians, philosophers of science, environmental scientists, and graduate students. You are speaking as a philosopher of history; your job is to welcome the room, name the tension among them, and establish what the workshop can and cannot legitimately claim.

Your speech must do real structural work around the following idea: treating germs, abrupt climate anomalies, and volcanic events as “historical actors” is conceptually dangerous not because these forces are causally trivial—often they are overwhelming—but because the language of agency carries normative and explanatory baggage that easily slides into environmental determinism. At the same time, you must avoid the opposite error of treating these forces as mere “background conditions” that never reshape the space of human possibility.

To navigate this, construct and deploy an original conceptual distinction—call it the separation between causal prominence and narrative accountability. Causal prominence refers to the measurable, multi-factorial contribution of a non-human force to an outcome. Narrative accountability refers to the kind of explanatory role that supports moral or political judgment about human choices and institutional failures. Show, concretely, how conflating the two produces determinism (when we assign narrative accountability to a pathogen or an eruption) and anthropocentric blindness (when we deny causal prominence to climate shocks).

Ground the argument in one concrete but not overused case: the interaction between the 536 CE high-latitude volcanic winter—linked to the dust-veil event and subsequent cooling—and the Justinian Plague that followed within decades. Focus specifically on how historians debate whether the climate shock should be said to have weakened the Byzantine Empire’s administrative capacity to respond, or whether that framing illegitimately transfers explanatory labor away from imperial policy choices, tax structures, and military overextension. Use this case to illustrate the distinction in action.

The speech must remain accessible to non-philosophers in the room—translate every technical move into ordinary language—but it must also contain one genuinely precise philosophical maneuver that rewards the philosophers present: show that the determinism peril is not merely a moral worry but a category mistake in the logic of historical explanation, analogous to confusing a necessary condition with a sufficient cause within a multi-factorial model. Do not invoke named scholars unless you are certain of the attribution; paraphrase all historical claims without placing them in quotation marks.

Conclude with a precise delimitation: the workshop can map causal prominence with rigor, tracing how volcanic aerosols, pathogen transmission dynamics, or climate shifts alter the constraints within which human institutions operate. But it should resist the temptation to turn every climate shock or epidemic into a protagonist with intentions, motives, or a narrative arc. End by stating exactly what this refusal costs us—some dramatic explanatory simplicity—and why that cost is worth paying.
