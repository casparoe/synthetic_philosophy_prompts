I am a novelist preparing to defend the philosophical claim that structures the narrative logic of my forthcoming historical fiction. Specifically, I argue that Popper's classic anti-historicist argument — that there can be no universal laws of historical development — leaves untouched a class of what I call "meso-scale institutional regularities": robust but non-universal patterns in how bureaucratic structures fragment under sustained resource scarcity. I contend these regularities are sufficient to generate the deterministic texture of my plot without committing me to the kind of macro-historical prediction Popper rightly rejects. I am presenting this to an interdisciplinary philosophy of history seminar consisting of three committed Popperians for whom the anti-historicist case is largely settled, two philosophers of social science who are vigilant about determinism in formal modelling, and a literary critic who regards the importation of philosophy of science into narrative craft as a kind of methodological imperialism. Please draft my Q&A prep sheet: the eight or so objections I am actually likely to face, ordered by how damaging they would be, each phrased in the vocabulary and framing that members of this particular audience would use. For each objection, provide a reply I can deliver in roughly two minutes, together with an explicit note on where that reply is soft or concedes ground I would rather not concede. Include also the single question I am hoping nobody asks. Write in British English. Finally, please indicate, for the prep sheet as a whole, how confident you are in its predictions, and identify the specific point about which you are least sure.
