I am drafting the final exam for an advanced undergraduate seminar on consciousness and personal identity, and I need one 45-minute essay question on split-brain cases and the unity of consciousness that cannot be answered by reciting the standard Sperry and Gazzaniga findings. The question should present a conceptually rigorous hypothetical case: a patient with a partial corpus callosum lesion that fully preserves tactile, proprioceptive, and affective integration between hemispheres, but completely severs visual and linguistic interhemispheric pathways. The result is that the left hemisphere can verbally report and reason about actions of the right hand, but cannot access or report on stimuli presented to the left visual field, while both hemispheres share a unified body schema and emotional valence system.

Students should be required to evaluate whether this patient possesses one consciousness or two, and specifically to adjudicate between three criteria for unity: (1) phenomenological co-presence (all experiences belong to a single subject of awareness); (2) functional access (mutual availability of mental contents for reasoning, decision, and control); and (3) narrative integration (a single, continuous autobiographical self-model). The crux is that this partial-integration case puts pressure on each criterion in different directions—shared body schema might support phenomenological unity, split visual-linguistic access undermines functional unity, and narrative coherence may fall somewhere in between.

The prompt must require students to do more than list these criteria. They should have to argue whether functional access can be graded rather than all-or-nothing, whether shared lower-level integration is sufficient for phenomenological unity, and whether narrative integration can be fragmented even when affective integration remains intact. They must also address a specific conceptual divergence: if graded unity is permitted, then “one person” may come apart from “one stream of consciousness,” and they must take a reasoned stance on whether this divergence is a reductio of the graded view or an acceptable consequence.

Please provide:
1. The essay question, calibrated for approximately 800–1000 words in 45 minutes.
2. A model answer of about 700 words.
3. A marking scheme built around specific required conceptual moves, with total marks out of 20. The scheme must clearly separate an A answer from a B answer by naming the exact moves each must contain—for instance, identifying the tension between shared proprioception and split visual-linguistic access; arguing for or against graded unity with a substantive reason rather than bare assertion; and addressing the person/consciousness divergence explicitly. List specific mistakes that should cost marks, such as treating the case as identical to a full split-brain, conflating functional access with phenomenological unity without argument, or ignoring the narrative criterion entirely. Assign points to each required move. Do not ask for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists.
