I'm working through Plato's Republic in a seminar on moral motivation and have become stuck on the shepherd's ring in Glaucon's challenge. The narrative describes an ancestor of Gyges who, while tending flocks, discovers a bronze horse containing a corpse that wears a golden ring; once put on, the ring renders its bearer invisible. The shepherd uses this power to enter the royal palace, kill the king, seduce the queen, and claim the throne. Glaucon then invites us to imagine two such rings distributed to a man widely regarded as just and to one widely regarded as unjust, predicting that both would behave identically—seizing wealth, power, and pleasure without restraint—since, as he suggests, people observe justice only for the rewards of reputation and the avoidance of punishment, not for any intrinsic value it holds.

My difficulty lies not with the fable itself but with a conceptual tension in how Glaucon moves from this scenario to his conclusion about human nature. If moral conduct is genuinely instrumental—motivated solely by anticipated external sanctions—then the just individual who receives the ring should feel no internal struggle whatsoever, because there are no sanctions left to anticipate. Yet Glaucon presents the just man's collapse into injustice as a revealing failure, as though some internal commitment had been betrayed. This makes me wonder whether his argument tacitly assumes that people possess an innate desire to maintain a self-image of justice even in private, or whether I am misreading the structure of the challenge. Specifically, I am considering an interpretation in which the ring does not merely hide the wearer from observers but dissolves the social framework within which the very concept of justice operates, so that the wearer is not violating an internal norm but rather inhabiting a condition where normative categories cease to apply. I want to know whether this reading can be sustained by the text, or whether it anachronistically imports a modern distinction between internal and external motivation that Glaucon's argument does not actually require.
