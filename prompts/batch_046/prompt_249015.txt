Consider the institutional pairing common in many jurisdictions: hospitals and physicians are required by law to stabilize patients in emergency conditions regardless of ability to pay, while ordinary citizens bear no comparable legal obligation to rescue strangers from serious harm at minimal personal risk. What conception of moral demandingness does this asymmetry presuppose? Does it imply that the stringency of an obligation depends on institutional role rather than on the moral urgency of the need itself, thereby licensing a division between stringent professional duties and weak universal ones? Or could a consequentialist defend the asymmetry on purely instrumental grounds—arguing that centralized mandates are more reliable than diffuse individual duties—without conceding that individuals may permissibly ignore comparable needs? Assess whether this practice is philosophically consistent with the consequentialist claim that demandingness objections fail because morality does not require extreme individual sacrifice, or whether it quietly relies on a non-consequentialist distinction between obligatory and supererogatory action.
