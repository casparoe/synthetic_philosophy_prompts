As a retired physicist who has only recently begun working through philosophy of language in any depth, I want to defend a particular reading of Quine’s indeterminacy thesis—the claim that for any consistent body of behavioral evidence there can exist translation manuals that are mutually incompatible yet empirically equivalent. Rather than treating this as a skeptical limit on our access to hidden mental meanings, I propose we understand it on the model of gauge freedom in physics: different translation manuals are like different coordinate choices describing the same underlying linguistic-behavioral manifold. On this view, “meaning” is not a determinate mental property that one manual correctly captures and another misses; it is a relational invariant that remains constant across the gauge orbit of acceptable translations, fixed only relative to background conditions of shared practice that are themselves part of the gauge-fixing framework.

What would have to be true for this “gauge” interpretation of Quine to fail? I want an honest map of its exposure. Which assumptions in Quine’s own argument—the indeterminacy of reference, the underdetermination of theory by evidence, the collapse of the analytic-synthetic distinction—would need to break, or be substantially revised, for the analogy to collapse? In particular, could there be a “preferred gauge” anchored in neural architecture, evolutionary function, or the internal causal structure of a speaker’s conceptual scheme that breaks the symmetry and makes one manual genuinely correct rather than conventionally selected? If such an anchor existed, would it restore a substantive, non-relational notion of meaning that Quine rejects, or could a defender of the gauge view absorb it by treating the anchor itself as part of the gauge-fixing conditions?

Please distinguish decisive defeaters—an empirical result, a formal proof, or a conceptual contradiction that the gauge view cannot accommodate—from merely awkward consequences, such as the intuition that we sometimes “just know” what a foreign speaker means. Also say what defenders of this position, Quine included, would themselves concede as genuinely damaging, not merely what critics claim. I am especially interested in whether the physics analogy is doing real philosophical work: if linguistic interpretation lacks the mathematical structure—well-defined symmetry groups, conserved quantities, differential constraints—that makes gauge freedom physically meaningful, does the position survive in a weaker form, or does it reduce to ordinary skepticism about other minds? Finally, if the view is well insulated against defeat, say so explicitly, and whether that insulation is a virtue or a vice.
