I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, but in reverse-engineering undocumented APIs I keep encountering the same interpretive puzzle. A system emits messages that are consistently false under standard terminology—say, every "success" flag actually correlates with failure conditions—yet the misalignment is perfectly regular, not random noise. If I apply the usual "charity" assumption that the speaker is mostly rational and shares my categories, I have to conclude the system is catastrophically broken. But if I instead attribute an internally consistent alien taxonomy, the messages become rational, though this requires assuming a conceptual scheme very different from the one the system was originally trained on. I want to know whether philosophy of language has a specific name for this exact tension: a case where charitable interpretation pulls not toward forgiving occasional errors within a shared framework, but toward positing a radically different framework altogether, especially when there is historical or design evidence that the speaker once operated within the standard framework. If there is an established concept, how does it differ from ordinary equivocation, from Quinean indeterminacy of translation, and from a simple failure of the principle of charity? If there is no established term, can you coin one, define it precisely, specify its boundary conditions, and explain what makes it distinct from the nearby cases I mentioned? I am not looking for a bibliography—just the conceptual carving.
