I'm in a graduate seminar on consciousness studies and have been arguing with a study-group friend who reads a lot of neuroscience blogs and rationalist forums. He summarized illusionism about phenomenal consciousness for us like this: "Illusionism holds that phenomenal consciousness is a cognitive illusion produced by introspective mechanisms that misrepresent brain states as possessing intrinsic qualitative properties. Illusionists don't deny beliefs about experience or information processing, but they do deny that phenomenal properties—qualia—are instantiated anywhere. Because neuroscience demonstrates that introspection is constructed and fallible, reports such as 'it feels like something to see red' are literally false descriptions of our mental lives, not just inaccurate descriptions of neural dynamics. As a result, illusionism collapses into eliminativism: if phenomenal properties don't exist, then consciousness, defined by their presence, doesn't exist either, leaving only unconscious information processing."

Something about this feels off, especially the slide from "illusion" to "eliminativism" and the way empirical neuroscience is made to carry the entire argument. But my friend insists this is the standard view among philosophers like Frankish and Dennett. Could you audit this summary? In particular, please separate what is accurate about the illusionist position, what is a defensible simplification, and what an illusionist would actively reject—especially regarding whether illusionists treat phenomenal reports as literally false, whether they deny all consciousness or only a specific qualitative kind, and whether their core argument is primarily empirical or conceptual. I'd like to understand whether the summary's conclusion that illusionism is just eliminativism in disguise survives correction, and I don't need any bibliography or reading list to follow the explanation.
