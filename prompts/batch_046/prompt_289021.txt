I am a retired experimental physicist who, since leaving the laboratory, have turned my attention seriously to philosophy of science, and I have become particularly interested in Karl Popper’s concept of verisimilitude—the thesis that scientific progress consists not in asymptotically approaching absolute truth, but in replacing false theories with rival theories that lie closer to the true state of affairs. I wish to apply this framework to the well-known episode of March 1989, when Fleischmann and Pons announced the achievement of tabletop nuclear fusion in palladium-deuterium electrochemical cells, and to the rapid, widespread failure of independent laboratories to replicate their calorimetric excess-heat claims over the following eighteen months.

Treating the cold-fusion hypothesis as a false theory—which it evidently was, given the persistent absence of expected fusion byproducts and the collapse of reproducibility—I want to understand whether Popper’s framework permits a meaningful comparison of its verisimilitude with that of the conventional hot-fusion model it sought to supplement. Specifically: did the Fleischmann-Pons claim possess any partial, local verisimilitude in virtue of having correctly identified an anomalous heat source—perhaps of electrochemical rather than nuclear origin—while being radically false about the underlying mechanism? Or does the falsity of the core mechanism render any subsidiary empirical accuracy irrelevant to the theory’s overall proximity to truth?

Moreover, the scientific community’s rejection of cold fusion seemed to turn less on the identification of an immediately available, closer-to-truth competitor, and more on the breakdown of reproducibility and the absence of characteristic nuclear signatures. Does this indicate that verisimilitude is the wrong metric for understanding the rejection, or does it rather suggest that reproducibility failures are themselves reliable indicators of low verisimilitude? I am also struck by the subsequent fringe literature that persisted in claiming partial experimental successes—does this represent a rational disagreement about degrees of verisimilitude, or a conceptual failure to apply the notion altogether?

Please analyze the episode through the lens of verisimilitude, indicating precisely where the theory illuminates the reasoning of the actors involved and where it fails to capture the actual dynamics of scientific rejection. In particular, address whether verisimilitude can be applied locally—to discrete sub-components or predictive domains of a theory—without being applied globally, and whether the episode ultimately supports or undermines Popper’s claim that science progresses by increasing verisimilitude rather than by securing empirical adequacy alone. I would also ask what the case predicts about a theory that is globally false yet makes one surprisingly accurate quantitative prediction in an unexpected domain—should we treat that prediction as evidence of hidden verisimilitude, or as an isolated coincidence that the framework should ignore?
