As a retired physicist who has only recently begun studying philosophy in earnest, I have been working through Dostoevsky's parable of the Grand Inquisitor in The Brothers Karamazov, particularly Ivan's claim that human beings will systematically surrender freedom in exchange for security, miracle, and authority. I would like you to read Aldous Huxley's Brave New World through the lens of that argument, treating the novel not as a mere illustration but as a serious interlocutor. Specifically, I want to know how the World State's instruments—soma, the feelies, Bokanovsky's Process, and the engineered abolition of suffering—map onto the Inquisitor's triad of miracle, mystery, and authority, and where the analogy breaks down because Huxley's regime is secular, scientific, and administrative rather than sacramental. Does Mustapha Mond operate as an Inquisitor who has replaced theology with social engineering, and does the confrontation between Mond and John in the lighthouse scene vindicate the value of freedom, or does John's ultimate withdrawal merely confirm Ivan's darker thesis that freedom is unbearable once happiness has been chemically assured? Please ground your reading in well-known scenes—such as Mond's justification for banning Shakespeare, the distribution of soma as a substitute for religious consolation, and Lenina's inability to comprehend John's rejection of comfort—while avoiding any reduction of the novel to a philosophical diagram. Finally, I am curious whether Huxley's technological vision adds something to the debate about freedom and happiness that Dostoevsky's explicitly religious framing cannot capture, especially regarding whether scientific governance solves the problem of human discontent more thoroughly than the Church ever could. I am preparing this for a discussion group of scientists turned humanists, so I want the analysis to respect both the literary texture of the novel and the conceptual rigor of the Inquisitor's challenge.
