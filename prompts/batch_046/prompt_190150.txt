In the course of preparing for a seminar on rational choice and self-reference, I have become preoccupied with two puzzles that sit at the boundary of decision theory and the metaphysics of agency. The first, often discussed under the heading of the Platonia dilemma, involves two agents who share identical reasoning capacities, complete information, and logical transparency: each must decide whether to pursue a dominant but mutually destructive strategy or a subordinate but mutually safe one, knowing that the other is running the exact same computation. The second is the so-called Luring Lottery, a superrationality experiment in which agents are rewarded for selecting unique values within a shared range, yet precisely because they reason identically they tend toward convergence on the same value, thereby defeating the reward structure. A colleague in my study circle has begun using a single analogy to cover both cases, and I find it suspiciously convenient.

She claims that facing either puzzle is structurally identical to two drivers playing chicken in cars with mechanically linked steering wheels: both can swerve or stay the course, both can see themselves in the other, and rationality demands swerving because the crash is symmetric and avoidable. She uses this to license the conclusion that superrational agents should always converge on cooperative or safe outcomes, since the other is just a mirror and coordination is therefore trivial. I want you to audit this analogy systematically. Provide an explicit correspondence table mapping elements of the chicken game onto the Platonia dilemma and onto the Luring Lottery separately—identifying what plays the role of the linked steering, the swerve option, the crash, the visual identification with the opponent, and the temporal dynamics of observation. Then isolate the disanalogies that matter for her conclusion: does the Platonia dilemma actually contain a swerve that is rationally available once dominance is calculated? In the Luring Lottery, is there any analog to swerving, given that uniqueness—not cooperation—is the reward condition, and convergence is the failure mode? Examine whether the analogy quietly imports an asymmetry of real-time observation or a shared preference for survival that these simultaneous, formal-choice structures do not contain. Finally, assess whether her conclusion—that rational symmetry always resolves into safe coordination—survives once the mapping is corrected, or whether it collapses once the analogy's hidden machinery is removed. If a different framework—perhaps drawn from distributed computation or mirror-game theory—better captures the logic of identical reasoning under these reward structures, propose it and explain precisely what the chicken frame obscures.
