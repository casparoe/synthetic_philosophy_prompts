Write a dialogue between two philosophers of science—call them Dr. Arden and Dr. Baines—who disagree about whether Milton Friedman’s “F-twist” successfully defends unrealistic assumptions in scientific models. Arden holds that predictive accuracy is the primary, perhaps sole, criterion for evaluating a model, so descriptively false assumptions are harmless if the model predicts well. Baines is skeptical: she introduces a distinction between “representational unrealisticness,” in which an assumption misdescribes the actual causal structure of the domain, and “computational unrealisticness,” in which an assumption merely simplifies calculation without claiming to represent the world. Baines argues that Friedman’s defense applies only to the latter, and she applies this distinction to a hypothetical behavioral model of market dynamics that assumes agents possess perfect foresight and infinite working memory. Arden should push back by arguing that the distinction collapses—every simplifying assumption is representational at some level of description—and that demanding realistic microfoundations paralyzes modeling. Baines should counter that without this distinction, model failure becomes uninformative: a predictive miss tells us nothing about whether to revise the core assumption or only an auxiliary condition. Keep the exchange tightly focused on this specific hypothetical model and on the conceptual distinction itself; neither character should be a strawman. Each should respond directly to the other’s previous point rather than changing the subject, and the dialogue should end without a forced resolution.
