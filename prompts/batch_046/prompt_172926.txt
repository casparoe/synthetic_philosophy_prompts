In evolutionary biology, the adaptationist program and the Gould–Lewontin spandrels critique have remained in tension since the late 1970s, yet the dispute rarely advances to resolution. Adaptationists treat natural selection as the primary architect of organismal form, insisting that any persistent trait should be tested against an optimality or engineering standard; critics counter that many features are non-adaptive byproducts—spandrels—of developmental architecture, structural constraints, or genetic drift, and that the burden of proof ought to rest on demonstrating adaptation rather than presuming it. Despite decades of empirical refinement in comparative methods and evo-devo, the debate feels curiously static: both camps concede that genuine adaptations and genuine spandrels exist, yet they continue to talk past each other about specific cases—such as the evolutionary origins of language-ready cognition or the modular structure of the brain—without any shared criterion for what would actually settle the matter.

My working hypothesis is that this is not chiefly an empirical stalemate, but a conceptual disagreement about the ontology of biological function, masked by shared vocabulary. The adaptationist typically asks a teleological question: what selective advantage could have shaped this trait? The spandrels critic typically asks a mechanistic question: could this trait have arisen without selection, as an unavoidable consequence of other selected features or of deep developmental bias? These are different explanatory targets—selected-effect functions versus causal-role functions—yet both sides present their answers as if they were competing explanations for the same phenomenon. Is this diagnosis accurate? Or is there a hidden empirical disagreement—perhaps about the statistical prevalence of developmental constraint, or about whether comparative phylogenetic methods can actually distinguish selection from drift at the required resolution—that genuinely drives the impasse?

If the disagreement is indeed conceptual rather than factual, what kind of progress should we expect? Would a rigorous formal distinction between “selected effect” and “causal role” dissolve the conflict, or is the stall deeper—rooted in incompatible intuitions about whether evolutionary explanations must always be framed as optimality narratives? And if the impasse is fundamentally about which question is being asked, does that render the debate ill-posed, or does it simply mean the field must bifurcate its research programs rather than seek a unified verdict? I want a diagnosis supported by what each side actually claims, together with an honest assessment of whether anything—a conceptual distinction, a formal result, or a change of framing—could actually move it.
