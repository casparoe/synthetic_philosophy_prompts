At a workshop on decision theory and long-term risk, two colleagues—Maya and Jonas—fell into a sustained argument about how a fixed philanthropic budget should be allocated. Maya insisted that the only coherent framework is pure expected-value maximization: Project A offers roughly a one-in-ten-thousand chance of preventing an existential catastrophe that would claim eight billion lives, yielding about 800,000 expected lives saved, whereas Project B will certainly save roughly 800 lives through direct malaria interventions. She maintained that anyone who refuses to fund Project A is either making a mathematical error or disguising emotional risk aversion as rationality. Jonas countered that treating a 0.01 percent probability as linearly commensurable with near-certainty ignores the epistemic structure of rational agency—there is a meaningful difference between acting to prevent a harm one can model and verify, and speculating on a singular, poorly constrained hypothetical. Maya replied that Jonas was smuggling in an unjustified discontinuity: if he truly rejected linear scaling, he should also deny that saving eight billion lives with certainty is ten thousand times more valuable than saving 800,000 with certainty, yet he clearly accepts that ordering. Jonas then argued that the issue is not linearity but actionability—rational agents must apply a filter that excludes hypotheses they have no independent means to discriminate from fiction, otherwise every decision collapses into paralysis over infinitely many low-probability catastrophes. Maya asked whether that filter was anything more than a psychological comfort zone, noting that by Jonas's logic no one should ever purchase insurance for risks with claim probabilities below one percent, since each policy is essentially a bet on a singular low-probability event. Jonas responded that insurance markets function through aggregation and law-of-large-numbers pooling, which is precisely the structural feature missing from a one-off philanthropic wager on an unobservable existential scenario. They turned to me, the bystander who had been taking notes, and asked me to referee.

Please go through their exchange point by point and say who is right about each specific claim: the arithmetic comparison of expected lives, the charge of hidden risk aversion, the appeal to linear scaling consistency, the actionability filter and its relation to decision paralysis, and the insurance-market analogy. Say what, if anything, both parties are missing—particularly regarding whether Maya's position genuinely commits her to funding only the longest-shot bets, and whether Jonas's filter can be principled rather than arbitrary. Finally, indicate who is more right overall, and whether either has pushed their view into a form that risks unfalsifiability or practical irrelevance.
