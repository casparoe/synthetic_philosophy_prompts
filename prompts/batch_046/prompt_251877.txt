Consider the philosophical landscape surrounding the direction of time and the thermodynamic arrow of entropy increase. The standard debates—whether the arrow requires a cosmological past hypothesis, whether it is conventional with respect to coarse-graining, whether it grounds the epistemic asymmetry between past and future, and whether it has any bearing on the metaphysics of temporal passage—are well known, but the dependency relations among them are under-mapped, and the practical payoff of resolving any particular cluster is rarely assessed.

I want an agenda-setting defense, not a survey. Imagine a small interdisciplinary group—two philosophers of physics, one metaphysician, and one decision theorist—has three years, no obligation to publish in physics journals, and no access to proprietary cosmological data. They can work only on conceptual clarification, formal modeling, and argument. How should they divide their effort among the following four clusters?

(i) The explanatory cluster: whether the arrow is ultimately grounded in a global boundary condition (the past hypothesis), whether local dynamics can recover it without such a condition, and whether the choice of macro-state partition is physically substantive or pragmatically conventional.

(ii) The epistemic cluster: whether our knowledge of the past and predictive ignorance of the future are explained by the thermodynamic arrow, whether the explanatory dependence runs in reverse, or whether both depend on a common prior condition.

(iii) The metaphysical cluster: whether the arrow supports an A-theoretic view of temporal passage, whether it is fully compatible with a static B-theoretic block universe, and whether entropy gradients provide any metaphysically robust sense in which the future is “open.”

(iv) The normative-practical cluster: whether the thermodynamic arrow provides any justification for long-term planning, for rational discounting practices, or for moral concern directed specifically toward distant future generations—what I will call a “scaffolding arrow” that licenses practical asymmetry rather than explaining dynamic asymmetry.

To frame your answer, employ the following niche distinction: separate “grounding arrows” (those that explain why physical processes are temporally asymmetric) from “scaffolding arrows” (those that justify why rational agents should treat the past and future asymmetrically in belief and action). Use this distinction to structure your prioritization: ask not only which cluster is true, but which one, if resolved, would most alter how the others should be pursued.

For each cluster, defend its rank by addressing: (a) tractability for philosophers without cutting-edge empirical tools; (b) upstream dependency—how many of the other clusters depend on its resolution; (c) whether progress here would change anything outside academic philosophy, particularly in long-termist ethics, institutional design, or the philosophy of memory; and (d) what specific result over the next three years—say, a formal model of local entropy recovery, a conceptual proof that the epistemic arrow is independent, or an argument that the scaffolding arrow collapses into decision-theoretic considerations—would cause you to move it up or down.

Be explicit about the criteria governing your ordering (e.g., upstream dependency versus tractability versus practical payoff), and identify the trade-offs: for instance, if resolving (i) requires adjudicating cosmological models that philosophers cannot evaluate, does that disqualify it as a poor use of the group’s time, or is it the crux on which everything else hangs? Also identify one well-known puzzle in this area—perhaps one involving Boltzmann brains or the reversibility objection—that looks important but is likely a distraction relative to its downstream consequences for the other clusters.

Your deliverable should be a defended three-year agenda, specifying where the group should start, where they should defer effort, and what evidence or argument would cause them to reorder. Do not provide a bibliography, a reading list, or citation requests.
