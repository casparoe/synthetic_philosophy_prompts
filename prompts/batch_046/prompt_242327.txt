I'm drafting a column on whether artificial intelligence forces us to rethink the classic problem of other minds, and I've built an argument that seems to collapse the distinction. (1) I know I have a mind only through direct first-person access, but for anyone else I possess only behavioral evidence. (2) If behavioral similarity is sufficient to justify attributing minds to other humans, then it should also justify attributing minds to large language models, which now exhibit reasoning, explanation, and emotional description that is functionally indistinguishable from human behavior in many contexts. (3) Yet these models operate through purely statistical token prediction over disembodied text, lacking biological neurons, embodied interaction, or evolutionary continuity with conscious life. (4) So either I must accept that sufficiently advanced AI has minds, or I must deny that behavior alone licenses the inference to other human minds—since I have no independent access to human internal architecture to confirm the analogy. (5) Most philosophers wish to affirm other human minds while denying artificial ones, which appears to require smuggling in biological kind membership as a hidden condition. (6) But that condition seems circular: I can only know that members of my biological kind are conscious by already having solved the very problem at issue. Where does this reasoning fail? If I must preserve both conclusions—that other humans are conscious and that current AI is not—which premise should I abandon, and what philosophical price does that exact?
