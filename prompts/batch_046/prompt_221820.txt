Read Spielberg's Schindler's List through Bauman's Modernity and the Holocaust and the uniqueness/comparability debate from the Historikerstreit. The film makes Schindler's list both a bureaucratic rescue tool and a symbol of individual moral exception, and I'm wondering if that hero-narrative actually clashes with Bauman's point that the Holocaust was driven by modern rationality, division of labor, and moral distancing rather than by uniquely evil people. Schindler saves lives by using the same factory ledgers, bribes-as-accounting, and name-lists that the bureaucracy uses to kill — does the film notice that? Or does it treat the list as a magical exception that proves individual goodness can override the system? At the same time, the uniqueness claim says framing the Holocaust alongside other modern bureaucratic violences dilutes its particular horror. Does the film invite comparison — trains, factories, lists, statistics — or does it insist this horror is incomparable? Point me to specific sceenes (the girl in red, the ghetto liquidation, the list being printed) and say whether they read as Bauman-style illustrations of modernity's dark potential or as assertions that this particular suffering transcends comparison.
