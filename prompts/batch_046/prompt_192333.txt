I am preparing interview questions for a philosopher of medicine who defends a position she calls calibrated dysfunction normativism. According to this view, a state counts as pathological only when two conditions are jointly satisfied: first, there is an objective, statistically atypical dysfunction in a biological mechanism in the sense developed by biostatistical theories; second, that dysfunction falls below a context-sensitive threshold of what she terms the agential baseline, which is not fixed by evolutionary design but is periodically revised by what she describes as epistemic communities of clinical practice. She maintains that this hybrid avoids the value-ladenness problems facing pure naturalism and the relativism facing pure normativism, though critics charge that making the baseline socially revisable collapses her view into conventionalism.

Please draft seven interview questions that probe the internal structure of this position rather than her biography or career. At least one question should press her on how the view distinguishes genuinely pathological conditions from socially stigmatized variations—such as certain neurodivergent profiles or intersex traits—without merely deferring to the clinical community's present consensus, and it should include a follow-up keyed to her most likely appeal to democratic revision of the baseline, specifically asking what institutional safeguards prevent majoritarian or historically contingent bias from defining disease. At least one other question should address the evolutionary component: ask whether a statistically typical but severely impairing condition—such as progressive age-related cognitive decline—can qualify as a disease on her account if the clinical community has not calibrated the agential baseline downward for that domain, and include a follow-up that anticipates her reply that evolutionary dysfunction is necessary but not sufficient, pressing her on conditions like chronic pain syndromes that lack a clear evolutionary module yet clearly impair agency. The remaining questions should examine the coherence of jointly requiring biological dysfunction and socially calibrated normative failure—particularly whether the two criteria can come apart in ways that generate indeterminacy, whether the view can accommodate cross-cultural disagreement about the baseline without fragmenting the concept of disease, and what empirical or conceptual evidence could motivate revising the baseline downward for a condition currently treated as non-pathological. Frame each question as a direct prompt suitable for spoken dialogue, with the follow-up clearly paired.
