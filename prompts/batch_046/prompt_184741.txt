I just finished a long, somewhat heated conversation with a friend about Tolstoy's later theory of art—specifically his idea that genuine art operates like a contagious feeling, carrying the artist's sincere emotional state into the audience in a way that bridges social division, together with his dismissal of much professional and aristocratic production as either false feeling or mere sensory decoration. My friend insisted that this framework collapses once the audience no longer shares the moral or religious horizon that makes the feeling intelligible; I countered that Tolstoy's own Christian-anarchist repudiation of his earlier novels shows he understood this risk, yet never resolved whether an artist can remain sincere about a conviction she now holds only as memory rather than living belief. We circled back repeatedly to the case of religious art preserved in museums—whether restoration and display inevitably convert revelation into heritage, and whether the artist's present belief or only her honest recollection of belief is what Tolstoy's criterion of sincerity actually demands.

Write a short story that takes this tension into its bones. Set the narrative in a small, deconsecrated chapel on the edge of a northern town, where an aging fresco painter—once a novice monk, now a state conservator—has been commissioned to repair a severely damaged Last Judgment on the apse wall. The museum board wants the restored faces softened and the demons made almost ornamental, so that visitors feel a pleasant historical awe rather than genuine moral terror; the painter remembers Tolstoy's distinction between art that truly infects and art that merely flatters, and she suspects her own belief in the judgment she is painting has thinned to something like nostalgic performance. Let the argument with the friend echo through her work—perhaps in unsent letters to an old colleague, or in quiet disagreements with a younger restorer who treats all sacred imagery as cultural documentation. The story should ask, without resolving neatly, whether sincerity in Tolstoy's sense requires the artist's present conviction or only her truthful memory of conviction, and whether an audience that receives the image as heritage rather than revelation makes the infection impossible by definition. Give the setting concrete sensory weight—the smell of lime plaster and wet stone, the particular cold of the chapel in winter, the way winter light strikes the scaffold at different hours—so that the philosophical problem is borne by physical detail rather than by dialogue alone.
