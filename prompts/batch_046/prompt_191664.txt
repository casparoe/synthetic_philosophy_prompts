I am a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, currently reading an early twentieth-century ethical work by G. E. Moore in which he evaluates what he takes to be the greatest intrinsic goods. In one section, Moore considers personal affection and the appreciation of beautiful objects, arguing that these are valuable not as instruments for producing pleasure but as ends in themselves. He describes such states as organic unities: the value, he suggests, belongs to the whole composed of consciousness, emotional response, and the object appreciated, and this value exceeds what one could derive by adding up the values of the separate parts. He uses this idea to resist hedonistic theories that reduce all good to pleasure.

My difficulty is in determining exactly what kind of claim this is. From my own work with optimization and feature representation, I am tempted to interpret the organic unity language as a claim about emergent properties in complex systems—something like saying that friendship or aesthetic experience generates value through interactions among components in a way that cannot be factorized into independent, linearly weighted contributions. But I worry I may be importing a conceptual framework that does not belong in Moore's argument. Is he advancing a metaphysical thesis about the composition of value, analogous to emergent behavior in distributed architectures? Or is he making a simpler normative point—that rational agents have direct intuitive access to the fact that these wholes are good—while using the organic unity metaphor only to block a reductive accounting?

Please help me understand the passage precisely. How should I read the relationship between these goods and pleasure? Does the concept of organic unity do substantive philosophical work, such as ruling out decompositional analyses of value, or is it primarily a rhetorical device defending the autonomy of friendship and beauty from hedonistic reduction? I would appreciate clarification on what Moore actually requires of our value judgments, especially if one attempts to model preferences without collapsing them into utility functions over pleasure states.
