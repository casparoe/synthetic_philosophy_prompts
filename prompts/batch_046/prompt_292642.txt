So I'm a retired cell biologist—spent thirty years staring at ion channels, now I'm trying to read philosophy of mind and it's like drinking from a firehose. There's this analogy I keep running into about Libet's readiness-potential experiments: people say the brain is like a factory assembly line, where the unconscious machinery builds the decision first and consciousness is just the quality-control inspector who stamps "approved" after the product is already made. The conclusion they draw is that free will is basically a illusion, since the RP starts firing before we feel we've decided.

But coming from a lab background, this feels like it might be conflating a preparatory potential with an execution signal. In electrophysiology, you see all kinds of background depolarization that don't necessarily commit the cell to firing. So I'm wondering: in this factory analogy, what exactly correspondances to the RP, what correspondances to the conscious veto Libet mentioned, and what correspondances to the actual muscle movment? Where does the analogy smuggle in the assumption that every pre-conscious neural blip is already a hidden decision? And if the RP is more like an engine idling—getting the system ready without locking in the turn—does the whole "inspector comes too late" story collapse?

I'd like someone to audit the mapping explicitly: table of correspondences if that helps, then separate the disanalogies that actually threaten the anti-free-will conclusion from the ones that are just cosmetic. Does Libet's veto idea turn consciousness into more of a brake pedal than an inspector, and if so, is there a better analogy that captures that? And honestly, does any version of this factory picture still license the strong claim that we don't cause our actions, once the broken parts are on the table?
