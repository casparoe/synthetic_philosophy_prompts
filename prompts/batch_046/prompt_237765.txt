I am preparing the closing session of a graduate seminar on the philosophy of history, and I want a poem that functions as a conceptual exercise rather than mere decoration. Write a villanelle—exactly nineteen lines, composed of five tercets followed by one quatrain, obeying the traditional rules for its two refrains—on the tension between historical inevitability and counterfactual contingency. The first refrain should state the inevitabilist claim: that large-scale historical structures are counterfactually robust, so that perturbing individual causal nodes does not alter the macro-trajectory. The second refrain should state the contingency claim: that history is path-dependent, with genuinely open branches at points of divergence that remain live possibilities rather than closed illusions. Inject the philosophical idea of counterfactual asymmetry—that causal dependency is traceable backward from an outcome to its conditions, but cannot be projected forward with equivalent confidence—and have the poem's architecture reflect it: place the inevitability refrain in lines that look retrospectively, and the contingency refrain in lines that look prospectively. In the closing quatrain, the two refrains must appear together side by side without being reconciled; the tension should remain structurally unresolved. Render the positions with enough precision that someone acquainted with debates about modal fragility, counterfactual historiography, and the logic of counterfactual dependence will recognize the arguments, but do not ask for named scholars, theories, or texts, and do not include any verbatim quotations from real works. The poem should be serious and accurate enough to be read aloud to philosophers without embarrassment, yet it should stand on its own for anyone who has never read the literature. Write in American English. End the response with a line that reads exactly "Summary:" followed by a single sentence summarizing the central tension the poem leaves open.
