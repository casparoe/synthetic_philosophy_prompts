The problem of old evidence in Bayesian epistemology has been stuck in roughly the same shape for decades: if confirmation is an increase in credence upon conditioning on evidence, then evidence already known—Mercury’s perihelion shift prior to Einstein’s formulation of general relativity, or the cosmic microwave background prior to refined cosmological modeling—seems unable to confirm any theory, yet we routinely treat such accommodations as powerful confirmations. One camp responds with counterfactual conditioning, asking what an agent’s credence in the theory would have been absent prior knowledge of the evidence; the other camp denies that old evidence requires special treatment, insisting that retrospective explanatory coherence, prior entrenchment, or structural probability adjustments do all the work, so that “confirmation” in these cases is either misapplied or purely atemporal. Both sides have developed sophisticated formal machinery—counterfactual probability spaces on one hand, coherence measures and belief-revision dynamics on the other—yet practitioners in adjacent fields still invoke old evidence without settling which framework governs it, and each formal refinement is met by an equally refined reply.

My working hypothesis is that this is not primarily a disagreement about the calculus of probabilities but about what normative role “confirmation” plays: is it a diachronic rule governing rational belief revision, or an atemporal relation of evidential support between propositions? But I want to push past that framing with a specific conceptual twist. Consider a case in which a theory predicts not merely that old evidence E obtains, but that E’s discovery was structurally redundant—had the theory been false, the same background theories and observational capacities that led us to discover E would have made E either surprising or unobservable, so that knowing both E and the conditions of its discovery jointly constrains the prior in a way that single-event conditioning cannot capture. Call this predictive redundancy in the discovery pathway, though I am not certain the terminology is standardized. Does this kind of case break the stalemate by showing that counterfactual conditioning must be applied not to isolated propositions but to discovery structures, or does it simply reveal that the two camps are answering different questions—one about the psychology of rational discovery, one about the logic of theory choice—under a single heading?

Diagnose the impasse for me. Is the disagreement verbal, centered on ambiguous uses of “learning” and “evidence”; substantive but hidden, perhaps turning on whether rational agents must have unique priors or whether explanatory power is reducible to probabilistic coherence; or a sign that the question is ill-posed? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress? Would it take a formal result showing that counterfactual conditioning collapses into standard conditioning under plausible independence assumptions; a conceptual distinction separating “discovery confirmation” from “justificatory confirmation”; or an argument that Bayesian epistemology should abandon the search for a unified account of old and new evidence altogether?
