I'm a philosophy prof who also mediates collaboration conflicts in our grad program — authorship fights, data-interpretation standoffs, that kind of mess. I've been trying to build a mediation framework around Adam Smith's sympathy and the impartial spectator, but it's too loose. I want a reusable decision procedre my staff can run in about an hour, even if they've never read Smith's ethics book.

My niche injection: I think we need three calibrated spectator distances, not one. The mediator cycles through (1) the intimate spectator — the other party's immediate emotional and intellectual stake, reconstructed sympathetically; (2) the institutional spectator — the norms of philosophical collaboration, credit, and honesty; and (3) the retrospective spectator — how this dispute looks from ten years out. The procedure only resolves when at least two converge, or when divergence is explicitly mapped.

Write the checklist: ordered questions, what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure stops with 'send to arbitration,' and where it hits a values wall that needs human judgment. Flag the hard cases — especially where sympathy for one party is strongest but the institutional or retrospective spectator pulls the other way.

Then run it on three cases: (a) a senior co-author substituted 'the research assistant' for a junior partner's name in a belief-report quotation in the final draft; (b) one author wants to drop a weakening counterexample to protect the joint argument, the other insists on including it; (c) a dispute over who bears moral responsibility when shared data turns out to be flawed and both signed off. Include one where the procedure gives the answer you personally wouldn't like.

Keep it under 550 words. Lead with a one-paragraph bottom line: what this procedre does and the one kind of conflict it will definatly misfire on. No citations, no reading list — just the steps and the cases.
