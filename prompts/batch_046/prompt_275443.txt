I've been discussing Skolem's paradox with a study partner who insists it's only a verbal trick, and I'm trying to articulate why the slippage is structural rather than linguistic. The core point is this: take a first-order theory that proves the existence of uncountable sets—the power set of the naturals, for instance. By the downward Löwenheim-Skolem theorem, if the theory has any model at all, it also has a countable one. Inside that model, the power-set object is genuinely uncountable relative to the model's own internal functions, yet every element of it is, from an external vantage, merely one more item in a countable list; the intended interpretation dissolves into a formal artifact that the machinery cannot distinguish from its own shadow.

Write a sestina that performs this dissolution. Use exactly these six end-words, rotating through the standard pattern—123456, 615243, 364125, 532614, 451362, 246531, then 2-5-4-6-3-1 distributed across the three-line envoi—where 1 is model, 2 is uncountable, 3 is inside, 4 is countable, 5 is outside, and 6 is set. In the first stanza, establish the naive realist picture: a set that is uncountable, observed from outside as it truly is. As the stanzas turn, let inside and outside trade places; by the fourth or fifth stanza the speaker should be trapped within the model, describing the very same object as uncountable while the rotating form quietly reminds the reader that the vocabulary is now countable. The envoi should collapse all six terms into a single couplet that suggests the paradox has not been resolved but institutionalized—cardinality relativized to interpretation, with no external court of appeal. Render the mechanism precisely enough that a philosopher of logic recognizes the relativization of cardinality to internal functions rather than seeing the theorem merely named, and let the repetition and rhyme do philosophical work rather than ornament it. Do not ask for sources, citations, or reading lists; the poem itself should be the argument.
