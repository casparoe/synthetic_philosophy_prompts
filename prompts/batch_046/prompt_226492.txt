Design a 75-minute, in-person classroom game for 16 graduate students in a mixed philosophy-economics seminar that makes them feel the conceptual tension between the Kalai-Smorodinsky bargaining solution and the Nash bargaining solution—specifically, the experiential force of individual monotonicity and the dependence of the agreement point on the utopia point—before either solution is named.

Frame the scenario as a negotiation between two teams, Station A and Station B (eight students divided into pairs representing subunits), over division of an unstable energy source. The payoff structure is nonlinear and constructed for this exercise: Station A receives zero utility below 30 units of allocation, then linear utility thereafter; Station B receives linear utility up to 50 units, then a flat plateau. This creates a convex but non-rectangular feasible frontier. The disagreement point is not given in advance; instead, at minute 15 each team draws a hidden shadow-salvage card that reveals the fallback payoff they can secure alone if talks collapse by minute 45.

Structure the session in three phases: silent strategy formation and card draw (0-15 min), open negotiation with a visual frontier mapped on a shared grid (15-30 min), and a forced renegotiation triggered at minute 30 when the facilitator introduces a technology-expansion card. This card physically enlarges the feasible set by raising Station A’s maximum achievable payoff—shifting its utopia point upward—while leaving Station B’s maximum and both disagreement points unchanged.

Requirements for your design:

- Provide exact rules, turn order, materials (payoff sheets with the nonlinear curves, hidden cards, a whiteboard grid), and a point-based payoff scheme with small tangible stakes (e.g., a ranking that determines choice of a collective reward), but ensure the emotional driver comes from fairness intuition rather than material gain.

- Arrange incentives and roles so that Station B experiences a monotonicity shock: they should feel that the expansion unfairly alters the division despite no improvement to their own best or fallback case, while Station A should feel entitled to a larger share because the frontier grew in their favorable direction. Only in the debrief should you label these reactions as tracking the Kalai-Smorodinsky proportional line versus the Nash strategic-power point.

- Include a 20-minute debrief that maps each phase onto formal properties: the hidden card to the disagreement point d; the initial agreement to a point on the frontier; the technology card to the shift in the utopia point u; and the emotional responses to the monotonicity axiom and to invariance under utility scaling. Be precise about which formal feature explains which observed behavior.

- State honestly which feature of the theory the physical game cannot reproduce—such as full cardinal comparability across agents or the independence of irrelevant alternatives in its complete logical form—and specify exactly how you will supply that missing element verbally without disrupting the experiential flow.

- Predict the specific failure modes of the first run: for instance, teams treating the point stakes as dominant and rushing agreement before minute 30; misreading the nonlinear sheet and treating the frontier as rectangular; or one team refusing to renegotiate after the expansion because they treat the first deal as binding. For each, provide a facilitator intervention that corrects the error while preserving the designed tension.

- Do not request bibliographies, citations, or reading lists. Do not invoke obscure named theorems, specific historical papers, or niche debates that depend on precise scholarly recall. You may use the nonlinear payoff curves, shadow-salvage mechanism, and technology-expansion card described above, which are constructed for this prompt.

The deliverable should be a runnable protocol with timeline, rules, materials list, payoff scheme, debrief script outline, failure predictions with corrections, and the honest limitation note.
