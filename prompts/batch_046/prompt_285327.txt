Next month I am presenting at a philosophy of mind colloquium a paper defending a specific form of illusionism about phenomenal consciousness. My thesis is that phenomenal properties—what-it-is-likeness—are not real intrinsic qualitative features of mental states, but rather that conscious subjects systematically generate introspective reports that misrepresent their own representational neural states as possessing such intrinsic qualities, and that the stability and cross-subject consistency of this error is best explained by predictive-processing mechanisms in interoceptive inference rather than by direct acquaintance with phenomenal properties. The audience will consist of eight philosophers: three representationalists about consciousness who accept that there is something it is like to have an experience but deny that this involves non-representational intrinsic qualities; two higher-order theorists who treat phenomenal consciousness as essentially a kind of meta-representational state; one panpsychist who regards phenomenal properties as fundamental and ubiquitous; and a cognitive psychologist who studies introspective failure and is skeptical of philosophical armchair claims about consciousness. They are all familiar with the standard illusionist literature but none defend it; they care especially about preserving first-person authority, avoiding self-defeating skepticism about experience, and keeping philosophical claims accountable to empirical work on introspection.
