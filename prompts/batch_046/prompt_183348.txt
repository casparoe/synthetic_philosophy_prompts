Read Mary Shelley's Frankenstein through the lens of Searle's Chinese Room argument, treating the creature's hidden education as a biological version of the room: he masters syntax—reading epic poetry, historical biography, and political philosophy from books discovered near his hovel—without being causally embedded in the human form of life that might ground semantic understanding. Does this illuminate why his speech, though rhetorically perfect, coexists with moral catastrophe? Focus on the moment he confronts Victor with the plea: "Oh, Frankenstein, be not equitable to every other, and trample upon me alone, to whom thy justice, and even thy clemency and affection, is most due. Remember, that I am thy creature; I ought to be thy Adam; but I am rather the fallen angel, whom thou drivest from joy for no misdeed. Everywhere I see bliss, from which I alone am irrevocably excluded. I was benevolent and good; misery made me a fiend. Make me happy, and I shall again be virtuous." Here the creature deploys figures—Adam, fallen angels, justice—with flawless grammar, yet he immediately follows this with demands that lead to further murder. Analyze whether Shelley presents his violence as a consequence of syntactic fluency without semantic moral grasp, or whether she attributes it to embodied suffering and social exclusion. Point to other scenes—such as his framing of Justine or his killing of William—where language and action either align with or resist the syntax-semantics split. End by saying whether the novel supports Searle's claim that syntax is never sufficient for semantics, particularly for a consciousness assembled from parts and educated solely through texts.
