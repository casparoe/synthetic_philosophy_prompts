I'm drafting a novel about an epidemiologist who stops trusting her own outbreak models, and last night my writing group turned it into a philosophy brawl that I still can't resolve. So here's the argument I scrawled on a napkin after too much coffee. (1) Any belief I form from the inside view — actually weighing evidence, running the logic myself — should be checked against the outside view: people with my background and cognitive biases get this kind of call wrong at rate R. (2) If R is high, rationality forces me to lower confidence. (3) But step 2 itself is a belief I reached by reasoning, so it falls under the same rule. (4) The outside view on people correctly deciding when to apply the outside view is terrible — we're consistently overconfident about thier own epistemic hygiene. (5) So I should apply the reduction to step 3, which means I shouldn't have lowered confidence in the first place. (6) Loop that and every belief either dissolves into agnosticism or gets arbitrarily shielded by where I decide to stop. (7) There's no principled halt that doesn't just beg the question in favor of modesty. (8) Therefore the inside/outside distinction doesn't actually constrain belief; it's either self-defeating or needs a special exemption that saves it by fiat. Where's the slip? I want to keep (1) and (2) — they feel like genuine humility — but I don't know what I'm paying if I do.
