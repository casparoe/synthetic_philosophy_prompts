I'm working through a problem in my philosophy of religion seminar about whether Molinism can accommodate genuine moral development. I constructed an argument that seems to prove middle knowledge makes moral reform metaphysically impossible, but the conclusion feels too strong—almost a reductio of the whole view. I think there's a hidden modal slide or an equivocation about the scope of divine knowledge, yet I can't pinpoint which premise to reject without undermining either libertarian freedom or the contingency of character. I'd like you to diagnose it precisely, taking a definite position rather than surveying options.

Here is the argument. (1) Molinism holds that God has middle knowledge: for every free agent S and every complete circumstance C, God knows the true counterfactual of creaturely freedom—whether S would freely choose A or not-A in C. (2) Divine knowledge is infallible and necessary in the epistemic mode: in every possible world, God knows exactly the same profile of counterfactual responses, since a false divine belief is impossible. (3) So, necessarily, if "In C, S freely chooses A" is part of that profile, then in every world where C is actualized, S freely chooses A—otherwise God would have known a falsehood. (4) Therefore, for each agent, the mapping from circumstances to free responses is world-invariant. (5) This entails that an agent's moral dispositions are essential rather than contingent: there is no feasible world in which S possesses the same intrinsic nature but, placed in C, chooses otherwise. (6) But moral education, blame, and reform all presuppose that agents can genuinely alter their dispositions, so that in future circumstances they might respond differently than they previously would have. (7) If dispositions are essential and invariant across worlds, then such development is metaphysically impossible, not merely difficult. (8) Hence Molinism is incompatible with the contingency of moral character required for genuine moral responsibility. (9) Yet Molinists standardly claim to preserve both libertarian free will and robust divine providence over feasible worlds. (10) So either Molinism collapses, or our practices of moral education rest on an illusion.

I want to keep premises (1) and (6): I think middle knowledge is a coherent doctrine, and I think moral development is real. I'm open to giving up (3) or (5), but I need to know exactly what each costs. Does rejecting (3) require abandoning divine infallibility, or only distinguishing epistemic necessity from metaphysical necessity? If we instead reject (5) by insisting dispositions are contingent but God still knows them, do we fall into the grounding objection—namely, that God cannot control which counterfactuals are true?
