Examination question:
Dr. Elena Vasquez, a meteorologist, publishes a preliminary climate model that contains an acknowledged simplifying assumption. A regional government misapplies her findings to justify inadequate flood defences. By pure chance, an anomalous atmospheric oscillation either nullifies the risk entirely or amplifies it into catastrophic inundation. Drawing on the philosophical literature concerning moral luck—particularly the distinctions between resultant, circumstantial, and constitutive varieties—analyse whether Vasquez bears different moral responsibility in the two outcomes. In your answer, consider whether this case also illustrates what might be termed predictive moral luck, in which culpability tracks the reliability of scientific forecasting under conditions of irreducible environmental stochasticity, rather than tracking outcomes alone.

Student's answer:
Dr. Vasquez is clearly subject to resultant moral luck, as Thomas Nagel describes. The harmless outcome and the flood are produced by identical actions and intentions, yet our moral judgement of Vasquez seems to shift with the result, just as Bernard Williams argues that Gauguin is judged differently according to whether he succeeds as a painter. The anomalous atmospheric oscillation is an instance of circumstantial luck, since it concerns the external situation Vasquez happens to encounter rather than any feature of her own character. Consequently, she lacks control over the outcome, and since ought implies can—a principle that Nagel endorses—she cannot be held responsible for the flood. Moreover, Nagel's analysis of constitutive luck shows that we never choose our own temperaments or cognitive capacities; therefore, no agent is ever genuinely responsible for any action. Vasquez thus bears no culpability whatsoever, and the framework of moral responsibility collapses entirely. The notion of predictive moral luck is irrelevant here, because the difficulty is purely one of outcome.
