Over the past year, since I began volunteering with a sanctuary for non-human primates after leaving clinical medicine, I have become preoccupied with whether moral progress has a discoverable structure or is merely a shift in fashion. I want to lead a single ninety-minute session for fellow physicians—none with formal philosophy training—on the logic of the expanding moral circle, but I want to frame it with a complication that standard presentations omit: the boundary of moral consideration is not a simple outward ring but a porous membrane that can contract under institutional pressure even as it expands under empathy. Plan the explanatory arc for me, not the explanation itself. First, establish the dependency order: what distinction—perhaps between descriptive moral psychology and normative boundary-drawing—must be fixed before the circle metaphor can do any work, and what must come immediately after? Second, identify the carrying example: I am inclined to use the divergence in welfare protections for laboratory primates versus wild rodents across the same historical period, but specify what this case must possess and what it must lack in order to carry the structural point rather than merely provoke sentiment. Third, map the misconceptions in the sequence they will likely emerge—such as the belief that expanding concern for distant beings necessarily dilutes care for kin, or that recognizing animal moral status requires attributing reciprocal duties to them—and indicate the precise point in the arc where each should be neutralized. Fourth, propose one diagnostic question I can pose at the close that reveals whether the audience has understood the porous-boundary thesis rather than simply adopted a more inclusive attitude. Finally, state clearly which adjacent theoretical framework—perhaps Rawlsian contractualism or evolutionary debunking of moral intuition—should be acknowledged as present but explicitly set aside at this level, and why opening it would collapse the session.
