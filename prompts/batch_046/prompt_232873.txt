I've been working out a personal framework for urban justice to present in my civics course, and I want a consistency check before I defend it. My view rests on five commitments. First, residential justice requires protecting what I call "social infrastructure lock-in"—the idea that long-term residents acquire a collective, non-reducible stake in the spatial layout of shared amenities like transit nodes, school catchments, and informal care networks, such that altering that layout harms them in a way that individual compensation cannot fully repair. Second, I hold that exclusionary zoning is morally wrong because it treats housing access as a positional good to be hoarded rather than a social resource distributed according to need. Third, I believe that immediate neighbors should possess binding democratic authority—not mere consultation—over any development that changes block-level density or demographic composition. Fourth, I maintain that housing is an unconditional human right: no one should face homelessness or severe housing insecurity regardless of income, employment status, or citizenship. Fifth, I think that market-rate high-rise development is usually the best available mechanism for generating the tax revenue and cross-subsidies needed to maintain and expand the public amenities that make neighborhoods worth protecting in the first place.

Do these hang together? I am particularly uncertain whether my defense of binding neighborhood authority conflicts with my rejection of zoning, and whether my unconditional right to housing can survive my endorsement of market-rate development that might displace existing social infrastructure. Please audit the set systematically: identify which pairs are genuinely in tension, which conflicts are only apparent and dissolve on closer inspection, rank the real tensions by severity, state what the cheapest revision would be in each case, and then tell me which single revision to the five commitments would resolve the greatest number of conflicts without collapsing the central idea of the framework.
