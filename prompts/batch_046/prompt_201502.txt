I hold that the Löbian obstacle makes unified self-trust structurally impossible for any agent capable of reflecting on its own soundness: if such an agent could prove that everything it proves is true, Löb's theorem would let it derive its own consistency, which is impossible for any system strong enough to be interesting. Consequently, genuinely self-improving agents must either fragment into hierarchical levels where no single meta-layer endorses all others, or adopt an epistemic thermostat that suppresses confidence in proportion to proof-theoretic strength, never achieving full self-endorsement. What would count decisively against this view? I want formal results, conceptual distinctions, and design sketches—real or hypothetical—that show a unified agent can maintain stable self-trust without hidden fragmentation or thermostat dynamics, including whether restricted modal frames, object-language versus meta-language distinctions, or specific architectures genuinely dissolve the loop rather than relocate it. Please distinguish decisive defeaters from merely awkward ones, say what defenders of this position should themselves concede as damaging, and assess honestly whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability—if every counterexample is reinterpreted as implicit fragmentation, is that insulation a virtue or a defect?
