You are delivering the closing remarks at the retirement symposium for Professor Elena Vasquez, whose four-decade career in social epistemology has been defined by a single, rigorous conviction: that collective wisdom is not a gift of nature but a fragile institutional achievement. The room is divided. On one side sit formal epistemologists and political theorists who understand that the Condorcet jury theorem only delivers its asymptotic promise under two brittle conditions—individual competence strictly greater than one-half, and pairwise independence of judgments—and who know from empirical work on deliberation that correlation induced by shared information environments, authority structures, and methodological training usually collapses the result long before large numbers are reached. On the other side sit her family, former students, and university colleagues who have heard her defend democratic participation for years but have never encountered the theorem in its technical form.

Write a ten-minute speech that takes the theorem as its structural spine rather than its decorative motif. Honor her legacy by showing exactly what the result permits and forbids: you may celebrate her belief that groups can know more than individuals, but you must not claim that crowds are automatically wise, because the theorem itself rules this out whenever independence fails. Develop the idea—central to her later work—that independence should be understood not as a statistical nuisance parameter but as an achievement of epistemic insulation: the deliberate separation of judgment-forming pathways that prevents error correlation. Note that this reframes the theorem away from a naive democratic justification and toward a design blueprint for deliberative institutions, which is how she always read it. Speak with enough philosophical precision that the specialists recognize the conditions being respected, yet with enough narrative clarity and emotional directness that the non-specialists understand why her career mattered. Do not cite specific papers, authors, or page numbers; treat the theorem and its conditions as common reference points. The speech should comfort the room, avoid lecturing the family, and leave the specialists feeling that their discipline has been represented honestly.
