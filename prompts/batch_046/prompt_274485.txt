I've come to accept a particular resolution to Thomson's lamp: rather than treating the limit state at t=2 as genuinely undefined, I take the supertask to instantiate a discrete-state system whose toggle history is indexed over a well-ordered dense sequence, such that the state at the limit point is fixed by the parity invariant of the sequence's tail—specifically, that the lamp inherits the state corresponding to the even-indexed toggle (given a standard initial condition), yielding a determinately stable output rather than an oscillation without resolution. On this view, appeals to indeterminacy rest on a category error: applying continuous-limit intuitions to a mechanism with discrete state-space transitions operating across a completed infinite sequence. I want to know what would genuinely count against this position. Not the generic claim that physical supertasks are impossible, but rather: which formal results in topology or discrete mathematics would undermine the parity-inheritance claim; which results in physics—perhaps analogues in quantum Zeno dynamics or discrete-time computation—would show that state inheritance at limit points behaves differently; which intuitions about persistence or temporal parts it conflicts with; and whether it collapses into a rival account, such as a continuous-state interpretation or an eliminativist denial of the limit state. For each potential defeater, indicate whether a defender of the discrete-parity view should concede it as fatal, treat it as awkward but absorbable through qualification, or dismiss it as irrelevant. Rank these from fatal to merely awkward. Finally, assess whether the view has been insulated by qualifications to the point that it risks nothing substantive, and whether that insulation is a virtue or a vice.
