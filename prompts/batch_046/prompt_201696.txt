Please adopt the role of a professional moral philosopher specializing in normative ethics and moral psychology. Before giving your final assessment, take a moment to brainstorm the logical dependencies among the commitments listed below, paying special attention to hidden conditions of agency versus contents of reasons.

I am auditing my own position for an advanced workshop on moral worth and acting for the right reasons. Here is roughly where I have landed:

(1) Moral worth attaches exclusively to the agent's acting for the right reasons, where the rightness of a reason is fixed by its correspondence to objective, mind-independent normative properties, not by its psychological origin or social function.

(2) Emotional states—sympathy, compassion, love—are not themselves moral reasons, and an agent who performs the right action with complete affective coldness is no less worthy than one who performs it with warmth, provided the rational recognition of the reason is intact.

(3) Nevertheless, I hold that moral agency requires a diachronically stable self, and that such a self is partly constituted by emotional attachments and commitments that are not themselves rationally justified or justified by moral reasons; they are brute conditions of agency.

(4) I reject all forms of moral luck: two agents with identical rational commitments and identical quality of will are equally worthy, regardless of whether external circumstances allow them to succeed, cause them to fail, or make success more costly.

(5) At the same time, I think that when an agent acts for the right reason in circumstances of extreme difficulty—where the psychological and material costs are high—their act reveals a more robust form of commitment than the same reason produces in easy circumstances, and that this robustness is a genuine increment in moral worth, not merely an epistemic marker for observers.

(6) Finally, I maintain that evolutionary explanations of moral belief are irrelevant to moral epistemology because moral properties are causally inert and therefore outside the scope of selectionist explanation, but I also grant that our emotional architecture—including the capacities mentioned in (3)—was shaped by selection pressures that systematically favor parochial altruism, and that this bias must be corrected by reason if moral worth is to be achieved.

Go through these commitments and tell me which pairs are in genuine tension and which only appear to conflict because they operate at different levels. For each real tension, rank them by severity, say how badly the pair pulls against each other, specify what the cheapest revision would be, and identify the single change that would clean up the most problems overall. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; work only with the commitments as stated.
