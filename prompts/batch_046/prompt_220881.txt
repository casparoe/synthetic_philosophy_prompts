I am working out a position that combines a particular reading of Zhuangzi with commitments in philosophy of mind and epistemology, and I want a consistency audit before I present it in a seminar. The view comprises the following commitments: (1) I take Zhuangzi’s butterfly dream not as a Cartesian skeptical scenario but as an argument for what I call “transformational indistinguishability”—the claim that there is no intrinsic phenomenological marker distinguishing the state of being Zhuang Zhou from that of being the butterfly, and therefore no privileged “base reality” from which to assess the other as illusory. (2) I think this actually undermines global dream skepticism, because Cartesian skepticism requires a stable epistemic subject who can be deceived about her state, whereas Zhuangzi’s point is precisely that the subject is not stable across transformations. (3) I am a physicalist: the transformation is ultimately a change in physical organization, and there is no non-physical soul that could serve as the bearer of deception or as a constant witness across states. (4) Nevertheless, I want to preserve the idea that phenomenal concepts are conceptually irreducible—there is something it is like to be the butterfly that is not captured by any physical description—while denying that this irreducibility entails any ontological dualism or a privileged first-person access that survives transformation. (5) I also hold that if there is no stable self across states, then there is no determinate fact about “who is dreaming,” which should dissolve the standard first-person formulation of dream skepticism (“how do I know I am not dreaming now?”). (6) At the same time, I think Zhuangzi’s argument supports a restricted, “local” skepticism: within any given state, we lack an internal criterion to determine whether it is the “waking” one, even though there is no global, enduring subject who suffers from this ignorance.

Do these hang together? Specifically: does (4) reintroduce the kind of privileged first-person access that (2) rejects? Does (3) undermine (1) by reducing transformation to mere neural reorganization, stripping it of its metaphysical force? Is (5) compatible with (6), given that local skepticism seems to require some subject to whom the criterion is lacking? Rank the tensions by severity, distinguish genuine conflicts from merely apparent ones, identify the least costly single revision that restores consistency—stating clearly what must be given up and what is preserved—and do not presume in advance which commitment should be protected.
