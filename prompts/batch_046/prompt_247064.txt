As a retired physicist who has only recently begun to read political philosophy in earnest, I have been attempting to systematise Amartya Sen's well-known argument that famines are seldom mere natural disasters but rather failures of entitlement and political accountability. From my reading, I have reconstructed a framework that seems to generate the following verdicts: the Bengal famine of 1943 is a political crime rather than a natural catastrophe, since aggregate food availability did not fall sharply and the mortality resulted from distributional collapse and colonial administrative neglect; the Irish famine of the 1840s is a compound case in which the potato blight provided a natural trigger, but the scale of death was politically produced by relief policy, land tenure structures, and continued export under British governance; the Great Leap Forward famine of 1959–1961 is an unambiguous political crime, arising from state-induced misinformation, suppression of feedback, and the absence of democratic mechanisms; the Ethiopian crisis of 1983–1985 is primarily a political failure exacerbated by drought, because authoritarian obstruction impeded information flow and humanitarian access; and I treat Sen's observation that functioning multi-party democracies with a free press have not suffered famine as a boundary condition of the framework rather than a coincidental correlation.

Consider now a novel case. In a prosperous liberal democracy with robust electoral competition and an uncensored press, an unforeseen interaction between climate-induced crop variability and an algorithmically optimised, globally distributed supply chain produces acute regional food insecurity. The government responds slowly, not through corruption or censorship, but because democratic deliberation and regulatory fragmentation impede rapid intervention, and because the technical complexity of the automated logistics network renders the entitlement collapse invisible to standard monitoring until deprivation is severe. No authoritarian suppression of data occurs, yet widespread harm ensues.

If I wish to remain consistent with the framework reconstructed above, what verdict ought I to reach? Is this crisis a political crime in the same sense as Bengal or the Great Leap Forward, or does it lie outside the framework because democratic institutions are intact and the causation includes an emergent technical systems failure? I ask you to identify at least two competing rules that jointly accommodate the five earlier verdicts—perhaps one centred on democratic information-flow and accountability, one on state capacity to prevent entitlement collapse regardless of regime type, and one that distinguishes intentional or negligent policy failure from emergent systemic risk. For each rule, predict the verdict it yields for this new case. Then recommend the verdict that best coheres with my precedents, identify the closest historical analog, and state honestly what principle I would be adopting with each possible choice, including if I were to classify the crisis as falling outside the framework entirely.

Furthermore, please separate what is genuinely settled in the historical and philosophical literature on famine causation—such as the importance of entitlement failure relative to food availability decline—from what remains genuinely contested among specialists, for instance the precise explanatory weight of democratic institutions versus market and administrative mechanisms in Sen's analysis. Do not present a disputed interpretation as consensus. Finally, specify what further hypothetical case would best distinguish the democratic-accountability rule from the state-capacity rule.
