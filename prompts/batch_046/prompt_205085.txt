After a long argument with a friend about whether scientific discoveries are inevitable and whether individual credit still matters, I have tried to formalize my position and want a consistency audit before presenting it. Here is the package I have landed on:

(1) I take simultaneous invention—calculus emerging independently from Newton and Leibniz, evolutionary theory from Darwin and Wallace—to be evidence of structural redundancy: once prior conceptual and empirical work closes a problem-space, the formal core of the solution is overdetermined, so convergence by independent agents is the default expectation, not a historical miracle.

(2) From this I draw an anti-heroic normative conclusion: singular credit is largely a retrospective narrative construct. We should understand calculus and evolutionary theory as emergent properties of the field's state at a given time, not as ex nihilo creations of individual genius.

(3) At the same time, I maintain that the specific articulation of any discovery is path-dependent and irreducible. Newton's fluxions carried commitments to actual infinitesimals and a geometric, kinematic ontology of motion, whereas Leibniz's differentials were embedded in a relational, analytic framework; similarly, Darwin's gradualist natural selection and Wallace's more abrupt, selectionist framing represent substantively different research programs with different ontological commitments, not mere notational variants of one identical idea.

(4) For normative credit—who should be celebrated, funded, and remembered—I apply a counterfactual robustness criterion: primary recognition belongs to the agent whose absence would have produced the longest delay or the most distorted version of the result, which is why Darwin still outranks Wallace despite their joint presentation.

(5) I reject counterfactual dependence as a legitimate tool in scientific historiography generally: tracking modal outcomes across institutional, rhetorical, and cognitive variables is epistemically unreliable, so historical explanation should remain anchored to actual causal chains. Nevertheless, I also hold that a discoverer's specific identity determines the theory's subsequent institutional embedding—embedding evolutionary theory through Darwin's network and rhetorical choices versus Wallace's social position would have generated different central problems, funding patterns, and theoretical offshoots—so they are not interchangeable nodes in an inevitable process.
