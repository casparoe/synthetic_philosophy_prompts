I've covered the definition-of-life debate for years, and I'm struck by how little movement there's been since the standard NASA working definition—viruses still sit in limbo, synthetic minimal cells are celebrated without resolving whether they "count," and every new discovery, from virophages to mirror-life chemistry proposals, gets absorbed into pre-existing camps. The argument seems to recycle the same trio of criteria—replication, metabolism, evolutionary adaptation—without anyone conceding ground. Is this actually an empirical standoff waiting for a decisive borderline case, or is it a deeper disagreement about what kind of answer we want: an operational checklist for astrobiologists, a metaphysical boundary in nature, or a cluster concept with no sharp edge? Diagnose the stall specifically—are the sides talking past each other on "function," hiding normative commitments about scientific unity, or defending bedrock intuitions about autonomy? And be honest: if we engineered a self-sustaining chemical network that evolves without nucleic acids, or an AI-governed synthetic cell that metabolizes but does not reproduce, would that genuinely shift anyone's view, or is the framework already too flexible to be threatened?
