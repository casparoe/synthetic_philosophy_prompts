In discussions of animal ethics I often hear the compressed claim—"on Kant we have only indirect duties, since cruelty corrupts the human agent rather than wronging the beast"—used to dismiss objections to practices like factory farming. Unpack the distinct precise theses hiding in that slogan: is it a metaphysical claim that rational nature alone grounds moral status; a normative claim that animal welfare never enters moral reasoning except instrumentally; a psychological claim about sympathy's corruption; or a legal-institutional claim about rights-structure? For each reading, indicate whether it is a trivial restatement of Kant's view, a substantive thesis that actually excludes direct obligations, or a misreading that conflates indirect-duty reasoning with mere instrumentalism. Then say which version the speaker needs for the dismissal to work, and whether it holds once you distinguish the published Doctrine of Virtue from the somewhat different treatment of animal suffering in Kant's ethics lectures.
