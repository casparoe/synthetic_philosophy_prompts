Exam question: Explain the argument from illusion and the disjunctivist response. Specifically, address whether disjunctivism entails denying that hallucination occurs; explain how the theory handles subjective indistinguishability between veridical and hallucinatory experience without positing an intrinsic mental property common to both; and evaluate whether predictive-processing accounts—which model perception and hallucination as outputs of the same hierarchical Bayesian machinery differentiated primarily by precision weighting—challenge disjunctivism, support it, or leave it untouched.

Student answer: Disjunctivism, as developed by philosophers such as John McDowell and Michael Martin, holds that in veridical perception the subject stands in a direct perceptual relation to an object and its properties, whereas in hallucination that relation is absent. Consequently, perception and hallucination are not two instances of a single mental kind; there is no highest common factor linking them. The traditional argument from illusion observes that a hallucination can be subjectively indistinguishable from a veridical perception and concludes that both must share an intrinsic phenomenal property. Disjunctivists reject this inference. They do not deny that hallucination is possible—on the contrary, they treat it as a real mental occurrence—but they deny that its subjective similarity to perception is explained by a shared mental kind. Instead, they typically account for indistinguishability in epistemic terms: the subject lacks the resources to recognize that her state is not relational.

When we turn to predictive processing, we see that both perception and hallucination are generated by the same hierarchical Bayesian inference mechanisms, with the difference lying mainly in the precision assigned to sensory prediction errors. Because the underlying computational architecture is continuous across both cases, predictive processing demonstrates that perception and hallucination are not categorically different mental kinds but rather points on a spectrum of inferential reliability. This poses a direct challenge to disjunctivism, which insists on a sharp mental division. Moreover, since disjunctivists sometimes claim that a perfect hallucination lacks phenomenal character altogether—being merely a cognitive failure—the continuity revealed by predictive processing is especially damaging: if the brain produces both states through identical processes, there is little reason to think one involves a direct relation to the world while the other involves no experience at all. Thus, while disjunctivism blocks the classical argument from illusion, it struggles to accommodate the mechanistic unity that contemporary cognitive science reveals.
