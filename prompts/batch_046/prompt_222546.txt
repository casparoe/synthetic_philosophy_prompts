I'm working through a tension in Marx's account of technology and automation that seems to prove too much, and I can't locate the faulty step. Here is the chain as I understand it.

(1) For Marx, machinery is not instrumentally neutral. It is dead labor—labor already performed and crystallized into capital—that dominates living labor within the factory system, so that the technical form of production embodies capitalist command.

(2) If a technology structurally embodies the social relations of its origin, then adopting it under different relations either reproduces those relations or requires a total reconstruction of the technology itself.

(3) Modern automation and artificial intelligence are developed, funded, and optimized within capitalist imperatives—surplus-value extraction, labor substitution, predictive management—so they structurally embody capitalist domination.

(4) Therefore, a post-capitalist society that deploys such automation without completely redesigning its architecture and purpose would be importing capitalist command in material form.

(5) Yet Marx also argues that large-scale automation reduces necessary labor to a minimum and thereby creates the material precondition for communism.

(6) So Marx is committed to both claims: socialism requires automation, but automation is structurally capitalist.

(7) The only consistent resolution is that socialism must construct entirely different machines—designed under different relations for different ends.

(8) But if machinery can be redesigned in this way, then its capitalist character was never structurally necessary; it was contingent on ownership and purpose.

(9) Therefore, Marx's technological critique either collapses into a generic ownership critique—making his emphasis on the factory system and machinery redundant—or it implies that socialism and modern automation are genuinely incompatible.

If I have to choose, I would rather keep (1) and (5) than (9), because the idea that technology embodies social relations seems central to Marx, and the vision of automated abundance reducing toil is equally central. But I want to know exactly which step fails and what giving it up costs.
