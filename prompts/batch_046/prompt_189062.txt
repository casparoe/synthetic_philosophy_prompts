Notes for an essay: I want to defend the claim that for texts with layered, collective, or redacted authorship—legal codes that accumulate amendments, religious texts shaped by successive editors, or philosophical dialogues with suspected interpolations—the question "what did the author intend?" is conceptually misdirected, since there is no single mental state to recover. I propose substituting what I call "editorial trajectory": the intentional structure visible in patterns of inclusion, omission, and revision across successive versions, regardless of whether any individual agent endorsed the final result. This is not a variant of the "death of the author" thesis, because meaning remains anchored in a distributed, diachronic process rather than projected by the reader; but the relevant agent is plural, like a path carved by many walkers rather than planned by one. I worry, however, that this collapses into reader-response whenever the trajectory is ambiguous, and that it makes textual meaning excessively historical—if later redactors systematically reverse earlier ones, does the meaning of the text flip with them? I also wonder whether the view applies only to obviously composite texts, or whether it extends to any work with a complex publication history. I would welcome comments on the coherence of this position, on whether "editorial trajectory" genuinely distinguishes itself from Gadamerian fusion of horizons and from standard redaction-critical methods, and on whether it can be defended without relying on specific textual examples I have not cited here.
