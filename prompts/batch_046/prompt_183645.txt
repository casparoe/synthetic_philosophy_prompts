I'm a retired ecologist who has recently begun reading philosophy of law, and I have agreed to lead one session for an adult-education discussion group—mostly fellow retirees with no legal background—on Raphael Lemkin's concept of genocide and the politics of naming atrocities. The participants will likely arrive treating genocide as an extreme quantitative threshold of individual killings rather than as a specific form of collective social destruction. I do not need the explanation delivered to me; I need a teaching plan for a forty-five-minute session: in what dependency order must the conceptual pieces be introduced so that the distinction between destroying a group's social pattern and committing mass homicide becomes structurally intelligible rather than merely semantic? Which single historical case should carry the entire explanation, and why should I avoid the examples they have already encountered in popular documentary coverage? I expect two misconceptions to form in sequence—first, that labeling an atrocity "genocide" is only a bureaucratic or political designation with no moral-conceptual difference, and second, that the category requires a clearly articulated psychological intent analogous to individual criminal intent—so at precisely which points in the arc should I head off each? Give me one concluding question I can ask that will reveal whether they have grasped why the legal category was constructed around group destruction rather than whether they now feel more outraged. Finally, tell me which element of Lemkin's broader framework—his treatment of cultural destruction or his proposal for international enforcement—I should explicitly set aside at this level, acknowledge as beyond our scope, and refuse to open.
