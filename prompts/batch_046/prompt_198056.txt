I am currently drafting a chapter for a philosophy of technology seminar that examines whether a Bostrom-style singleton — a world order in which a single agency holds binding decision-making authority at the global level — can possess independent moral interests in its own continued existence. The question has become personally urgent to me because my research group is divided: some colleagues treat such an entity as a mere procedural arrangement, while others argue that its unitary structure might ground a form of collective moral patienthood. Rather than asking for a bibliography or a list of further readings, I want to isolate the conceptual tension through a precisely engineered scenario.

Please construct a thought experiment to the following specifications. Design a realistic, near-future institutional case — not science fiction — in which a unified global climate-adaptation authority (the singleton) holds binding power over all national emissions and resource-allocation policies, and must decide whether to dissolve its unitary authority and devolve decision-making to regional bodies, or to maintain its centralized structure. Crucially, inject this structural feature into the architecture of the singleton: its decision-making is carried out by geographically distributed modules that do not share internal representations, beliefs, or a unified self-model; collective outputs are produced solely through a non-transparent aggregation protocol, with no central integrative process and no binding conscious experience linking the modules together. The scenario should be described in a single paragraph. Then, engineer the case so that two theories of collective moral agency deliver opposite verdicts in a direction that is not trivially stipulated. First, the integrated-subject theory, which grants an institution independent moral interests only if it possesses a genuinely unified consciousness or self-model that can be benefited or harmed as a whole, should recommend dissolution, on the grounds that the absence of internal integration undermines any claim to unitary subjecthood and therefore removes any independent interest in survival. Second, the procedural-contractarian theory, which holds that an institution's moral legitimacy and protective status derive solely from the reliability, fairness, and stability of its decision procedures regardless of internal phenomenological integration, should recommend persistence, on the grounds that the aggregation protocol remains epistemically sound and that dissolving the framework would harm affected populations by removing a stable procedural safeguard. After presenting the scenario, walk through each theory's reasoning step by step; identify the exact structural feature that does the work in separating the two verdicts; explain whether removing that feature collapses the disagreement back into convergence; and conclude with the strongest objection a defender of the integrated-subject theory could mount — specifically, an objection that does not merely deny the fairness of the aggregation protocol, but targets the inference from procedural reliability to institutional persistence in the absence of unitary subjecthood.
