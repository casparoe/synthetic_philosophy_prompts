I'm trying to work out what I actually think about telling history without a hidden center, going by gut on some cases. First: a syllabus that gives every region equal pages but organizes units around "The Age of Discovery," "The Age of Empire," etc., always describing European actions first. I call that sneaky-eurocentric, not okay. Second: a sugar-cultivation narrative that starts in India, moves through the Mediterranean and Atlantic islands, then Brazil and the Caribbean, tracking labor and ecology locally at each stop; Europeans eventually run the Caribbean plantations, and that fact is included, but the story never treats Europe as the main plot. That feels okay. Third: writing about 17th-century science as three paralell streams — Chinese astronomy, Islamic math, European mechanics — with their own internal logics, but arranging the chapters chronlogically so that "the Scientific Revolution" is still one chapter among others. I flip-flop on whether that chronological frame ruins the decentering. Fourth: telling the Haitian Revolution purely through Haitian actors and local politics, mentioning France only when it directly interferes. That feels right, but then I wonder: if in case two I'm okay with Europeans being present as dominant actors, why in case four does it feel wrong to center them? So is there a principle that sorts these? Maybe it's about whose causal priorities drive the narrative structure, or about chronology, or about who gets to be the grammatical subject? Propose a principle or a few candidates, say which verdict each saves and which it overturns, and please be explicit about how confident you are and point to the claim you're least sure about — especially whether case three is salvageable and whether case two and case four are actually in tension.
