I'm designing a graduate seminar handout on self-amending legal rules. Please write a decision tree that takes a student from the single starting commitment—a legal system contains at least one rule governing how rules can be changed—to a named or unnamed position on the paradox of legal self-reference. Build the tree from logical space rather than from a list of names: each branch should split on a concrete claim about circularity, viciousness, hierarchy, or temporal displacement. Specifically, use these four layers: (1) whether the amendment rule can apply to itself without circularity; (2) if circularity is unavoidable, whether it is vicious or benign; (3) whether resolution requires an external meta-rule or can be handled by internal hierarchical ordering of rules; (4) whether a temporal shield—a clause delaying an amendment's effect until after it has already altered the amendment procedure—escapes the paradox or merely displaces it. At every leaf, provide the position name (e.g., Hartian hierarchical ordering, Ross-style regress, Kelsenian external basic norm, or unnamed) and one sentence describing its most awkward commitment regarding legal continuity. Note any unoccupied combinations in the logical space and briefly diagnose why they are empty, whether incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Flag any pair of leaves your tree genuinely cannot separate, especially where internal-hierarchical and temporal-shield solutions converge. Every node should be phrased as a question or claim a respondent can endorse or reject without already knowing which interpretation it leads to.
