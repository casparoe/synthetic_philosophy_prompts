A familiar analogy in debates about presentism compares studying the past to visiting a foreign country: the inhabitants speak differently, observe unfamiliar customs, and live under norms that may conflict with one's own. Anti-presentists often use this image to argue that historians should suspend present moral and conceptual standards to understand past agents on their own terms. Presentists reply that a visitor never fully surrenders her passport—the legal and cognitive framework that makes judgment possible—so complete neutrality is impossible, and perhaps undesirable.

Third, assess whether the analogy's intended conclusion—that historians can achieve a form of moral and epistemic tourism largely free of presentist contamination—survives once these disanalogies are fully registered. Fourth, if a better analogy exists for the historian's relation to the past—perhaps drawn from translation theory, clinical diagnosis, or archaeological reconstruction—propose it and say precisely what it corrects.
