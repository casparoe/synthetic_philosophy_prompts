In arguments about lethal autonomous weapons and high-stakes decision systems, defenders often analogize the system to a soldier under command: the commander sets rules of engagement, the system executes within those bounds, and if harm results we assign blame to the commander (or to the soldier for exceeding orders) — with the implication that because the system cannot understand orders or face court-martial, responsibility never leaves the human deployer. Critics reply that this hides a genuine gap, especially in the "rubber-stamp human-in-the-loop" case where a human approves outputs too complex to evaluate.
