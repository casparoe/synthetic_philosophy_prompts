As a doctoral student in political philosophy focusing on collective agency and labor ethics, I have been participating in a graduate seminar on the moral architecture of strikes, where we recently debated whether solidarity strikes carry the same normative weight as strikes grounded in direct grievance. During the discussion, a colleague repeatedly invoked the old labor slogan "an injury to one is an injury to all" to collapse the distinction between affected and unaffected workers, arguing that if harm to one is literally harm to all, then contractual proximity or geographic separation cannot weaken the duty to strike. The slogan was being made to do heavy philosophical work: it was meant to show that sympathy strikes are not merely permissible strategic alliances but obligatory responses to an injury that is, by its nature, already shared. I want to return to the seminar with a more precise disambiguation, so I ask you to lay out the slogan's possible precise formulations as a menu rather than selecting one.

Specifically, please distinguish at least the following readings and render a verdict on each—trivial, defensible but substantive, or plainly false—while indicating which, if any, actually entail that a worker in an unaffected plant has the identical strike-obligation as the directly injured party: (i) a causal-interdependence claim about shared economic vulnerability under capitalism; (ii) a strategic-coordination claim about pooled bargaining power being maximized only by universal response; (iii) a normative-universalist claim that class membership dissolves individual boundaries of obligation, making direct and indirect grievance normatively indistinguishable; and (iv) an intermediate associative-duty claim—perhaps treating solidarity obligations as non-aggregative and anchored in shared institutional identity rather than summed individual interests—which might preserve some normative distance between direct and indirect harm without reducing solidarity to mere prudence. I am especially interested in whether that associative intermediate can rescue the slogan from the implausible implication that geographic or contractual separation never weakens moral demands, and if so, what precise principle it commits us to that pure strategic or pure universalist readings avoid.
