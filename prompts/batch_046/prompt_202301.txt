As a novelist long preoccupied with questions of narrative authority, collective authorship, and who owns the ending of a shared story, I find myself holding contradictory intuitions about direct democracy that I have been unable to reconcile through plot alone. I should like to report my verdicts on a series of concrete cases—some drawn from history, others hypothetical—and ask what principle, if any, captures the pattern, what it predicts about cases I have not considered, and whether some of my verdicts must be abandoned in the name of coherence.

My gut reactions are these. First, the Swiss system of mandatory constitutional referendums combined with popular initiatives: citizens may propose amendments, and all constitutional changes require approval by both a popular majority and a majority of cantons. Verdict: legitimate, even admirable. Second, the 2016 United Kingdom referendum on European Union membership: an advisory vote conducted with simple majority rule, no territorial weighting, no supermajority threshold, and subsequently treated by Parliament as politically binding. Verdict: illegitimate as a decision-procedure, though I grant it revealed something politically significant. Third, California Proposition 8, the ballot initiative that amended the state constitution to remove the right of same-sex couples to marry, thereby overriding a recent state supreme court ruling. Verdict: illegitimate. Fourth, California Proposition 22, the ballot initiative—heavily funded by gig-economy corporations and drafted in dense statutory language—that reclassified app-based drivers as independent contractors. Verdict: also illegitimate, yet I feel differently about it than about Proposition 8, and I cannot articulate precisely why; they seem to fail for different reasons, or perhaps the same reason with different weights. Fifth, a hypothetical mechanism I shall call deliberative ratification: a randomly selected citizens’ assembly spends six weeks studying a proposed constitutional change; only if the assembly approves it by two-thirds does the proposal advance to a popular vote, which then requires fifty-five per cent approval. Verdict: legitimate. Sixth, a nested veto mechanism: a local minority comprising fifteen per cent of a region’s population may trigger a binding national referendum on an issue that affects only that region, with the national electorate deciding the outcome. Verdict: illegitimate. Seventh, and this is the case to which I keep returning, a silent-threshold rule under which a referendum is automatically void if voter turnout falls below forty per cent, preserving the status quo even when ninety-five per cent of those who did vote favoured change. Verdict: legitimate, though this sits uneasily beside my rejection of the Brexit referendum’s simple-majority logic.

Is there a principle—perhaps turning on epistemic standing, on protection of minority narrative rights, or on some distinction I have not yet named—that captures all or nearly all of these? Or must I choose between a more complex principle that saves every verdict and a simpler one that overturns at least one?
