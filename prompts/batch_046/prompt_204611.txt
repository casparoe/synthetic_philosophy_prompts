Assume the role of a philosopher of practical rationality and institutional decision-making advising the Continuation Committee of the Meridian Regional Archive Foundation. Before drafting, take a moment to map the conceptual terrain: distinguish between sunk costs that are purely instrumental—resources expended on a means now obsolete—and those that are constitutive—investments that have become internal to the value-identity of the end being sought. Consider carefully whether honoring the latter can ever be rational rather than fallacious, and outline briefly how that distinction applies to the case below.

The Foundation must vote Thursday on whether to approve a 36-month, $420,000 Phase-3 extension of the Analog Voices Project, launched in 2014. The project recorded oral histories using a proprietary reel-to-reel substrate that is now discontinued. After Phase 2, 78% of the collected tapes are degraded beyond acoustic recovery; only 22% remain intact. The principal investigator now argues that the degradation patterns themselves constitute an unforeseen scientific dataset—corrosion metadata that documents storage-environment histories absent from any other archive—and that terminating the project now would not only abandon the remaining recoverable interviews but also dissolve a decade-long web of community trust built through the interview process.

Write the formal memo you will circulate before the vote. Recommend a concrete course of action—approve the extension outright, reject it, or approve it only under sharply defined conditions—with an explicit timeline, budgetary boundary, and hard-stop criteria. Your memo must treat the philosophical issue in the weeds: is honoring sunk costs always irrational, or can there be cases where continuing is justified by future-oriented reasons that genuinely depend on the past investment? Apply the instrumental/constitutive distinction you outlined above to this specific case, and do not treat the phrase “sunk cost fallacy” as a dismissive conversation-ender.

Engage seriously with the strongest consideration on the losing side. If you recommend termination, explain why the corrosion-metadata argument and the community-trust claim do not overcome the rational prohibition on honoring sunk costs, without reducing those claims to mere sentimentality or public-relations risk. If you recommend continuation, specify exactly what Phase 3 must deliver—quantitative milestones, concrete deliverables, and an unambiguous pull-the-plug condition—and explain why this decision is not emotional attachment to a failed investment. Write for board members who are not philosophers: avoid unexplained jargon, do not include bibliographies, citations, or reading lists, and do not invoke obscure named theories or scholars that a well-read generalist model would not reliably recognize. The memo should be polished, formal, and immediately actionable.
