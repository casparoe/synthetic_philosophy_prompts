I've been running a graduate seminar on political ethics, and for the past several weeks we've been stuck on the dirty-hands problem in a way that feels structurally different from the usual literature stalemate. We all accept the canonical framing: a democratic leader may have to do something morally prohibited—say, authorizing a covert operation that will certainly kill non-combatants—to prevent catastrophic harm, and the philosophical question is what this means for the leader's moral standing. But the group has split into two camps that agree on every descriptive fact yet talk past each other on everything normative. One camp treats dirty hands as a problem of moral residue: there is a genuine wrong committed, it leaves a stain that demands some form of acknowledgment or atonement, and the leader's personal integrity is permanently altered in a way that requires public recognition. The other camp treats it as a problem of role distortion: the wrongness is not best understood as a personal stain but as a structural feature of democratic authority itself, where institutional design requires that certain decisions remain hidden, which means the usual mechanisms of accountability and moral reasoning are systematically disabled rather than personally violated. Both sides agree on the same concrete case—imagine a head of state who secretly approves a cyber intrusion that disables an enemy weapons system but also collapses a civilian hospital network—and both agree the leader acted rightly, all things considered. Yet they violently disagree about whether the leader should publicly confess, whether citizens have a right to know, and whether democratic complicity is even a coherent concept. The debate has stalled: each side cites the same historical examples, neither moves the other, and I suspect we are not dealing with a simple substantive disagreement about how much moral weight to assign. Is this actually a verbal dispute about what "integrity" or "accountability" means? Are the two sides answering different questions—one about personal ethics, one about institutional architecture—under the single heading of "dirty hands"? Or is there a hidden normative or empirical disagreement about what democratic citizens can actually be expected to understand or forgive, which neither side has made explicit? Given your diagnosis, what would count as genuine progress: a conceptual distinction between personal and structural wrongdoing, an institutional analysis of democratic secrecy, or perhaps the recognition that the question is ill-posed because it conflates two separate problems that should be separated?
