You're a professional philosopher and experienced seminar facilitor — think carefully before you plan anything. First, do a quick brainstorm: how do you make a group feel the absence of an essence in a concept they think is neat? Then design the session.

Setting: a philosophy seminar, 14 third-year students, 90 min, no prior reading except that they've heard Wittgenstein's name. The idea to feel: family resemblance and language-games — categories held by criss-crossing similarities, not a hidden core, and fixing an essence makes the concept either useless or arbitrary.

Don't use "game" — too familiar. Instead inject a new category for the activty: "threshold practices", actions marking a passage between states. Give groups cards describing eight cases: a wedding ceremony; a software update wiping old data; a coming-of-age trial; demolition followed by rebuilding; a diplomatic apology ending conflict; medical discharge; a seasonal harvest rite; a password reset. In groups of three, they must agree on one necessary-and-sufficient condition for being a "genuine" threshold practice, then trade cards with another group who applies that rule strictly. Every proposed condition breaks on at least one card everyone initially wanted to include. Small stakes: the winning group picks the music for the last 5 min.

Only after they've fought over boundaries and watched thier own rules fail should the debrief name family resemblance. Map each broken criterion onto Wittgenstein's points — different rules in different contexts, no common thread, resemblance like family traits. Say what predictably goes wrong: students will try to save the criterion by redefining a card rather than dropping the essence, and how to steer it back (ask what they'd have to reject to keep the rule). Be honest about what this card format can't reproduce: meaning as use embedded in a form of life. Supply that verbally at the end.

Write in British English. No citations, no reading list, keep it casual.
