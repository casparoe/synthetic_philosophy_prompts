At a philosophy reading group, two members—call them A and B—debated Kierkegaard’s account of Abraham in Fear and Trembling. B summarized the exchange afterward and asked for an outside judgment. Here is the reconstructed argument, presented in eight moves.

(1) A opened by saying that Abraham’s willingness to sacrifice Isaac is straightforwardly immoral. The universal ethical command prohibits killing one’s child, and appealing to faith to override it is merely a rationalization of religious violence.

(2) B replied that this misses the specific structure of the teleological suspension of the ethical. The ethical is universal and applies to everyone, but Abraham stands in an absolute relation to the Absolute that is higher than the universal. His act is therefore not a simple violation but a suspension of the ethical sphere for a singular, non-generalizable purpose.

(3) A countered that if the universal can be suspended whenever someone claims a private relation to a higher end, then ethics collapses into arbitrariness. The very concept of a universal law requires that it not admit private exceptions.

(4) B responded by distinguishing Abraham from a tragic hero such as Agamemnon. Agamemnon sacrifices Iphigenia for the universal good of the fleet and can justify himself publicly. Abraham sacrifices Isaac for a purpose that is entirely incommunicable; he can justify himself to no one. Because the exception is singular and silent, it cannot be generalized into a rule, so there is no slippery slope.

(5) A then argued that incommunicability does not distinguish faith from madness. If someone today claimed divine instruction to kill a child, we would diagnose psychosis, not praise a knight of faith. The phrase teleological suspension risks dressing up delusion in philosophical language.

(6) B answered by invoking the double movement of faith: first, infinite resignation, in which Abraham genuinely gives up Isaac within the finite ethical world; second, by virtue of the absurd, he receives Isaac back. This is not denial of reality but a paradoxical holding-together of the finite and the infinite that exceeds rational comprehension without contradicting it.

(7) A then introduced an extension that B had not addressed: that Abraham’s act also involves a teleological suspension of the epistemic. Just as he suspends the ethical prohibition, he suspends the universal demand for public justification and evidence. His silence is not merely ethical incommunicability but an abandonment of rational accountability altogether.

(8) B rejected this extension. She said Kierkegaard never extends the suspension into epistemic norms; Abraham’s faith operates where reason reaches its limit and is transcended, not violated. The absurd is not a logical contradiction but the boundary at which human understanding ends and faith begins. To call this an epistemic suspension confuses religious transcendence with skepticism or fideism.

Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the eight moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with brief justification for each rating. Then say who is more right overall, and identify what both parties miss—particularly regarding the distinction between the knight of faith and the knight of infinite resignation, and whether the absurd functions primarily as a cognitive limit or as a positive content of faith.
