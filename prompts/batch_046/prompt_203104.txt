You are a philosopher of law specializing in comparative federalism and the normative logic of multi-level governance. Before adjudicating, think carefully about the difference between an allocation principle and an efficiency criterion, and about how judicial deference shapes formal rules in practice. Two colleagues debated subsidiarity over lunch, and I listened without taking sides. The first said that true subsidiarity requires that when lower units—say, Swiss cantons or German Länder—can solve a cross-border problem like alpine water management through an inter-cantonal treaty, then central or EU-level regulation is illegitimate, no matter how efficient Brussels might be; he claimed the EU framework fails this test because it permits central action whenever the objective can be better achieved at the higher level, reducing federalism to cost-benefit analysis. The second replied that this misreads the structure: there is a two-step test in which one first asks whether the objective can be sufficiently achieved at the lower level, and only if that fails does one ask whether the Union level is better, so if cantons actually form an effective treaty the first prong blocks centralization regardless of efficiency. The first countered that in practice courts defer almost entirely to the legislature's judgment of sufficient achievement, meaning the first prong is a formality and the second step—where being better achieved dominates—does all the substantive work, making the framework a centralizing tool disguised in federalist language. The second answered that even with deference, the framework remains conceptually different from US federalism, which relies on enumerated powers rather than an explicit allocation rule, so importing subsidiarity into US constitutional doctrine would actually constrain Congress by forcing it to demonstrate a failure of state-level capacity rather than invoke a general welfare connection. The first then argued that this assumes courts can neutrally identify failure of capacity without smuggling in hidden normative judgments about what counts as sufficient, and without an independent metric—perhaps a principle of jurisdictional redundancy—subsidiarity either licenses centralization whenever a legislature asserts it or demands an impossible objective measure of local inadequacy. Please adjudicate move by move: provide exactly five numbered judgments, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion, stating for each turn which speaker makes the stronger point and briefly why, and within the sequence indicate who is more right overall about whether subsidiarity protects lower-level autonomy or licenses centralization.
