I'm preparing a position statement for a philosophy of ethics reading group and want a consistency audit before I present. Here are the commitments I've arrived at, all shaped by Quinean naturalism but applied specifically to moral value:

(1) I accept Quine's criterion of ontological commitment without exception: an entity belongs in our ontology only if it is indispensable to our best overall scientific theory, quantified over in its formalization. Moral properties—wrongness, obligation, virtue—are not so quantified, so I deny ontological commitment to irreducible moral entities.

(2) I am nonetheless a robust moral cognitivist: moral judgments express beliefs with genuine truth-values, moral error is real, and at least some moral knowledge is possible.

(3) I reject the analytic-synthetic distinction entirely and extend Quinean holism to normative belief: in principle any ethical commitment can be revised in response to recalcitrant experience, though in practice many are central to the web.

(4) Despite (3), I maintain that there is a stable, non-collapsible boundary between descriptive and normative discourse. The failure of the analytic-synthetic divide does not entail that the fact-value distinction collapses; normative claims retain a distinctive functional role in the web.

(5) Reflective equilibrium is the appropriate method for ethics, but I understand it strictly as coherence-seeking analogous to scientific theory revision: it does not involve tracking sui generis moral facts, but rather achieving stability among normative and empirical commitments.

(6) Moral disagreement is, at root, disagreement about empirical predictions—concerning human flourishing, social coordination, or psychological well-being—so that there are no "purely normative" cruxes that survive complete empirical resolution.

(7) I reject evolutionary debunking arguments against morality. Because justification is holistic, the causal origins of individual moral beliefs (evolutionary, cultural) are epistemically irrelevant; isolating them by genealogy is a mistake.

(8) Quinean underdetermination applies to ethics as well: there may be empirically equivalent but normatively distinct ethical frameworks, neither uniquely compelled by the evidence.

My question is whether this package hangs together. Specifically:

- Which pairs are in genuine tension, and which only appear to conflict but dissolve on inspection? I want them ordered by severity, not listed as equally problematic.
- For each genuine tension, state precisely what pulls against what, rate the severity, and identify the least costly revision that restores consistency.
- Is there a single commitment whose abandonment or revision would resolve the most tensions at once? If so, which one and what does giving it up cost?
- Please do not treat all eight as equally in conflict; discriminate carefully.

Write this as an audit of my position set, not a general lecture on Quine or metaethics.
