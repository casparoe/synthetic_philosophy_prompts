I am preparing a feature for a science-and-ideas publication on the boundary between rational choice theory and the philosophy of agency, and I want to include the draft joint statement that two opposing camps might produce after a sustained adversarial collaboration on Buridan's ass and the possibility of choice without preference.

The two camps are these. The first — call them the Comparative-Judgment Rationalists — hold that rational choice is constitutively guided by comparative evaluation: to choose rationally is to select the option one judges better, and in the absence of any such judgment there is no rational basis for selection; tie-breaking without preference is therefore not rational agency but either arbitrary mechanism or disguised determinism. The second — call them the Arbitrary-Resolution Volitionists — hold that rational agency includes the capacity to resolve ties through a rule, stochastic process, or direct act of will that does not presuppose a hidden preference; they maintain that requiring a comparative reason for every selection confuses rationality with utility maximization and illegitimately rules out genuinely open choice structures.

Please draft the document they should have produced. Format it as follows.

First, a numbered section of agreed claims: statements about the formal structure of the classic paradox, about the standard assumptions of preference-based decision theory, about whether physically identical options are metaphysically or epistemically distinguishable, and about the distinction between rationality, determinism, and randomness — all in wording both camps could sign.

Second, a disagreements table. Each row should present one precise divergence, phrased in wording that the camp holding that view would accept for its own position, with a parallel neutral description of the opposing stance. Do not let either formulation sound like a straw man.

Third, identify the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one camp. I suspect two are particularly important: whether a tie-breaking rule must itself be justified by a further preference (risking regress), and whether an agent who breaks ties arbitrarily can still be held responsible for the outcome in the same way as one who chooses on reasons.

Fourth, for each crux, specify a concrete case, thought experiment, or formal result that both camps agree bears on it, and record in advance what each camp would predict about that case. One case I want included involves an algorithmic decision system that uses a deterministic hash of ambient noise to break ties between equally scored options: both camps should address whether this resolves the paradox, and if not, why not. Please also address whether the paradox survives if we replace cardinal utility with a purely ordinal framework that permits incommensurable values.

Please avoid bibliographies, reading lists, or citation requests. Do not invoke obscure formalisms or named theories that are not part of general philosophical literacy; write so that a well-informed reader without web access can follow every step. Where you need to reference specific texts or formulations, paraphrase without quotation marks rather than quoting from memory.

Write in a polished, formal register appropriate to a journalist covering conceptual debates for an educated general audience.
