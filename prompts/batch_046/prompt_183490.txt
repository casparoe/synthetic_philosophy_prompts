My friend and I spent several hours arguing about Robert Nozick's position on animals—specifically whether his entitlement theory and broader moral framework leave any direct, non-derivative constraints on how we treat non-human sentient beings. We kept returning to the same four moves without resolution, and I would like an impartial adjudication of the exchange as it actually unfolded, not a generic summary of Nozick's ethics.

The argument proceeded as follows.

She began by arguing that animals fall almost entirely outside Nozick's domain of justice. Justice, on this view, is constituted by rights, self-ownership, and entitlement; animals lack rational autonomy, cannot hold property, and cannot be parties to contracts or reciprocal obligations. Consequently, they possess no direct moral rights and are not protected by side-constraints. The only moral limits on harming them are indirect: cruelty may corrupt the agent's character, or injuring another person's property (such as a pet) violates the owner's entitlement. Therefore, she concluded, industrial practices that inflict widespread suffering—factory farming, for example—are not unjust in any distinctively Nozickian sense, however unpleasant they may be.

I objected that this overlooks Nozick's well-known structural distinction: he applies a utilitarian calculus to animals while applying Kantian side-constraints to people. If animals possess welfare that can be summed, compared, and traded across lives, then they are not morally invisible; they are moral patients of a specific kind. Indeed, this makes their situation potentially worse than if they had rights, because it permits justifying severe suffering to one animal by appealing to greater aggregate benefits elsewhere—precisely the kind of cross-personal aggregation that Nozick rejects for humans.

She answered that I was importing an incoherence into his theory. His rejection of utilitarian aggregation for people is grounded in the separateness of persons: each human life is an end in itself, and suffering in one life cannot be canceled by pleasure in another. But if animals lack that separateness—if they are not distinct moral individuals with inviolable boundaries—then there is no reason to aggregate their welfare either. Aggregation presupposes a framework that identifies who counts and how; without rights to establish those boundaries, there is no metric of justice that applies to animals. Thus, the claim that we apply utilitarian reasoning to animals must be interpreted narrowly: it governs practical choices about animals already within our domain (e.g., veterinary triage), not a general moral standing that protects them from being used.

At this point I introduced a consideration she had not addressed. The Lockean proviso, which governs just acquisition from the commons, need not apply only to potential property-holders. If we understand the commons broadly to include the ecological and experiential baseline available to sentient beings, then systematically degrading that baseline—creating lives of suffering worse than non-existence—might violate the proviso indirectly. On this reading, extreme industrial farming does not wrong individual animals by violating their rights, but it wrongs them collectively by worsening their condition relative to the baseline in which they are not brought into existence under such conditions. This provides a principled, non-sentimental reason to condemn practices that destroy the shared conditions of animal life.

She replied that this extension is a category mistake. The proviso is designed to ensure that appropriation leaves enough and as good for others who are capable of appropriation. Animals are not potential appropriators; applying the proviso to them transforms a theory of property and justice among rational agents into a vague ecological ethics. Moreover, without a rights-based threshold, the judgment that a life is worse than non-existence is metaphysical or aesthetic, not a claim of justice. Nozick's framework requires clear violations of entitlements; it does not support baselines grounded in collective welfare or ecological conditions.

We ended in stalemate. Please adjudicate move by move. First, is she correct that animals fall outside Nozick's justice framework because they lack rights and autonomy? Second, am I correct that Nozick's structural distinction implies animals have aggregable welfare that grants them direct moral weight? Third, is she correct that without rights, aggregation is incoherent, and that the utilitarian-for-animals claim applies only narrowly to practical management? Fourth, is my Lockean-proviso extension valid within a Nozickian framework, or does it collapse into the category mistake she describes?

Beyond the point-by-point verdict, please identify what each of us is missing. Is there a consistent middle position that avoids both the moral invisibility of animals and the incoherence of rights-free aggregation? Does Nozick's own framework contain resources—perhaps in his discussion of side-constraints, meaning, or the conditions for a meaningful life—that we have both overlooked, and that would allow a more nuanced verdict? Do not provide bibliographies, citations, or reading lists; I want an adjudication of the arguments exactly as presented.
