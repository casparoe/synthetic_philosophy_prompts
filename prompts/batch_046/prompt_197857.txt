Having spent my career in condensed-matter physics, I have recently begun studying cross-cultural moral psychology, particularly the finding that WEIRD populations are statistical outliers in weighting harm and fairness over loyalty, authority, and purity. This has led me to a conceptual puzzle I cannot resolve without philosophical help. In the natural sciences, an objective quantity—thermal conductivity, for instance—yields convergent measurements across laboratories once protocols and units are standardized; persistent disagreement is usually traced to instrumental error or unit mismatch, not to ontological pluralism. Applying this model to morality, I reason that if moral properties are genuinely objective, then folk moral judgments should exhibit comparable cross-cultural convergence once we correct for framing and translation effects. Instead, the divergence between WEIRD and traditional societies appears structural rather than noisy: these societies seem to employ different moral “coordinate systems,” not merely imprecise measurements of a single underlying value. My question is therefore this: does the anthropological data undermine moral realism by demonstrating that there is no universal calibration metric—no neutral zero-point analogous to absolute temperature—beneath the cultural variation? Or is the error in my reasoning precisely the assumption that moral objectivity must mirror physical objectivity, so that the WEIRD divergence actually exposes a misapplied criterion of convergence rather than a failure of moral truth itself?
