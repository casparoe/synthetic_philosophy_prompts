Here's an argument I worked out after wondering whether objects can really have politics, and now I find it suspicious because it seems to show that mountains determine society. (1) A material form embodies a political order if it makes one pattern of human life structurally necessary and the alternatives structurally impossible without destroying or fundamentally altering that form. (2) Some parkway bridges are built with clearances so low that buses cannot pass; changing this requires destroying the bridge, so they make car ownership structurally necessary and bus travel impossible—thus they embody a car-centric politics. (3) A deep canyon makes flat settlement structurally impossible and terraced settlement necessary; changing this requires destroying the canyon walls, so by (1) the canyon embodies a terraced-settlement politics. (4) If natural landforms can embody political orders in exactly this way, then politics is not a human construction but a feature of physical geography—a kind of geological determinism. (5) But politics is clearly something humans construct. (6) Therefore either (1) is false, or we must accept that rivers and mountains determine social organization. I would rather keep (1) and (2) intact than abandon (5), but I want to know precisely what that costs me. Where does the reasoning go wrong? Is there a hidden assumption about human design or reversibility that (1) leaves unstated but that saves it from applying to canyons? Also, if my framing rests on a confused premise—say, if the difference between artifacts and nature is not what I think—please say so plainly rather than smoothing it over.
