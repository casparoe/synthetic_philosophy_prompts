A fellow essayist who read an early draft of my novel summarized Borges's philosophy of time for me over dinner, and I want to test whether I can build a narrative architecture on it without misrepresenting him. He said: In essays such as A New Refutation of Time and The Doctrine of Cycles, Borges denies that temporal succession is real, arguing instead that past, present, and future coincide in an impersonal consciousness. He draws on Schopenhauer's denial of the enduring individual will and applies it to eternal recurrence: since there is no self that persists across moments, recurrence is not the repetition of a personal biography but the eternal presence of every instant at once. That is why The Garden of Forking Paths and The Circular Ruins are essentially the same metaphysical claim—branching and cyclical structures are both devices for showing that no event genuinely precedes another. He also took J. W. Dunne's model of serial consciousness from An Experiment with Time, but discarded Dunne's empirical claims about prophetic dreams, keeping only the idea that causality is a fiction we impose on a static, spatialized reality. For Borges, this means narrative plot itself is a kind of false consciousness: cause and effect are merely the order in which we read symbols arranged on a page, not a feature of the world, which is why his own fiction often collapses chronology into simultaneous tableaux.

As a novelist attempting to render time as a spatial field rather than a flowing stream, I need to know whether this account is fair. Is this summary accurate, or does it conflate separate threads in Borges's work? Specifically: does Borges deny succession in the strong metaphysical sense implied here, or is his denial more narrowly directed at the reality of temporal flow and the measurability of duration? How exactly does Schopenhauer's denial of individuality function in Borges's argument about recurrence—does it dissolve personal identity across cycles, or is that a separate concern? Are branching and cyclical temporalities genuinely interchangeable for Borges, or do they address different conceptual problems, one about determinism and possibility, the other about repetition and identity? And does his engagement with Dunne support the claim that causality is a fiction of reading and spatial arrangement, or is that an overreading that imports a theory of narrative he never endorsed? Finally, if his view does undermine linear causality, does it necessarily undermine narrative form, or might fiction accommodate an atemporal structure without becoming incoherent?

Please separate what is textually faithful, what is a defensible simplification for a non-specialist, and what subtly misstates Borges's commitments—particularly any conflation of his idealist arguments with his narrative techniques.
