Draft the joint statement that an adversarial collaboration between two opposing camps on the ontology of historical periodization might produce. Camp A holds that 'the Middle Ages', 'the Renaissance', and 'the Scientific Revolution' are largely retrospective historiographical constructs with little natural unity, shaped by 19th-century nationalism and Whiggish narratives of progress. Camp B holds that these labels denote real, causally integrated clusters of institutional, intellectual, and material change that would be recognizable even to contemporaries, albeit under different names.

Structure your response as follows:

1. Agreed claims: List the claims about the actual distribution of events, texts, and practices across roughly 1300 to 1750, and about the documented role of 19th-century historians in codifying these labels, that both sides can sign without reservation.

2. Precise disagreements: State the residual disagreements in wording that each camp would accept for its own position. These should cover at least: (i) whether the discontinuity around 1450-1550 is large enough relative to internal variation to warrant a period boundary; (ii) whether the clustering of Copernican astronomy, Baconian method, and mechanistic philosophy in the 17th century reflects a shared causal mechanism or is largely retrospective packaging; and (iii) whether contemporaries experienced these shifts as epochal breaks.

3. Cruxes: Identify at least three sub-questions such that resolving them in one direction would significantly move at least one camp. Include at least one empirical crux (for example, whether quantitative patterns in manuscript production and university foundation across Europe show smooth curves or step changes near 1500) and at least one conceptual crux (for example, whether 'natural kind' is the appropriate standard for assessing historical categories, or whether some weaker form of objective clustering suffices). Introduce and apply the distinction between periodization-as-taxonomy and periodization-as-genealogy in framing one of these cruxes.

4. Tests and predictions: For each crux, specify a concrete case or body of evidence both camps agree bears on it, and record in advance what each camp predicts that evidence will show. For instance, if citation-network analysis of European university texts revealed no structural discontinuity at 1500, state exactly what inference Camp A and Camp B respectively draw.

5. Verbal dispute check: Assess whether any portion of the disagreement collapses once terms like 'real' are replaced with more precise alternatives—such as 'useful for predictive explanation' versus 'carving nature at its joints'—and say which part, if any, remains genuinely substantive.

Keep the reasoning formal, do not request bibliographies or specific unread texts, and work from general conceptual and historical knowledge alone.
