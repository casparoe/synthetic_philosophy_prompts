I am a retired field ecologist who now sits on a regional wildlife conservation board and has recently begun serious study of philosophy of science. Our board must repeatedly decide whether an observed morphological or behavioral trait in an endangered population is an adaptation that warrants active preservation, or a spandrel—an evolutionary byproduct that should not drive management priorities—drawing on the Gould–Lewontin critique of pan-adaptationism. Design a reusable decision procedure that a reviewer with biological expertise but no philosophical training can apply in about thirty minutes per case: present it as an ordered sequence of tests with explicit pass/fail criteria, specifying the required evidence (field observation, phylogenetic comparison, experimental manipulation, or developmental analysis) at each stage, and distinguish adaptations from spandrels, exaptations, and developmental byproducts. Flag every point where phylogenetic inertia, developmental coupling, or missing data make the distinction genuinely indeterminate and a board vote is unavoidable rather than algorithmic. Apply the procedure to three worked cases: one clear adaptation, one clear spandrel, and one ambiguous case where the procedure yields a counterintuitive recommendation that conflicts with intuitive conservation value. Do not request bibliographies, citations, or references to obscure theoretical literature; I will supply those myself. The deliverable should be practical, honest about where judgment cannot be eliminated, and usable by a board that needs to sign off on each decision.
