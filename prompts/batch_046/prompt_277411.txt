Consider the following exchange, reported to me by two graduate students working on the philosophy of language. They are debating whether Grice’s account of conversational implicature can accommodate what they call adversarial implicature—cases in which a speaker intends an implicature that she fully expects the hearer to miss, precisely in order to maintain deniability.

Elena takes the orthodox Gricean line: speaker meaning is essentially a matter of audience-directed intention, and an implicature that the speaker does not intend the hearer to recover is not an implicature at all but something else—perhaps a concealed assertion or a private mental reservation. She argues that Grice’s calculability condition requires not merely that the hearer could, in principle, derive the implicature, but that the speaker intends the hearer to recognize her intention to communicate it. If the speaker expects non-recovery, the intention structure collapses, and we should describe the utterance as deceptive rather than implicative.

Marcus responds that Elena is over-intellectualizing the recognition condition. He grants that successful communication requires mutual recognition, but insists that speaker meaning is fixed by the speaker’s communicative intentions, not by the hearer’s actual uptake. He introduces a distinction between first-order implicature (intended for recovery) and shadow implicature (intended as a latent meaning that would be recoverable if the context were different, and that serves strategic purposes—such as providing the speaker with a fallback interpretation should she be challenged). Marcus claims that shadow implicatures are still calculable in principle, still cancelable, and still non-detachable, and therefore fall squarely within Grice’s taxonomy. Elena counters that Marcus has simply renamed strategic deception: if the speaker actively structures her utterance so that recovery is unlikely, she is not engaging in implicature but in what Grice called exploitation of the maxims, which produces misleading implicatures only when the hearer does recover them. Marcus replies that exploitation is a relation between maxims and utterance design, not a failure of speaker meaning, and that Elena’s insistence on actual uptake conflates pragmatics with epistemology.

Please adjudicate this exchange step by step. For each of Elena’s and Marcus’s principal moves, indicate whether it is sound, partly sound but overstated, or unsound, with brief justification. Then assess whether Marcus’s notion of shadow implicature can be coherently integrated into a Gricean framework without collapsing the distinction between implicature and mere strategic ambiguity, or whether Elena’s stricter recognition condition is necessary to preserve that distinction. Finally, identify what both interlocutors appear to be missing about the relationship between speaker meaning and hearer recovery in adversarial contexts.
