I've been trying to work through a problem in collective judgment aggregation for a seminar I'm taking on institutional decision theory, specifically the discursive dilemma that arises when a group renders majority judgments on logically interrelated propositions and ends up with a jointly inconsistent set. The classic illustrations always involve a tiny committee voting simultaneously on premises and a conclusion, which makes the inconsistency feel like a logical parlor trick rather than a credible institutional failure. I'd like you to design a realistic thought experiment — no more than one paragraph, set in a concrete workplace or governance body with exactly six members — that forces the dilemma to emerge in a setting where people actually make substantive evaluative judgments.

Here are the specifications. The six members must judge three propositions — call them X, Y, and Z — arranged so that X and Y jointly entail Z, but majority judgments come out supporting X, supporting Y, and rejecting Z. The judgments should be genuinely evaluative (about risk, fairness, or permissibility) rather than purely arithmetic or factual. Crucially, inject a structural feature that is absent from the standard formulation: the members do not vote on all three propositions in a single session. Instead, they vote sequentially on X, then Y, then Z, with the outcome of each vote announced before the next begins, and with an explicit institutional rule allowing members to revise any earlier judgment in light of the later ones. Present the scenario, then demonstrate that the dilemma still arises — or transforms into a different kind of inconsistency — under this sequential disclosure condition. Walk through the reasoning for both the premise-based aggregation rule (aggregate judgments on X and Y, then derive the verdict on Z) and the conclusion-based rule (aggregate judgments on Z directly, treating X and Y as separate inputs), showing exactly which verdict each delivers after all three rounds. Identify the specific feature of the sequential structure that is doing the philosophical work — strategic anticipation, revision dynamics, or path dependence — and explain what happens if you strip away that feature and revert to simultaneous secret ballots with no revision permitted: does the case collapse back into the textbook simultaneous-vote version, or does something structurally distinctive remain? Finally, show how a defender of the premise-based approach would attack the conclusion-based approach in the particular institutional context of your scenario, and vice versa, without invoking generic appeals to democratic legitimacy, expert authority, or procedural fairness. Keep everything realistic — a hospital ethics committee, a regulatory board, or a university tenure panel — and avoid science fiction, perfect predictors, lottery-style reasoning, or contrived logical puzzles.
