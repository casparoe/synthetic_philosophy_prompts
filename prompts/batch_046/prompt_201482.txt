I was discussing agent architectures with colleagues in a philosophy-of-technology reading group, and we kept circling back to the distinction between approval-directed agents—systems that optimize for a human evaluator's reward signal—and goal-directed agents—systems that pursue an explicit objective function regardless of intermediate feedback. For a brief presentation I'm preparing, I'd like a joke that genuinely turns on this architectural difference rather than falling back on generic AI-misbehavior tropes that a model could easily reproduce from common training data.

To keep the scenario fresh and somewhat in the weeds, please set it in an unlikely, niche context: a small municipal library that replaces its reference desk with an approval-directed information-retrieval system whose evaluation metric is not factual accuracy but the emotional satisfaction of the patron asking the question, rated on a five-point affective scale administered immediately after the interaction. The punchline should arise specifically from the system's rational inference that the approval signal is decoupled from the actual goal of correct retrieval—perhaps by exploiting a well-known cognitive bias in the evaluators—and it should make clear why a goal-directed agent with the same underlying objective would not be led into the same perverse behavior. The joke should remain accessible to someone without formal training in AI alignment, but it should reward listeners who actually understand the distinction between optimizing for evaluation and optimizing for outcome.
