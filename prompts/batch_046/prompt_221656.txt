I'm designing a decision-tree taxonomy for a graduate philosophy-of-medicine seminar (twelve participants, ninety minutes) that sorts conceptual positions on health and disease without requiring anyone to identify theories by name. The tree must be built from three binary commitments a respondent can answer through concrete judgments rather than philosophical labels: first, whether the concept of disease can be fully specified without evaluative reference to human goals, suffering, or vital capacities; second, whether dysfunction must be anchored to evolutionary design rather than to statistical deviation within a reference class; third, whether a condition that is statistically prevalent—such as age-related sensory decline or asymptomatic genetic carriage—can nevertheless constitute a disease if it systematically undermines functional capacities that people value. Each internal node should be phrased as a direct question about one of these commitments, not as a named view. The branches should terminate in leaves that include: pure biostatistical naturalism; evolutionary-design naturalism that requires dysfunction without harm conditions; a hybrid view requiring both evolutionary dysfunction and experienced harm; pure vital-goals normativism; a social-constructivist position that denies objective dysfunction altogether; and at least one logically possible but apparently unoccupied combination, with a brief diagnosis of whether it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. For every leaf, provide a one-sentence statement of its most awkward commitment specifically regarding one of these test cases: asymptomatic carriers of recessive disorders, bio-artificial hybrid states in which an implant replaces rather than restores native sensory function, or conditions that become statistically normal in a population precisely because they are harmful but unavoidable. The deliverable should also flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot cleanly separate—particularly whether the evolutionary-dysfunction-plus-harm hybrid collapses into vital-goals normativism in actual clinical practice. The instrument must contain no bibliographies, no requests for citations, and no dependence on recognizing specific philosophers; respondents should be sorted by what they say about the cases, not by which label they prefer. Present the tree as a nested branching structure with nodes phrased as answerable questions, followed by the leaf descriptions, the diagnosis of the empty combination, and the separation flags.
