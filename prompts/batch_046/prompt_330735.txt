I was talking with some friends last weekend—none of us are historians—about how strange it is that the Athenian assembly could vote one day to spare every man in Mytilene and reverse itself the very next morning, yet still march into Sicily years later with the same overconfidence, and eventually condemn Socrates by a narrow margin after listening to emotional speeches. The usual account treats these as separate failures—mercy undone by rhetoric, imperial overreach, religious intolerance. But I keep returning to a more specific coupling: the very procedural openness that made the Mytilene reversal possible—immediate voice votes, no written record binding future assemblies, speakers rewarded for emotional urgency—seems structurally identical to the mechanism that prevented any institutional learning. The assembly could undo a mistake precisely because it had no memory architecture to preserve the reasoning behind the original decision, which is why it could repeat analogous errors in different contexts. I want a workshop design that lets people feel this coupling in their bodies before anyone gives it a name.

The setting is a community reading group of about ten adults, meeting for ninety minutes in a circle with no lecturer at the front. I need a structured deliberation—something like a simulated ekklesia with assigned speaker roles, hidden information cards, and real but modest stakes, perhaps pooled points that determine which charity receives a small donation or who chooses the next session's reading—so participants genuinely care about outcomes without being overwhelmed. The rules should enforce three successive crisis votes, with an explicit prohibition on referencing or binding earlier rounds: each vote is fresh, the assembly has no minutes, and speakers earn extra influence by appealing to urgency rather than consistency. Before the debrief, no one should hear the phrase "institutional amnesia" or be told they are replaying Athens; the design should make them experience how liberating it feels to reverse a harsh decision in round one, and how unsettlingly easy it becomes to escalate in round two using the same rhetorical moves, until by round three they find themselves closing off dissent in a way that mirrors the trial's emotional closure.

Please include precise roles—perhaps a presiding officer with limited power, two assigned rhetors who gain points for swaying the vote quickly, and silent citizens who may only speak if they surrender influence—along with a timeline showing when hidden information is revealed and when stakes escalate. The debrief must map each round onto the historical cases: the Mytilene reversal and its immediate erasure, the Sicilian expedition's disregard for prior warnings, and the Socratic trial's substitution of procedural openness for reflective judgment. Be frank about what this simulation cannot reproduce—the presence of enslaved labor that made citizen leisure possible, the specific charge of impiety, and the physical scale of death in Sicily—and tell me exactly where the facilitator must supply those missing dimensions verbally rather than through rules. I want the idea to emerge from the incentives and the forgetting, not from anything I say after the fact.
