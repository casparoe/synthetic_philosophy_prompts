Consider advance directives that specify not a particular medical intervention to refuse, but a qualitative threshold for a life worth continuing—such as retaining the capacity to recognize close relations or maintaining a minimal form of narrative self-continuity. In these cases, authority over the later self becomes complicated: surrogates must interpret whether the threshold is met, the later self may experience the condition differently than the earlier self anticipated, and standard accounts of personal identity may not clearly determine whether the directive binds. If you were directing a philosophy research group with about three years to work on the conceptual foundations of surrogate decision-making, which open questions in this area should they prioritize—e.g., the conditions under which qualitative thresholds retain normative authority, the relationship between interpretive ambiguity and the earlier self's rights, or the downstream implications for theories of diachronic personal identity—and in what order? Defend the ranking explicitly: for each, indicate whether it is upstream of practical policy design, how tractable it is for conceptual work without clinical data, and what kind of result over the next few years would move it up or down the list.
