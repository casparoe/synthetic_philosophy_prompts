I'm currently taking an upper-level seminar on Simone Weil's political philosophy, and I'm also interning with a civic-audit NGO that evaluates whether advocacy groups are functioning as democratic associations or as mechanisms of collective conformity. For my course assignment, I need to operationalize Weil's critique of political parties—not only the familiar claim from her essay on party suppression that organizations can substitute collective passion for individual judgment, but the more specific framework she develops across The Need for Roots and her writings on attention, where she distinguishes between strategic coordination (which preserves what she calls attention, the faculty she describes as the rarest and purest form of generosity) and what she terms an organization of collective blindness, which demands that members replace I think with we think on questions they have never personally examined.

Please construct a reusable audit procedure—ordered questions with pass/fail thresholds, evidence requirements, explicit handoff points to human judgment, and clear stop conditions—that an auditor without philosophical training could run in under two hours to determine whether a given civic group (formal parliamentary party, advocacy coalition, or local association) has become a Weilian party in this technical sense. The procedure must distinguish organizational discipline from the manufacturing of unexamined consensus; it must include specific tests for whether members are required to adopt positions they have not individually scrutinized; and it must account for the difference between rooted local work and national partisan affiliation.

The deliverable should be a checklist or flowchart in words, honest about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and it should specify exactly where the auditor's own values enter irreducibly. Then apply it to two cases: first, a formal parliamentary party with strict whip discipline and a published ideological platform; second, a grassroots mutual-aid collective that the auditor personally supports and that lacks formal party status, where the procedure should yield a negative determination that the asker will find unwelcome. Finally, state what the procedure cannot settle—particularly for hybrid organizations that combine rooted community service with partisan endorsement—and indicate where an auditor must stop and hand the decision to a person rather than to the protocol.
