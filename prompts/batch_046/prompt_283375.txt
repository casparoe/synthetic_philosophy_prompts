In The Querist, Berkeley presses a series of rhetorical questions about whether money consists essentially in gold and silver, or whether it functions rather as a counter or ticket for conveying goods and labor. He asks whether a nation might not be genuinely wealthy despite possessing little bullion, provided it commands abundant labor and commodities, and he treats banking instruments that circulate without metallic backing as objects of suspicion—suggesting that their reliability depends on some further guarantee linking them to productive capacity. I am trying to understand the precise logical structure of his concern: is he objecting to the absence of intrinsic value in the instrument itself, or to the absence of a transparent mechanism tying circulation back to real wealth?

To work this out, I want to test Berkeley's framework against an artificial but conceptually sharp case. Imagine an economic system in which the only circulating instruments are revocable claim-checks, each tied not to a commodity but to a specific quantum of past productive labor. These checks carry no value apart from social recognition of the underlying obligation; they can be cancelled if the corresponding labor is never performed; and they serve to settle debts, pay taxes, and purchase goods. Would Berkeley classify these as money—functional counters analogous to coin—or as defective simulacra that fail because they lack both intrinsic worth and a direct commodity anchor?

More generally, does Berkeley's account permit monetary instruments to be ontologically heterogeneous—some tracking goods, others tracking labor commitments, others tracking future tax obligations—while still serving a unified economic function? Or does his appeal to labor and land as real wealth imply that any legitimate counter must ultimately be reducible to, or at least transparently backed by, tangible productive assets?

Please do not provide a bibliography or references to modern monetary economics. I am looking for an interpretation drawn strictly from the internal logic of Berkeley's questions about backing, circulation, and the distinction between wealth and its tokens, together with an honest assessment of where that logic reaches its limit.
