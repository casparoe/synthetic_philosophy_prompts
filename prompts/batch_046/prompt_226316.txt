I've been working through recent debates about illusionism regarding phenomenal consciousness—the view that experience lacks any intrinsic "what-it's-like" quality and instead involves representational or functional states that we systematically misrepresent as carrying phenomenal properties. I keep noticing a specific dialectical pattern that I don't have a precise term for, and I'm hoping to understand whether it has been named, how it relates to nearby concepts, and where exactly its boundaries lie.

The pattern unfolds in three concrete steps. First, the illusionist denies first-order phenomenal properties—say, an intrinsic redness-quality—treating the visual experience of red as a representational state that misrepresents itself as possessing such a quality. Second, to explain why the illusion is so persistent and why ordinary people resist eliminativism, the illusionist appeals to the fact that it genuinely seems to subjects as though there is something it is like to see red. They characterize this seeming in purely functional or representational terms, perhaps as a higher-order monitoring state or a belief about one's own representational condition. Third, however, the explanatory force of that appeal still depends on there being a distinctive phenomenology of seeming: it doesn't feel like a cold functional misfiring, but rather like being struck by the presence of a quality. So the illusionist eliminates phenomenal consciousness at the object-level, yet seems to smuggle in a second-order phenomenal consciousness—a "what-it's-like" to have the illusion—in order to motivate why the illusion needs explaining at all. I have seen this structure appear in discussions of strong illusionism as well as in weaker representationalist frameworks, but I lack the vocabulary for the specific asymmetry.

Is there an established concept for this loop—denying phenomenal properties at the first-order while implicitly relying on them at the meta-level to ground the intuition of illusion? How does it differ from an ordinary self-refutation, and how is it related to but distinct from higher-order representation theories of consciousness that deliberately build second-order awareness into their architecture? If there is no specific name, what would be the appropriate way to characterize its boundary conditions: does it apply whenever an eliminativist theory uses introspective data to motivate the need for elimination, or is it narrower, confined specifically to cases where the meta-level state retains a phenomenological character that the theory officially denies? I want to know precisely where the loop closes, whether there is a principled way for the illusionist to break it without abandoning the core view, and what the nearest named failures of reasoning are, so I can see whether this is a unique problem for illusionism or a more general feature of eliminativist arguments about subjective experience.
