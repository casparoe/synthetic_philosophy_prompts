I am a software engineer without formal training in philosophy, and I have come to hold a unified-deception view about lying and misleading. The position is that the two are not morally different in kind: both are intentional manipulations of another agent’s belief-forming conditions through information asymmetry, and the only ethically relevant factor is the content and magnitude of the induced false belief, not the mechanism—whether it is a direct false assertion or a technically true statement embedded in a deceptive framing context. I am inclined to treat common intuitions that direct lying is worse as artifacts of speech-act convention, or of our social preference for explicit contractual transparency, analogous in software design to the difference between returning a false status code and returning a technically accurate but contextually misleading payload that causes the same downstream failure.

What would genuinely count against this view—not merely the familiar objection that lying uniquely violates autonomy or breaks an implicit promise of sincerity, but which concrete case intuitions, which boundary conditions drawn from other domains (for instance, legal doctrines distinguishing fraud by omission from affirmative misrepresentation, or the ethics of dark patterns in user-interface design), and which conceptual or formal results (for example, about the ontology of assertion, the logic of partial disclosure, or the structure of communicative intentions) would have to obtain for the unified view to fail? I want to know what predictions this view makes that its rivals do not, whether it has been insulated by qualifications that render it unfalsifiable, and which defeaters its own defenders would concede as damaging rather than dismissible. Please rank these from fatal—showing the view collapses into its rival or generates an unacceptable consequence—to merely awkward—requiring an ad hoc adjustment but leaving the core intact. In particular, is there any structurally irreducible harm in direct false assertion that framing and strategic omission cannot in principle replicate, and if not, does that indicate the view is well insulated or that it misses something important?
