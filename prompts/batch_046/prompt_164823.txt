Philosophers and clinicians often analogize pain to a building's integrated fire-alarm system: nociceptors are smoke sensors, the affective dimension is the audible siren, and protective withdrawal is the automated evacuation protocol. This analogy is then deployed to interpret pain asymbolia—patients who report tissue damage and locate sensation precisely, yet deny any aversive quality or urge to escape—by likening them to structures where detectors function and sirens sound, but the evacuation circuitry has been selectively disabled, allegedly proving that the badness of pain resides not in the sensory signal itself but in its downstream motivational coupling. Audit this analogy systematically. Provide the correspondence table mapping sensors, processor, alarm tone, command signal, and building architecture onto the nociceptive, affective, and behavioral components of pain. Then identify where the mapping fractures: for instance, whether treating asymbolia as a "disconnected siren" mischaracterizes the clinical presentation (which often involves intact sensory discrimination alongside a dissociated evaluative response), whether the analogy illicitly imports an instrumentalist view of affect that makes the badness of pain contingent on behavioral output, and whether it obscures the possibility that the "alarm" itself carries representational content about bodily threat that is already evaluative. Finally, determine whether the conclusion—that pain lacks intrinsic badness—survives once these disanalogies are explicit, or whether the analogy was doing all the argumentative work; if it collapses, propose what a more adequate model—perhaps one drawn from evaluative representation rather than control engineering—would need to include.
