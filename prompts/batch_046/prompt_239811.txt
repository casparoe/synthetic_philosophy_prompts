Throughout the module we have been distinguishing between "scalar rescaling"—the adjustment of utility indices within a single metric—and "dimensional translation", in which well-being is reconstructed across heterogeneous functioning spaces without collapsing them into one cardinal scale. The examination question was: "Can Sen's capability approach resolve the problem of interpersonal utility comparison without falling back on either an arbitrary scalar or a covert form of paternalism? In your answer, distinguish carefully between functionings and capabilities, and address whether dimensional translation genuinely eliminates the need for weighting or merely relocates it."

The student wrote the following: "Interpersonal utility comparison is problematic because utility is subjective and only ordinally measurable, so we cannot say that one person's gain exceeds another's without imposing an arbitrary scale. Harsanyi addressed this through an extended preference model in which an impartial observer compares overall situations, but this retains a single utility metric and risks paternalism by substituting the observer's judgements. Sen's capability approach improves matters by abandoning utility in favour of objective achievements—what people actually manage to do, which are called functionings. Because capabilities cover plural dimensions such as health, education and mobility, they allow comparison across individuals without reducing welfare to one number, thereby avoiding scalar rescaling entirely. Once welfare is translated into separate dimensions, there is no further need to weight them against one another, since each can be evaluated independently; consequently, dimensional translation removes both arbitrariness and paternalism, as no external agent must rank one functioning above another."
