I keep seeing this pattern in disaster reports and museum exhibits where big non-human events -- like the 1815 Tambora eruption, COVID waves, or the Little Ice Age -- get treated as actual historical actors with agency. They "cause" famines, they "reshape" politics, they "drive" migration. Meanwhile the human stuff that turns a bad harvest into mass starvation -- grain requisition policies, colonial extraction, hospital funding cuts -- gets filed under "vulnerability context" or "mediating factors." Its like the germ or the climate is the protagonist and the social system is just backdrop.

What philosophical commitments does that framing presuppose? Is it assuming that only things breaking a statistical baseline count as real causes? Or that agency requires being the kind of thing that can violate expectations? And is the practice even consistent, given that in economic reports we never say "the recession acted on unemployment" -- we always point to specific policy choices?

I'm also wondering if this quietly depends on a specific view of counterfactual dependence -- like, we call Tambora a cause because if it hadn't erupted the harvest wouldn't have failed, but we don't say the same about feudal tax law because that was "already there." Does that distinction actually hold up? And what would disaster reporting have to change if we adopted a flat causal model where human institutions and volcanic winters are always co-primary actors, not background versus foreground?

There's also this weird asymmetry: climate scientists talk about anthropogenic forcing as human agency, but when they discuss past climate shifts they treat them like exogenous shocks. So is the practice philosophically underdetermined -- could it fit both an interventionist causation view and a kind of naive enviromental determinism -- or does it strictly commit to one? If we dropped the implicit determinism, would we have to rewrite how we assign blame in famines, or just change the narrative framing?
