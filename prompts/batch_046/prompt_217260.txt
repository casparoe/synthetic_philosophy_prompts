I'm in my final year of secondary school, and for my capstone philosophy seminar I have been working with a mentor on issues in metaphysics rather than ethics. This past week we turned to the special composition question, and my study partner constructed an argument that seems to show that not only do bizarre scattered objects fail to exist, but also that I myself am not a unified being—only a temporary arrangement of smaller parts. The reasoning proceeds in numbered steps: (1) For parts to genuinely compose a whole, the whole must stand in an asymmetric relation of ontological dependence to its parts—that is, the parts ground the whole, but the whole does not ground the parts—while also possessing at least one property that is not fully determined by the properties and relations of the parts taken separately. (2) In every apparent case of composition, whether a table, a cell, or a human body, every property of the supposed whole—mass, shape, biological function, even consciousness—either is fully determined by the arranged parts or is an epistemic projection we make onto the arrangement. (3) Therefore no whole possesses a property that exceeds what its parts provide, which means the relation in (1) collapses into mereological redundancy: the "whole" is nothing over and above the parts arranged that way. (4) But if the whole is nothing over and above the parts, then saying "there is an organism" adds no ontological commitment beyond saying "there are cells arranged organism-wise," and the same applies to artifacts and social groups. (5) By parity, unrestricted universalism is false because scattered parts lack even relational coherence, and restricted composition is false because the coherence of arranged parts never yields an additional entity. (6) Hence nihilism about composite objects is true, and I am not genuinely one thing but merely a sequence of atomic configurations. I find (6) impossible to accept, yet I cannot see which premise fails. I am particularly attached to (1), since without some asymmetry and some emergent determination, composition seems like a useless label. Is there a subtle equivocation between "determined by" and "identical to," or perhaps an illicit modal slide between "could not exist without these parts" and "is nothing but these parts"? Please diagnose the precise failure—whether in (2), (3), or elsewhere—and then explain what unrestricted universalism, restricted composition, and mereological nihilism each must concede or pay in order to reject that particular step, especially regarding how they treat biological organisms. I am writing this without expecting citations or a reading list; I want a conceptual analysis I can follow without searching for sources.
