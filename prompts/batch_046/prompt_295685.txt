In the philosophy of animal ethics and philosophy of mind, a long-running dispute about whether moral status tracks sentience continuously or only at a sharp threshold has reached what looks like an impasse. On one side, gradualists argue that there is a graded continuum running from simple feedback systems—thermostats with homeostatic loops—through insects with nociception but questionable phenomenal consciousness, up to mammals with integrated subjective experience; on this view, moral consideration attenuates smoothly, so an insect merits less protection than a pig, and a thermostat merits only the faintest derivative concern, if any. On the other side, thresholdists insist that direct moral status is binary: either an entity possesses phenomenal consciousness and thereby has interests that can be harmed, or it lacks them entirely; functional complexity without subjective experience—whether in a thermostat or, they often add, in an insect if its nociception is purely reflexive—generates no direct obligations, though indirect ones may remain.

The stalemate is peculiar. Both camps accept the same empirical literature on nociception and neural architecture; both agree that suffering is bad and that thermostats are not conscious. The gradualists do not claim that insects suffer as intensely as humans, only that they suffer somewhat, and the thresholdists do not deny that insects behave as if in pain. Yet neither side has moved in decades. My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is not empirical but structural: the gradualists are asking how to distribute a scalar moral weight across a scalar descriptive property, while the thresholdists are asking whether the concept of moral status can apply at all without a subject of experience, and these are different questions being pursued under the single heading of sentience as a matter of degree.

Is my diagnosis correct? I want an honest assessment of what kind of disagreement this actually is—whether it is verbal, centered on ambiguous uses of sentience and interest; substantive but hidden, about whether moral reasons must be grounded in phenomenal states; or bedrock, resting on incompatible intuitions about continuity versus discontinuity in nature. I also want you to say whether there is a hidden formal or empirical question doing the work—for instance, whether the discovery of global workspace dynamics in insects would resolve anything, or whether a precise formal model of moral aggregation could settle the normative structure. If the impasse is real and not illusory, what would count as progress: a conceptual distinction that separates descriptive gradation from normative status, a change in the framing question, or an argument that one side is simply answering a different problem? And if you think no progress is possible—because the dispute is ultimately about whether morality requires a subject—explain why, and say whether that makes the debate empty or merely intractable.

Please do not survey every position in the literature; instead, engage the two sides I have described, diagnose the stall using what they actually say about continuity, functional equivalence, and the conditions for having interests, and tell me whether anything—an experiment, a theorem, a distinction—could actually move it.
