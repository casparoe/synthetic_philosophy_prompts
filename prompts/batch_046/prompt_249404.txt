I am designing a taxonomy handout for a graduate-level seminar in moral and political philosophy that asks whether commonplace conventions—tipping service workers, queuing for public goods, and obeying or violating pedestrian-signal norms—amount to genuine moral obligations, and whether temporary visitors are bound by the local versions they encounter. Rather than organizing the tree around named theorists, I want it constructed from the logical space of answers: each branching node should present a concrete case or normative claim on which positions actually split, phrased so that someone can classify themselves without knowing philosophical labels in advance.

Begin at the root with the question of whether these conventions carry any moral weight independent of legal enforcement or mere etiquette. From there, split by the source of any such weight—whether it arises from implicit consent, distributive fairness, or harm-avoidance—then by scope: whether the obligation applies only to members of the local practice or also to outsiders and tourists. Add a further branch distinguishing physical, face-to-face conventions from algorithm-mediated equivalents, such as in-app delivery tipping or virtual queue systems, asking whether digitization removes the moral salience or merely relocates it. Each terminal leaf should be labeled either with a recognized position or with 'unnamed,' accompanied by a single sentence stating its most awkward or counterintuitive commitment—for instance, a view that must treat all queue-jumping as seriously wrong even when no one is delayed, or one that denies visitors any obligation to adapt despite clear local expectations.

Please include roughly eight to ten leaves, flag any pair that your taxonomy cannot genuinely separate, and mark any logically available combination that remains unoccupied, with a brief diagnosis of whether it is incoherent, unexplored, or simply unattractive. The audience is familiar with basic normative ethics but has not specialized in the philosophy of social norms, so avoid relying on highly technical apparatus from game theory or legal philosophy.
