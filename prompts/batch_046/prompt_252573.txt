In philosophical commentary on Hobbes, one frequently encounters the claim that his political philosophy operates more geometrico — that Leviathan constructs the commonwealth with the same demonstrative necessity with which Euclid constructs a triangle. Proponents use this to argue that Hobbes's conclusions about sovereignty are not contingent political preferences but deductions as secure as any in mathematics; critics reply that politics lacks the axiomatic clarity required for geometric proof. Be specific about Hobbes's own texts and concepts — for example, his treatment of the state of nature, the laws of nature, and the generation of the sovereign.

Second, examine the disanalogies that matter for the intended conclusion. Here you should attend to Hobbes's distinctive view, developed in his controversies with Wallis and elsewhere, that geometric objects alone are genuinely demonstrable because they are generated by the geometer's own motion, whereas natural bodies are merely observed. Does this mean the commonwealth, if it is to be genuinely 'generated' in the geometric sense, requires a kind of constructive act that Hobbes's theory of representation and consent cannot supply? Finally, propose whether a different analogy — perhaps to mechanics, or to the synthetic method in seventeenth-century natural philosophy — captures Hobbes's actual procedure more faithfully, or whether the geometric comparison was always intended as normative rather than descriptive.
