I was recently appointed to a regional disaster-ethics committee drafting triage protocols for catastrophic flooding, which has made abstract debates about rationing suddenly concrete and personally urgent. In a planning brief circulated to the committee, an unnamed consultant summarized Garrett Hardin’s lifeboat ethics approximately as follows: wealthy nations are lifeboats with a fixed carrying capacity—Hardin specified fifty occupants with room for ten more—while impoverished regions are like swimmers in open water. The consultant claims that Hardin proved admitting even one passenger past capacity triggers a nonlinear structural collapse: once the load exceeds roughly 110 percent of design limits, integrity fails catastrophically and everyone drowns, so the ethical rule must be unconditional refusal regardless of whether physical rescue is feasible. The summary further asserts that lifeboat ethics is strictly identical to the tragedy of the commons applied to human migration, meaning individual charitable admissions are rational for each actor but collectively suicidal, and therefore any policy permitting conditional admission—such as temporary shelter coupled with mandatory return—is self-defeating because it establishes a precedent that accelerates overloading. The consultant concludes that this framework mandates absolute closure as the only rational response to climate displacement, and implies that distinguishing between food aid, physical migration, and different boat capacities is a sentimental evasion rather than part of the theory itself.

Please audit this summary claim by claim. For each part, distinguish what accurately reflects Hardin’s 1974 argument, what is a defensible popular simplification, what is a subtle conflation with other resource-degradation models, and what is an imported error—particularly regarding the alleged 110 percent nonlinear collapse threshold, the equivalence between conditional admission and systemic failure, and the suppression of distinctions among types of aid. I also need to know whether the inference of unconditional refusal actually follows from Hardin’s premises, or whether it overstates them, since I must decide which sentences can be responsibly cited in a public report and which must be discarded before we present our recommendations.
