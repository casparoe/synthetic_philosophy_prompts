For a philosophy workshop of about 15 advanced students with 80 minutes, design an in-class simulation on advance directives and the authority of the earlier self. Center the scenario on a patient who executed a refusal of life-extending care, then underwent neurocognitive change; she now consistently affirms life, enjoys her present state, and explicitly rejects her earlier values as belonging to "someone else who was afraid." Inject the framing of a "value-successor" — a psychologically continuous but axiologically discontinuous agent — so participants treat the conflict not as simple preference reversal but as a potential inter-personal authority dispute within one body. Structure three rounds: first, students act as an ethics committee allocating "binding authority tokens" between the planner-self and the successor-self with required justifications; second, a forced-choice round in which every criterion they invoke (psychological continuity, narrative identity, present welfare, sovereignty) is shown either to fail to distinguish the two sharply or to collapse them into one; third, a secret ballot on whether the directive binds, protects, or is void, revealed only after reasoning is shared. Specify roles or rules, materials, and a payoff or scoring scheme that needs no real money. Include a debrief that maps token distributions and vote patterns onto philosophical positions without treating labels as mandatory, an honest note on which feature of diachronic authority the token mechanism cannot reproduce and that the facilitator must supply, and a prediction of what will predictably misfire the first time the activity is run. Keep it fully runnable without outside research, and do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
