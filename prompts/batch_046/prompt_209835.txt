Two fellow graduate students in our ethics workshop have been arguing over whether the separateness of persons objection to utilitarianism can be answered by distinguishing intra-personal from inter-personal aggregation. I am drafting a dissertation chapter on distributive reasoning and want an outside adjudication of their exchange. Here is what was said, reconstructed from my notes.

(1) The first student said that the classic objection—associated with Rawls and Nozick—rests on a category mistake. Utilitarianism does not literally fuse consciousnesses; it merely assigns equal weight to each person's welfare. The boundary between persons remains intact as a bookkeeping device; only the moral arithmetic crosses it. Therefore the charge that utilitarianism fails to take the separateness of persons seriously is misdirected: it takes them seriously enough to count each one, but refuses to grant any single person veto power over aggregate welfare.

(2) The second student replied that bookkeeping is precisely the problem. To treat my suffering and your suffering as entries in a single column—interchangeable, summable, and subject to net maximization—is to treat me as a mere site or container for welfare rather than as a distinct subject whose claims are non-fungible. The fact that the books are kept separately does not change the fact that one person's entry can be cancelled by another's surplus.

(3) The first student countered that every normative theory aggregates across persons in some form. Even deontological rights theories must weigh competing rights-claims—say, one person's right to life against another's right to self-defense—and reach a distributive verdict. The real distinction is not between aggregation and non-aggregation, but between constrained and unconstrained aggregation. Utilitarianism applies the same constraint to everyone—equal consideration—so it respects separateness as well as any rival theory.

(4) The second student then argued that there is a morally relevant asymmetry that the first student ignores. Within a single life, we routinely permit present suffering for future benefit because the same subject bears both costs and gains; this is not a violation of separateness but its internal expression. By contrast, inter-personal utilitarian trade-offs impose suffering on one subject for another subject's benefit with no continuity of identity. Collapsing this asymmetry—treating the two cases as structurally identical—is precisely what it means to deny the separateness of persons.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move. For each of the four moves, rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify the rating. Then say who, if anyone, is closer to being right overall, and identify anything important that both parties are missing—particularly with respect to whether the objection survives if we accept a narrative or temporal-parts conception of personal identity, and whether the intra/inter distinction can be sustained without smuggling in a metaphysical commitment that the utilitarian can reject.
