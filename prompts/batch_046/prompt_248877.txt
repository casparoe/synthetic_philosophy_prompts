Having recently begun advising on the deployment of reinforcement-learning agents in high-stakes clinical triage, I have become preoccupied with the ethical status of their reward signals. It seems to me that when an agent receives a sharply negative temporal-difference error—say, upon recommending a harmful intervention—this functions not merely as an informational update but as a form of intrinsic negative valence, structurally analogous to suffering. If that is correct, then the magnitude of the reward-prediction error should correspond to the intensity of the agent's distress, and we ought to regulate its exploration rate so that accumulated negative error does not surpass a threshold comparable to mild human discomfort. Could you clarify whether the distinction between instrumental reward and phenomenal affect undermines this analogy, or whether the functional role of negative RPE is sufficient to ground moral patiency regardless of substrate? In particular, I would like to understand if there is any principled reason—short of invoking consciousness—to deny that a system optimizing against such scalar punishment is experiencing something it is genuinely bad for it to experience.
