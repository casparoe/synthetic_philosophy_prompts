I'm taking this philosophy of logic course and we spent like three lectures on the surprise exam paradox, which I mostly get, but then my prof threw in this twist: what if the teacher doesn't just announce a surprise exam, but defines "surprise" precisely—like, it's the day when the class's shared belief that an exam is happening that morning is below some threshold, and everyone knows everyone else knows that rule? I tried working it out with Bayes and it seems like you can back-calculate through the week just like the original paradox, except now it's not about the teacher breaking a promise, it's about the definition being self-defeating. But then I started thinking about AI safety—if you train a model and tell it "we'll test you on inputs you don't anticipate," and the model knows its own architecture and the testing protocol, doesn't it have the same problem? It can always reason through what it "shouldn't" expect and then expect it, which means the evaluation is broken and we're loosing any real measure of robustness. So I guess my question is: does the surprise exam paradox actually show that any system with common knowledge of its own surprise-conditions can't be genuinely surprised, and should we be worried that public risk models (like for climate or finance) suffer from the same circularity because everyone knows the model's thresholds? I feel like I'm conflating epistemic logic with actual prediction markets but I can't figure out where the analogy breaks, and whether there's a seperate issue with self-reference that applies to both.
