I am designing a single fifty-minute tutorial for first-year philosophy undergraduates on whether the number of people saved should determine rescue priority, centred on John Taurek’s argument that numerical superiority does not create a stronger collective claim. I need a teaching plan rather than an explanation of the view itself. Please structure your response in the following sections, in this order, with the approximate lengths indicated.

1. Dependency chain (about 180 words): The exact order in which conceptual pieces must be introduced so that Taurek’s conclusion is reachable rather than shocking. Specify what must come before the aggregation intuition is named, why the distinction between comparative harm and non-comparative moral weight needs to be secured first, and how to frame the idea of the separateness of persons so it functions as a premise rather than a slogan. Indicate where in this chain the standard utilitarian calculus should be presented — not as a straw man, but as a genuinely attractive position that makes the counter-move intelligible.

2. The carrying scenario (about 160 words): The single concrete case that should bear the entire discussion. It must involve one indivisible rescue resource and two groups of unequal size, with no differences in age, culpability, or identifiability, so that emotional partiality and merit-based distinctions are both neutralised. Explain why a modified bridge-collapse scenario with rescue boats works better here than the classic burning-building or lifeboat examples, and specify exactly what the example must include (identical prognosis, simultaneous need, no relational ties) and what it must deliberately exclude.

3. Misconception map (about 260 words): The misconceptions students will bring or construct, in probable order of appearance. I anticipate at least: (a) that rejecting aggregation is equivalent to denying basic arithmetic; (b) that if numbers do not count, a fair coin-flip becomes morally obligatory; (c) that the view secretly licenses egoism whenever the rescuer is among the potential victims; and (d) that Taurek is advocating indifference rather than equal consideration of each claim. For each, identify the precise moment in the dependency chain where it should be pre-empted, and provide the single question or distinction that neutralises it without derailing the arc. Include one less obvious misconception — perhaps that equal consideration requires treating groups as if they were single composite persons — and show where it hides.

Understanding check (about 120 words): One specific exercise or question for the final ten minutes that distinguishes students who have grasped the underlying structure — that each person’s claim is non-cumulative and does not aggregate into a stronger collective claim — from those who have only memorised that saving the larger group is not always obligatory. The check should work without requiring students to reproduce Taurek’s terminology.

5. Explicit exclusions (about 100 words): What must be openly left out at this level and acknowledged with a brief phrase. I have in mind: the formal decision-theoretic framing of rescue choices; the comparison with contractualist or Scanlonian accounts; and the recent literature on ex ante versus ex post evaluation of risk. For each, state the one-sentence boundary I should draw, so that students know the omission is deliberate rather than an oversight.

Write in British English. Do not include any bibliography, reading list, or citation request. Do not ask for quotations from texts; paraphrase any reference to Taurek’s position. The plan should assume I can deliver it without access to searchable sources.
