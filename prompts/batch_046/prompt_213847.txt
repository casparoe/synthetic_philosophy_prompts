I am applying for a one-year interdisciplinary fellowship that supports fiction writers developing philosophically rigorous world-building, and I need a polished two-page research proposal. My novel-in-progress imagines a generation-ship society that, after centuries of autonomous development, must decide whether to establish permanent settlements on a planet with a pre-existing microbial biosphere. Rather than asking whether colonization is morally permissible in the abstract, I want to investigate whether the ethics of space colonization constitutes a single unified problem or several irreducible ones—specifically, whether obligations to future inhabitants, obligations to Earth-based originators, and obligations to non-sentient but functioning ecosystems can be governed by one principle or necessarily require distinct ethical vocabularies. To make the question tractable, I propose constructing a three-part taxonomy—replicative settlement (reproducing Earth social institutions in closed habitats), extractive presence (temporary resource use with minimal ecological footprint), and transformative redesign (deliberately engineering both biological and political systems for off-world conditions)—and then stress-testing whether any unified framework, such as an expanded stewardship principle or a contractarian model, can cover all three types without contradiction or arbitrary exclusion. Write the proposal with the following elements: a precise statement of the fracture question and why treating colonization ethics as monolithic obscures more than it reveals; an explanation of why empirical case studies alone cannot settle the conceptual division; the method, month by month (taxonomy construction, principle selection, stress-testing across cases, synthesis); a description of what a successful result looks like (either a unified framework with explicit scope conditions, or a demonstrated impossibility proof with clear boundaries between domains); and what failure would mean (the taxonomy collapses into one dominant case, or the principles prove too vague to distinguish obligations). The deliverable should be accessible to both philosophers and policy readers; do not include a bibliography, literature review by citation, or reading list, as I will supply those myself. Begin with the proposal itself, with no preface, commentary, or restatement of the prompt.
