A recent popular essay summarizes prioritarianism—the view that benefiting a person carries greater moral weight the worse off that person is—in the following terms. The essay claims that prioritarianism assigns every individual a moral weight governed by a convex curve over well-being, so that the lower one's well-being, the steeper the marginal value of an improvement, which implies that even a trivial gain near the bottom always outweighs an enormous gain near the top. From this it infers that prioritarianism must require continuous downward redistribution until well-being is fully equalized, and that it therefore faces a mirror-image difficulty the author dubs the reverse leveling-up objection: any policy or innovation that disproportionately benefits the already well-off, even if it harms no one, is strictly forbidden. The essay concludes that prioritarianism is therefore just egalitarianism without the classic leveling-down problem but burdened by an equally implausible leveling-up counterpart, and that the view collapses into the same errors it sought to avoid.

Separate what accurately captures prioritarian reasoning—such as the idea of diminishing marginal moral value and the rejection of equality as an intrinsic good—from standard simplifications and from substantive misrepresentations. In particular, address whether the weighting function is correctly characterized, whether prioritarianism genuinely demands full equality, and whether the so-called reverse leveling-up objection is actually entailed. Finally, evaluate whether the essay's conclusion—that prioritarianism is disguised egalitarianism refuted by its own mirror-image problem—survives once the misrepresentations are corrected.
