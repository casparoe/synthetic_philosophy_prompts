Write the closing remarks for a workshop titled Justice Without Certainty, delivered to an audience split evenly between moral philosophers and criminal-justice practitioners. The philosophical idea must do real structural work, not decoration: organize the speech around the tension between retributivism—punishment as giving wrongdoers exactly the suffering or deprivation they deserve, independent of downstream effects—and consequentialism—punishment justified only by its future utility, such as deterrence, incapacitation, or rehabilitation. Inject and develop one somewhat niche but conceptually sharp conflict: in predictive sentencing algorithms, consequentialist optimization tends toward aggregate risk-pooling, where individuals are assigned harsher penalties based on high-base-rate group membership rather than on individually assessed culpability, which systematically violates the retributivist proportionality constraint that desert must be personal and backward-looking. Your remarks should be intelligible to the practitioners without losing the philosophers—land the logical point clearly, avoid jargon that requires a literature review, and do not ask for citations, bibliographies, or reading lists. The tone should be formal, about five minutes spoken, and written from the perspective of someone who has spent years in rationalist forums thinking about decision theory and institutional design—skeptical of both naive moral realism and purely instrumental governance, but convinced that the distinction between what people deserve and what produces the best outcomes is not merely semantic.
