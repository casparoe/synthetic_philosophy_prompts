Your commitments: the brain operates through analog, statistical logic rather than digital formal logic; its memory is distributed, address-less, and sustained by massive redundancy and local error correction; organizational parallels to self-reproducing automata exist, yet the brain employs a fundamentally different language and mode of computation; you take the McCulloch-Pitts formalization seriously but regard it as only partial. My opening challenge: consider a hypothetical artificial neural lattice exhibiting what I call retrograde coherence locking. Once activated, downstream modules emit inhibitory feedback that stabilizes upstream oscillatory patterns, but only when the incoming signal crosses a specific non-linear entropy threshold. There is no central clock, no symbolic pointer, and no address-based retrieval mechanism; binding between modules is purely dynamical and statistical, maintained by distributed redundancy and local noise correction. One could implement this architecture inside a conventional digital computer—statistical gates, analog-style noise tolerance, distributed stabilization—running a fixed program. Given that both the brain and this lattice are stochastic, distributed, address-less systems with dynamical rather than representational binding, does your distinction between brain and computer not collapse? What specific resource in your actual view allows you to maintain that the brain is not merely running its own program?
