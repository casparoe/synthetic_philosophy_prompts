I've been trying to figure out if honor, dignity, and victimhood cultures are actually morally different, or if they just repackage the same instincts with different branding. Here are my verdicts, going by gut.

In an honor culture, a guy kills his sister's insulter to defend family status. I think thats clearly wrong, but I also feel the internal logic - he's protecting a shared social asset. In a dignity culture, every person has inherent worth, which sounds right, but then I notice dignity cultures still run on hidden status games, just masked by merit talk. So dignity feels universal but often hollow. In victimhood culture, using old trauma to shut down a present argument feels toxic. But recognizing that some people carry scars others dont feels necessary and good.

Now the hard cases. I think honor-based loyalty - like standing by your friend even when its costly - is genuinely admirable. But I also think a dignity culture that builds elaborate bureacratic rituals to document every dignity violation is basically victimhood with paperwork. And heres the made-up case I cant place: a society where people accumulate 'moral credit' for enduring injustice, which they can spend to demand deference. Is that victimhood culture? Or honor culture inverted? I keep going back and forth.

I'm looking for one principle, or maybe a small family of them, that captures why I praise loyalty but condemn killing, why dignity feels hollow but still better than open hierarchy, and why victimhood recognition is good but 'moral credit' spending feels wrong. It should also tell me what to say about the bureacratic-ritual case. Be honest: which of my verdicts has to go? Does the principle say my sympathy for honor logic is just nostalgia, or that my dislike of moral-credit societies is inconsistent? And what does it predict for a case where someone in a dignity culture refuses to acknowledge any special burden from past harm - is that principled equality or moral blindness?
