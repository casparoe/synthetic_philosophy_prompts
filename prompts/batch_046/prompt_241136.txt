As a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy of science, I am trying to understand whether thought experiments can actually yield new scientific knowledge, or whether they only reorganize commitments we already hold. Please write a dialogue in American English between two equally competent interlocutors—Dr. Chen and Dr. Alvarez—who hold opposing views on this specific question.

Dr. Chen should defend the position that thought experiments generate knowledge by functioning like isolated mental sandboxes: they strip away confounding variables and expose necessary structural relationships that empirical observation alone, with all its noise, cannot cleanly isolate. Dr. Alvarez should argue that thought experiments are essentially compressed logical arguments whose conclusions are already implicit in the premises or in prior empirical data; she should specifically deny that running a mental model without any physical instantiation can produce genuinely new epistemic content, and she should challenge any analogy to software debugging or system isolation.

The dialogue must contain real back-and-forth rather than parallel monologues: each speaker should respond directly to the other's previous point. To keep the discussion away from well-worn examples such as Galileo's falling bodies or Einstein's elevator, center the exchange on a niche but fully self-contained scenario: two distributed quantum clocks that attempt to maintain coherence using only local operations, with no direct communication channel between them. Have the interlocutors debate whether mentally working through this "quantum synchronization" case reveals a novel constraint on non-locality, or whether it merely unpacks relationships already present in the formal mathematics.

Please ensure neither character is a strawman. Include at least three rounds of direct exchange in which one raises a concrete objection—such as hidden premises embedded in the mental model, underdetermination of the scenario's conclusions, or the breakdown of the debugging analogy when no code is actually executed—and the other answers that objection specifically rather than changing the subject. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, citations, or references to specific philosophical texts, authors, or page numbers. Maintain a formal and polished tone throughout, and use American English spelling and conventions exclusively.
