I was arguing with my roommate after our intro philosophy of mind course mentioned epiphenomenalism in passing, and he went and listened to a podcast that summed up the view together with something called the paradox of phenominal judgment. He said epiphenomenalism holds that mental states—like feeling pain or seeing red—are fully caused by brain activity, but they don't cause anything physical in return, not even your own speech. Then he explained the paradox: if consciousness doesn't cause your belief that you're conscious, then a zombie without any inner life could still have that exact belief, so the belief is never actually justified by the experience it's about. From this he said it follow that when an epiphenomenalist reports being in pain, the pain plays zero causal role in producing the report, which means the speaker is basically lying or at least has no legitimate ground for saying it. He also brought up those weird anesthesia-awareness cases where patients later remember being awake and feeling surgery while totally paralyzed, arguing that if mental states were truly causally inert then those reports couldn't be sincere or accurate, so the view collapses. Something about this feels like it's mixing up knowledge problems with honesty problems, but I can't tell exactly where the errors are. Is this summary fair? Which parts match what philosophers actually say about the judgment paradox, which parts conflate it with other objections, and does his point about lying and the medical cases actually refute anything? I'd especially like to know what a defender would reply about those clinical reports.
