I was sitting in on a graduate seminar in evolutionary theory where two students, Mara and Leo, got into a sustained argument about evolutionary transitions in individuality—specifically whether the origin of multicellularity supports multilevel selection or is fully reducible to gene-level dynamics. I stayed out of it, but I genuinely could not tell who was carrying the stronger point. Here is approximately how their exchange went, reconstructed from my notes.

Mara opened by saying that during a transition like the move to multicellularity, within-group selection is actively suppressed—cheater cells that refuse to cooperate are eliminated—while between-group selection favors cooperative clusters. She argued that if genes were truly the only unit of selection, we should expect cheaters to invade and destroy cooperation every time, because any gene that promotes selfish replication within the cluster should outcompete policing genes locally. The fact that multicellularity evolved repeatedly and remains stable, she said, shows that selection was operating at the group level: the cluster itself had heritable fitness differences, and that is the defining feature of a unit of selection.

Leo replied that this commits the averaging fallacy. The suppression of cheating, he said, is not a mysterious group-level force but is implemented by specific gene-encoded mechanisms—germ-soma separation, tumor-suppressor pathways, and kin-recognition loci. Those policing genes spread precisely because they benefit the genome that carries them, even if they impose costs on individual cells. So what looks like group-level selection is really gene-level selection for linkage and policing architecture. He added that calling this “group selection” adds no predictive power: both frameworks predict that multicellularity evolves only when genetic architecture can suppress internal conflict. The gene-level view just refuses to reify the cluster as an autonomous unit.

Mara countered that Leo’s move makes multilevel selection unfalsifiable by definition. If every apparent group-level pattern can be redescribed as gene-level policing after the fact, then the gene-centric view wins by semantic fiat, not by empirical test. She said that in actual ETIs, causal efficacy is distributed—what Sober and Wilson call causal democracy—so arbitrarily privileging the gene as “the real” unit is an ontological prejudice rather than a scientific conclusion.

Leo answered that “causal democracy” describes multi-level causation, not a tie for unit status. The unit of selection, he insisted, is defined by heritability and fitness variance at a given level. Genes still have far higher fidelity of replication and far more direct fitness effects than transient cell clusters, so they remain the primary replicators even when vehicles are nested. He conceded that clusters are vehicles, but insisted that being a vehicle is not the same as being a unit of selection.

Mara then asked whether Leo’s distinction between replicators and vehicles actually resolves anything empirically. If the policing mechanisms are themselves subject to mutation and breakdown—cancer being the obvious example—doesn’t that show that the cluster-level environment is doing selective work that gene-level descriptions miss? Leo said cancer proves the opposite: it is precisely the failure of gene-encoded policing that destroys the cluster, confirming that the stability of the transition depends on genetic architecture, not on an independent group-level selective regime.
