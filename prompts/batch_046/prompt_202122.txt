I was arguing with my friends about that recent biopic where they basically invented a secret affair for a well-known 19th-century reformer just to explain her political drive, and I found myself defending this position: since dead people don't have interests or standing, dramatizing them can't harm them directly, and every historical account is already selective interpretation, so there's no principled line between "responsible" and "irresponsible" fiction except getting the checkable public facts right. If the movie gets the speeches and election results correct but gives her a totally fabricated inner life or romantic subplot, that's just artistic license—no different from borrowing a real name for a fictional character. The only duty we owe is not to misrepresent verifiable events in ways that serve present-day ideology.

So I'm asking: what would genuinely count against this view? Not just the gut reaction that it "feels disrespectful"—i want to know whether there's a coherent objection that survives once you deny the dead are moral patients. Could it be that misrepresenting someone's moral biography corrupts our collective historical memory even if no one is hurt? Does it matter if descendants or living communities see their heritage distorted? And is there an epistemic risk—like, if we allow enough invented psychological motivation, do we eventually lose the capacity to learn from the past altogether? I'd like you to rank these defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, say which ones a defender of my view would actually concede, and tell me honestly whether the position has been insulated into unfalsifiability or if it genuinely risks something.
