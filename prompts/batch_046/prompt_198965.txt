Read Tolstoy's The Death of Ivan Ilyich through the lens of the Epicurean argument that death is not bad for the one who dies, since there is no subject remaining to suffer harm. Focus on Ivan's final days: he moves from terror of annihilation to acceptance, and the narrative frames his prior existence as wasted in bureaucratic pretense. Does Tolstoy present Ivan's fear as a rational response to the loss of future goods, or as a conceptual mistake about non-existence? In particular, consider whether the novel introduces a harm that Epicureanism struggles to capture—not experiential suffering after death, but the disintegration of narrative coherence in Ivan's retrospective judgment that his life had missed its essential purpose. Would an Epicurean reply that the terror is irrational because there will be no remaining self to be harmed, or does the text suggest the damage lies in the truncation of a self that never achieved meaning? Refer to the black sack and the final illumination, explain where the philosophical reading clarifies the scene, and say where the novel resists being treated as a mere illustration of Epicurus.
