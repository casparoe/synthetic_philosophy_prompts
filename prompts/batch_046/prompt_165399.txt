In my graduate seminar on historical epistemology we have been tracing the evolution of source criticism from Herodotus—whose method relies on direct inquiry, the examination of visible monuments, and the critical sifting of oral testimony—through Thucydides, who introduced a stricter asymmetry by privileging contemporary eyewitness accounts, distrusting poetic embellishment, and treating retrospective reconstruction with systematic suspicion. Our present unit concerns what I call the “silent” distant past: those centuries or millennia prior to durable textual transmission, where inference about social organization depends almost entirely on archaeological traces and on structural analogies drawn from better-documented later societies. Last week we examined a skeptical argument regarding the presence of certain complex institutional practices in deep prehistory—practices for which there is no direct material signature in the surviving record, though their organizational logic can be reconstructed indirectly from subsequent societal formations. A student resisted this skepticism by invoking the familiar maxim: “Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence.” I want to understand precisely what this slogan is being made to do in that specific argumentative context, rather than treating it as a generic principle of philosophy of science.

Can you lay out the distinct precise principles that “absence of evidence is not evidence of absence” might be expressing here? I would prefer a menu of readings to a single interpretation: distinguish, for example, logical, epistemic, and methodological formulations; clarify whose evidential base is at issue (the individual researcher, the collective disciplinary archive, or the theoretical model being tested); specify whether the absence in question is total or partial, localized or systemic, and whether it is the kind of absence one should expect given well-known preservation biases and the Thucydidean preference for written contemporaneity. For each reading, indicate whether it is trivial, whether it is substantive and defensible, and—crucially—whether it actually supports the student’s resistance to skepticism about deep prehistoric institutions, or whether it instead licenses an unwarranted confidence that collapses under scrutiny. In particular, address how the slogan’s argumentative force changes when we move from a Herodotean framework, in which absence may simply reflect the limits of oral transmission and monumental visibility, to a Thucydidean framework, in which absence within the written record is treated as a positive epistemic datum rather than a mere gap to be filled by analogical inference.
