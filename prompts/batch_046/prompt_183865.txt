I am a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and next Tuesday I am presenting a seminar paper to a philosophy of action reading group at a research university. I want to be prepared for the Q&A as if I were defending the thesis in person.

The thesis I am defending is that Parfit's hitchhiker scenario is not, as usually read, a demonstration that rationality sometimes requires being disposed to act against one's present interests—what Parfit calls "rational irrationality." Instead, the scenario reveals a missing commitment mechanism in a multi-node decision architecture. If we model the hitchhiker not as a single agent evaluating diachronic expected utility, but as a system with a planning node and an execution node that do not share state by default, then the execution node's refusal to pay is perfectly rational locally. The global problem is not an internal conflict within rationality, but an unbound protocol between nodes—functionally analogous to a distributed system lacking an escrow or smart-contract layer. Once that architectural separation is explicit, the need for the execution node to behave "irrationally" dissolves; rationality applies to protocol design, not to forcing a local optimizer to act against its own parameters.

To help you target the objections, here is the sketch of my argument: I begin from Parfit's setup, in which a hitchhiker promises a driver payment upon arrival at a distant hospital, knowing that once arrived he will have no incentive to pay, and therefore needs to be credibly committed to paying despite that. I argue that Parfit implicitly treats the agent as a unitary evaluator across time. I propose separating the planning-phase evaluation (which selects the protocol) from the execution-phase evaluation (which optimizes given local information). The execution node, lacking access to the planning node's commitments, optimizes correctly by not paying. The rationality constraint binds on the system designer—who should have installed a binding mechanism—not on the execution node, which should not be expected to violate its local utility function. Therefore there is no paradox of rational irrationality, only a design failure in inter-node commitment.
