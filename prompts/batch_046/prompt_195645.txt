I have been thinking about whether being a person is fundamentally something you possess on your own, or whether it only takes shape through your relationships with others. One view, often associated with Ubuntu, holds that personhood is relational: you become a full moral being through community, mutual recognition, and shared obligation, so that your identity is partly constituted by the people around you. The opposing view holds that personhood is fundamentally individual: you have moral status, rational agency, and dignity in yourself, and while social bonds are profoundly important, they are not what make you a person in the first place.

Please draft the joint statement that advocates of these two positions — call them the Relational camp and the Individualist camp — could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. The document should be structured as follows.

First, a numbered section of agreed claims: statements about human dependency, moral development, and the significance of social relationships that both camps can accept, phrased in neutral language.

Second, a disagreements table that sets out the precise points of divergence, with each camp's position written in wording that camp itself would endorse.

Third, the cruxes — the specific sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side. I believe the key cruxes are these: whether a human raised in complete isolation retains full moral personhood; whether a relational conception can justify individual rights that protect someone against their own community without secretly importing individualist assumptions; and whether the capacity for reciprocal social recognition is a strict threshold for personhood or merely an enriching feature that deepens an already-existing status.

Fourth, for each crux, identify one concrete case or thought experiment that both sides agree is relevant — for example, the moral status of an adult with severe cognitive disabilities who cannot reciprocate recognition, or the obligations owed to a hermit who has withdrawn permanently from social life — and state in advance what each camp predicts about that case.

Please do not ask for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists. I want the disagreement mapped with conceptual precision rather than resolved by authority. Also, indicate whether any portion of the dispute is merely verbal — for instance, if both camps agree that social bonds matter deeply and differ only over whether to describe that relation as constitutive — and explain which portion that is, if any.
