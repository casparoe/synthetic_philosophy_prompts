Having spent my career in experimental physics and only recently begun studying philosophy of mind, I want a precise specification of what an adequate theory of mental causation must deliver, given the pressure of the causal exclusion argument. Write the desiderata: the phenomena the theory must account for—such as conscious deliberation directing motor action, qualitative experience shaping associative learning, and downward causal influence in biological homeostasis—the distinctions it must preserve—between causal efficacy and mere causal relevance, between token-identity and type-autonomy of mental properties, and between synchronous and diachronic mental-physical relations—and the puzzles it must dissolve or accommodate, including the overdetermination threat, the exclusion of irreducible mental properties by physical closure, and the apparent redundancy of mental explanations within a causally closed system. For each desideratum, provide the motivating case or conceptual consideration, and flag any pairs you take to be jointly unsatisfiable—especially whether maintaining both the causal closure of physics and genuinely irreducible mental efficacy is possible without collapsing into epiphenomenalism or reductionism. Briefly indicate how non-reductive physicalism, reductive identity theory, and an emergentist view that employs interventionist causation fare against the list, but keep the weight on the criteria themselves rather than on the scorecard, since adequacy is purpose-relative: a theory adequate for intentional action may not be adequate for neuroscience.
