At a workshop on decision theory, two colleagues—Nadia and Elias—got into a sustained disagreement about a hiring protocol they had both observed at a partner firm. The protocol works as follows: the hiring manager interviews candidates sequentially and applies a fixed competence cutoff; she stops and hires the first candidate who exceeds it. However, if she encounters two consecutive applicants who surpass the original cutoff by a large margin, she raises her cutoff by one standard deviation and continues searching. Nadia called this a clear case of satisficing in Herbert Simon’s sense: the manager never compares the full pool globally, uses a local stopping rule, and adjusts her aspiration level heuristically rather than computing an optimal strategy. Elias disagreed. He argued that the upward adjustment is functionally a Bayesian update on the talent distribution, and that the whole protocol approximates the optimal reservation-value rule from sequential search theory; the manager is therefore not using a distinct “bounded” mode of reasoning but is instead maximizing expected hire quality net of search costs, with the heuristic form merely reflecting computational limits. Nadia replied that Elias was conflating descriptive procedure with normative outcome: the manager does not compute probabilities, expected values, or global optima; she applies a simple “good enough” comparison with a locally movable threshold. The correlation with an optimal stopping rule, she said, is an instance of ecological rationality—simple heuristics matching environments—not proof that she is optimizing. Elias countered that this makes “satisficing” unfalsifiable: any adaptive behavior can be relabeled a heuristic. He insisted that to preserve Simon’s distinction, one needs evidence about internal cognitive architecture, and that without it, describing the behavior as “bounded rationality” adds nothing beyond saying “she optimizes with costs.” Nadia responded that the distinction is structural, not psychological: satisficing involves comparing options against an aspiration level one at a time, never constructing a global ranking or utility comparison across all alternatives, regardless of whether the aspiration level shifts. Even an endogenous threshold, she argued, keeps the mental operation local.

They turned to me and asked me to referee. Please adjudicate point by point: who is right about whether this protocol counts as satisficing, about whether ecological rationality saves the distinction, and about whether the unfalsifiability objection succeeds. Then say who is more right overall, and identify what both are missing—whether about the role of path-dependence in aspiration adaptation, about the normative-descriptive boundary, or about anything else that matters to the disagreement.
