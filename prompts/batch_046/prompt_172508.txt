I'm trying to clarify my own view of when the Anthropocene begins and who has standing to fix that date, by testing my intuitions against concrete proposals.

My verdicts are these. The coal-and-steam transition around 1780 is the historical origin of the trajectory, but it should not be the formal geological boundary, because its signature was not globally synchronous or immediately detectable in a single stratigraphic layer. The 1610 Orbis spike—the global biological exchange and demographic collapse following transoceanic contact—is a profound ecological shift, yet I do not think it counts as the epoch's start; it lacks a novel, planet-wide geochemical signature. The mid-twentieth-century layer of plutonium-239, synthetic microplastics, and concrete in sediments should be the boundary marker, meaning the epoch begins around 1950, even though the social and economic drivers (the Great Acceleration) are usually dated to 1945. The scaling of synthetic nitrogen fixation and concrete production in the early 1900s introduced irreversible biogeochemical changes, but because their global sedimentary dominance arrives only with that 1950 layer, I place the boundary with the layer, not the technology's invention. On authority: stratigraphers should set the geological boundary via a Global Boundary Stratotype Section and Point, since the claim is about rock, ice, and sediment cores; however, historians or affected communities may legitimately date a historical Anthropocene to 1780 or 1610, and I think those are conceptually distinct questions that should not be collapsed into one decision procedure.

The case I keep reversing on is this: if future sediments reveal a more durable and globally uniform techno-signature—for instance, a worldwide layer of engineered-organism biomarkers that becomes detectable around 2050—should the boundary be moved forward? I feel it should not; the boundary ought to remain with the first irreversible global shift, not the clearest fossil. But I am unsure whether that principle is consistent with my preference for the 1950 layer over the 1780 origin.

Is there a coherent principle—perhaps one that distinguishes historical causation from stratigraphic detectability and assigns each to a different kind of authority—that captures all of these verdicts? Which verdict, if any, must I drop to keep the principle simple? And what does the resulting account predict for the future techno-signature case, and for a proposal that the boundary be set by a vote of climate-affected nations rather than by geological evidence?
