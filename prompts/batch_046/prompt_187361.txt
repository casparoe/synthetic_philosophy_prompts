Read William Styron's Sophie's Choice through the lens of Kierkegaard's teleological suspension of the ethical in Fear and Trembling. Sophie is forced by an SS doctor to choose which of her two children will be gassed immediately; she saves her son and loses her daughter, then lives with irreparable guilt until her suicide. Treat this not merely as an impossible moral dilemma but as a potential case of ethical suspension: is there any teleological end—whether survival, divine command, or future redemption—that could justify overriding the universal duty not to choose between one's children? Use Kierkegaard's distinction between the knight of infinite resignation and the knight of faith to ask whether Sophie achieves the private, incommunicable relation to the absolute that Abraham maintains, or whether her subsequent confessions to Stingo and her self-destruction reveal that her choice remains within the tragic-aesthetic sphere, lacking the leap that returns the ethical on higher ground. Where does the novel's rendering of her silence, her alcoholism, and the flashback structure support or resist this framing? Finally, does the work suggest something about the conditions for suspension—particularly whether it requires an absolute that can be named, or whether coercion and absence of faith collapse suspension into pure trauma—that philosophical argument about Abraham alone does not capture?
