I spent the better part of last evening arguing with a friend about whether collective judgment in philosophy is just democratic voting with extra steps, and we kept circling back to the discursive dilemma in judgment aggregation. To settle my own thinking, I drafted the exam question below for a course I am assisting, along with a sample student response. I am grading it as an instructor and need an objective assessment: please assign a letter or numerical grade and provide point-by-point comments that justify it. Do not tell me whether you think the answer is good or bad in general; I want a specific evaluation of where it is accurate, where it slips, and which errors are serious versus cosmetic.

Question:
Consider a three-person evaluation committee judging whether a philosophical argument A is sound. The committee votes separately on two premises — P1: A contains no hidden premises; P2: A's explicit inference steps are all valid — and on the conclusion C: A is sound, where C is taken to follow logically from P1 and P2 together. The votes are:
Member 1: P1 Yes, P2 Yes, C Yes
Member 2: P1 Yes, P2 No, C No
Member 3: P1 No, P2 Yes, C No
(a) Demonstrate the discursive dilemma that arises when majority rule is applied separately to the premises and to the conclusion.
(b) Does this conflict show that the committee is collectively irrational? Distinguish procedural rationality (each member applies inference rules consistently within their own view) from substantive rationality (the group endorses the conclusion that a majority actually supports), and state precisely which form of aggregation preserves each.
(c) A colleague proposes resolving such conflicts by assigning double weight to the votes of members with formal logic training, but only on P2. Assess whether this solves the dilemma, relocates it, or fails to address it entirely.

Student Answer:
Under majority rule, P1 gets Yes from members 1 and 2, so Yes. P2 gets Yes from members 1 and 3, so Yes. C gets Yes only from member 1, so No. Because P1 and P2 together entail C, the committee endorses the premises but rejects the conclusion. This is the discursive dilemma, also known as the doctrinal paradox (List and Pettit 2002).

The conflict is not collective irrationality. Instead, premise-based aggregation preserves procedural rationality, because each individual applies valid inference rules within their own belief set, whereas conclusion-based aggregation preserves substantive rationality, because it respects whatever conclusion the majority actually holds. List and Pettit maintain that procedural rationality should always take precedence over substantive rationality in these cases, which means we should use premise-based aggregation and accept C even though a majority voted against it.

Regarding the weighting proposal: giving logic experts double weight on P2 strengthens the premise vote — member 1's Yes counts twice, so P2 wins 3-to-1 instead of 2-to-1. This removes the ambiguity in the premises and therefore resolves the dilemma, because once the premises are firmly established by expert judgment, the logical derivation of C is unproblematic. So weighted premise aggregation eliminates the conflict.
