I am a retired physicist who has recently turned to philosophy of mind, and I have been studying higher-order representational theories of consciousness—the view that a mental state becomes phenomenally conscious only when it is the object of a suitable higher-order representation, rather than being conscious in virtue of its first-order properties alone. The distinction strikes me as conceptually sharp, but I find the literature polarized and the ramifications for empirical science somewhat under-discussed in the texts I have encountered. Could you lay out the most important implications of taking this higher-order framework seriously? Specifically, I want to understand what follows for three areas: first, the interpretation of clinical dissociations such as blindsight, in which subjects exhibit accurate visual discrimination while denying any phenomenal visual experience; second, the epistemic status of first-person reports in scientific consciousness research, particularly whether they should be treated as direct access to phenomenal states or rather as meta-cognitive judgments about those states; and third, the conditions for attributing consciousness to artificial systems that might implement sophisticated first-order information processing without any recursive self-modeling architecture. Please separate what is relatively settled within philosophy of mind from what remains genuinely contested among specialists, and do not present a disputed claim—such as the idea that higher-order theories necessarily deny phenomenal consciousness to non-linguistic animals—as though it were the consensus position.
