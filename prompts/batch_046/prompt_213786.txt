I serve on a technical review panel for an international water-infrastructure consortium, and over the past year we have faced a growing volume of post-incident reports proposing competing explanations for anomalous system failures—specifically, sudden drops in purification efficacy across three distributed nodes that do not match any documented single-component failure mode. In a recent audit course on conceptual foundations of scientific reasoning I encountered the framework of inference to the best explanation, and I want to translate it into an operational checklist that my panel can run in about forty-five minutes during a review session, without requiring anyone present to have formal training in epistemology or philosophy of science.

The procedure should take the form of ordered questions mapped onto the core evaluative criteria—explanatory scope across all affected nodes, parsimony or simplicity, coherence with established hydraulic and microbiological principles, and resistance to ad hoc adjustments—with explicit thresholds defining what counts as a sufficient answer at each stage, clear termination points where an explanation is rejected, and designated hand-off points where the question is one of disciplinary judgment rather than observable fact. Because the panel comprises three members with different engineering specializations, the checklist should specify which verification steps require unanimous agreement, which allow a majority determination, and which must be decided by a designated lead reviewer. Please apply this procedure to two hypothetical reports: the first attributes the failure to a previously undocumented biofilm interaction spanning the nodes, and the second attributes it to a sequential valve malfunction contingent on an undocumented maintenance error at the intermediate node. Include at least one worked case where the procedure recommends accepting an explanation that a conventionally trained engineer will find intuitively unsatisfying—perhaps selecting a more complex model over a simpler one on scope grounds—and conclude by stating precisely what the procedure is structurally incapable of resolving, such as disagreements over the baseline prior probability of unprecedented biological mechanisms versus conventional mechanical failures.
