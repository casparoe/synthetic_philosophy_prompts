In my reporting on the intersection of cognitive science and philosophy of religion, I keep returning to a philosophical debate that has calcified over decades: does the Buddhist doctrine of anatta—no-self—entail the elimination of personal identity, or does it preserve a conventional, functional self? The reductionist camp, drawing on Abhidhamma analyses of consciousness into momentary mental events (cittas), insists that without a permanent substrate, diachronic personal identity collapses into a narrative fiction sustained only by linguistic convention. The preservationist camp, citing the practical coherence of moral agency, the continuity of karma, and the rebirth narratives in canonical texts, argues that anatta targets only a metaphysically substantial self (atta), leaving intact a processual, conventional identity adequate for ethical life. For years, the two sides have traded the same textual references and analogies—Ship of Theseus variants applied to rebirth, debates over whether psychological continuity requires a persisting subject—without either conceding ground.

My working hypothesis, offered only as conjecture, is that the stall is not merely verbal but involves a hidden disagreement about explanatory priority: reductionists treat first-person phenomenological continuity as data to be explained away by momentary processes, while preservationists treat that continuity as a constraint any adequate ontology must accommodate. I also suspect that a rarely integrated framework—the Theravada distinction between conventional truth (sammuti-sacca) and ultimate truth (paramattha-sacca)—is doing silent work, such that the sides may be answering different questions under one name: one asking what ultimately exists, the other asking what must be denied for soteriological progress. Yet I remain unsure whether this diagnosis holds, or whether the dispute instead rests on bedrock intuitions about the self that neither side can argue for from shared premises, or whether the very framing—elimination versus preservation—forces a false binary onto a doctrine never intended as an ontology of persons.

What is your diagnosis? Is this disagreement substantive, verbal, or a mixture in which shared vocabulary conceals different standards of evidence and different counts of what data matter? Is a hidden empirical question—perhaps about how practitioners actually conceptualize the self, or about the neural correlates of narrative coherence—doing the work? Or are the sides answering genuinely different questions under the single heading of anatta? Given your diagnosis, what would count as progress: a formal model of process identity that satisfies both camps, an empirical study of conceptual practice, a distinction that dissolves the opposition, or is the impasse a sign that the question is ill-posed and should be reframed? And if you judge that no progress should be expected, what exactly makes this dispute resistant to resolution?
