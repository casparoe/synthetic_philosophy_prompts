Take the role of an expert in political philosophy and an experienced interview host who specializes in drawing out precise conceptual distinctions rather than biography. Before drafting anything, briefly brainstorm the main fault lines in the literature on political obligation and civil disobedience — especially where Rawlsian liberal accounts, philosophical anarchist denials of special obligation, and consent-based theories clash — and then use that map to inform your sequence.

I am preparing a podcast interview with Dr. Elena Vasquez, who has recently defended a view she calls contractual maintenance theory. On her account, citizens do not possess a direct, content-independent duty to obey particular laws; instead, they bear an ongoing obligation to preserve the institutional conditions that render the social contract legitimate. Civil disobedience is therefore not an exception to obligation but its most demanding form: it consists of deliberately public, non-violent legitimacy-correcting acts calibrated to repair the contract’s credibility rather than to change specific policies. Vasquez distinguishes her position from the Rawlsian framing of disobedience as a last-resort appeal to shared principles of justice, and she rejects anarchist conclusions by insisting that the maintenance duty binds even those who never explicitly consented. Central to her framework is a normative measure she terms the legitimacy deficit, assessed through a hypothetical procedure she calls the veil of institutional ignorance: citizens evaluate institutional divergence from what they would consent to if they retained knowledge of their risk preferences and capacities but were ignorant of their specific social roles. She further insists that anonymous or covert resistance is morally defective on her view, since maintenance requires public acknowledgment and cannot operate through hidden correction.

Draft eight to ten interview questions that avoid generic motivation or career-path queries. The questions should turn on the actual architecture of her view: how the maintenance obligation is triggered, whether it applies to citizens of partially just regimes, how the legitimacy deficit avoids collapsing into mere preference aggregation, and in what respects her veil differs from standard contractarian devices. For at least three questions, include a sharp follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give — for example, if she claims the deficit is qualitative rather than quantitative, ask what makes a qualitative judgment intersubjectively binding across role-divergent citizens; if she appeals to publicness as essential to the moral logic of disobedience, ask whether a later-revealed anonymous act retroactively acquires standing or remains permanently defective; if she distinguishes her view from Rawls by emphasizing contract repair over justice appeals, ask whether a dissenter who rejects contractarian premises altogether is still bound by the maintenance duty. Make sure the sequence builds in conceptual difficulty, ending with a question about what empirical or normative discovery — for instance, persistent institutional failure despite repeated corrective acts, or a demonstration that hypothetical consent is ineliminably indeterminate — could lead her to abandon the maintenance framework in favor of either anarchism or straightforward obedience-based theories. Think carefully about where the standard literature presses — on the risk of licensing excessive disobedience, on the problem of partial compliance, and on whether her view can accommodate deep moral pluralism — and ensure those pressure points appear in your questions.
