I'm running a 90-min grad seminar on personal identity (12 people, analytic plus some cog-sci folks). We've been chewing on Hofstadter's claim that the self is basically a pattern that can echo inside other people's narrative machinery — not a full copy-paste, but a partial persistence where your I survives in modified form inside someone else's associative web. I want an in-class game that makes participants actually feel the dislocation of locating that I, rather than just nodding along.

To keep it from being generic brain-upload sci-fi, I'm injecting this framing: a memory-escrow protocol where Person A's episodic traces are continuously integrated into Person B's associative network over three rounds, but B also starts generating counterfactual first-person continuations of those memories — things A never lived. Meanwhile A's original substrate is simulated-degrading. The group has to make allocation decisions (who gets to speak for the shared past? whose preferences govern future actions?) using only point-stakes and social pressure, no real cash.

Design the rules, role-cards, round-structure, and payoff scheme for the 90 minutes. Make sure the mechanics force people to run into the coordination failure — everyone knowing the pattern is distributed but nobody being able to point to a single owner — before the debrief names it. Include the debrief mapping specific moments to Hofstadter's distinction between substrate-dependance and pattern-persistence, plus an honest note on what the game can't reproduce about the strange-loop idea and what I'd need to supply verbally. Also tell me what's gonna break the first time I try it — I have a feeling the role-players will get weirdly attached to their assigned substrate and refuse to migrate the points.
