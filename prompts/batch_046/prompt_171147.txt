I chair a university curriculum committee that revises our world-history survey every five years. We currently organize the course around three blocks—"Middle Ages," "Renaissance," and "Scientific Revolution"—and we need a reusable decision procedure that faculty without historiographical training can run in under an hour to determine whether a proposed chronological label should be retained, split, renamed, dropped, or explicitly flagged as Eurocentric. Ground the procedure in the philosophical and historical debate over whether these divisions mark real structural discontinuities or are retrospective constructions, but translate it into ordered questions with clear stopping conditions: where the answer is a matter of fact about the evidence, where it requires a value judgment about pedagogical coherence, and where it must be handed to a committee vote. The procedure must include a cross-regional check: if a label cannot be mapped onto at least one non-European timeline (for example, Ming, Ottoman, or Inca) without serious distortion, it should be retained only if the committee votes to keep it as an explicitly parochial unit. Apply the procedure to three proposals: (1) retaining "Scientific Revolution" as a 1543–1687 block anchored to specific cosmological and physical achievements; (2) splitting the "Renaissance" into separate Italian and Northern units because the proposed pivot events—patronage shifts versus printing and Reformation—do not coincide chronologically; and (3) replacing both late-medieval and early-modern labels with a single "Long Fifteenth Century" (c. 1350–1550) that treats the boundary as an artificial historiographical artifact. Ensure one application yields an answer that conflicts with standard pedagogical practice, and specify exactly what kinds of boundary disputes—such as competing global timelines or contested causal pivots—the procedure is not designed to resolve.
