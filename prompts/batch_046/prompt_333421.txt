I am a retired experimental physicist who, since leaving the laboratory, has developed an intense interest in formal decision theory and the philosophy of science. Next month I will deliver the opening remarks—approximately fifteen minutes—at a small interdisciplinary workshop attended by information economists, formal decision theorists, and graduate students in bioethics who have little mathematical background. I would like you to write these remarks for me. They should be polished, relatively formal, and composed in American English. I do not want any bibliography, reading list, or citation list included; the remarks must stand on their own for an audience that cannot search the web.

The philosophical idea should do real structural work, not merely decorate the introduction. I want to build the remarks around Good's theorem—the result that, for a single rational agent with a well-defined action space, the expected value of acquiring further information is non-negative—while exploring a tension I have noticed in clinical trial ethics. Specifically, trial protocols often require full disclosure of incidental genetic findings to preserve statistical power and the collective value of information, yet individual participants sometimes have good reason to prefer not to learn certain risks, since learning them offers them no actionable benefit and may cause harm. I want the speech to ask whether this tension exposes a hidden assumption in the theorem—namely, that the agent who receives the information is the same agent whose actions determine the payoff—without turning the remarks into a mathematics lecture. Please avoid quoting any published text verbatim; if you refer to the result, describe it in your own words. Write for a mixed room: the specialists should recognize the reference immediately, and the non-specialists should understand the human stakes without being lost.
