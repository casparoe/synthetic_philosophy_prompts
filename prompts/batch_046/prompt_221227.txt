As a journalist covering philosophy of mind, I keep encountering one particular line in debates about Douglas Hofstadter’s suggestion that the self might persist across brains: “The self is only a pattern, and patterns don’t care which brain they inhabit.” Speakers usually deploy it in contexts involving high-bandwidth inter-brain coupling or gradual neural prosthesis replacement, using it to dismiss worries about identity duplication by claiming that if functional symmetry is maintained, an “I” could be genuinely distributed rather than merely copied. I want to know what precise theses are actually packed into that sentence. Could you lay out, as a menu, the distinct claims it could be expressing—from the trivial observation that mental properties supervene on organizational structure, through the substantive metaphysical claim that personal identity is purely informational and therefore multiply instantiable, to the radical view that continuous functional coupling between two brains could literally constitute one shared subject? For each reading, indicate whether it is a platitude, whether it needs independent argument, and whether it is false. Then tell me which of these readings is actually doing the work when the slogan is used to argue that a person might be “spread across” two brains linked by prosthetic feedback loops, and whether that specific reading survives the objection that pattern duplication is not the same as pattern continuation.
