I am a retired experimental physicist who has recently taken up philosophy of science and political philosophy in earnest, and I find that the conceptual distinction between common-pool resources and pure public goods—usually mapped onto rivalrous versus non-rivalrous consumption and excludable versus non-excludable access—is clear in principle but frustratingly indeterminate when one tries to apply it to actual governance decisions. I want to operationalize this distinction into a reusable decision procedure for a practitioner—specifically, the director of a shared scientific-data repository who lacks philosophical training and must apply it under administrative time pressure—who needs to determine whether a given resource is threatened by a tragedy-of-the-commons dynamic, a free-rider problem characteristic of pure public goods, or a hybrid failure mode, including the under-discussed case of 'threshold goods' in which provision requires reaching a critical mass of contributors yet consumption remains non-rival once that threshold is met. The procedure should take the form of an ordered sequence of empirical and conceptual tests that can be run without appealing to contested normative theories; for each step, specify what counts as observable evidence of a pass or fail, indicate where a judgment about values rather than facts forces the procedure to halt and defer to a governance board, and flag any step that tends to misfire for non-material or epistemic goods. Then run the procedure on two proposals: first, a regional groundwater aquifer used by competing agricultural cooperatives; second, an open-access repository of climate-model outputs that requires continuous curatorial labor but allows unlimited non-rival downloads. Include at least one case in which the procedure recommends an institutional form—such as restricted access or usage fees—that runs counter to the intuitive moral expectation that open data should always be maximally accessible, and conclude by stating precisely what the procedure cannot settle—whether that gap is a failure of the taxonomy or an irreducible space for democratic deliberation.
