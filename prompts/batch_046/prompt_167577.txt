I am taking a seminar on the constitutional history of the Roman Republic, and I have grown dissatisfied with the standard analogies to modern democratic stress that rely on broad notions of corruption or populist demagoguery. For my term paper, I want to test a more granular institutional framework—one drawn from our readings on late-republican decay but refined through my own focus on what I call a “precedent cascade.” By this I mean the process by which emergency constitutional instruments, initially deployed within formal legality—the senatus consultum ultimum, the prorogation of provincial commands, and the selective suspension of tribunician intercessio—gradually become routinized, dissolving the boundary between ordinary and extraordinary authority without requiring a single decisive rupture.

To see whether this model illuminates anything beyond Rome itself, I want to apply it to the American electoral certification disputes of 2020–2021 and the subsequent restructuring of state-level election administration boards that occurred between 2021 and 2023. Please analyze this modern episode through the lens of the precedent-cascade thesis. First, map the Roman institutional roles onto the modern crisis with care: who or what functions as the consuls, the senate, and the tribunes; what counts as an emergency decree versus ordinary legislation; and which specific actions—whether at the federal or state level—correspond to the prorogation of authority or the suspension of veto powers? Second, determine whether the modern crisis involved an actual constitutional rupture or rather a series of legally defensible moves that, taken together, produced a cascade effect.

Third, assess whether the Roman framework predicts recovery or terminal decay in a polity whose legitimacy rests on popular sovereignty rather than aristocratic consensus, and identify exactly where the analogy breaks—particularly regarding federalism and judicial review, institutions that have no Roman counterpart. Finally, tell me whether the Roman lens genuinely clarifies the modern episode, or whether it imposes a false parallel that obscures the distinctive role of legal formalism in contemporary democratic backsliding. I am not asking for a bibliography or for citations to specific historians; I want a disciplined conceptual mapping that runs in both directions, with the case testing the theory as much as the theory illuminates the case.
