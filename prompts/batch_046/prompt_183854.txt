i've been thinking about writing a workshop pitch for next years conference on philosophy of mind, and i keep coming back to this weird gap in the predictive processing literature. everyone talks about precision-weighting and free energy minimization inside individual brains, but almost nobody asks what happens when two agents are coupled so tightly that they share parts of a generative model - not just joint attention, but actually co-constructing the priors that govern each other's predictions. i want a proposal for a short research project, maybe a semester term paper around 6000 words, that asks whether relational predictive processing - where the "model" isn't inside one skull but distributed across a social interaction - can make sense of intersubjective recognition without collapsing into either standard social cognition or full-blown enactivism.

the specific question should be stated precisely: can the free energy principle be extended to dyadic systems where precision is weighted not just by an individual's expected uncertainty, but by the relational history of the pair? and if so, does this give us a formal way to distinguish between "misprediction due to noisy input" and "misprediction due to a shared prior that systematically discounts one partner's sensory evidence"? i think existing answers leave this open because Friston-style active inference takes the agent-environment boundary as given, while relational critics say the boundary is the problem, but neither side actually tries to model the boundary as dynamically negotiated through coupled free-energy landscapes.

the proposal should explain the approach: take one concrete case study, probably the clinical distinction between "interactional" and "structural" explanations of psychosis in family systems (like the expressed emotion literature), and try to formalize both explanations in predictive terms. if i can show that the interactional explanation requires shared precision-weighting parameters that can't be reduced to individual brains, then free energy actually supports the relational view; if not, maybe the relational critics are right that active inference smuggles in an individualist ontology. say why this middle option is better than the obvious alternatives - either just doing standard individualist modeling or just doing philosophy-of-science critique of enactivism. then lay out what success and failure would actually look like, and be honest about scope: probably restrict to one family-therapy model and one mathematical sketch rather than empirical data collection, and say whether that's genuinely doable in one semester or still too big. don't include a bibliography or citation list - i'll fill those in myself. just frame the question, defend why it's open, pick the angle, and assess the risk that the project collapses into either being too technical or too vague.
