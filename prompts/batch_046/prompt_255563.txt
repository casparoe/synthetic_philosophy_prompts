I'm giving the opening remarks at a three-day workshop comparing how Germany, Japan, Turkey, and the American South have institutionalized memory of state violence and defeat. The audience is split between academic specialists—historians of Vergangenheitsbewältigung, Japanese postwar reckoning, Turkish archival politics regarding the Armenian genocide, and American Lost Cause historiography—and local activists and archivists who live in these regions but are not scholars. I want a talk of about fifteen minutes that does not welcome them with platitudes or rehearse the standard "honest Germany versus denialist others" taxonomy. Instead, I want to restructure the comparison around a distinction I'm introducing: mnemonic asymmetry versus mnemonic symmetry. By asymmetry I mean a public culture in which the state permits, funds, and protects remembrance practices that directly contradict the official national narrative, without requiring those counter-memories to translate themselves into state-sanctioned language. By symmetry I mean any culture that insists all public remembrance converge on a single authorized account, whether that account is self-critical or self-justifying. The speech should show that this axis cuts across the usual ranking—perhaps one of the "failed" cases permits more genuine asymmetry in specific institutions than the celebrated case does elsewhere, while another case's problem is not absence of guilt but enforced symmetry of a particular kind. Include one concrete, recognizable institutional example for each of the four regions—no citations needed—and make the distinction clear to the non-academics without reducing it to a slogan. The concept should shape what can and can't be said: for instance, it should prevent false equivalences but also block the reflex to treat every divergence from the German model as moral failure. End with an open question that frames the workshop's task without resolving it. Write the speech.
