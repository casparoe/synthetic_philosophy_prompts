I'm trying to work out what I actually think about ethical evaluation in Buddhist frameworks, testing whether my judgments line up with a consequentialist calculus, a rule-based precept ethics, or something in between. Here are my verdicts on concrete cases, and I want to know whether there's a coherent principle behind them, or which ones I should abandon.

First, a physician with deep compassion actively administers a lethal dose to a terminal patient who has begged for death. The act is deliberate, the suffering ends, and the physician's mind is free of hatred or personal gain. I think this is wrong — it breaks the precept against taking life, and the good outcome does not redeem the act.

Second, a physician refuses pain relief to a suffering patient because she fears accumulating negative karma for herself, even though the patient is pleading. This is also wrong, but for the opposite reason: here the outcome is bad and the motivation is selfish.

Third, a layperson calmly lies to an armed aggressor about where refugees are hiding, saving dozens of lives. The lie is told with no personal benefit and with genuine care for all involved, including the aggressor's future karma. I think this is right.

Fourth, a wealthy patron donates enormous sums to build hospitals that save thousands of lives, but does so chiefly to gain merit for a favorable rebirth and public praise. The outcome is excellent; the intention is largely self-interested. I think the act is ethically inferior — it produces good in the world, but it is not a genuinely virtuous act.

Fifth, a teacher knows that a group of students will only begin serious practice if they are told that meditation grants supernatural powers. The claim is false, but the teacher intends only to guide them eventually to correct understanding and liberation, and over time they do reach genuine insight. I think this is wrong — deceptive teaching corrupts the path, even when the long-run consequences are liberating.

Sixth, a ruler with pure compassion and no hatred orders the forced relocation of a small community, knowing it will cause them significant suffering, to prevent an ecological collapse that would harm thousands. The ruler accepts personal karmic risk without seeking reward. I think this is permissible, perhaps even required.

These judgments feel stable, but they are hard to square. Cases three and five both involve deliberate deception with good outcomes and compassionate motives, yet I approve the lie and reject the false teaching. Cases one and six both involve direct harm to some for the benefit of others, yet I reject the physician's killing and accept the ruler's coercion. Case four shows that good outcomes do not automatically make an act virtuous, which might seem to support a non-consequentialist view, yet case six seems to weigh outcomes heavily.

Is there a single principle — perhaps centered on intention (cetana), the specific precept violated, the agent's mental purification, or some hybrid — that delivers all six verdicts? If not, which verdict should I overturn to achieve coherence, and what does the best remaining principle predict for a case I have not listed: a meditator who achieves deep equanimity in lifelong retreat, benefiting no one directly but cultivating a mind completely free of harmful intention?
