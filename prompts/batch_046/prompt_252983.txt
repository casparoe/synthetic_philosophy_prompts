The view that simultaneous discoveries—Newton and Leibniz on calculus, Darwin and Wallace on natural selection—demonstrate that scientific and mathematical breakthroughs are structurally overdetermined, so that once the relevant conceptual apparatus and empirical anomalies accumulate, the solution is effectively inevitable regardless of which particular agent produces it. This position is often taken to imply that disputes over priority are mostly ceremonial, that individual genius is largely a narrative overlay on collective epistemic conditions, and that if we counterfactually remove one discoverer the other (or a near-substitute) arrives at essentially the same result within a short window. Play it straight: what would have to be true for that conclusion to be wrong? Which of its supporting assumptions—about the determinacy of problem-spaces, the fungibility of cognitive agents, the uniformity of conceptual resources across contexts—could in principle fail, what kind of historical or conceptual evidence would undermine each, and which of these escape routes are live rather than merely logically possible? I want an honest assessment of how exposed the inevitability thesis actually is: does it risk anything concrete about the form discoveries take, about the time-lags involved, or about the role of idiosyncratic framing, or has it been insulated by qualifications that make it unfalsifiable?
