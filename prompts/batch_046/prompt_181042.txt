Having spent thirty years in condensed-matter physics before turning, in retirement, to philosophy of history and its methodological analogies, I find the quest for the historical Jesus structurally puzzling. In my former work we reconstructed unknown microscopic states from noisy macroscopic signals by applying filtering criteria—discarding artifacts, demanding cross-instrument reproducibility, enforcing theoretical coherence—and we expected convergence as data accumulated. Jesus scholarship applies an apparently parallel filter: criteria of authenticity such as embarrassment, dissimilarity, multiple attestation, coherence, and traces of Aramaic substratum, applied to the canonical gospels, Pauline letters, and a small cluster of external references, with the aim of stripping away later ecclesiastical accretion and recovering the figure behind them. Yet rather than converging on a stable profile, these same criteria support radically incompatible reconstructions—apocalyptic prophet, wisdom sage, social reformer, eschatological mystic—without any agreed metric of residual error or confidence interval. The analogy therefore breaks in a way I want to examine precisely.

The claim I ask you to evaluate is this: the standard criteria of authenticity, even when applied with full methodological rigor and by investigators who genuinely bracket their theological commitments, can in principle settle—meaning yield a biographical and intentional profile of Jesus of Nazareth that is stable across independent scholars—who he was and what he intended. I do not ask whether they have already done so; I ask whether the epistemic architecture of the problem permits convergence at all, analogous to a well-conditioned inverse problem, or whether it is irreducibly underdetermined, with investigator priors functioning not as reducible noise but as systematic corruption of the filter itself.

Please state your overall credence that the claim is true, expressed as a number between 0 and 1, and decompose it through the following sub-questions, assigning each its own rough probability with a single line of justification and indicating how they combine: (a) whether the available source base—canonical texts, Pauline material, Josephus, Tacitus, and the extra-canonical corpora—supplies enough genuinely independent data points to constrain a high-dimensional historical profile without overfitting; (b) whether the criteria themselves possess demonstrably low false-positive and false-negative rates, or whether they operate more as coherence checks vulnerable to circular reasoning; (c) whether the influence of scholars' prior commitments behaves like random error that averages out with more investigators, or like a directional bias that systematically shifts the reconstructed parameters; and (d) whether the concept of "settling" a first-century biography makes sense at all when the earliest witnesses are already interpretive reconstructions of primarily oral performances. Then identify which of these four terms carries the greatest uncertainty for you, and specify what kind of evidence—archaeological, textual-critical, or methodological—would most substantially move your overall number. I am especially interested in whether you judge the inclusion of non-canonical texts such as the Gospel of Thomas or the Nag Hammadi library to improve the conditioning of the problem through additional constraints, or to worsen it by multiplying free parameters without adequate cross-validation.
