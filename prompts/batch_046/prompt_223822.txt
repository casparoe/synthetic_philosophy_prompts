I have recently been asked to help redesign the certification protocol for a technical apprenticeship in precision manufacturing. The existing system awards mastery solely through a timed practical examination in which candidates must diagnose and repair a complex mechanical failure; examiners are explicitly forbidden to ask candidates to explain their reasoning during the task, on the principle that verbal articulation disrupts procedural fluency. A proposed amendment would add a mandatory "reflective reconstruction" session immediately after the repair, requiring candidates to narrate their decision sequence in explicit propositional form, with decertification possible if the account is incoherent even when the repair itself was flawless.

What theory of knowledge—specifically of the relationship between knowing-how and knowing-that—does this institutional architecture presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit epistemology: does the separation of practical demonstration from verbal justification assume that the two capacities are genuinely independent, or does it treat articulation as merely an unreliable indicator of an underlying unified competence? Say whether the practice is philosophically underdetermined, compatible with anti-intellectualist, intellectualist, and intermediate positions, and explain what the certification criteria would have to become if we replaced the implicit view with the claim that all expert competence is in principle fully articulable.
