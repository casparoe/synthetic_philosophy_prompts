I sit on the allocation committee for a cooperative that collectively owns a residential building with two currently vacant units. A recent contamination event—caused by a manufacturing supplier whose below-market pricing directly reduced our renovation costs—displaced households from a neighboring district. Our committee is divided over whom to house. One group argues we should restrict admission to those who have received formal state designation as displaced persons; another maintains we should prioritize families whose property was directly affected by this particular supplier's waste, even if they lack that formal designation; a third holds that because our own building's upkeep was partially subsidized by the very externalized harm that caused the displacement, we carry a distinctive repair obligation to anyone downstream of that harm, an obligation that should take precedence over both bureaucratic classification and geographic proximity.

I am unsure whether these positions represent genuinely conflicting moral reasons or whether they are different routes to the same conclusion. Does having empty space generate a uniform duty toward anyone in need, or does the specific pathway by which our resources became available—through cost savings linked to another community's loss—alter the strength or direction of that duty? I am taking a course in political philosophy, and I find it difficult to move from abstract talk about harm, membership, and rescue to an actual decision I must help make at next week's meeting.

Could you advise me on how to weigh these considerations? Please be explicit about how confident you are in the reasoning you offer, and point to the particular claim—whether about the moral weight of legal status, the force of causal or benefit-based responsibility, or the proper ordering of rescue obligations—where your confidence is lowest.
