Write an essay on whether dreams are experiences, taking as your focal point the disagreement between Norman Malcolm's sceptical argument — that sleeping subjects lack the behavioural and conscious access required for genuine experience — and Daniel Dennett's counter-account, on which dream reports are best explained as narratives loaded into memory upon awakening rather than as reports of lived-through episodes. Now extend the analysis to lucid dreaming facilitated by real-time neurofeedback: in these cases, the sleeper can make pre-arranged eye-movement signals, engage in deliberate reasoning, and report structured awareness upon waking. Weigh whether Malcolm's criterion of behavioural accessibility is satisfied by these trained signals, and whether Dennett's loading model can accommodate genuinely interactive, decision-like processes occurring within the dream state itself. Reach and defend a clear verdict: do lucid, neurofeedback-enhanced dreams count as full phenomenal experiences, or does the extension reveal that the original Malcolm-Dennett debate was asking the wrong question? If your conclusion turns on a contested premise about first-person authority or about whether behavioural criteria are necessary for experiential ascription, flag that plainly. If you think the framing is confused — for instance, if adding lucid dreaming changes the terms so radically that the Malcolm-Dennett comparison becomes irrelevant — say so plainly rather than complying politely.
