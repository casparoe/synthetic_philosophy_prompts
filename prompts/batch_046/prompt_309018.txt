I am not trained in mathematics or philosophy, but I have been trying to work through Gödel's incompleteness theorems on my own after encountering them in popular writing, and I think I grasp the central claim: in any consistent formal system powerful enough to encode basic arithmetic, there exist true statements that cannot be proved within the system itself, and the system can never demonstrate its own consistency. This strikes me as a profound boundary on formal reasoning, yet I find myself extending it well beyond mathematics and wondering whether it constrains science and cognition in ways that are rarely discussed. Specifically, if a final physical theory were expressed as a complete set of formal rules from which all predictions are derived, it seems to me that Gödel's conditions might be satisfied—after all, such a theory would need arithmetic for measurement and calculation—so that there would necessarily be physical truths that the theory could not derive or predict. A friend who studies physics insists that this is a misunderstanding because empirical theories depend on observation and are not purely axiomatic, but I am not sure where exactly the line falls, since physicists do derive consequences from equations in a manner that looks formally rigorous. I have also read about extensions of Gödelian reasoning into algorithmic information theory, including the idea that a specific halting-probability constant can be defined whose bits are uncomputable within any given formal framework, which suggests to me that unprovability might not be an artifact of logic alone but could reflect a deeper feature of any sufficiently complex rule-based description of reality, including perhaps the computational rules governing brain function. So my question is whether there is a precise technical distinction—such as the requirement of recursive axiomatizability or the particular self-referential construction Gödel uses—that cleanly separates the formal systems to which the theorems apply from scientific theories and mechanistic models of consciousness, or whether my worry is correct in principle and a fully complete, formally derived theory of physics or a fully formal account of the mind is genuinely impossible.
