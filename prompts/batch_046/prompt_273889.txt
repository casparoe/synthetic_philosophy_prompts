Question: Epiphenomenalism holds that conscious mental states have no causal efficacy upon physical processes, including the neurophysiological mechanisms that produce verbal reports. This gives rise to the paradox of phenomenal judgement: if my belief that I am experiencing pain is caused entirely by brain states in which the pain itself plays no part, then my judgement appears to be formed without any causal connection to the phenomenal state it describes. Some philosophers argue that this renders epiphenomenalism self-defeating, since belief in the doctrine would itself be causally disconnected from its rational grounds; others reply that reliability can be preserved through a non-causal correlation, such as psycho-physical parallelism. Does the paradox genuinely refute epiphenomenalism, or can it be dissolved whilst retaining the causal closure of the physical?

Student answer: Epiphenomenalism, classically formulated by Thomas Huxley, holds that mental states are causally inert by-products of cerebral activity—like steam from a locomotive that does no work itself. The paradox of phenomenal judgement arises because, if consciousness plays no causal role, then the physical processes generating my utterance "I am in pain" never involve the pain itself. Hence my judgements about phenomenal states appear to be formed without any causal link to those states, threatening their reliability. One might object that this is self-defeating: if epiphenomenalism is true, then my belief in it is likewise caused solely by brain states, not by rational insight. But this objection confuses the epistemic status of the theory with the reliability of first-person reports; the real problem is evolutionary. Natural selection operates only on behaviour, not on phenomenal accuracy, so there is no evolutionary guarantee that our reports match our experiences. Defenders of the view—including Huxley in his later correspondence—have appealed to a Leibnizian pre-established harmony to preserve correlation without causation. On this account, mental and physical sequences run in perfect parallel, so my verbal report reliably tracks my pain even though the pain does not cause the report. Therefore the paradox does not refute epiphenomenalism; it merely shows that we must supplement the causal closure of the physical with a principle of psycho-physical parallelism.
