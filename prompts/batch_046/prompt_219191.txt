I cover the intersection of science and philosophy for a general-interest publication, and at a recent interdisciplinary symposium on evolutionary ethics I witnessed an exchange that I now find impossible to report accurately. A behavioral ecologist presented cross-species data showing that reciprocal altruism functions as an evolutionarily stable strategy—a descriptive claim about cooperative dynamics in mammalian groups—and then argued that municipal institutions ought therefore to design incentive structures that reward reciprocity. A philosopher in the audience immediately objected by invoking what he called the naturalistic fallacy: one cannot derive an ought from an is. The audience treated this as a knock-down rebuttal, yet as I attempted to draft the feature, I realized I could not specify exactly which logical, semantic, or motivational thesis the objection rested upon, or whether any version of it genuinely blocked the ecologist’s inference rather than merely relabeling it.

Could you unpack the slogan—often traced to Hume and later sharpened by G. E. Moore—into the distinct precise claims it might be expressing? I want a conceptual disambiguation rather than a historical survey: distinguish the purely deductive claim that normative conclusions do not validly follow from non-normative premises alone; the motivational claim that recognizing a descriptive regularity provides no rational impetus to act; the semantic claim that moral terms cannot be reduced to natural properties without leaving an open question; and the meta-ethical claim that any purported derivation commits a category mistake about the kind of reason-giving at stake. For each reading, indicate whether it actually undermines the move from evolutionary stability to institutional design, or whether it merely changes the subject by introducing a hidden premise about human psychology, social authority, or first-person practical reasoning. I am particularly interested in whether a restricted version of the gap—limited to individual deliberation—could survive intact while still permitting third-person, policy-level recommendations of the kind the ecologist offered, and whether that restriction exposes the objection as doing empirical work disguised as logical hygiene. Please avoid bibliographies, citation lists, or references to specific editions; I need the architecture of the argument clear enough to explain to readers who have no access to academic databases and who are encountering this debate for the first time through my reporting.
