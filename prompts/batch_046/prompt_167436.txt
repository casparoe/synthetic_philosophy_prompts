You serve as the lead materials-risk assessor for a national archive that acquires treated artifacts from private restoration workshops. Each week you must sign off on whether an object has genuinely acquired a long-term stability disposition—moisture resistance, corrosion inhibition, or dimensional fixity—through treatment, or whether its apparent health is philosophically spurious: finked, masked, or sustained by a hidden environmental crutch that will vanish once the object moves to public display conditions. Your decision determines whether the artifact can enter low-cost, uncontrolled storage or must remain under permanent active conservation, which is expensive and space-limited. You have thirty minutes per case, no destructive testing is permitted, and your report must distinguish mechanical steps from unavoidable judgment calls.

Translate the philosophical analysis of dispositions, finks, antidotes, and masks into a reusable four-stage assessment procedure that a conservator without philosophy training can run: (1) substrate baseline—what reactive tendencies does the untreated material actually possess?; (2) treatment-layer interaction—does the treatment confer a new disposition, suppress an old one, or form a composite system with emergent properties?; (3) counterfactual stress audit—if the target environment shifts in humidity, temperature, pH, or oxidant load, does the stability persist, vanish precisely under manifestation conditions (fink), or require an absent suppressor (mask removal)?; (4) dependency check—does the observed stability depend on an ongoing external condition (climate control, adjacent chemical reservoir, unobservable monitoring) that will not travel with the object?

For each stage, specify the observable indicators you can gather in thirty minutes, the decision thresholds, and the explicit points where theory underdetermines observation. The procedure must also handle three recurring misfire patterns: (a) composite-system dispositions, where the substrate lacks the property but the treated whole genuinely possesses it; (b) antidote confusion, where the treatment suppresses a reactive tendency rather than building a positive resistant one; and (c) the “dispositional decoy,” an object that behaves stably not because of internal finkishness but because a hidden contextual dependency—present in the lab but absent in display—will be removed, blurring the boundary between mask and external fink.

Apply the procedure step-by-step to three artifacts:

(i) A nineteenth-century iron navigational instrument treated with a microcrystalline wax infusion. After six months at 55% relative humidity it shows zero oxidation, but the wax begins to sublime at 60% RH, and the intended storage climate fluctuates seasonally between 50% and 65%.

(ii) A wooden ritual mask consolidated with an ethyl-cellulose polymer. It remains dimensionally stable in testing, but the polymer hydrolyzes if local atmospheric pH falls below 5.5, which occurs seasonally in the region where it will be displayed.

(iii) A bronze fragment carrying a synthetic aesthetic patina applied for visual unity. The patina chemically suppresses further oxidation, yet it contains chlorides that, should the suppressive layer ever be breached, accelerate pitting—so the object simultaneously exhibits a masked reactive disposition and a finked protective one.

For each case, run all four stages, identify where the procedure forces an unwelcome recommendation (for example, mandating costly active conservation for an object that looks perfectly healthy), and name exactly which philosophical distinction—genuine disposition, fink, antidote, mask, or decoy—is doing the decisive work. Then state the two structural limits of the procedure: situations that require microscopic chemical-pathway knowledge you do not have, and situations where the institutional ontology of the object (is the polymer part of the artifact or an external interference?) is genuinely contested, leaving the classification underdetermined even after the procedure is complete. Preserve all distinctions that affect the sign-off decision, but present them as operational tests rather than theoretical labels.
