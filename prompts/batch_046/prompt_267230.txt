You're a philosophy-of-mind educator and part-time classroom-game designer -- please think carefully, do a quick brainstorm, then lay out the full plan. I need a runnable 90-minute seminar activity for about 12 upper-level students that makes them feel the force of the extended mind idea before anyone names Clark & Chalmers or uses the word 'parity.' Don't lecture up front. Split them into groups of four with these roles: one 'Core Agent' whose working memeory is artificially restricted (they can physically hold only two premise-cards at once), one 'Coupled Notebook' (holds six more premises, is instantly readable, automatically trusted, and sits right next to them), one 'Phone Archive' (holds identical premises but requires a 30-second 'search' and isn't fully trusted), and one 'Social Memory' (holds premises but can only speak when asked, once per minute). Give every group the same complex staged case -- judging whether a fictional AI governance plan meets conditions for privacy, autonomy, fairness, transparency, and accountability -- that requires integrating all ten premises to reach a justified call. Round one is 20 minutes: solve it with all resources available. Then, without warning, the facilitator confiscates the Notebook and says it's lost; round two is 10 minutes to finish reasoning without it. After that, restore the information only through the Phone or Social roles, so access is possible but friction-heavy. The rules should make the Notebook feel like part of the reasoning loop in round one, and its absence in round two should feel like a genuine cognitive gap -- not just an inconvenience. Follow with a 30-minute debrief that maps moments onto the idea: the Notebook as a coupled artifact, the automatic retrieval as meeting access/transparency conditions, the collapse after removal as the parity principle in action. Be frank about the limit: the activity can show functional dependency but can't reproduce the metaphysical claim that the paper literally is part of the mind, so you have to supply that argument yourself. Also say predictibly what will go wrong -- some students will call it 'just a tool,' others will ignore the Social Memory rules -- and how to handle it, especially the crutch objection. Include a brief timeline, material list (index cards, timer, case sheet), and a note on what participants should come away seeing. Keep it casual; a typo or two is fine.
