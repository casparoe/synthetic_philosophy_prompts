Is there a name for this? I keep seeing a move in intuition pumps, especially in personal identity or free will debates, and I'm not sure if it's a recognized failure mode or just sloppy arguing. The setup usually comes in two layers. First the author paints a really thick, almost literary scenario: a named character, a specific accident, emotional fallout, the works. That pulls a strong intuition — say, that the person survives or that blame sticks. Then a critic points out that this intuition actually depends on some hidden structural feature, like psychological continuity or a specific causal chain being intact. Instead of defending that feature, the author redescribes the case with bare variables: Subject S, Event E, Outcome O. The hidden feature is still technically there, but stripped of descriptive weight, so the objection now feels like it's attacking a different case, and the original intuition comes back. It's exacly the same underlying structure, just toggling between narrative density and structural sparsity to lock the intuition in place. It's not motte-and-bailey because both versions are equally contentious, and it's not plain equivocation since the claim hasn't technically changed — it's more like using framing thickness to shield the pump from critique. Does this have an established name? If not, can you coin one with clear boundaries — like, what distinguishes it from the framing effect, from moving the goalposts, or from just being a bad analogy? I want you to take a definite position: is this a real distinct phenomenon, and if so what should we call it and where does it stop being that and start being ordinary imprecision? Lead with your bottom line in the very first sentence, then give your reasoning, with no preamble and don't just restate my question back at me.
