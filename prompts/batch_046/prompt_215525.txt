I was chatting with my roommate last night about philosophy of biology and she mentioned this strange comparison people draw between DNA replication and Gödel's incompleteness proof — the idea that DNA is a self-referential code, since it stores instructions for the machinery that copies and reads itself, structurally similar to Gödel numbering where arithmetic encodes statements about itself. She called it the DNA-Gödel isomorphism, though I'm not sure that's an actual term or just something she made up after a late-night wikipedia spiral.

Anyway, I want to use this lens on the real 1953 Watson-Crick discovery. The basic facts: Watson and Crick build the double helix model using Franklin's Photo 51 and Chargaff's complimentary base-pairing rules, realize the geometery allows each strand to serve as a template, and in their Nature paper they include that closing remark that the pairing immediately suggests a copying mechanism. Analyze the episode through the self-replication framework. By the lights of the DNA-Gödel isomorphism, what kind of problem were Watson and Crick actually facing — was it just structural chemistry, or was it uncovering a biological analog of formal self-reference? Does the theory explain why the replication mechanism was hidden in the shape of the helix, or does it misdescribe the chemical reality? Where does it miss — for instance, does DNA actually "know" its own code the way a Gödel sentence "knows" its own unprovability? And in turn, does the fact that they solved replication by modeling physical structure rather than logical syntax show the isomorphism is just a metaphor, or does it reveal something deeper about how life encodes its own continuity?
