I have been turning over a line of reasoning that started with the well-known thought experiment in which Black stands ready to force Jones to do something, but Jones does it on his own, so Black never intervenes; even though Jones could not have done otherwise, we still hold him responsible. I tried to apply the same logic to ignorance and excuse, and now I cannot see where the argument breaks without breaking something I want to keep. Here is how it runs.

(1) In that case, Jones is responsible for his choice even though he lacked any genuine alternative, because the mechanism that would have blocked the alternative never actually entered the causal chain.
(2) So moral responsibility tracks the actual causal history of an agent’s decision — what his own mental states produced — rather than the mere existence of unrealised possibilities.
(3) If that is right, then responsibility should be unaffected by influences that were absent rather than active: a missing cause that never operated on the agent’s will is, causally speaking, on a par with Black standing idle.
(4) Now consider someone who commits a serious harm but has never, through no fault of his own, encountered any reason to think it wrong — raised in extreme isolation, say, or in conditions that made moral learning impossible. The absence of that knowledge is not an active force overriding his will; it is precisely an absent influence, like Black never stepping in.
(5) By (2) and (3), his own mental states caused the harm, and the missing moral knowledge never actually intervened, so he should be fully responsible, to exactly the same degree as someone who knew it was wrong and chose it anyway.
(6) But that conclusion feels intolerable: we ordinarily think unavoidable ignorance genuinely diminishes culpability, and treating the two agents alike seems like a failure of moral judgement rather than its perfection.
(7) Therefore either the Frankfurt-style reasoning fails to extend to conditions of knowledge and excuse, or we must accept that ignorance never excuses, which I am reluctant to do.

If I must choose, I would rather preserve (1) and (2) and find the fault elsewhere, perhaps in (3) or (4). Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Is there a hidden distinction between alternative actions and alternative beliefs that saves the intuition about ignorance without collapsing back into the idea that responsibility always requires that we could have done otherwise? I want to know whether the problem is an equivocation, a scope shift, or simply that I have to pay a price I have not yet named.
