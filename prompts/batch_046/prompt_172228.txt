Please explain the conceptual mechanics that separate republican liberty—freedom understood as the absence of domination rather than the absence of interference—from both negative and positive liberty, with special attention to what I will call the counterfactual tracking problem. On the republican view, an individual can suffer a liberty deficit even when no one actually interferes, so long as they live under the arbitrary power of another. This invites a modal question: does domination require that interference be genuinely available in nearby counterfactual situations, or does it depend solely on the present status relation of subordination regardless of modal accessibility? If domination tracks counterfactual interference, republican freedom appears to incorporate a modal expectation that negative liberty, which is concerned only with actual obstacles, does not. If domination does not track counterfactuals and instead attaches purely to status, then the classic case of a benevolent but structurally unchecked authority—one who genuinely never interferes and lacks any disposition to do so—remains a case of domination, but it becomes difficult to distinguish the harm done from a mere status insult or from the psychological discomfort that belongs to the domain of positive liberty and self-mastery. Please clarify how the republican account resists collapsing into either a refined negative-liberty calculus based on expected interference, or into a positive-liberty concern with recognition and psychological security. In doing so, address the role of the eyeball test—the notion that freedom involves being able to meet another's gaze without reason for fear or deference—and specify whether this test serves as an epistemic indicator of the absence of domination or as part of what constitutes domination's absence. I am looking for a precise conceptual analysis rather than a historical survey, and the explanation should be accessible to someone working without access to specific texts or citation databases.
