I've been in this philosophy of langauge seminar and we got stuck on the liar paradox for like two weeks, and I ended up thinking the usual Tarski hierarchy is way too rigid because it fixes levels syntactically, but also that revision theories feel like they're just kicking the can down the road with every update. So I kind of cooked up this view — maybe it's already out there but I haven't seen it in the readings — where the level of a liar-type sentence isn't fixed by syntax or by revision stages, but by the speaker's current refelctive stance. Like, when I say "This sentence is false" at a basic level I'm at L0, but if I then think about what I just said, I'm now operating at L1 and the same token gets re-evaluated there, and if I keep reflecting it climbs without ever bottoming out. The idea is that there's no single "the" evaluation, just evaluation-relative-to-reflective-level, so there's no contradiction, just a kind of cognitive escalation. I think this avoids both Tarski's artifical language restrictions and the revisionist problem of eventually settling on a false value.

But I realize I'm probably just insulating myself from counterexamples by shifting the level whenever it's convenient. So I want to know: what would actually count against this reflective-hierarchy view? Not just "it feels weird" — I mean, what kind of formal result, what kind of intuitive case, or what kind of problem in other areas (like self-reference in belief revision, or hierarchical models in math) should make me drop it? Which objections would defenders of this kind of view concede are real hits, and which ones are just misunderstandings? And specifically, is there a risk that by tying truth-levels to cognitive states I've made the view unfalsifiable — like, any apparent counterexample I can just say "well you were reflecting at a different level"? If so, is that a fatal flaw for a theory of truth, or is it okay for a view that's meant to be pragmatic? I want the defeaters ranked, from "this kills it" to "this is awkward but I can live with it", and I'd especially like to hear about whether there's some hidden collapse into either pure contextualism or back into Tarski's fixed levels that I'm missing.
