At a recent interdisciplinary workshop on mechanistic explanation in artificial systems, two colleagues of mine—Dr. Elena Voss, a philosopher of science, and Dr. Marcus Chen, a machine-learning researcher—became locked in a disagreement over whether two anomaly-detection systems instantiate the same mechanism. The first is an isolation-forest algorithm monitoring vibration spectra in turbine manufacturing; the second is a variational autoencoder monitoring packet-flow patterns in network security. Both flag outliers by measuring deviation from a learned baseline, yet they differ in substrate, in the concrete entities involved, and in domain.

Voss argued that they share a single mechanism: both realize the same interventionist invariance—perturbing input features raises path-length or reconstruction error, which triggers a flag—so that the correct mechanistic description abstracts away from tree nodes versus neural weights. Chen replied that on any serious mechanistic account entities and activities must be concrete and domain-specific; the entities in one case are sensor readings and split conditions, in the other packet headers and latent variables, so there is no shared mechanism, only a shared functional profile. Voss then pressed a reductio: if Chen’s concreteness requirement is correct, then the working-memory mechanism in two human brains with slightly different neural architectures would also fail to count as the same mechanism, undermining the very possibility of mechanistic generalization in neuroscience. Chen conceded that neural mechanisms allow some intra-domain implementation variability, but insisted that cross-domain portability—what he called mechanistic tourism—is precisely what a rigorous ontology should reject; anomaly detection is not a natural kind of mechanism but a family of locally optimized procedures. Voss countered by proposing structural portability: a mechanism is preserved across realizers when its organizational schema supports the same set of interventionist counterfactuals, regardless of substrate. Chen objected that this collapses mechanistic explanation into causal-modeling, stripping away the ontological specificity that distinguishes mechanistic accounts from purely functional ones.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move. For each of Voss’s and Chen’s principal arguments, indicate whether it is well-taken, problematic, or ambiguous, and explain why. In particular, address whether the reductio involving working-memory mechanisms succeeds, whether Chen’s distinction between intra-domain variability and cross-domain portability holds water, and whether Voss’s notion of structural portability genuinely splits the difference or merely renames a functionalist position. Finally, say who is more right overall, and identify what both parties are missing—especially regarding whether anomaly detection can coherently be treated as having a unified mechanistic ontology at all.
