Student answer:
Austin splits speech into three layers. The locutionary act is saying words with meaning, the illocutionary act is what you do in saying them (like promising or ordering), and the perlocutionary act is the effect you achieve in the hearer, such as convincing them. Pure performatives like "I promise" don't describe reality; they perform an action, so they aren't true or false. They can fail in different ways — Austin calls this being "unhappy," dividing it into misfires and abuses. The phrase "felicity conditions" was actually coined by Searle later on, because Austin himself never used that term.

The retroactive anchoring view says that when you promise, you don't just generate a future obligation; you also restructure how your prior mental state should be interpreted, so it counts as having been an intention to commit all along. I think this is fully compatable with Austin. Austin insists performatives are actions, not descriptions, but anchoring only describes the preconditions that made the action possible — namely, the speaker's prior psychology. So it adds a descriptive layer about the past without turning the performative itself into a constative. That solves the binding problem of intentional commitment: private intentions get publicly anchored without collapsing the distinction between doing and describing. So Austin should have no objection.
