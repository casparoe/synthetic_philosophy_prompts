I'm a heritage officer for a local council in the Midlands and we keep recieving boxes of papers from a defunct residential school — punishment ledgers, nurse reports, clinical notes, and letters the residents wrote but never sent. I need a reusable checklist, drawn from Foucault's ideas about power, knowledge and discipline, to decide what gets digitised and made public, what gets restricted, and how to describe it when the record is tangeled up with the institution's gaze. It has to be something I can actually run when I'm knackered and the box smells of mould, with clear steps but honest about where I'm just making a judgement call. Key things: how to tell if a document reproduces the disciplinary gaze or carries the resident's own voice; what to do when both are in the same page; how to apply this idea I'm playing with — 'counter-archival friction', where making something visible can extend the original power relation rather than break it; and what to write on the access form I have to sign. Work through three cases: a punishment ledger with margin notes that might be the residents writing back, a medical report that frames clear abuse in neutral clinical language, and a stash of unsent letters found behind a wall. One of these should come out the opposite of what I'd want — say the checklist tells me to publish but my gut wants to bury it, or the other way round. Also say plainly what the checklist can't do, especially about consent from people long dead, and where it will misfire if I treat it like a formula instead of a tool.
