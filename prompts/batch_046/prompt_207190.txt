I am working on comparative metaphysics and have noticed a recurring argumentative pattern in treatments of Buddhist anatta and its Western analogues that I have not been able to map cleanly onto the standard distinctions in the literature. A philosopher denies any ultimately real, persisting self, reducing the person to impermanent skandhas or to a bundle of psychological states without substantial unity. Yet within the same framework, diachronic commitments—promises, debts, moral accountability—are treated as binding across precisely those impermanent states, without an independent account of what makes the earlier bundle the legitimate bearer of obligations incurred by the later one. The denial is metaphysically rigorous, but normative continuity is reintroduced through talk of the same person that functions exactly like the substantialist language the thinker has rejected. I have observed this not only in certain Abhidharmic reductionist readings but also in contemporary Western bundle theories and in popular expositions of anatta where karma is said to affect the same being across lives despite the explicit denial of a transmigrating soul. The pattern differs from an explicit conventional-ultimate distinction, because the conventional self is not being appealed to as a conscious fiction; instead, it operates as if it carried real normative weight while its metaphysical basis remains unexamined. Does this specific tension have an established name in Buddhist philosophy, in analytic metaphysics of personal identity, or in philosophy of action? If not, how should it be distinguished from ordinary conventionalism, from mere inconsistency, and from the standard reductionist appeal to causal-psychological continuity?
