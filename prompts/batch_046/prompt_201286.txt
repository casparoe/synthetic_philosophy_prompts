I'm preparing a consistency audit for my school's philosophy seminar, and I want to check whether my commitments on welfare comparison and distributive justice actually cohere. Here is where I have landed:

(1) Any viable theory of distributive justice must be able to make interpersonal utility comparisons; without them we lack a principled reason to prioritize relieving severe deprivation in one person over a minor inconvenience to another.
(2) I reject revealed-preference and market-behavior accounts of welfare, since adaptive preferences and structural false consciousness can cause people to sincerely prefer conditions that diminish their objective well-being.
(3) In place of preference satisfaction, I have adopted a "phenomenal bandwidth" criterion: welfare comparisons should rest on the range and resolution of subjective experience permitted by an individual's cognitive architecture, treated as a content-neutral capacity rather than a measure of any particular conception of the good.
(4) I also hold that conceptions of flourishing are radically plural and incommensurable—what makes life genuinely worth living for one person may be literally unintelligible as a goal to another—so any universal welfare metric risks imposing a parochial vision of human good.
(5) Despite that pluralism, I endorse using disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) in global health prioritization, which treats years of healthy life as uniformly commensurable across all patients regardless of personal values or cultural frameworks.

Do these hang together? I can see potential friction, but I am unsure which conflicts are genuine and which only appear so. Specifically: does (5) conflict with (2), given that DALY disability weights are partly derived from population preference surveys? Does (3) genuinely conflict with (4), or does the content-neutrality of phenomenal bandwidth allow radical pluralism about flourishing to survive intact? And is there a deeper tension between (1)'s demand for cross-person comparability and (4)'s claim of incommensurability that forces me to abandon one? Please rank the real tensions by severity, identify which ones dissolve upon closer inspection, and indicate the least costly revision—without presuming which commitment I would be willing to drop.
