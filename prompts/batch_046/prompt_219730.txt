I am a high school student in an independent-study course on translation theory, and after encountering Douglas Hofstadter’s 1997 book Le Ton beau de Marot—which examines dozens of English renderings of Clément Marot’s brief lyric Ma Mignonne to show that perfect translation is impossible—I have become dissatisfied with the usual literal-versus-free dichotomy. I want to map the actual logical space of translator commitments when a poem must preserve, simultaneously, rhyme scheme, syllabic meter, syntactic inversion, lexical archaism, emotional register, and what I will call pronoun-ambiguity fidelity: the obligation not to resolve grammatically hidden gender relations that French leaves suspended but English pronouns force into the open. Please construct for me a decision-tree taxonomy that sorts translator philosophies by the concrete answers they give at each branching point, not by the school names they wear. The root node should ask whether a formal feature carries intrinsic value or only instrumental value in conveying meaning; subsequent nodes should present specific dilemmas, such as whether altering the rhyme scheme destroys the poem’s identity, whether syntactic mirroring is obligatory even when it yields ungrammatical English, whether archaism may be traded for modern diction if the emotional register survives intact, and whether resolving the speaker’s gender in translation counts as a betrayal of the original ambiguity. Each leaf should name or identify a position—include, for example, a Hofstadter-style pluralist who embraces multiplicity, a Benjamin-inspired advocate of pure-language syntax over semantic sense, a Nabokovian literalist, and whatever other combinations emerge logically—together with a one-sentence statement of its most awkward commitment. Build the tree from the logical combinations of answers rather than from a pre-existing list of schools; flag any pair of leaves that the tree genuinely cannot separate, especially where pronoun-ambiguity fidelity collapses into another dimension; and note any unoccupied branches with a brief diagnosis of whether they seem incoherent, merely unexplored, or unattractive. I do not need a bibliography or reading list, since I am working without external access and need an instrument I can apply directly to arguments I encounter in discussion.
