For an independent study project in my high school philosophy course, I have been examining whether genuine moral dilemmas—situations in which an agent is genuinely required to perform two incompatible actions and will necessarily violate a duty—are actually possible. I have tentatively adopted a reframing that treats apparent dilemmas not as conflicts within a single, unified moral system, but as cases of what I will call structural moral overload: a condition in which commitments drawn from incommensurable normative domains, such as deontic constraints versus virtue-based ideals of character, are applied simultaneously to one decision context without any available meta-rule that can adjudicate between them. Rather than asking you to evaluate whether this reframing is correct, I would like you to draw out its most important implications.

Specifically, please describe the major ramifications of this structural moral overload framework for three areas. First, how should it inform the design of ethical guidelines for autonomous decision systems—such as medical triage algorithms or autonomous vehicles—that must navigate conflicts between hard safety protocols and more flexible obligations to respect user autonomy? Second, if no unified moral system is violated and only an overload between domains occurs, what becomes of the normative significance of post-decisional moral regret or residue; does such regret still serve a rational function, or does it become a kind of psychological misfire? Third, how does this view affect the widely shared intuition that a well-formed moral agent ought to experience lasting, appropriate guilt after making tragic choices in extreme circumstances?

In your response, please distinguish clearly between points that rest on widely accepted logical or conceptual distinctions—such as the formal structure of conflicting obligations—and points that depend on genuinely contested philosophical commitments, for example regarding whether practical reason must be unified or whether moral emotions must track actual violations of duty. Do not present any contested position as though it represents the consensus among philosophers working on these questions.
