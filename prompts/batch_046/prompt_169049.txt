Next week I'm presenting to a joint phil-of-bio / cog-sci colloquim on what I'm tentativley calling the 'double decoupling' idea. The basic sketch is that Darwin's inversion — design without a designer — and Turing's inversion — computation without comprehension — aren't just cute analogies but share a deeper mechanistic structure: in both cases you get normative success (adaptation, correct output) that is decoupled from any internal tracking of the success conditions. I want to argue this breaks the standard representationalist fallback, were philosophers say the system doesn't understand but implicitly represents the solution; my claim is that implicit representation is just a band-aid over a structural gap, and recognizing the decoupling should push us away from both teleosemantic accounts of biological function and syntax-first accounts of computation. The room will have evolutionary biologists who are totally on board with blind selection but suspcious of extending it to cognition, a couple AI researchers who think computation needs semantic interpretation layered on syntax, and some enactivists who'll say I'm still smuggling representationalism in through the back door by framing everything as computation. I need a prep sheet: the objections in the order they'll actually come, phrased like each camp would put them, with what I should concede, what I should hold, and where each reply is weakest. Please flag my own weakest point — I think it's the leap from historical fitness (retrospective and population-level) to normative correctness (prospective and token-level) — and include the one objection I'm praying nobody asks, probably about whether neural networks or quantum computing break the decoupling analogy.
