Write a villanelle—nineteen lines, five tercets followed by a quatrain, with two repeating refrains in an ABA rhyme scheme—that stages Berkeley's master argument as an exchange between Philonous and Hylas. Use exactly these two refrains: "You conceive it, therefore you perceive" and "A tree that stands where no mind sees." In the first five stanzas, let the refrains alternate so that Hylas's attempt to imagine an unperceived tree (refrain B) is met each time by Philonous's reply that the very act of conception places the tree in the conceiver's mind (refrain A). Choose rhyme words—such as "sees" with "trees," "mind" with "find," and "perceive" with "conceive"—so that the sonic structure binds the opposing claims together rather than letting them sit apart. In the final quatrain, bring both refrains together in the last two lines (lines 18 and 19); the closing should show that refrain B collapses into refrain A, so that the "tree where no mind sees" is revealed as nothing but an idea in the very mind attempting to think it outside itself. Do not resolve the tension with a sermon on immaterialism; let the formal repetition of the villanelle do the philosophical work, so that the reader feels the loop of the reductio rather than being told the conclusion.

The poem should render the master argument accurately rather than reducing Berkeley to the slogan "to be is to be perceived." Keep the argument specific to the inseparability of the conceived object from the conceiving act: Hylas tries to frame a thought of matter existing unperceived, and Philonous demonstrates that framing the thought is itself a perceiving. A reader familiar with Berkeley's Principles should recognize this structure; someone unfamiliar should still find the poem coherent as a closed logical loop.

Also note any liberty taken with the voices of Philonous and Hylas, and whether binding "sees" to "trees" in the rhyme scheme risks overstating the link between visual perception and existence in Berkeley's view.
