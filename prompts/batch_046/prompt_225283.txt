Among philosophers of physics, the long-running dispute over whether deterministic chaos undermines determinism has reached a stalemate. One camp holds that Hamiltonian systems with positive Lyapunov exponents pose no philosophical problem: the governing equations remain fully deterministic, our predictive failure is purely epistemic, and the fact that unpredictable trajectories occupy a set of measure zero shows chaos is statistically negligible. The other camp replies that determinism must govern actual, specific trajectories rather than merely holding almost everywhere with respect to Lebesgue measure; since any physically realizable specification of initial conditions is necessarily granular, exponential divergence renders the future of the very system we inhabit genuinely open—unless one accepts an ontology of infinitely precise real-valued initial conditions that physics cannot instantiate. Both sides accept the same formal dynamics and bracket quantum mechanics. Is the disagreement best understood as verbal—hinging on whether determinism requires pointwise applicability or only measure-theoretic governance—as masking a hidden empirical question about whether physical magnitudes are continuous or discrete, or as a bedrock clash over whether determinism must entail in-principle predictability? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress: a formal distinction between pointwise and statistical determinism, an empirical argument about the ontology of initial conditions, a reframing of the question in terms of agency or explanation, or an argument that the impasse reflects irreconcilable starting intuitions and should not be expected to yield?
