I have spent the last decade as a clinical biostatistician designing Phase III cardiovascular trials, and I am thoroughly fluent in the vocabulary of estimands, intercurrent events, treatment-policy strategies, composite endpoints, hazard ratios with confidence intervals, regulatory risk-benefit frameworks, and protocol amendments, though my formal philosophical training extends no further than an undergraduate logic course. The topic has become personally urgent: I was recently appointed to an institutional review board evaluating a trial that had reached its primary endpoint with an unambiguous hazard ratio of 0.62 and a p-value far below the pre-specified alpha boundary. The descriptive results were clear, yet the committee deadlocked over whether these statistical findings generated an obligation to amend the protocol to grant broader access to the investigational therapy. One colleague remarked that you cannot derive an ought from a Kaplan-Meier curve, which struck me as an invocation of something like Hume's is-ought gap; another argued that defining "ethical approval" purely in terms of favorable efficacy and safety profiles was committing what philosophers call the naturalistic fallacy.

Please restate both positions—Hume's claim that no normative conclusion can be validly inferred solely from descriptive premises, and Moore's open-question argument that defining "good" in terms of natural properties always leaves a meaningful further question—entirely within the vocabulary of clinical-trial methodology and regulatory science I have described. Be precise about logical structure: show exactly how a descriptive statistical result, such as a treatment-effect estimate with its confidence interval and corresponding p-value, might or might not entail a normative regulatory decision without an intermediate normative premise, and show whether identifying "a good trial decision" with "a decision supported by statistically significant efficacy and acceptable safety data" collapses into tautology or leaves an open analog of Moore's question. Use concepts like primary and secondary endpoints, intercurrent events, estimand frameworks, equipoise, and risk-benefit assessment as your translation terms, not as loose metaphors.

Then provide the residue report. Identify which elements of these philosophical positions map cleanly onto statistical or regulatory concepts; which can only be translated by distorting either the philosophy or the trial vocabulary—for instance, by treating moral sentiment as analogous to regulatory preference, or by forcing "natural properties" to cover only biologically measurable endpoints while excluding normative protocol criteria; and which elements have no counterpart whatsoever in how clinical research is actually designed, analyzed, or governed. Finally, say whether the residue indicates that the statistical-regulatory framework is genuinely missing a normative dimension it needs, or whether it suggests that the philosophical distinctions, once honestly translated, depend on distinctions that do not do substantive work in practice. I am not looking for bibliographic references or a survey of the literature; I want an honest translation whose value lies as much in what fails to carry over as in what does.
