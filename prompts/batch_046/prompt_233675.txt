I serve on the certification board of an intergovernmental deep-sea registry tasked with deciding whether entities detected solely through indirect instrumentation may be admitted into the canonical biological ontology. A research consortium has submitted an application for provisional entry of a hadal-zone acoustic signature tracked for eighteen months via hydrophone arrays and sediment-displacement modeling; no visual observation or tissue sample has ever been obtained, and the extreme pressure makes direct capture technically impossible with current technology. The applicants argue that the signature exhibits consistent behavioral patterns, responds differentially to stimuli in predictively successful ways, and integrates structurally into local ecosystem models with accuracy comparable to that of directly observed species, suggesting that withholding ontological status arbitrarily privileges observational accessibility over structural coherence.

Half the board contends that ontology should include only entities for which direct epistemic access is not permanently blocked; they warn that admitting the signature collapses the distinction between a being and a well-calibrated theoretical artifact, pushing our registry toward instrumentalism and undermining the normative authority of direct identification in taxonomy. The other half holds that a rigid accessibility criterion is itself ontologically parochial, excluding entities that function causally and structurally like admitted species merely because of contingent technological limits, and that predictive success should carry equal weight with direct observation in conditions where the latter is impossible by physical law rather than mere practical difficulty.

I need to draft the board's formal decision memo for circulation before Thursday's vote. It should recommend a concrete course of action—provisional admission with explicit conditions, outright rejection, or the creation of an intermediate ontological tier—along with a clear time frame for re-evaluation. The reasoning must be actionable for the researchers, engage seriously with the strongest form of the accessibility objection rather than dismissing it as confusion, and specify what kind of indirect or future direct evidence would be sufficient to upgrade, maintain, or remove the entry. Please avoid appeals to specific philosophers by name and do not include bibliographic references; the memo should stand on its own reasoning.
