I organize a small private reading group—eight adults, none with formal philosophy training, meeting for ninety minutes every other week—to work through conceptual puzzles that don’t require specialist vocabulary. Lately we have been circling a problem about how we should reason when we do not know how many observers exist, or could exist, in different possible worlds. On one side, it feels natural to treat ourselves as randomly drawn from all observers who ever live, so that finding ourselves in a world with many observers should count as evidence for that kind of world. On the other side, there is a strange inversion: if we treat our own existence as strong evidence that observers are plentiful, we seem to be assuming in advance that the universe is arranged to produce people like us—an assumption that feels presumptuous, as though we are placing ourselves at the center of the reasoning. I want a structured in-group activity, not a lecture, in which the participants actually feel both pulls before anyone names them. Design a game with hidden rules and real, small stakes—tokens, cards, or sealed envelopes—representing different cosmological configurations: for instance, one world with a trillion brief, non-reflective observers; one with a billion long-lived, memory-bearing ones; and one in which exactly our group exists and nothing comparable does. The mechanics should force the group to make predictions or place bets about which world they inhabit, using only the fact of their own presence and some partial information about observer lifespans, so that they discover their intuitive betting strategy depends on a hidden premise about whether they are typical. Include a clear timeline for the ninety minutes, rules that need no external materials beyond what you specify, a payoff scheme that uses points or privileges rather than money, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto the two sampling assumptions and onto the charge of being presumptuous—so that the feeling of presumption emerges from the mechanics, not from my explanation afterward. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and be frank about which feature of the philosophical dispute—perhaps the exact formalization of observer moments or the problem of defining a reference class—the game cannot reproduce and I will need to supply by hand. Do not include bibliographies, citations, or requests for further reading; build the design directly from the concepts.
