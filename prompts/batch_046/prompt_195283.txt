Following a recent bereavement that has led me to reconsider questions of divine action and natural order, I have been reading introductory accounts of Al-Ghazālī's occasionalism in preparation for an interdisciplinary seminar I am organising. One well-known essay summarises his position in his critique of the philosophers as follows: Al-Ghazālī argues that fire does not burn cotton by any power intrinsic to fire itself; rather, God creates the burning directly, using the presence of fire merely as an occasion. The essay claims this is essentially Hume's scepticism about necessary connection, except that where Hume stops at bare regularities, Ghazālī supplies God as the hidden mechanism binding them together. It further asserts that Ghazālī denies all secondary causation—created things possess no causal efficacy whatsoever—including in mental life, where the human soul serves only as an occasion for God's direct production of thoughts and actions. From this the essay draws two conclusions: first, that Ghazālī's view is flatly incompatible with empirical science that presupposes stable dispositions in nature; second, that contemporary analytic occasionalists have revived this exact medieval position unchanged. The essay also proposes a further interpretive claim it calls "modal occasionalism": that for Ghazālī, not merely actual causal relations but counterfactual dependencies—what cotton would do in the absence of fire—are not grounded in created natures but sustained by separate divine decrees for each possible situation.

Is this a fair summary? In particular, assess whether the "modal occasionalism" reading is defensible or an anachronistic projection, and whether the conclusion about incompatibility with science survives once the misrepresentations are corrected.
