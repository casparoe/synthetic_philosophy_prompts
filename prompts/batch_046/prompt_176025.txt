I have been working through a long-running disagreement in my seminar on twentieth-century political violence regarding the Holocaust's status as a historically unique event and its relationship to modernity. On one side stands the intentionalist position, which insists that the genocide is incomparable because it was driven by a singular, totalizing racial ideology specific to Nazism; on the other stands the structural or functionalist account—associated with Zygmunt Bauman's analysis of bureaucratic rationality and modern administrative distancing—which treats the Holocaust as paradigmatically modern precisely because its scalability depended on generic features of rational bureaucracy rather than on uniquely German pathologies. What frustrates me is that both sides now share extensive empirical agreement about deportation logistics, camp administration, and the role of bureaucratic compartmentalization, and philosophers on both sides concede that modern organizational techniques can diminish moral inhibition; yet the dispute has not moved in years.

My working hypothesis is that the stall is not about missing evidence but about a hidden conflation: "uniqueness" is doing triple duty as a claim about moral singularity, about the non-replicability of Nazi ideological intent, and about the taxonomic isolation of the event from comparative genocide studies, while "modernity" is being read either as an explanatory mechanism or as a normalization thesis that threatens commemorative exceptionalism. Is the disagreement therefore verbal—different groups using "unique" and "modern" for different concepts—or is it substantive but mislocated, with one side answering a question about historical taxonomy and the other about moral ontology? Could there also be an unacknowledged normative disagreement about whether comparing the Holocaust to colonial violence or other bureaucratic atrocities diminishes its commemorative status, with uniqueness functioning as a proxy for an ethical claim rather than an empirical one? I want a diagnosis supported by what each side actually says: do they differ about standards of comparison, about which cases count as relevant analogues, about whether explanation requires ideological specificity, or about bedrock intuitions regarding the relationship between moral weight and historical singularity? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress—an empirical study of bureaucratic genocide outside the Nazi context, a sharp conceptual separation between moral uniqueness and structural uniqueness, or a deliberate reframing of the question away from comparative history and toward commemorative ethics—rather than another round of the same exchange?
