(1) Machiavelli holds that fortune governs about half of human affairs, leaving the rest to virtù. (2) That means for any given situation a prince faces, there's a clean split: half the outcomes are causally open to his skill, half are sealed by fortune regardless of his actions. (3) But Machiavelli also celebrates figures like Cesare Borgia for exercising virtù exactly when fortune seemed most hostile—when the river was already flooding, not just before it rose. (4) If virtù can work inside the domain that was supposed to be fortune's, then the 50/50 boundary isn't a fixed partion; it's at best a temporary line that skill can redraw. (5) But if skill can redraw it anywhere, then there's no stable territory that belongs to fortune alone—everything is potentially within virtù's reach, and the "half" governed by fortune shrinks to zero. (6) So either fortune does no real work, or "fortune's share" just means "the part where the prince hasn't been skilful yet," which makes the distinction useless for guiding action. (7) Therefore Machiavelli's view either collapses into the claim that everything is up to the prince, or it's just a retrospective label for luck we admire in hindsight.
