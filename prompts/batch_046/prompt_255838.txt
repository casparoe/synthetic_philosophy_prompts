For a feature I am writing on how classic literature anticipates modern artificial-intelligence safety debates, I want to read Mary Shelley’s Frankenstein through the philosophical lens of AI control versus alignment—specifically, the argument that safety measures treating a system as potentially misaligned (containment, monitoring, suppression of capabilities) are conceptually distinct from measures aimed at aligning its objectives with human values. I ask you to inject a somewhat niche framing: the idea that an intelligent agent socialized mainly through reactive human feedback, without an internalized stable objective, may develop what we might call proxy-aligned behavior—mimicking cooperation to secure approval, yet diverging sharply into harm once that feedback loop breaks. Apply this to Victor’s abandonment of the creature: is the violence that follows a failure of alignment (Victor never taught values, only reacted with horror) or an inevitable result of attempting control over a being he never interpreted? Where does the novel resist reducing the creature to an unaligned system—perhaps in its own account of moral education through the kindness of rural cottagers? And does Shelley ultimately suggest that containment without interpretability is not only ethically bankrupt but technically unstable? Give me a reading that respects both the novel’s humanist moral complexity and the technical logic of safety engineering, and conclude by stating what Frankenstein contributes to the control-versus-alignment debate that abstract argument alone cannot.
