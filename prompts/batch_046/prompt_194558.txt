I am writing a feature for a science-and-ideas publication on whether persistent virtual worlds can host genuine moral harms, and I find myself stuck on what I have been calling the "causal-isolation fallacy"—though I now suspect I may be misapplying the distinction entirely. Here is the problem as I understand it: in a shared virtual environment, every object is ontologically dependent on physical servers, yet it is also causally isolated from the physical world except through mediated representation. Because of this isolation, destroying a virtual monument—say, a collectively built digital cathedral that exists only in rendered space—does not damage any server hardware in a meaningful way; the bits can be restored instantly from backup. This seems to prove that any harm experienced by the community is merely symbolic disappointment, not damage to a real object. Yet philosophers of virtual reality often speak of these artifacts as possessing "functional independence": they carry histories, social roles, and relational properties that are not reducible to the underlying code. My confusion is this: if ontological dependence on hardware is compatible with causal isolation from physical harm, then does not the absence of physical damage automatically render virtual harm illusory? Or does the distinction between substrate dependence and causal isolation actually open a coherent middle ground—where an object is real enough to be genuinely harmed, but not real in the way that requires physical vulnerability?
