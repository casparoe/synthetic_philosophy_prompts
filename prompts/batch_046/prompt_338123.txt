I am a philosophy professor preparing for a seminar defense of a revised account of superrational coordination failures. The thesis I will defend is that both the Platonia dilemma and the Luring Lottery dissolve once we replace the standard assumption of unbounded common-knowledge rationality with a bounded, costly meta-reasoning constraint—specifically, that agents can perform at most k nested levels of reasoning about the other’s reasoning before incurring a catastrophic failure cost, where k is finite, privately held, and unknown to the other party. Under this k-stratification, I argue that superrational cooperation is rational only within parity classes of k (even-k agents coordinate with even-k, odd with odd), while cross-parity encounters necessarily collapse into defection. This resolves the Platonia paradox without abandoning superrationality and shows that in the Luring Lottery the so-called lure is not universally dominant but parity-dependent.

The audience is a philosophy of science and formal epistemology workshop consisting of: (i) three formal epistemologists who treat common knowledge as a standard fixed-point and are skeptical of costly-cognition modifications to game-theoretic models; (ii) one philosopher of mathematics concerned with whether unknown-parity reasoning can be given a coherent formal model without circularity or self-reference; (iii) a cognitive scientist who studies bounded rationality but will ask whether this maps onto any empirically observed reasoning limit; and (iv) a visiting decision theorist committed to causal decision theory who regards any appeal to reasoning-trace correlation as evidentialist confusion.

Please prepare my Q&A defense sheet. Order the objections by the damage each would do to the proposal if left unanswered, not by the order of speaking. For each objection, phrase it exactly as the objector would put it—using their disciplinary vocabulary—and indicate from whom it will come (by role, not by name). For each, specify: (a) what I should concede immediately; (b) what I must hold and defend; (c) a concise reply suitable for a two-minute response in a seminar; and (d) the precise point where that reply is weakest. Identify the single weakest point in my overall position—the one I should prepare to defend actively rather than hope goes unnoticed. Finally, include the one question I am hoping nobody asks, phrased in terms that both the cognitive scientist and the decision theorist could jointly press, with a reply that admits its speculative status and notes the gap that remains open.

The sketch of my argument is as follows. I begin with the standard Platonia setup—two agents with identical reasoning capacities facing a shared decision to cooperate or defect with a payoff structure that rewards mutual cooperation and punishes unilateral cooperation—and the standard Luring Lottery setup—one agent offers a transparent mechanism that makes cooperation dominant if the other is superrational. I then introduce the k-constraint: each agent knows their own k but only knows that the other agent’s k is drawn from a common-knowledge prior over {1,...,N}. The cost of attempting k+1 reasoning is not computational delay but structural failure, such as missing the decision window entirely. I derive that an agent should cooperate if and only if they assign nonzero probability to the other agent having the same parity of k, weighted by the prior. This yields a stratified equilibrium. I claim this is not an ad hoc patch but reveals that superrationality was always implicitly parameterized by reasoning depth.
