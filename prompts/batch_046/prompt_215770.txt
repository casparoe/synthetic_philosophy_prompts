I have been revisiting Bernard Williams's integrity objection in preparation for an advanced seminar, particularly his worry that impartial moral theories require agents to treat their deepest commitments—what he calls ground projects—as fungible inputs to a calculus of overall good, a failure he illustrates with the husband who saves his wife only after inserting a mediating moral permission that amounts to one thought too many. A colleague in our department has begun pressing an analogy I find both suggestive and potentially misleading: she likens an agent's ground projects to the clinker-built ribs of a traditional wooden vessel, arguing that just as replacing a single oak rib with a steel beam does not merely repair the ship but transforms its stress distribution, its buoyancy, and its historical identity as that specific craft, so substituting or suspending a ground project for moral reasons does not modify the agent but dissolves the practical identity that makes them the particular person they are. She uses this to license the stronger ontological claim that ground projects are not merely cherished attachments but structurally constitutive of agency itself, which she takes to vindicate Williams against critics who read him as merely defending sentimental partiality. I want you to audit this analogy with real precision: lay out the explicit correspondences between the ship's construction and the agent's practical identity, identify exactly where the mapping fractures—whether in the reversibility of project-change versus rib-replacement, in the normative status of historical authenticity, or in the fact that agents can reflectively endorse revisions that shipwrights cannot perform on vessels—state clearly whether the analogy smuggles in a conservative bias about preservation that Williams's own view does not require, and then take a committed position on whether the conclusion—that ground projects are ontologically non-fungible rather than merely psychologically central—actually survives once those disanalogies are fully on the table. Do not organize your answer with headings, bullet points, boldface, or tables; write it as continuous prose in the manner of a formal letter, using American English, and do not merely survey possibilities—choose a definite verdict and defend it with your reasons.
