As a novelist accustomed to weighing the moral weight of minor characters who possess no autonomous plot arc of their own, I have been sketching an essay that applies Kant's indirect-duty view of animals to narrative ethics. The core claim is that Kant's refusal to grant direct moral duties to non-rational beings—while insisting that cruelty toward them is wrong because it hardens the agent toward rational beings—maps surprisingly well onto the novelist's obligation to treat even instrumental figures with a certain restrained dignity. In fiction, a peripheral character exists largely to illuminate the protagonist's choices; yet if the author subjects that figure to gratuitous degradation purely for dramatic effect, the corruption extends beyond the page, habituating both writer and reader to a kind of aesthetic callousness. I propose that animals occupy a structurally analogous position in Kant's moral universe: they are not ends in themselves, but their suffering is not morally neutral because it shapes the character of those who cause it. I also anticipate an objection I want to examine: that the analogy collapses because fictional characters are artifacts of the author's will, whereas animals are independent beings, so treating them as "narrative instruments" risks precisely the kind of instrumentalization that Kant warns against in human relations. I would be grateful if you could comment on these notes—particularly on whether I have represented Kant's distinction between direct and indirect duties with reasonable accuracy, whether the narrative analogy illuminates anything genuinely useful about animal ethics, and where the comparison between fictional artifacts and living creatures becomes strained.
