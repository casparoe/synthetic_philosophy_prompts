I have just concluded a rather protracted argument with a friend about Derek Parfit’s teletransporter thought experiment, and I am left with what feels like a deep tension in the standard interpretation. The usual presentation asks whether I survive if my body is destroyed and a psychologically continuous replica is constructed elsewhere; the answer is meant to show that personal identity is not what matters, since I should care about the replica’s existence despite the absence of physical continuity. My friend, however, insisted that psychological continuity is not an all-or-nothing relation but comes in degrees—stronger or weaker connections over time. If that is correct, then the teletransporter is merely an extreme case of perfect continuity, not a special boundary case. But this raises a difficulty: suppose we imagine a modified machine that reconstructs only sixty per cent of my psychological traits, leaving the remainder altered. In that scenario, is it correct to say that I survive to a degree of sixty per cent? If so, then survival itself becomes a graded notion, which appears to violate the logical structure of identity—something either is me or it is not. Yet if we deny that survival can be partial, we seem forced to draw an arbitrary threshold somewhere along the continuum of psychological connection. So my question is this: does Parfit’s argument actually depend on treating psychological continuity as binary after all, and if not, how can we maintain that identity is not what matters without either embracing a logically incoherent notion of partial identity or imposing an unmotivated cut-off point?
