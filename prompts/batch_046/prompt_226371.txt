I am a high school junior in an advanced philosophy seminar, and I am designing a take-home exam for my peers on the mechanics of moral revolution. I want one essay question that works with Bernard Williams's claim that norms like dueling died through shifts in honor rather than through direct moral argument, and with Kwame Anthony Appiah's counter that moral arguments can drive change when they align with shifts in honor and interest. Rather than letting students simply summarize these positions, I want the prompt to push them into the weeds: it should ask them to analyze the rapid decline of indoor workplace smoking in the United States between approximately 1990 and 2010—a case that is familiar but rarely treated in the philosophical literature—through both frameworks. Specifically, the question should require students to explain whether the collapse of that norm is better accounted for by interest-based explanations (litigation costs, ventilation economics), by Appiah-style moral argument about secondhand harm that gained traction only after honor codes shifted, or by Williams-style dynamics in which smoking itself lost social prestige and thus its defenders lost standing. Crucially, the prompt must force them to confront an internal tension: if Williams is right that honor dynamics do the real explanatory work, then why did the same honor culture not block the initial rise of workplace smoking in the first place? The respondent should be asked to defend a specific verdict about which factor was dominant and to show exactly where the competing framework breaks down in this case.

Please provide the exam question itself, a model answer of roughly 650 to 800 words that demonstrates how to navigate the tension without merely reciting the standard slavery-and-dueling examples, and a marking scheme for a 90-minute exam that distinguishes an A answer—one that identifies the tension, applies both Williams and Appiah with precision, and offers a defended verdict—from a B answer that applies both frameworks correctly but either misses the tension or fails to commit to a verdict. The scheme should list the specific conceptual moves required, assign point values that total 25, and note the typical mistakes that cost marks at each stage.

Please write everything in American English, keep the prompt accessible to a student who cannot search the web, and do not include requests for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists.
