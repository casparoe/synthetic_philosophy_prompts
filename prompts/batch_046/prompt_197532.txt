As a retired experimental physicist who has only recently begun reading philosophy in earnest, I find myself returning to a pattern I notice whenever prison design is discussed, and I wonder whether philosophers have already named and mapped it precisely.

Consider two architectures of punishment. On one side stands the Norwegian Halden prison: private cells, unarmed staff, daily education and vocational training, and a physical environment deliberately normalized—quiet, comfortable, almost domestic. The explicit rationale is that the punishment consists solely in the deprivation of liberty; every other form of suffering has been engineered away, as if one were isolating a single variable in a controlled experiment. On the other side stands the American model of mass incarceration: overcrowded facilities, prolonged solitary confinement, inadequate care, and a culture of violence that seems inseparable from the sentence itself. Here the suffering is not incidental; it is woven into the structure.

What interests me is the conceptual asymmetry that emerges when defenders of each model are pressed. Advocates of the Halden approach say that once liberty is removed, the state has no further moral claim to inflict hardship; the treatment is pure deprivation, and everything else is noise that should be minimized. Advocates of punitive incarceration, by contrast, often imply that liberty-loss alone is insufficient to match the moral gravity of serious crime—there must be an added ingredient of hardship, a kind of active suffering that completes the punishment.

This looks to me like a disagreement not merely about policy but about the ontology of punishment itself: whether imprisonment is essentially a single-factor phenomenon (the removal of freedom) or an inherently compound phenomenon in which suffering is a necessary constituent rather than a confounding variable. I have tried to find the literature that distinguishes these two conceptions cleanly—perhaps a term for the view that punishment should be pure liberty-deprivation, and another for the error of treating avoidable suffering as part of the penal dose—but I lack the vocabulary.

Does this distinction have an established name? Is there a recognized concept for the methodological intuition that humane prison design controls for suffering in order to study rehabilitation, analogous to isolating variables in an experiment? Or for the opposing view that such control actually dissolves the punishment, leaving only detention? If philosophers have carved this terrain, I would like to know the precise boundaries: which positions treat suffering as an accidental byproduct, which treat it as essential, and whether there is a middle view that accepts compound punishment without endorsing cruelty. I am not asking for a bibliography, only for the conceptual map—what the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges lie.
