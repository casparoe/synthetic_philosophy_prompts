I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, but I've been trying to formalize a worry about archaeological inference and ended up with an argument that seems to show the field is impossible. Here are the steps: (1) If archaeologists want to recover a belief B held by a past group, B must be linked to some material trace T that B causally explains. (2) For any such T, there exists an incompatible belief B* that, given the same technological constraints and social functions, would have produced an artifact with the same observable properties—call this the functional polysemy of the material record. (3) So the archaeological evidence for B is always symmetrically compatible with B*. (4) If evidence is genuinely symmetric between incompatible hypotheses, rational belief-formation requires withholding judgment rather than selecting one. (5) Therefore no archaeologist can ever be justified in attributing a specific belief B rather than B*. (6) But institutions are constituted by shared beliefs; if no beliefs are knowable, no institutions are either, which would make all institutional archaeology impossible—clearly too strong a conclusion. (7) So belief-attribution in archaeology must be impossible, which can't be right. Which premise or step should I reject, and what does giving it up cost? I want to keep the idea that artifacts don't uniquely determine mental content, and I'd prefer not to abandon basic rational standards of belief. Please respond as a numbered list with exactly five items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
