I am drafting a constitutional drama set in Strasbourg, and I have given my skeptical jurist an argument against the coherence of EU shared competences that sounds airtight yet must contain a hidden fracture—because I need the rebuttal scene to land honestly rather than by authorial fiat. Here is his reasoning, laid out in steps:

(1) The principle of subsidiarity, codified in Article 5(3) TEU, permits Union action only when the objectives of proposed measures cannot be sufficiently achieved by the Member States, whether at central, regional, or local level.

(2) The phrase sufficiently achieved must be interpreted comparatively: the Union may act only when it can achieve the objective more effectively than Member States acting alone.

(3) For any policy domain, one can always construct a hypothetical local pilot that achieves comparable outcomes at lower administrative cost, drawing on tacit local knowledge—particular regulatory cultures, informal enforcement networks, geographically specific incentives—that central planners cannot replicate.

(4) Therefore, for virtually any proposed Union measure within a shared competence, there exists some Member State or sub-national unit that could, in principle, achieve the objective locally well enough to satisfy the subsidiarity test.

(5) From (1) and (4) it follows that the Union should never exercise a shared competence, since the condition for legitimate central action is never uniquely met.

(6) Yet the Treaties explicitly enumerate shared competences—environment, consumer protection, transport, energy—where Union and national powers overlap and Union legislation is valid.

(7) To avoid contradiction, one might read sufficiently achieved not as optimal local performance but as meeting a minimal adequacy threshold; once that threshold is crossed locally, the Union is barred regardless of how much better central coordination might be.

(8) Apply this minimal-threshold reading to climate policy: each Member State can meet a modest national emissions-reduction target, so the Union could never justify a unified emissions-trading scheme, even though unilateral state efforts are undermined by carbon leakage across open borders.

(9) Carbon leakage demonstrates that for interdependent objectives, local achievement is not genuinely possible—what is achieved in one jurisdiction is undone by spillovers from others—so (4) fails for problems with strong cross-border externalities.

(10) But digital-market harmonization, labor mobility, and product safety present precisely the same interdependence: purely local rules are eroded by cross-border movement. Thus the spillover exception applies to every shared competence, collapsing the category either downward into exclusive local authority or upward into exclusive Union authority, with no stable intermediate zone.

(11) Hence the constitutional architecture of shared competences is conceptually unstable; subsidiarity either dissolves it upward or downward, leaving no coherent middle ground.

My jurist is determined to preserve (5)—the claim that shared competences are practically empty—because it serves his dramatic argument for dissolution. Yet he suspects the flaw lies in an equivocation between can achieve and can achieve without being undermined by external effects. Which step should he surrender, what does that surrender cost him, and is there a hidden reading—perhaps drawing on the Swiss cantonal experience or on a distinction between procedural and substantive subsidiarity—that saves shared competences without rendering the principle toothless?
