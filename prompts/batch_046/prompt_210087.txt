I am currently taking a postgraduate module on the philosophy of psychiatry, and last week our seminar focused on Thomas Szasz's argument that mental illness is a myth—specifically, that conditions such as schizophrenia and depression are not diseases of the brain but problems in living that have been medicalised for social control. During a discussion afterwards, a colleague constructed an extension of Szasz's reasoning that now strikes me as proving far too much, yet I cannot locate the precise fault.

Here is her argument, laid out exactly as she presented it. (1) For any condition to count genuinely as an illness, there must be an identifiable pathological lesion, infectious agent, or specific biochemical dysfunction that explains the presenting symptoms; without this, we are dealing with a metaphor rather than medicine. (2) Szasz demonstrated that so-called mental illnesses fail this test: they are diagnosed through behaviour, speech patterns, and subjective reports, with no consistently observable tissue damage or uniform biological marker. (3) Hence psychiatric disorders are not illnesses but conflicts between an individual and her social environment—problems in living misclassified by a medical vocabulary. (4) Yet the same absence of a single, necessary biological substrate applies to numerous conditions that general medicine treats without controversy: essential hypertension, chronic non-specific lower back pain, irritable bowel syndrome, and even obesity in many clinical presentations are defined by symptom clusters and behavioural criteria rather than by one universal pathophysiology. (5) Therefore, if Szasz's criterion is applied consistently rather than selectively to psychiatry, these conditions must also be reclassified as problems in living rather than true diseases. (6) But this conclusion is intolerable: it would imply that large domains of internal medicine, rheumatology, and gastroenterology are engaged in the same medicalisation of social and behavioural conflicts that Szasz criticises in psychiatry. (7) So either Szasz's distinction collapses under generalisation, or we must accept an arbitrary exemption for physical medicine that we cannot justify.

My colleague insists that she wants to preserve premise (1) and the conclusion that psychiatry is uniquely problematic, but she recognises that the argument as it stands undermines her own position. I feel the error may be a subtle equivocation between absence of a single necessary substrate and absence of any biological involvement whatsoever, or perhaps a modal slide between what is required for diagnosis and what is required for the condition's existence, but I am unsure which step bears the weight. Could you identify which premise or inference I should reject? Please explain why that particular step seemed plausible—what intuition or definitional assumption made it look sound—and then lay out what each alternative rejection costs. In particular, if I abandon (1), can I still maintain a modified Szaszian critique of psychiatric classification without committing myself to biological reductionism across all of medicine? And if I reject (4) or (5) instead, what am I conceding about the relationship between mental and physical conditions? Please provide your answer in British English.
