I serve on the institutional review board of a neurotechnology research consortium. A partner firm has proposed a pilot study called "Gradual Substrate Migration": over a period of seven years, synthetic analogues gradually replace a volunteer's prefrontal cortical neurons, replicating both synaptic weights and temporal dynamics in real time. Concurrently, the synthetic substrate writes only the state differences—never a full snapshot—to an external archive each night, which the firm describes as a "continuous identity backup" that could be reconstructed if the biological substrate fails. Our board must render a decision on whether to approve the pilot for three volunteers, specifying conditions related to legal personhood, employment and liability continuity, and termination protocols.

The central conceptual tension is this: because the archive contains only differential logs, any reconstruction from it might omit emergent properties that depend on the full network topology at a given moment, not merely on recent changes; meanwhile, by year four the gradually replaced biological original and a fully reconstructed digital version could both exist with equally plausible claims to being the same person, creating a branching-identity conflict that our liability framework does not address. I am a software engineer by training and do not have a formal background in philosophy, so please explain any conceptual distinctions in plain language rather than invoking named theories or texts.

Draft the board's formal decision memo: recommend approval, conditional approval, or rejection; include a concrete timeline or conditions if you choose the middle option; treat the branching-identity objection with genuine seriousness rather than dismissing it; and present the strongest argument for the position you do not ultimately endorse. The memo should be written as a document the board could circulate and act upon.
