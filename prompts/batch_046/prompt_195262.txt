I am a PhD candidate in philosophy of science, and next week I am facilitating a seventy-five-minute session of an upper-level graduate seminar—about fourteen students—on metaphysics and epistemic risk. I want to design an in-class simulation that makes the group feel the practical force of the simulation argument, not merely the familiar statistical intuition about ancestor simulations, but a more specific planning paralysis: if one takes the disjunction seriously, then long-range institutional planning appears to lose its point because our planning horizon may terminate inside a nested simulation, yet we are structurally compelled to continue allocating resources as if the horizon were open-ended. I was discussing this with a colleague who argued that the argument’s real bite lies in undermining our default assumption that planning extends into an unmediated base reality. The activity should therefore assign roles—“base-reality planners,” “nested-level administrators,” and “epistemic auditors” who do not know their nesting level—together with small non-monetary stakes (points that determine whether the group earns a shared reward, such as exemption from a brief reading response). Over several decision rounds the groups must commit resources to long-term projects without knowing whether those commitments will be evaluated by a higher-level simulator or erased entirely, so that rational strategies diverge sharply once nesting uncertainty is introduced and the participants encounter the coordination failure themselves before any formal labeling occurs.

Please provide a complete, runnable design: specific rules for role assignment and information hiding, the materials needed (index cards, sealed envelopes, a simple payoff table), a timeline broken into ten-minute segments, and the payoff scheme that creates genuine tension without using real money. Include a debrief that maps the moments of the activity onto the structure of the simulation argument—the prior probability computation, the disjunction between technological maturity and extinction, and the precise step where planning rationality collapses—while being explicit about which feature of the argument the simulation cannot reproduce (for instance, the actual statistical estimation of simulation prevalence across civilizations) and how I should supply that limitation verbally. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it—likely either a rapid collapse of the information-hiding mechanism or a subgroup that resolves the nesting uncertainty too quickly through meta-reasoning—and how to handle that failure without destroying the pedagogical point.
