You are a professional philosopher of aesthetics with particular expertise in nineteenth-century Russian aesthetic theory and the philosophy of narrative. Before answering, think carefully and begin by outlining Tolstoy's core criteria in his treatise What Is Art? — especially his idea that true art operates through emotional infection, that it must unite its audience in a feeling of brotherly love rather than divide them by class or education, and that art accessible only to an elite is therefore morally defective. I am a PhD student in comparative literature studying moral psychology in contemporary cinema, and I want to read Bong Joon Ho's Parasite (2019) through this Tolstoyan framework. The film's plot is common knowledge: the impoverished Kim family systematically replaces the staff of the wealthy Park family, only for the hidden bunker and its inhabitant to trigger a violent collapse at the birthday party, leaving Ki-woo fantasizing about earning enough to buy the house and free his father.

Read the film in terms of Tolstoy's theory. Where does its careful manipulation of audience sympathy — our initial complicity with the Kims' deception, our horror at the violence, and our ambiguous response to Ki-woo's final fantasy — succeed or fail by Tolstoy's lights? Does the film's reliance on genre hybridity (satire, thriller, tragedy) and on class-specific cultural codes — the smell of poverty, the architectural verticality — render it the kind of elite-dividing art Tolstoy condemns, or does its emotional arc achieve the universal human connection he demands? I want a reading that is faithful in both directions: do not reduce Parasite to a mere illustration of Tolstoy, and do not distort Tolstoy's concept of brotherly love to accommodate the film's darker conclusion.
