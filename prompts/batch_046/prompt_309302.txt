Schrödinger died in 1961, well before split-brain studies, global workspace models, and brain-computer interfaces became central to the philosophy of mind. He was also deeply influenced by Advaita Vedanta and argued that individual consciousness is an illusion—there is only one universal consciousness, the Atman identical with Brahman, and the appearance of many minds is a product of maya. I want you to predict how he would have reacted to a post-1961 conceptual challenge I will call the “distributed sakshi problem”: suppose empirical or technological developments—say, corpus-callosum division yielding two hemispheres with intact first-person reporting, or a single neural substrate driving two fully embodied, sensorially independent agents via interfaces—suggest that one biological origin can sustain two apparently complete centers of awareness, each experiencing itself as the singular witness (sakshi) with no direct access to the other. Would Schrödinger have taken this as (a) a deeper confirmation that multiplicity is maya, since even “two” witnesses are just refracted manifestations of the one undivided consciousness; (b) a genuine empirical threat that should push him to abandon Vedantic monism for a plural ontology of minds; or (c) proof that true consciousness never resides in the divided substrate at all, so the experiments only reveal the illusory vehicle (upadhi) and leave the unitary witness untouched? In constructing your answer, draw on the reasoning he actually employed about the unity of mind and the illusory nature of plurality, but also identify exactly where modern neuroscience or philosophy of mind would have forced him to revise or qualify his view. Do not provide citations, a bibliography, or reading list; build the position from conceptual commitments alone. At the end, state how confident you are in this reconstruction overall and single out the specific inference—whether about his interpretation of empirical data, his commitment to non-dualism, or the Vedantic analogy—about which you are least certain.
