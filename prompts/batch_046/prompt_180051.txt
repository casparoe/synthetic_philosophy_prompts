Hey, I was thinking about this argument a grad student brought me, and I can't figure out where the sleight of hand is. Maybe you can help?

(1) Compatiblists say determinism doesn't threaten free will, because what matters is that the action flows from the agent's own reasons and desires, not whether they could have done otherwise.

(2) In a standard Frankfurt case, an agent acts on her own reasons, and there's a hidden controller who'd step in only if she wavers, but she never wavers. The agent couldn't have done otherwise, yet compatiblists usually say she still acts freely, since the controller never actually interferes.

(3) Now imagine a nested version: not just one controller, but a deterministic cascade. Every level of the agent's reasoning is itself the inevitable product of prior causes stretching back to the big bang. If she had reasoned differently, some earlier physical state would have been different, but that state was itself fixed.

(4) So in this nested case, the agent acts entirely from her own internal reasons, yet there's a deterministic chain that guarantees she won't deviate. Structurally, she's in the same position as the Frankfurt agent—except the "controller" is just physics.

(5) If compatiblists grant free will in the simple Frankfurt case, they should grant it here too: her reasons are genuinely hers, and no external manipulator is actually interfering.

(6) But if determinism is true, then every real-world agent is in exactly this nested position. Every human choice is the inevitable output of a causal chain that started long before birth.

(7) So the compatiblist must say either that no one ever has free will (giving up their view), or that every determined being—including rocks, thermostats, and current language models—has free will, as long as their outputs flow from their internal functional states.

(8) The second option proves way too much.

My gut says the problem is in step (5) or maybe (4), but I can't quite pin it down. Which premise should I reject, and why did it look so plausible? If I reject it, do I have to bite the bullet on the principle of alternate possiblities after all? Or is there some other cost I'm missing?
