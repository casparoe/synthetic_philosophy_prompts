Since I began volunteering at a sanctuary that rehabilitates injured corvids and cephalopods, I have found myself holding a cluster of views about consciousness across species, and I want an audit before I present them in a seminar. I hold: (a) that consciousness is genuinely gradated, with no sharp boundary between sentient and non-sentient systems; (b) that integrated information in distributed biological networks can yield emergent phenomenal properties not reducible to the parts, so that an octopus arm or a tightly coordinated corvid flock might instantiate a distinct conscious subject rather than merely a collection of conscious nodes; (c) that moral consideration should track the intensity and integration of phenomenal experience, not taxonomy or evolutionary proximity to humans; and (d) that if a collective entity achieves unified phenomenal integration, harming its individual constituents is not necessarily equivalent to harming the collective subject, and vice versa. Do these four commitments hang together? In particular, does gradualism about individual consciousness conflict with the emergence claim for collectives? If moral weight tracks phenomenal intensity, am I committed to treating a highly integrated flock as having greater standing than the summed standing of its members, and is there a hidden tension between distributive and collective welfare? Please rank any genuine conflicts by severity, distinguish apparent tensions that dissolve on inspection, and state the least costly revision should consistency require giving something up.
