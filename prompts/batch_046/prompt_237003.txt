I am part of an interdisciplinary working group—philosophers, legal theorists, and one systems engineer—with roughly eighteen months and modest funding. Our aim is not to survey existing literature but to settle a small number of conceptual questions about privacy that could actually shape durable policy frameworks, especially in settings where technological mechanisms change faster than regulations can adapt. We are deliberately avoiding technology-specific prescriptions; we want conceptual results that survive changes in implementation.

Within this scope, I see at least four open lines of work, but I am unsure how to rank them given our constraints. First, the classic question of whether privacy possesses intrinsic moral value or is purely instrumental to autonomy, dignity, or social coordination—settling this seems upstream of any normative framework. Second, what I will call the aggregation-inference problem: whether a privacy violation can occur solely through the automated synthesis of distributed, individually non-sensitive traces into a composite profile that reveals something sensitive, even when no single trace is disclosed and no human intentionally observes the synthesis. Third, the observer-dependency problem: whether the concept of privacy is conceptually tied to intentional human observation, or whether distributed, non-intentional processing by multi-agent automated systems can constitute a morally relevant privacy breach in a sense that demands legal recognition. Fourth, whether contextual-integrity frameworks can survive the collapse of discrete social contexts—work, health, domestic life—into single pervasive environments without clear boundary mechanisms.

Given our eighteen-month horizon, our philosophical rather than empirical or engineering focus, and our need for results that inform policy without requiring constant technological updating, which two or three of these lines (or others you think I have omitted or mischaracterized) deserve the bulk of our effort? Please defend your prioritization with explicit criteria: which questions are upstream of others, which are tractable for a group without access to proprietary data or advanced ML infrastructure, which would genuinely change how a regulator or court should think about privacy, and which should be deprioritized because resolving them would not alter much downstream work or policy design. I am not looking for a bibliography, a reading list, or citations to specific texts; I want a reasoned agenda. Please write your response in American English.
