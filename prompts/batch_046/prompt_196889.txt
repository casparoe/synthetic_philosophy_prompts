I'm delivering the opening remarks at a two-day workshop on democratic innovation that brings together political theorists who specialize in citizens' assemblies and municipal officials who have actually convened local sortition panels but have never read the theory literature. The room is split almost evenly: half will immediately recognize references to descriptive representation and deliberative polling, and the other half will have managed selection processes for neighborhood juries without ever encountering those terms. I need a formal five-minute address that does real structural work rather than welcoming guests and listing panels—something that shapes what the workshop can and cannot claim over the next two days.

The conceptual architecture I want you to build aloud centers on a tension inside the justification for sortition that is usually treated as seamless but actually pulls in opposite directions. On one side, random selection is defended as producing a statistical microcosm: a sample that mirrors the population's demographics and therefore carries descriptive legitimacy, which implies that dissenting voices within the assembly are noise to be corrected by better sampling and that recommendations should be weighted by how closely the group tracks broader public opinion. On the other side, sortition is defended as realizing a form of political equality—anyone might be called to govern—which gives each selected citizen an authorization independent of whether they represent anyone in particular, meaning that weighting by representativeness or treating members as data points undermines the very democratic commitment that justified the lottery in the first place. I want you to lay out this conflict precisely, without resolving it cheaply, and to use it to frame the workshop's practical stakes: whether citizens' assemblies should hold advisory or binding authority depends in part on which of these two justifications we are actually running, and conflating them produces institutional designs that promise both statistical fidelity and democratic authorization without delivering either consistently. Speak clearly enough that the practitioners see their own experience of running selection processes reflected in the concepts, and precisely enough that the theorists recognize this is not the standard election-versus-sortition comparison they have heard in every other keynote. Please do not offer citations, reading lists, or references to specific authors and texts; construct the argument from the concepts themselves, and keep the tone formal but not stiff—this is an opening address, not a lecture.
