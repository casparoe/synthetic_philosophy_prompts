Having recently examined family archives linking my ancestors to the administration of a plantation colony governed by mercantile monopoly, I have found Adam Smith’s analysis of empire unexpectedly personal, and I am struggling with a specific conceptual tension in his Wealth of Nations. Smith contends that colonies founded for extraction primarily benefit the mother country through a monopoly of trade rather than through free exchange, and that the fiscal and military costs of defending them typically exceed any commercial gain. He also seems to suggest that colonies of settlement may eventually mature into self-governing societies compatible with his system of natural liberty. My question concerns the consistency of this distinction: if Smith believes that imperial institutions structurally reproduce exclusive privileges and distorted incentives—thereby trapping extractive colonies in permanent mercantilist corruption—does he possess a principled account of why settlement colonies can escape that same institutional path dependence? Or is his acceptance of temporary imperial oversight for them conceptually at odds with his broader claim that state monopoly and intervention necessarily corrupt economic order? Please explain whether Smith’s framework contains a rigorous boundary between colonies capable of liberalization and those permanently confined to extractive monopoly, and how that boundary relates to his general critique of mercantilist state power. I do not need citations or a reading list; I am seeking a precise conceptual clarification.
