I am writing as a student in a philosophy of mind seminar that has spent the last month on the ontology of representation and downward causation. Our discussions have repeatedly circled back to Hofstadter’s careenium image—the cranium reconceived as a space where high-level symbols (symmballs) and low-level neural substrates (marbles) interact—and specifically to the question of whether symbols genuinely push the substrate around or are merely pushed by it. On one side of the room sit the dynamical systems advocates, who treat symmballs as useful descriptive fictions and insist that all causal work is done by biochemical and electrical dynamics among marbles; on the other side sit the representationalists, who argue that symbolic structures acquire causal autonomy once implemented, and that describing the system without reference to symbol-level constraints misses real patterns of influence. The frustrating feature of our stall is that both sides accept essentially the same empirical picture—that brains realize computation, that neural dynamics are modulated by learning, and that high-level cognitive states correlate with large-scale network patterns—yet neither has conceded a single inch in eight weeks.

I have begun to wonder whether we are not arguing about neuroscience at all, but about the metaphysics of causation at different scales, or perhaps about whether “pushing” requires energy transfer in a way that symbolic relations necessarily fail to satisfy. To sharpen this, I have been experimenting with a distinction that does not seem to appear in the standard literature: what I call representational viscosity, the idea that symbols do not exert point-force downward causation on individual marbles but instead alter the dynamical landscape through which substrate processes flow, functioning more like a viscous medium guiding trajectories than like one billiard ball striking another. If that distinction holds, then both sides might be correct about what they observe but wrong about the framing—the reductionist correctly denies that symmballs strike marbles, while the representationalist correctly detects a guiding influence, but both mistake the modality of that influence. Given this diagnosis, which I offer only as a working hypothesis, I want to ask what kind of disagreement this actually is.

Is it a substantive dispute about the ontology of levels and the conditions for causal efficacy, a verbal dispute about whether “cause” and “push” must be interpreted mechanistically, or a case in which the two camps are answering different questions—one about explanatory practice and the other about metaphysical constitution? Furthermore, if representational viscosity is a coherent intermediate category, could it break the impasse by giving each side something to concede without surrendering their core commitment, or would it merely relocate the same intuitions into a new vocabulary? And finally, what would genuinely count as progress here: an empirical result from neuroscience, a formal model showing how symbolic constraints alter attractor landscapes, a conceptual clarification of downward causation that rules out one reading, or should we conclude that the careenium question is ultimately an expression of bedrock intuitions about emergence that no argument can resolve?
