I have been trying to extend Pascal’s reflections on justice and force—roughly, that justice lacks any transcendent or natural foundation and is therefore conventional and variable; that without force it is powerless; and that people obey power because they take it to be just, a mechanism of belief-formation sustained by power itself—into a diagnosis of democratic legitimacy. In doing so I have constructed an argument that seems to erase the distinction between a legitimate state and a durable tyranny, and I cannot locate the precise misstep.

(1) Justice is conventional: there is no universal, non-institutional standard that makes a law just independently of the customs and enforcement practices that happen to prevail in a given place and time.

(2) Justice without force is impotent, while force without justice is tyrannical; therefore the stable order is one in which force and justice coincide, so that subjects obey because they mistake effective power for rightful authority.

(3) Add a further sociological claim: in any regime that persists across generations, the population’s belief that its laws are just is causally sustained by the regime’s enforcement capacity and institutional continuity, not by any independent moral reasoning that the subjects could exercise outside the framework the regime provides.

(4) If there is no external standard of justice (from 1), then the only conventions available to serve as normative standards are precisely those that the regime succeeds in enforcing and maintaining over time.

(5) So in a long-lasting tyranny, the enforced conventions are the only conventions in play; the subjects’ beliefs track those conventions correctly, because there is no other standard against which to measure them and find them wanting.

(6) Conversely, a genuinely equitable but fragile order—one that fails to generate stable enforcement or sustained belief—will lack the corresponding conventions and will therefore count as unjust, or at least as not-just, relative to the only available measure.

(7) Hence durability—successful domination sustained across generations—is sufficient for justice on this framework, and fragility—moral aspiration without cohesive power—is sufficient for injustice.

This proves too much. I want to preserve Pascal’s core insight that justice needs force and that people confuse power with right, but I refuse to accept that every enduring tyranny is thereby just. Which step should be rejected, and what does the rejection cost? Does abandoning (3) require an implausible picture of moral belief as causally insulated from power? Does abandoning (1) mean giving up conventionalism for a natural-law view that Pascal explicitly rejects? Or is there a hidden slide—perhaps an equivocation between “the conventions that exist” and “the conventions that are authoritative”—between (4) and (5) that makes the collapse only apparent?
