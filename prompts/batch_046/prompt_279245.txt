As a journalist who covers philosophy and the sciences, I am preparing a forty-five-minute public lecture for an audience of educated laypeople—roughly thirty people with no prior background in moral philosophy—on P.F. Strawson's distinction between reactive attitudes and the objective attitude, and on how this distinction recasts the problem of moral responsibility. Rather than explaining the view to me, I need you to construct the pedagogical architecture: begin by laying out the precise sequence in which the conceptual pieces must appear, from the lived experience of resentment and gratitude through the suspension of those attitudes in the objective stance, and on to the conclusion that responsibility is governed by the appropriateness of reactive attitudes rather than by metaphysical freedom, noting at each stage what conceptually depends on the prior one and why reversing the order would collapse the argument. Next, identify the single carrying example that should anchor the entire lecture; I have been considering a comparison between our reactive posture toward a negligent human caregiver and our objective posture toward a malfunctioning predictive algorithm that causes comparable harm, and I want you to evaluate whether this pairing can bear the weight or whether it introduces confusions—specifically, tell me what features the example must possess and what features it must lack in order to illuminate the boundary between exemption conditions and mere excuses without invoking technical vocabulary. Then map the predictable misconceptions: I anticipate that listeners will reduce reactive attitudes to transient emotional states, will mistake the objective attitude for a form of moral indifference rather than a strategic suspension of interpersonal engagement, and will continue to assume that if determinism were true then all resentment would be irrational; for each of these, specify the exact point in the sequence where it should be intercepted and neutralized. Provide also one final check for understanding—a single question that reveals whether the audience has grasped that the issue is not whether the agent could have done otherwise in some metaphysical sense, but whether our reactive attitudes remain appropriate given what we know about them—without asking listeners to cite any particular philosopher or text. Finally, state explicitly what must be left out at this level, such as the full taxonomy of vicarious and self-reactive attitudes or the detailed exchange with libertarian metaphysics, and acknowledge that omission plainly. Write all of this in continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, bold type, tables, or any other visual formatting, in the manner of a formal letter or email.
