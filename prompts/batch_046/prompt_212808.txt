After a long argument last night with a close friend about family policy, gender justice, and what the state owes parents, I have tried to formalize exactly where I stand so that I can see whether my commitments actually cohere. Here is my position, as precisely as I can state it.

(1) Parental leave should be structured through individual, non-transferable entitlements for each parent—what policy analysts sometimes call a use-it-or-lose-it design—because transferable leave tends to be absorbed by mothers and reinforces a specialization equilibrium in which small initial career interruptions compound into large long-run wage and human-capital gaps.

(2) Justice within the family requires recognizing an asymmetrical biological burden: gestation, childbirth recovery, and lactation generate needs that no gender-neutral framework can fully equalize, so treating parents identically in every dimension is not treating them equally.

(3) The dual-earner household is both an economic necessity and a moral ideal; I reject any cultural or policy structure that pushes families toward a single-earner model, and I hold that neither parent should bear the primary career cost of childbearing by default.

(4) I accept what child-development researchers call a critical-window thesis: consistent, substantial caregiver presence during roughly the first twelve to eighteen months produces durable attachment and cognitive benefits that high-quality substitute childcare can mitigate but not fully replicate.

(5) The liberal state should remain neutral among reasonable conceptions of the good life, including neutrality between parents who prefer an intensive early-caregiving model and those who prefer rapid return to paid work, provided only that children’s basic developmental interests are protected.

My question is whether this package hangs together. Specifically, is there a genuine tension between (1) and (2) that forces me to choose between formal equality of entitlement and substantive equality of burden? Does (3) conflict with (4) in a way that renders the dual-earner ideal unsustainable without sacrificing child welfare, or is the conflict only apparent? Is (5) genuinely incompatible with (4), or does the tension dissolve once we distinguish state neutrality about parental lifestyle choices from the state’s duty to protect objective child-development interests? And is there a hidden tension—perhaps between my endorsement of the specialization-equilibrium argument and my commitment to state neutrality—since disrupting that equilibrium seems to require the state to take sides on family structure?

Please audit these commitments. For each pair that is actually in tension, state how severe the conflict is, identify the cheapest single revision that would restore consistency, and say exactly what that revision costs me in terms of the other commitments I value. For pairs that only appear to conflict, explain precisely why the tension dissolves on inspection. Rank all genuine tensions by severity rather than treating them as equally damaging. Finally, tell me which single revision—modifying or abandoning one commitment—would clean up the most problems at once, and whether, all things considered, that revision is worth the price.
