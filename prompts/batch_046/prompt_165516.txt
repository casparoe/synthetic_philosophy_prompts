I'm drafting a scene where my protaganist, a historian of science, gets drunk at a writing retreat and starts arguing with a novelist friend about whether the 'Scientific Revolution' is a real thing or just a 19th-century branding exercise. The novelist says it's obviously an invention — you can see the seams when you compare Galileo's actual notebooks with the heroic narrative that got packaged in 1830s textbooks. The historian fires back that even if the narrative is packaged, there's a genuine discontinuaty in how errors were treated: pre-1600 corrections were mostly theological emendatons, post-1600 they start being empirical falsifications, and that's not just storytelling. Then the novelist throws in this concept she calls 'chronological othering' — she means societies invent a primitive past to make their present feel inevitable. The historian says that's just Foucauldian wordplay with no predictive power. Neither of them will back down. Can you adjudicate who is right on the specific points? Is there actually a cluster there, or is it packaging? And is 'chronological othering' doing real work here, or is it just a fancy way to say people like stories?
