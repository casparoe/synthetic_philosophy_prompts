I want a rigorous audit of how far this analogy actually carries the explanatory load. Second, identify the disanalogies that matter for the intended argument: the policemen acted in peer groups rather than alone, often under alcohol and ritualized camaraderie, with prior ideological socialization in Nazi Germany, and with orders that were ambiguous rather than explicit scientific directives. Third, ask whether the Milgram framing quietly imports a universalist moral psychology that Goldhagen's thesis of specifically German eliminationist antisemitism would reject—in particular, whether the "agentic state" can explain why the violence took the form of racial murder rather than generic obedience. Fourth, assess whether the analogy genuinely supports Browning's conclusion that "ordinary men" become killers through situational pressure alone, or whether Goldhagen can co-opt the same structural features (group dynamics, incremental involvement) to argue that only a culture already saturated with hatred could direct the "shift" toward this specific target. Say what would change your verdict—for example, if archival evidence showed that prior exposure to antisemitic propaganda was the strongest predictor of volunteering for shootings, or if Milgram's own data revealed that ideological conviction rather than situational authority was the dominant predictor of full obedience. Keep your answer under 350 words, and flag the point in your reasoning that is most likely to fail.
