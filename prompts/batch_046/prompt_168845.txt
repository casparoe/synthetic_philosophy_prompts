Write a dialogue between a material-constitution theorist—who holds that a statue is ontologically distinct from, yet dependent on, the clay or matter composing it—and a four-dimensionalist mereologist, who treats both as differently bounded spacetime worms with no primitive constitution relation. Do not rehearse the standard Lump-of-Clay example. Instead, have them debate a sculpture fabricated from biodegradable polymer that is replaced cell-by-cell with living fungal tissue over fifty years, leaving none of the original substrate intact while preserving shape, artistic intent, and causal history. The constitution theorist should defend the statue’s survival by arguing that constitution is not transitive through substrate replacement when functional-causal continuity and authorial intent provide the anchoring conditions; the four-dimensionalist should counter that this merely describes one discontinuous worm with changing material parts, and that invoking an extra “constitution” layer violates parsimony without adding predictive or explanatory power. Require each speaker to address the other’s specific points—especially whether artistic intent can fix persistence conditions independently of material overlap, and whether blocking transitivity of constitution is principled or ad hoc—so the exchange is a genuine philosophical contest rather than parallel monologues. Keep both voices precise, charitable, and equally sharp; neither should be a strawman.
