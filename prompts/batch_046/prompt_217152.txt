Write a formal dialogue between a frequentist philosopher of statistics and a propensity theorist, both equally well-versed, debating whether the presumption of statistical independence in risk modeling can be justified without covert reliance on subjective priors. Center the exchange on a concrete single-case scenario: estimating the first-year failure risk of a novel suspension-bridge design that has no direct historical analog. The frequentist should defend reference-class reasoning and long-run frequency data, arguing that any assignment of a single-case probability requires an implicit choice of reference class that is itself epistemically grounded in observed frequencies. The propensity theorist should counter that the bridge possesses an objective, physically instantiated disposition to fail or survive that is not reducible to class membership, and that treating independence as merely a modeling convenience ignores the causal structure generating the outcome. Require real back-and-forth: the frequentist must press the propensity view on how dispositions are calibrated without circular appeal to frequencies, while the propensity theorist must press the frequentist on why reference-class selection is not an irreducibly subjective judgment. Do not let either speaker become a strawman; each should acknowledge the force of the opposing point before answering it. Conclude with both identifying the precise locus of their disagreement—whether it is metaphysical (the existence of single-case dispositions), epistemic (the legitimacy of subjective prior assignment), or methodological (the role of independence assumptions in prediction)—without resolving the dispute.
