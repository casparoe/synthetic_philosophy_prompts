I have been trying to extend Jain anekāntavāda into the philosophy of mathematics and logic, and the result seems to collapse into self-defeat. Here is the chain.

(1) Classical anekāntavāda holds that every substance possesses infinite, irreducible properties (ananta-dharma) and that any single judgment captures only one of these from one epistemic standpoint (naya).

(2) The doctrine of syādvāda formalizes this: for any property P we predicate it with a conditional qualifier, allowing that not-P also holds in another respect, without either judgment being false.

(3) I propose extending this not merely to empirical properties but to formal and logical relations. The relation "is smaller than" between a set and its proper subset is not an absolute feature but a mode manifesting only under a cardinality-standpoint I will call saṅkhyā-naya, whereas a structural-mapping-standpoint (saṃbandha-naya) reveals a bijection, so that "the subset is smaller" and "the subset is not smaller" are both true syādvāda expressions of different modes.

(4) If logical relations are standpoint-dependent in this way, then for any deductive argument there exists a legitimate standpoint under which one of its premises fails or its conclusion is negated.

(5) This means deductive reasoning has no universal normative force: an argument is valid only relative to a standpoint that privileges its premises, but an equally legitimate standpoint can always be adopted to block the inference.

(6) But the defense of anekāntavāda itself relies on deductive reasoning—inferring from the ontological complexity of reality to the inadequacy of single-perspective claims.

(7) Therefore, if (3) is correct, anekāntavāda undermines the very reasoning that establishes it, which is absurd.

(8) So (3) must be rejected: logical and mathematical relations are not among the standpoint-variable properties.

Yet rejecting (3) looks ad hoc. If empirical properties like color or permanence are genuinely multi-aspect, what principle immunizes formal relations? And does invoking that principle violate the spirit of anekānta?

Where exactly does the slide occur—between (2) and (3) by illicitly extending syādvāda to formal domains, between (4) and (5) by conflating "true in a respect" with "rationally available for rejection," or elsewhere? If I must choose, I would rather preserve (1) and (2) intact; what is the least costly place to stop the argument, and what do the standard Jain distinctions between dravyārthika and paryāyārthika naya have to pay if they are used to draw the line?
