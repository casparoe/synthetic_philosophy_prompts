I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I am preparing an essay for a technical forum in which I examine causation by omission through the lens of distributed systems design. Rather than a finished draft, I have assembled the following notes and arguments; I would appreciate your comments on their philosophical coherence, on whether they illuminate the underlying issue rather than relying on superficial analogy, and on any missteps or missed distinctions.

My central observation is that in distributed computing, an omission—specifically, a missing heartbeat message from a node—is routinely treated as causally efficacious: the absence triggers a failure detector, which in turn initiates leader election. I want to use this to frame the philosophical question of whether omissions can be genuine causes of human or natural events, as when someone fails to call emergency services and harm results.

Within the engineering analogy, I distinguish two modes of tracking absence. In positive-tracking architectures, every expected message is explicitly logged, and the failure to log produces a derived event record that carries its own causal weight. In negative-tracking architectures, the log contains only present messages; the omission is inferred from silence, with no independent entry. I suggest that philosophical debates about omission mirror this design choice. Some accounts appear to treat omissions as real negative events with ontological standing—analogous to positive tracking—while others treat them as mere absences, leaving all causal work to be done by counterfactual expectations about what should have occurred—analogous to negative tracking.

From this I propose a hybrid position I have not encountered in standard discussions, which I call protocol-relative omission. On this view, an omission possesses causal status only relative to a governing protocol—whether a technical specification or a social or moral norm—that defines an expected action or signal. Without such a protocol specifying, for example, that a heartbeat should arrive every 100 milliseconds, the observed silence is merely noise, not a cause of failover. By extension, without a normative framework specifying that a particular intervention was expected, a human omission is not a metaphysically primitive cause but a bare absence. The causal efficacy is conferred derivatively by the rules of the system in question.

I also consider an objection: if omissions are protocol-relative, then altering the protocol seems to alter the causal facts themselves, which sounds implausible. I reply that this is consistent with engineering practice—disabling the heartbeat check means the same physical silence no longer causes leader election—without requiring any change in the physical world, only in the causal-explanatory framework we apply.

Please comment specifically on the following points:

1. Does my distinction between positive and negative tracking conflate epistemological concerns about how we detect omissions with metaphysical concerns about whether omissions exist or cause? If so, how should the distinction be redrawn?

2. Is protocol-relative omission a genuinely novel and defensible position, or does it collapse into existing counterfactual or normative theories of causation that I have not named? If it collapses, into which?

3. Does the distributed-systems analogy genuinely illuminate philosophical causation by omission, or is it merely a decorative metaphor that obscures more than it reveals?

4. Given that I lack formal philosophical background, are there standard objections, distinctions, or alternative frameworks—particularly regarding the relationship between norms and causation—that I should address before developing this further?

I am not seeking bibliographies or reading lists; I want an accuracy audit and conceptual guidance I can act on without further research.
