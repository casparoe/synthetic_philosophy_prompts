I'm working on a piece about why economists still fight over Aumann's agreement theorem, and I overheard two people go at it in a conference bar. Maya, who does decision theory, said the common prior assumption is the whole ballgame: if rational agents share a prior and have common knowledge of their posteriors, they can't agree to disagree, so any real stalemate — say over climate risk — must come from hidden information or genuinely different priors. Leo, a philosopher of science, said that's backwards: in practice people often disagree not about probabilities inside one shared model, but about the model itself — different sample spaces, different causal structures — so they don't even have a common prior to begin with, it's not just hidden. Maya replied you can always embed both models in a larger shared space, pushing the prior up a level, so the assumption is never actually violated. Leo then brought up that continuous-state counterexample where even with a shared prior and common knowledge of beliefs, agreement fails because of infinite belief hierarchies, which means the theorem's conditions are more brittle than she admits. Maya called that a niche technicality that doesn't apply to discrete, computable reasoning. Leo said human reasoning is neither discrete nor computably bounded in the way the theorem requires, so dismissing the exception misses the point. They asked me to referee but I'm just the journalist with a notebook. Can you go through their exchange move by move — rate each claim, say who's right and where — and take a definately stand on whether the common prior assumption is a useful idealization or basically a dead end for explaining real disagreement? Don't just lay out the options; actually adjudicate, and say what both sides are missing.
