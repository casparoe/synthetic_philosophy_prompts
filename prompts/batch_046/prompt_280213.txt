I've been trying to take Hegel's defense of war seriously, not as some 19th-century quirk but as a claim about what keeps political life from collapsing into pure administration. The view I keep come back to is something like: a state that never risks its exsistance in conflict gradually loses the capacity for genuine political decision, because citizens stop seeing themselves as bound to a collective that could ask for sacrifice. War, on this reading, isn't good because people die—it's good because it forces the state to remain an ethical idea rather than a bureaucratic machine. I even think modern precision warfare and sanctions regimes kind of prove Hegel's point by accident: when states can project force without risking citizens, civic virtue atrophies faster, not slower.

But I'm not sure if I've just insulated this view from criticism by making it about 'existential risk' rather than actual shooting wars. So what would actually count against it? I want the arguments or historical cases that should move me—maybe examples where prolonged peace led to civic decay, or where war destroyed rather than preserved ethical life, or formal problems with treating risk as ethically necessary. I'd also like to know if the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability: if every modern conflict either 'counts' or 'doesn't count' depending on how I define risk, is that a problem? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and say which ones defenders of this kind of Hegelian position would concede versus dismiss.
