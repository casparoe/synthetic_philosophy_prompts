Since retiring from a long career in microbial ecology, I have found myself increasingly drawn to the philosophy of history, particularly to the question of whether non-human entities can be said to possess a form of causal efficacy that exceeds mere background conditioning without amounting to full-blown agency. Next month I am to present at an interdisciplinary seminar—attended by social historians wary of determinism, philosophers of science preoccupied with causal ontology, and a historian of medicine specialising in the epidemiology of the fourteenth century. The thesis I shall defend is that we ought to abandon the dichotomy between human agency and passive environmental setting in favour of what I term 'asymmetric causal contribution.' On this view, phenomena such as the bacterium responsible for the Black Death, the climatic disruptions following the 1815 eruption of Mount Tambora, and prolonged aridification cycles are best understood not as agents with intentions, but as 'historical vectors': their dynamics structurally constrain and enable human institutions in ways irreducible to the intentions of historical actors. I argue that this framework explains, for instance, why the plague's demographic impact produced divergent feudal transformations across Eurasia without reducing those outcomes either to pure human contingency or to climatic determinism.

Please prepare me for the objections I am likely to encounter, arranged in the order they will probably be raised, and phrased precisely as members of that audience would put them. I should like specific guidance on what to concede and what to hold against: the social historian who suspects that 'historical vector' is merely environmental determinism dressed in philosophical neologism; the philosopher of science who asks whether attributing 'asymmetric contribution' to a volcano or a microbe involves a category error in our ontology of causation; and the epidemiological specialist who may challenge my empirical premise about differential plague transmission routes in Western Europe versus Central Asia. For each, indicate the weakest point in my reply and the reply I should nevertheless maintain. I am particularly anxious about the objection I hope nobody raises—namely, that my framework adds no explanatory power beyond a standard multi-causal narrative and therefore amounts to verbal decoration. Please present your answer as a concise prep sheet rather than a full defence, and kindly write in British English.
