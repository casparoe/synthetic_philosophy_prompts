I am a graduate student in a philosophy of physics seminar, and next week I will defend a short paper that reinterprets Black's symmetric-universe scenario. Rather than reading the two spheres as qualitatively identical yet numerically distinct objects that violate Leibniz's principle of the identity of indiscernibles, I argue that they are not objects at all prior to a local symmetry-breaking perturbation—say, a minute internal structural irregularity that establishes a relational frame. Once that frame is present, one region becomes countable as a single node and the other as a second, but prior to it there are only redundant coordinate labels within an extended field. From this I draw what I am calling "pre-objectual relationalism": the principle of identity of indiscernibles should be reformulated not as a constraint on object-properties but as a constraint on the conditions under which a relational field yields countable nodes. The audience will be about twelve people: three metaphysicians committed to structural realism who reject bare particulars; two historians of philosophy who specialize in early modern metaphysics and are quick to spot anachronism; four physics graduate students who will tolerate metaphysics only if it connects to gauge symmetry or quantum entanglement; and three philosophers of science who regard empty-space thought experiments as empirically idle. I need a Q&A prep sheet ordered by how likely and damaging each objection is. Please also include the objection I am most hoping nobody asks, with an explanation of why it is especially dangerous and how I might survive it if it is raised.
