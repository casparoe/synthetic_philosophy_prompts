I'm organizing an interdisciplinary workshop titled Obligations to Those Not Yet Named, held in memory of a mentor who spent decades on decision theory and population ethics. The room will be split: philosophers and economists working on longtermism on one side, and on the other the mentor's adult children, partner, and non-academic friends who are here because they loved him, not because they read the literature. I need opening remarks—about six to seven minutes, spoken, warm but precise—that make the nonidentity problem do real structural work rather than serving as decoration.

Approach this as both a professional philosopher and an experienced speechwriter. Before drafting, think step by step. First, identify exactly what the speaker is permitted and forbidden to say once the logic of the problem is taken seriously. Second, brainstorm three concrete analogies—deliberately avoiding the standard climate-policy or reproductive-choice examples—that could make the framing visceral for intelligent non-specialists without losing the specialists. Third, outline the emotional arc, remembering that the room is in mourning and that sentimental comfort must not violate the philosophy.

The core idea to embed is the standard nonidentity problem: actions that determine which specific people come to exist seem to escape harm-based evaluation, because there is no stable subject who is made worse off. I want you to extend this into what I will call the archival gap—the idea that a non-existent person's absence leaves no entry in any metaphysical or institutional record, so there is literally nowhere for a grievance to be filed. This should shape the architecture of the speech: begin with the intuitive comfort of grievance-based morality (we know how to apologize to a specific person we have wronged), then reveal that under different choices the future people are different people with no archive of absence, and conclude that obligations to them must be reconstructed without the usual moral bookkeeping. Crucially, the speaker must not retreat into platitudes like "he lives on in the generations he shaped," because the nonidentity logic undermines that kind of identity continuity across worlds.

Constraints:

- Do not quote any actual text verbatim; paraphrase without quotation marks.

- End with a brief structural note: what feature of the nonidentity problem does the speech successfully reproduce, and what feature—such as precise numerical comparison of lives across possible worlds—does it necessarily flatten for rhetorical purposes?

Write the remarks in a voice appropriate for a memorial workshop opening: formal but not stiff, precise but not cold. Include a brief summary of your brainstorming, then the speech itself, then the structural note.
