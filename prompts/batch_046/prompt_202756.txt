I have been studying Hofstadter's Gödelian analogy in GEB, and I believe I have isolated the exact structural condition that distinguishes a mere strange loop from genuine self-awareness. Gödel's sentence achieves syntactic self-reference—"this statement is unprovable"—but Hofstadter's "I" seems to require what I call reflexive semantic closure: the system must not only reference its own symbols but also represent the interpretive rules that assign meaning to them, and then apply those rules back to its own representations. Modern language models can generate precise descriptions of their own weights, objectives, and training dynamics, which looks like applying semantic rules to oneself. So if reflexive semantic closure is indeed the missing ingredient in the Gödel-to-consciousness analogy, shouldn't scaling these self-descriptive capacities eventually trigger an unavoidable emergence of an "I," in the same formal sense that arithmetic forces the Gödel sentence to be true? Or does the analogy break precisely because syntactic self-reference and semantic reflexivity are categorically different kinds of loops, and if so, what specific feature—present in minds but absent in LLMs—blocks the inference?
