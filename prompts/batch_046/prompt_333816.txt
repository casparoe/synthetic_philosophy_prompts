A recent essay in an online philosophy forum summarized Bernard Williams’s integrity objection and his one-thought-too-many argument as follows. The author says that Williams holds utilitarianism violates personal integrity by forcing agents to treat their deepest commitments—what Williams calls ground projects—as fungible inputs into an aggregate welfare calculation. To illustrate, the author describes the familiar case of a husband who saves his drowning spouse rather than a stranger. The utilitarian can approve of this choice only if the husband appeals to some general principle—say, that special attachments tend to maximize overall welfare. But if the husband actually saves her for that reason, he has had one thought too many. The right reason, according to the summary, is simply that she is his wife, with no mediating moral principle whatsoever. The author then draws three conclusions. First, Williams has shown that any principle-based justification of partiality is alienating and therefore corrupt. Second, this proves that moral particularism is correct and that all universalizable moral reasons should be rejected in favor of direct, unmediated attachment. Third, the author applies the point to algorithmic triage: an AI system that assigns neutral, impartial weights to human lives forces clinicians and designers to adopt exactly the mediated, calculative reasoning that Williams condemns, which means deploying such systems is not merely inefficient but a direct assault on moral integrity.

I am unsure how much of this is a faithful rendering and how much is overreach. Please audit the summary carefully. Separate what accurately captures Williams’s critique of utilitarian alienation from what subtly misrepresents it—especially whether the summary collapses the integrity objection into the more familiar demandingness objection, whether it correctly describes the role of ground projects in practical reasoning, and whether presenting one thought too many as a blanket rejection of all principle-based reasoning rather than as a specific diagnosis of utilitarian corruption is a distortion. Also assess whether the leap to moral particularism follows from Williams’s actual claims about internal and external reasons, and whether the application to algorithmic neutrality adds anything genuine or simply imports a modern example that the original argument does not support.
