I'm preparing a graduate seminar on philosophy of action and have been revisiting the absent-minded driver problem—the familiar case in which an agent forms the explicit intention to take a particular highway exit, yet drives past it and continues along the accustomed route without noticing. Rather than asking my students to rehearse the standard Davidsonian or Anscombean treatments, I want them to assess a three-part taxonomy I've developed for parsing the different failure modes that can produce this single outward behavior.

Specifically, I distinguish the following explanatory frameworks: (1) attentional decoupling, where the agent's intention remains occurrent but is temporarily disconnected from perceptual monitoring and from the motor-correction routines that would normally execute it; (2) scripted automatism, where the driving proceeds entirely via habituated motor scripts that have momentarily displaced any concurrent intentional guidance, so that the agent is, in a localized sense, not acting under the description "going to the exit" at all; and (3) counterfactual blindness, where the agent retains full executive capacity and perceptual uptake, but the conditions that would ordinarily trigger self-correction—recognizing the missed landmark—are masked by an absorbing cognitive task, without the driving routine itself being disrupted or replaced by habit.

Could you please rank these three frameworks in order of overall explanatory adequacy for absent-minded driving failures, assigning each an overall score from 1 to 10, and justify the ranking by applying each framework to three concrete variants: (a) a driver who misses the exit because she is deeply absorbed in mentally rehearsing a difficult conversation; (b) a driver who misses the exit because he is on the well-worn commute home despite having formed the explicit prior intention to drive to a new office; and (c) a driver who misses the exit because a sudden, startling noise captures her attention for a brief moment, yet she continues driving smoothly without any felt disruption to her sense of agency. In particular, I want to know whether any single framework can preserve both the intuition that the driver is not fully acting intentionally with respect to the missed exit and the intuition that she remains responsible for having placed herself in a state of diminished attentiveness, or whether we must accept that different variants genuinely call for different explanations and therefore resist unification under one principle.
