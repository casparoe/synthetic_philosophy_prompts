As a novelist accustomed to thinking about fiction in terms of plot architecture, diegetic contingency, character agency, authorial intervention, and the distinction between driving action and reacting to it, I ask you to restate Machiavelli's conception of fortune and virtù entirely within that vocabulary. Treat fortuna as the forces of narrative contingency that exceed any protagonist's intentions—structural events, historical pressures, or plot-level accidents that reshape the arc—while treating virtù as the craft by which a character, or perhaps the narrative design itself, redirects, exploits, or adapts to those forces without ever fully mastering them. Be precise about where Machiavelli's political-historical framing maps onto narrative structure and where it resists.

Then provide the residue report: which elements of the fortune-virtù distinction translate cleanly into narrative terms, which must be distorted to fit—particularly whether the martial, masculine, and state-centered dimensions of virtù can survive translation into purely aesthetic or dramatic competence—and which elements have no counterpart at all in how fiction handles contingency and agency. Specifically, address whether there is something in Machiavelli's view of fortune as an external, almost meteorological historical force that cannot be captured by either plot-level coincidence or structural authorial constraint. I want to determine whether my working intuition—that Machiavelli offers essentially a theory of improvisatory agency under conditions of irreducible narrative contingency—is sound, or whether that reading suppresses something essential about his framework that fiction's vocabulary simply cannot express.
