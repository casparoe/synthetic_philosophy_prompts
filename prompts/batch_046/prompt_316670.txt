The methodological review board of an interdisciplinary social-science funder has ruled on whether explanatory proposals meet its standard of methodological adequacy. Its track record is as follows. It approved a study that explained the collapse of a regional craft tradition solely through macroeconomic restructuring and the withdrawal of state subsidies, with no reference to individual artisans’ decisions. It rejected a study that explained the same collapse solely through individual artisans’ rational cost-benefit calculations, on the grounds that structural conditions were ignored. It approved a study that explained the persistence of a particular professional greeting ritual solely through network-level coordination games and institutional incentives. It rejected a study that explained the rapid spread of a new dietary restriction in a city solely through updates to individual health beliefs, because restaurant infrastructure and social signalling were absent. Finally, it rejected a proposal that explained the collapse of a cooperative housing scheme solely through collective governance structures, insisting that individual members’ defection incentives and strategic reasoning had to be modelled.

The board now faces a proposal that explains the sudden, geographically dispersed convergence of ethical framing language in corporate carbon-offset reports solely through the standardisation of ESG reporting frameworks and automated compliance algorithms, with no reference to individual managers’ interpretive practices, professional socialisation, or internal moral reasoning. What should the board decide if it wants to be consistent with itself? Which of its earlier rulings is the closest analog, and why? If its precedents genuinely underdetermine this case, what principle would it be adopting with each possible choice? Give your best guess, with the reasons for preferring it, and name one further hypothetical case that would best distinguish the competing rules that fit its track record.
