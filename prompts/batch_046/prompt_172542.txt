I'm in my final year of a philosophy of science seminar, and we've just finished a unit on Imre Lakatos's methodology of scientific research programmes. For our closing session, I'm giving a short presentation on whether a programme can remain formally progressive even as its protective belt grows thicker, and I'd like to end with a poem that does philosophical work rather than merely decorate the slide. I was hoping you could write something that treats the framework itself as an object of reflection—something my classmates will recognize immediately, but that doesn't just list the concepts in verse form.

Could you write a villanelle—nineteen lines, five tercets and a closing quatrain, with two refrains in the standard ABA rhyme scheme—spoken in the voice of a research programme defending its own existence? I want one refrain to act like the hard core: it should return with almost no alteration, an axiom that refuses direct revision. The other refrain should correspond to the negative heuristic, shifting slightly with each appearance—adding a defensive clause, qualifying its scope, absorbing an anomaly—so that the reader can watch the protective belt thickening line by line. The volta should occur where the two refrains begin to converge, marking the moment an anomaly threatens to breach the belt and reach the core. By the final couplet, where both refrains appear together, the poem should leave genuinely ambiguous whether their convergence signals a progressive problemshift—the core generating a new prediction—or a degenerating one, with the belt collapsing backward onto the core to save it from falsification. Don't resolve that tension; embody it in the rhyme.

Please keep the imagery away from generic scientific props—no telescopes, no planets, no laboratories—and instead draw on architecture, the logic of rules, or the geometry of belief revision. The language should be precise enough that someone familiar with Lakatos recognizes the distinction between progressive and degenerating shifts, the role of the positive heuristic, and the prohibition against direct assault on the hard core, without turning the poem into a glossary or mnemonic. I'd like it to work as a poem for an audience that knows the theory, but also to stand on its own for anyone listening without having memorized the terminology—something that closes a seminar honestly, in the open-ended place where our discussion actually ended up.
