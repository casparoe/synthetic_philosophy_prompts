You are an expert in archival theory and the philosophy of historical knowledge, with particular attention to how institutional records produce silences and whose voices are preserved. I am writing this request from my university seminar on Archives and Power. Over the semester my professor has ruled on six archival situations. First, she called the plantation ledger that lists enslaved people by first name only—grouped with livestock and without family relations—an active dehumanizing silence that is ethically culpable, not a neutral gap. Second, she treated municipal records destroyed in a 1952 flood as a structural absence: tragic, but not an ethical failure of archival practice. Third, she said that feminist collective minutes surviving only inside a male observer's papers constitute derivative preservation that distorts the collective's autonomy, making the silence ethically problematic. Fourth, she judged a government agency's routine shredding of administrative memos under a legal retention schedule to be legitimate curation with no ethical problem. Fifth, she argued that museum artifacts acquired only through police seizure of a protest, with no accompanying community narrative, reproduce state violence, and that the absence of community voice is an active harm. Sixth, she said that a newspaper digitization project excluding ethnic-language press due to copyright and digitization costs creates an economic exclusion that falsely represents the public sphere, hence an ethically significant silence.

Next week's case is this: our university's oral history archive holds two hundred interviews with faculty and administrators documenting campus life from 1960 to 2000, but contains none with custodial and food-service staff who worked in the same buildings. The archive is now being presented publicly as a record of the university's institutional history. The university is applying for a digitization grant that requires representative institutional coverage.
