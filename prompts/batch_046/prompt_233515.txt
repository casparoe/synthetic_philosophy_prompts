Assume the role of a senior philosophy-of-mind instructor and experiential-curriculum designer. Before you begin, take a moment to think carefully: briefly list three distinct ways that a small group of students could physically or decisionally encounter the tension between substrate-level dynamics and symbolic-level causation without being told in advance which view they are testing.

Then, design a complete, runnable 90-minute seminar activity for 12 graduate-level students. The session revolves around a deliberately niche conceptual framework—do not treat these terms as references to established literature, but as working constructs for the exercise:

- The careenium: a hypothetical causal layer in which symbolic tokens are not merely implemented by substrate dynamics, but are actively "careened"—pushed, redirected, and organized—by those dynamics, such that the substrate's subsequent behavior is partly structured by the syntactic properties of the tokens.
- Simmballs: hypothetical symbol-objects that exist only as stable patterns within substrate dynamics, yet are claimed to exert downward causal influence on those same dynamics.

The guiding question is: do symbols push the substrate around? Specifically, if simmballs are realized in the careenium, can the syntactic form of a simmball be a genuine cause of substrate behavior, or does all causal efficacy remain at the underlying physical level?

Your deliverable must include:
1. A brief brainstorming section (the three approaches you considered).
2. The activity design: setting, learning objective, roles/materials, rules of interaction, a timeline for the 90 minutes, and a payoff or stakes structure that requires no real money but creates genuine decision pressure.
3. The mechanism by which participants encounter the problem themselves: arrange rules or incentives so that the group experiences a coordination failure or causal ambiguity before anyone names it.
4. A debrief that maps specific moments of the activity onto (a) pure substrate causation, (b) careenium-style redirection, and (c) downward causation by simmballs. Identify what will predictably go wrong the first time the activity is run and how to handle it.
5. An honest note on which feature of the metaphysical problem—such as causal exclusion, overdetermination, or the implementation/realization distinction—the activity cannot reproduce, and how the facilitator must supply that missing element verbally.

Write the response in American English. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, citations, or requests for outside reading. The students should derive the tension from their own actions during the session.
