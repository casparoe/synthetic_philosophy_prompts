Consider an electoral system that treats a deliberately blank ballot as a formal category distinct from both abstention and an invalid vote: election officials tally it separately, report it in official results, and sometimes record it in parliamentary returns, yet they exclude it from the denominator used to determine whether a candidate has reached the required majority. At the same time, the legal framework declares the vote inalienable—proxy voting and vote markets are prohibited on the ground that the ballot expresses an irreducibly personal judgment—while political discourse routinely condemns strategic voting for the "lesser evil" as a betrayal of authentic preference, even though the first-past-the-post structure makes such strategy rational. What philosophical theory of voting does this combination of practices presuppose? Does it conceive of voting chiefly as an expressive act whose value is independent of causal efficacy, as a civic duty to select the least objectionable option, or as an instrumental contribution to collective choice? Reconstruct the implicit account that makes it coherent to honor blank ballots with an official tally while denying them any effect on the outcome, and assess whether that account is consistent with both the inalienability rule and the moral pressure to vote strategically. Then ask what would become of these institutions—separate blank-ballot reporting, prohibitions on vote-transfer, and the stigma against tactical voting—if we replaced the underlying view with a straightforwardly probabilistic one, according to which a ballot has value only in proportion to its expected marginal impact on the result.
