I am a software engineer without formal philosophy training, and I am organizing a 75-minute workshop for about fourteen colleagues—mostly engineers and a few product designers—on the conceptual inversion that Darwin and Turing both exploited: that sophisticated competence can arise without any corresponding comprehension in the system producing it. Rather than lecture on evolutionary adaptation or universal computation, I want the group to feel the inversion bodily and procedurally before anyone names it. I have in mind a distributed simulation in which local agents execute purely syntactic rules with no semantic access to a global task, yet the collective output achieves something that looks strategically intelligent—mirroring how natural selection or a Turing machine can generate fit or correct behavior without representing fitness or meaning. Please design a self-contained, in-room activity using only paper, pens, envelopes, and a point-based payoff scheme that requires no real money.

The simulation should split the fourteen participants into three functional layers: four to six Generators who apply blind mechanical transformation rules to input cards; four Selectors who see only the outputs and vote to retain or discard them based on a hidden target pattern they do not fully understand; and two to three Observers who record the sequence but are forbidden from explaining mechanisms to the others. The hidden target should be a structural or logical pattern that feels like it demands insight—perhaps generating a valid syllogistic form or matching a nested symmetry—yet is reachable through iterative, rule-bound mutation. Structure the rules and incentives so that by round four or five the collective produces a highly competent result, while any individual Generator or Selector remains unable to articulate why the rules work. The payoff should reward collective accuracy, not individual understanding, and you should build in predictable failure modes: Selectors will likely try to reverse-engineer the hidden target and cheat, while Generators may attempt to interpret the rules semantically rather than mechanically. Tell me exactly how to run each round, what materials to prepare in advance, and how to intercept these failures without breaking the illusion.

Finally, provide a debrief that maps specific moments in the simulation onto the Darwinian and Turing-style inversions—where comprehension was absent but competence accumulated, and where the system's success outstripped any agent's knowledge. Be frank about what this classroom format cannot reproduce—probably the temporal scale of evolutionary selection or the formal universality of computation—and indicate which part of the philosophical idea I will need to supply verbally after the activity. Do not include reading lists, citations, or bibliographic references; the workshop must be fully runnable without anyone consulting outside sources.
