I am a novelist, and next Thursday I am speaking at an interdisciplinary workshop—“Agency, Time, and Law”—hosted by a law school and philosophy department. I will defend the claim that constitutional precommitment, especially entrenched rights provisions that bind future majorities, is not a collective Ulysses contract but a category mistake: it treats successive democratic electorates as temporal stages of a single continuing agent, yet demographic turnover, immigration, and value pluralism break the diachronic identity conditions that make individual self-binding rational rather than paternalistic. I do not invoke the analogy to save it; I invoke it to show where it collapses, arguing that what looks like democratic self-binding is in fact present citizens binding future strangers, and that this is defensible only if one abandons the autonomy rationale that makes personal Ulysses contracts attractive in the first place.

The audience is specific and skeptical in different directions: three constitutional lawyers who defend entrenched bills of rights as necessary precommitments against populist passion; two political theorists who have written on intergenerational justice and democratic agency; one philosopher of personal identity who denies that groups have diachronic identity conditions at all; and a cluster of graduate students whose clarifying questions tend to expose weak joints. They care about institutional legitimacy and conceptual coherence, not literary framing, though they are aware I am not a professional jurist.

Prepare me for the objections I will actually face, in the rough order they are likely to come. For each objection: phrase it as that particular audience member would phrase it; indicate whether it targets my analogy, my identity conditions, or my normative conclusion; tell me what I should concede up front, what I must hold without retreat, and where my reply is softest. Identify the weakest point in my own position—the one I should prepare to defend actively rather than hope they overlook. Include the single objection I am praying nobody raises, with a brief reply that must land if my argument is to survive the room.

If this request rests on a confused premise—if, for example, collective agency is available in precisely the form I deny, or if constitutional precommitment does not require the diachronic identity conditions I claim are missing—say so plainly instead of complying politely. I invite direct pushback, not diplomatic agreement.
