Question: Max Black's classic two-sphere universe contains only two spheres that are qualitatively identical in every intrinsic respect—same radius, mass, temperature, and material composition—separated in continuous space. Consider a modified version in which space is a discrete lattice with no continuous metric; the spheres occupy nodes separated by exactly seven lattice links, share every local non-relational property, and there are no other objects. Does this lattice scenario still furnish a counterexample to Leibniz's principle of the identity of indiscernibles? In your answer, distinguish intrinsic from relational properties in this setting, explain whether lattice separation should be treated as intrinsic or relational, and discuss whether allowing relational properties saves the principle only at the cost of rendering it trivial.

Student answer:
Leibniz's principle of the identity of indiscernibles is a biconditional: if two objects are identical then they share all properties, and if they share all properties then they are identical. In the lattice case, sphere A and sphere B have the same radius, mass, and internal structure. The only difference is positional: A occupies node (0,0,0) while B occupies node (7,0,0), so they are separated by seven links. Because the lattice nodes are absolutely individuated, this separation is an intrinsic property of each sphere—A intrinsically has the property of being at node (0,0,0), which B lacks. Consequently, the spheres are not truly indiscernible; they differ in an intrinsic positional feature, and Black's counterexample collapses in the discrete model.

The standard reply is that separation is relational, not intrinsic: A stands in the relation 'seven links away from B,' and B stands in the corresponding inverse relation. If we count such relations as properties, then A and B differ—each bears a different relational property—so Leibniz's principle is satisfied. However, this rescue makes the principle analytically true: once every symmetric relation can be converted into a distinct monadic property, no two distinct objects could ever share all properties by definition. Therefore, the philosophically substantial version of the principle—the version Black actually attacked—is the one restricted to intrinsic, non-relational properties, and that version is false.
