As part of a feature I am writing on embodied pedagogy in moral philosophy, I need a rigorous classroom simulation for an advanced seminar. The setting is a seventy-five-minute graduate workshop with sixteen students, all familiar with basic welfare economics but not with the specific debate I have in mind; I want them to physically encounter the tension between giving greater weight to benefits for the worst off and the apparently absurd option of destroying surplus to achieve equality, without anyone naming prioritarianism or the leveling-down objection until the debrief. To keep the design out of well-trodden territory, build it around an invented mechanism—the Asymmetric Threshold Protocol—in which each participant draws a hidden baseline-welfare card, receives colored tokens representing utility units, and must vote collectively on redistribution schemes that include both a Pareto improvement leaving the worst-off exactly at their hidden threshold and a destructive “leveling” move that confiscates tokens from the best-off without transferring them downward.

The activity should assign clear roles, use only symbolic stakes, proceed through a timed sequence of hidden revelation and collective choice, and conclude with a debrief that maps each decision point onto either prioritarian weighting or the leveling-down objection, together with a frank note on what predictably goes wrong the first time and which formal distinction—most likely the ex ante versus ex post evaluation of welfare states—cannot be reproduced in the room. Present your design as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion, addressing in order the setup and hidden cards, the voting rules and token mechanics, the debrief mapping, the predictable first-run failure, and the feature that remains irreproducible.
