For my philosophy of biology independent study, I am trying to specify what any adequate theory must deliver if it assigns or denies moral status to insects, especially in light of the scale argument: with perhaps 10^18 or more individual insects alive at any given moment, even a very low per-individual probability of sentience can yield enormous aggregate expected moral weight. Before I compare particular positions, I want the full specification.

Please write the complete list of desiderata—the requirements an adequate theory in this area must satisfy to count as suitable for guiding both ethical reasoning and empirical research priorities. For each requirement, state the phenomenon, case, or distinction it must handle, the consideration behind it, and whether it is non-negotiable or merely the preference of a particular camp. In particular, address the following:

- The distinction between possessing moral status at all and possessing a high or distinctive degree of it, with the cases that motivate keeping them separate.
- Consistency with broad biological findings about nociception, neural integration, and behavioral flexibility in arthropods, without requiring citation of specific studies.
- Whether the theory must itself resolve the scale argument—for instance, by showing that aggregate weight does not scale linearly with population size, or by arguing that the base-rate probability of sentience is effectively zero—or whether it may remain agnostic on aggregation rules.
- How it handles what I will call the replacement asymmetry: in many insect populations, individuals are constantly replaced while the overall population persists, so harming a particular individual may not reduce future welfare if another functionally identical individual takes its ecological place. Does this asymmetry undermine individual-level moral status, and does the answer differ depending on whether the theory is grounded in phenomenal consciousness versus preference-satisfaction?
- Whether the theory must draw a sharp categorical boundary between sentient and non-sentient organisms, or whether it may accept continuous gradation in moral status, together with the costs of each choice—especially the risk of arbitrary line-drawing versus the risk of sliding-scale absurdities.
- Whether it can guide practical decision-making under epistemic opacity, where we must choose courses of action without knowing which specific organisms are sentient.

Then identify any pair or subset of desiderata you suspect are jointly unsatisfiable—for example, if demanding both a non-arbitrary sharp boundary and full consistency with continuous biological gradation in neural architecture produces a contradiction.

Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases rather than on exhaustive evaluation. At the end, in no more than a brief paragraph per view, indicate how a sentience-threshold account, a functionalist capacity-based account, and an ecological or relational account fare against your list. Please do not include bibliographies, reading lists, citations, or requests for further sources.
