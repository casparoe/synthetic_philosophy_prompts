I am not an academic, but I have spent some time thinking about what populist movements actually mean when they invoke "the will of the people," and I have written a set of notes proposing a structure I have not seen clearly named. First, an authentic core is defined not by legal citizenship or voting records but by shared suffering, labor, or cultural belonging, and this core's preferences are treated as the only legitimate source of political authority. Second, opposing preferences are not treated as internal disagreement but as corruption or influence imported from outside the core—so the people, in this framing, never genuinely disagree with themselves, and any dissent is taken as proof of contamination rather than legitimate opposition. Third, and this is the part I find most puzzling, there seems to be a retroactive revision of membership: once a decision is made or an election is won, supporters are retrospectively folded into the authentic core, while resisters are placed outside it regardless of their prior standing. This allows a movement to claim that a lost election was invalid because the wrong people participated, and then, after winning, to claim that those same people were never part of the true people to begin with. I am trying to formalize this as a kind of "moving boundary" problem in democratic representation, but I worry I may be either overanalyzing ordinary political disagreement or missing a simpler explanation. I would like comments on whether this three-part structure is coherent, whether it genuinely distinguishes populism from other forms of democratic politics, and especially whether my third claim—the retroactive reclassification—is defensible or merely an overreading. I am not asking for bibliographies or references to particular authors; I want an honest assessment of the reasoning itself, and whether there is a logical tension between treating the people as sovereign and treating only a subset of them as ever capable of being wrong about what they want.
