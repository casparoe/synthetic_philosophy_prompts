Write an essay on the special composition question as it applies to artifacts whose parts are replaced not sequentially but in a distributed, non-local manner—specifically, a legal statute that is continuously amended by different legislative bodies across jurisdictions, with amendments being adopted, repealed, and merged back into a shared text with no authoritative master copy. Weigh whether mereological universalism, restrictionism, or a moderate view like restricted composition with essential origins best accounts for the identity conditions of such an artifact over time. In particular, consider the objection that without a continuous spatiotemporal boundary or an essential core of parts, restrictionism collapses into an implausible multiplication of transient statutory objects, and consider the universalist reply that we should accept that multiplication and treat statutory identity as a pragmatic projection rather than a metaphysical fact. Reach and justify a specific verdict—either defend universalism with pragmatic reconciliation, defend restrictionism by identifying a hidden unifying relation (such as institutional continuity or functional equivalence), or argue that the case shows both standard views to be inadequate and requires a process-ontology of legal artifacts. Your conclusion should be explicit and argued for, not a balanced summary.
