After a long and somewhat heated argument with a friend about whether large language models can be said to take part in Wittgensteinian language games, I drafted the following notes for a short essay. I would appreciate your comments—particularly on whether the analogy between family resemblance and synthetic discourse holds, whether I have distorted Wittgenstein’s descriptive method, and whether the proposed idea of a resemblance gradient is philosophically coherent or merely evades the hard question of attribution. Please state how confident you are in your main judgments, and identify the specific claim about which you feel least sure.

Notes for an essay tentatively titled Family Resemblance and Synthetic Speech at the Margins:

Wittgenstein rejects the assumption that concepts like game are unified by a single essence; instead they exhibit family resemblance, a network of overlapping similarities without one common thread running through every case. I propose that language game functions in the same way. There is no necessary and sufficient condition—neither conscious intention, emotional engagement, nor embeddedness in a form of life—that definitively marks a practice as linguistic in the relevant sense. Rather, linguistic practices share criss-crossing features that fade gradually at the borders.

My friend argued that this dissolution of essences implies we cannot distinguish human conversation from high-quality synthetic dialogue in any principled way, since any distinguishing feature we might cite can in principle be simulated or approximated. Rather than answering by positing a hidden mental essence, I want to introduce a resemblance gradient: a comparative assessment of how densely a discursive practice is woven into the broader fabric of human forms of life, including labor, ritual, moral accountability, and embodied interaction. Human conversational practices sit near the dense center of this web; synthetic outputs occupy positions farther out, not because they lack an inner mental state, but because their connections to these practices are thinner and largely derivative.

This framing is meant to avoid both essentialism and an indiscriminate behaviorism. The private language argument already undermines the idea that private mental contents could ground linguistic meaning; yet the gradient permits us to say that some practices belong more fully to our language games than others, without requiring a sharp boundary line.

My main concerns are whether this gradient genuinely extends Wittgenstein’s descriptive project or illicitly introduces a normative hierarchy he would reject, and whether comparing synthetic dialogue to borderline human cases—such as mechanical translation or highly rehearsed speech—strengthens or weakens the argument. I am also unsure whether the gradient can do any real philosophical work if every axis of comparison can itself be approximated by a sufficiently advanced system.
