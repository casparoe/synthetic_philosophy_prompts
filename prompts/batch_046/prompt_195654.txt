I'm putting together a four-month fellowship proposal — nothing huge, just enough to cover a summer of thinking, not alot of funding — and I want to pitch something on prioritarianism and the leveling-down objection, but with a framing that I think gets missed in the usual back-and-forth. The standard story is that prioritarianism, which weights benefits to the worse off more heavily, seems to imply we should level down — make the well-off worse — whenever we can't level up. The standard replies either bite the bullet, deny the intuition, or patch the weighting function with sufficientarian thresholds. But here's the twist I want to investigate: is the leveling-down objection actually doing hidden work by assuming a comparative baseline for equality that prioritarianism doesn't accept? Specifically, I want to distinguish three "baseline architectures" — pure comparative, absolute non-comparative, and what I'm calling threshold-relative — and ask whether the objection survives intact across all three, or whether it collapses once equality is treated as an outcome-property rather than a relational one.

The proposal needs to lay out: the precise question (does prioritarianism avoid leveling-down only by rejecting equality's intrinsic value, or can it accommodate equality within each baseline architecture?), why existing answers don't settle it (they conflate the three baselines and treat the objection as monolithic), and why a philosophical rather than empirical project helps (we're doing conceptual taxonomy, not intution-pumping surveys). I also need to specify the deliverble — probably a paper that maps the objection onto the three baselines and proposes a revised distributive formula that is immune to at least the threshold-relative version — and define failure (if the objection turns out to be baseline-independent, then my framing doesn't actually do any work). Scope should be realistic for one person over four months, no lab, no data collection.
