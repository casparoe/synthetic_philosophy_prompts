Two colleagues in my philosophy reading group have been arguing over Benatar’s asymmetry. The first said that the claim that the absence of pain is good even if there is no one to enjoy it, whilst the absence of pleasure is not bad even if there is no one to miss it, collapses as soon as you apply it consistently to potential lives. He argued that if non-existence is genuinely neutral for pleasure, then bringing a being into existence who will experience net pleasure should also be neutral, because there was no prior subject deprived; yet Benatar treats this case differently by denying it is good, which he said smuggles in an unjustified preference for non-creation. His interlocutor replied that he was conflating two directions of evaluation: the absence of suffering is good relative to a counterfactual in which that suffering occurs, whereas the absence of pleasure is not bad relative to a counterfactual in which it occurs, precisely because there is no subject in either counterfactual to bear the deprivation. She insisted that this directional structure survives his objection intact. He then countered that her defence still leaves antinatalism hostage to epistemic risk: if we can never be certain a future person will suffer, and if creating a happy person is at worst neutral, then the asymmetry provides no decisive reason to refrain from procreation unless suffering is guaranteed. She conceded that practical application requires probabilistic judgement, but maintained he had shown only that antinatalism is conditional, not that the underlying asymmetry is incoherent, and accused him of mistaking a metaphysical principle for a decision procedure.

As a bystander, I could not determine where the real fault lines lay. Please adjudicate this exchange point by point. Specifically: is the first speaker correct that Benatar’s treatment of pleasure and pain involves an unjustified asymmetry in the evaluation of potential existence itself, or does the second speaker’s directional defence successfully block this? Does the first speaker’s epistemic-risk argument genuinely undermine the practical force of antinatalism, or does the second speaker’s distinction between metaphysical principle and decision procedure hold up? Finally, identify what, if anything, both parties are neglecting—particularly regarding whether the asymmetry can be sustained without presupposing a particular view about the moral status of merely potential beings.
