You sit on the Institutional Review Board of a research university that has received a proposal titled "Adaptive Consent in Progressive Cognitive Decline." The study recruits patients in the earliest detectable stage of a neurodegenerative condition and implants a monitoring device that tracks decision-making capacity in real time via a proprietary algorithm. The protocol introduces a two-track consent system: Track A applies while the algorithm scores the patient above a threshold on a "volitional coherence index"; Track B is triggered automatically when scores drop below that threshold. In Track B, disclosure is reduced to a simplified summary that omits statistical risks of device malfunction and the full range of secondary data uses, and family co-signatories are required but can be overridden by the algorithm if it detects what the protocol calls "residual autonomous preference."

The investigators argue that standard informed consent fails these patients twice: first by overwhelming them with information they can no longer process, and second by allowing family members—who may have conflicts of interest regarding care burden or estate management—to veto participation that the patient genuinely wants. They claim Track B respects "situated autonomy" rather than abstract rational competence, and that algorithmic override of family objections preserves the patient's core volitional trajectory.

Half the board views this as an acceptable, philosophically informed refinement of consent for fluctuating capacity; the other half views it as institutionalized deception combined with algorithmic coercion, since the patient never explicitly consents to the reduced-disclosure track and the family-override mechanism treats consent as a calculable output rather than a relational, revocable act.

Draft the board's formal decision memo. Recommend one of three outcomes—approval, rejection, or approval with conditions—and specify a concrete implementation time frame (for example, a 90-day pilot with mandatory interim audit, a permanent moratorium pending external review, or unconditional approval with annual re-certification). Your reasoning should directly address the validity conditions for consent—particularly whether a shift in disclosure scope without explicit re-consent, an algorithmic determination of competence, and a structured family-override mechanism can be compatible with valid consent—and should treat seriously the strongest argument on the opposing side. Do not include bibliographies, citations, or reading lists.

End with a one-line verdict in this exact format: Verdict: [your recommendation].
