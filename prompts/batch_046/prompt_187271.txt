Write a dialogue between two equally capable interlocutors—call them Mira and Jonas—who disagree about whether everyday social conventions such as tipping service workers, queuing in orderly lines, and obeying local jaywalking norms constitute genuine moral obligations, and whether short-term visitors are bound by them. Mira defends a position she terms “normative tenancy”: she holds that local conventions generate moral duties only for those who have tacitly entered a shared normative system through sustained participation, socialization, or explicit acceptance, so that a visitor occupies a distinct moral status—more like a guest—who must avoid serious harm and gross disrespect but is not strictly bound by conventions that lack universal moral justification. Jonas argues for “reciprocal coordination universalism”: he maintains that anyone who physically enters and benefits from a functioning social order immediately acquires a minimal but real duty of compliance, because defection undermines the coordination mechanism that protects locals and visitors alike; on his view, “I am not from here” is not a moral excuse, though the stringency of the obligation may scale with the burden of compliance.

The dialogue should feature genuine back-and-forth rather than parallel monologues: each speaker must respond directly to the other’s strongest point. In particular, have Jonas press Mira on whether her “normative tenancy” can genuinely explain why a visitor who deliberately cuts a queue to catch a flight has done something morally wrong rather than merely rude, and whether she can avoid making local customs morally inert for anyone who has not signed a metaphorical lease. Have Mira press Jonas on whether his view implausibly demands that tourists master every local custom upon arrival, and on how he distinguishes a moral failure from a breach of etiquette in cases like tipping in a country where service wages depend on it but the practice is not legally required. They should also examine at least one hard case—perhaps a visitor who refuses to tip where workers rely on it, contrasted with a local who ignores a queue—where both frameworks seem to pull in opposite directions, and neither speaker should be reduced to a strawman. The exchange should run about 800 to 1,000 words, using precise philosophical language but without invoking named citations, bibliographies, or references to specific texts.
