I work as a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy or history, and I am helping draft assessment materials for an upper-level undergraduate seminar on explanation in the human sciences. I need a single, polished final-exam essay prompt—designed for a 90-minute sitting, carrying 40 marks—together with a model answer and a granular marking scheme.

The domain is whether culture can be a cause, centered specifically on Max Weber’s Protestant ethic thesis and the broader problem of explaining social outcomes with values. I want to avoid a regurgitable summary that simply repeats that Calvinist predestination led to capitalist accumulation. The exam should ask students to analyze whether Weber’s thesis is best read as claiming that Calvinist asceticism caused capitalism, that it selected for capitalism among competing forms, or that it did both, and—crucially—whether selection without efficient causation can still count as a genuine causal explanation of the origin of the outcome rather than merely of its persistence.

The model answer should lay out the specific argumentative moves a top response must make: separating genesis from stabilization; addressing whether unconscious value-orientation undermines the claim that culture is causally active; assessing the risk of functionalist circularity if we explain capitalism’s survival by reference to the values that capitalism itself helped reproduce; and clarifying whether treating culture as a relational pattern rather than a substance resolves or deepens the causal puzzle. The marking scheme should specify point allocations—for example, 10 marks for accurate reconstruction of Weber’s elective-affinity claim, 10 for the philosophical distinction between efficient and selective causation, 10 for evaluation of whether selection explains origin, and 10 for coherence and critical engagement—and should name specific errors that should cost marks, such as treating culture as a substance with independent causal powers, conflating Weber with simple economic determinism, or assuming that if values are not consciously held they cannot be explanatory.

Please do not ask students for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists, and do not include any verbatim quotations from Weber’s original texts in the prompt or answer key; paraphrase without quotation marks if needed. The language should be rigorous but should not depend on obscure historiographical debates that a small language model would be unlikely to have properly memorized. Feel free to use analogies drawn from system design or feedback loops to illuminate the philosophical point, since that matches my background, but keep the prompt formally appropriate for an academic exam.
