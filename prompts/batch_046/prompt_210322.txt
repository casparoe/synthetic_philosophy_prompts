Write the desiderata that any adequate theory of moral responsibility must satisfy, with particular attention to the regress pressure articulated in Galen Strawson's Basic Argument. Specify, for each requirement, the phenomenon or conceptual commitment that motivates it: (i) accounting for the difference between responsibility for a particular act and responsibility for the enduring character that disposes one to such acts; (ii) blocking the infinite regress of self-creation without appealing to a causally arbitrary "buck-stopping" point; (iii) preserving the phenomenology of deliberation—specifically, the experience that one's choices are not merely the outputs of an opaque causal chain but are shaped by reasons one endorses; (iv) maintaining coherence with empirical findings on decision-making and the neural antecedents of action; (v) safeguarding the normative practices of blame, praise, and reactive attitude that structure interpersonal life; and (vi) explaining "true desert"—the idea that suffering or reward can be genuinely warranted by moral failure or success, not merely useful as incentive or expression. For each desideratum, indicate whether it is negotiable (a preference of one camp) or non-negotiable (any abandonment dissolves the concept under examination). Then identify which subset of these requirements is jointly unsatisfiable if one accepts Strawson's premise that one cannot be causa sui, and say whether the unsatisfiability is logical or contingent on further empirical or metaphysical assumptions. Briefly score how compatibilism, libertarianism, and Strawsonian skepticism handle the list—naming the specific desideratum each is compelled to weaken. End by defending a verdict: either that one of these positions succeeds in meeting the non-negotiable core, or that the core itself contains an internal contradiction that forces a revision of our pre-theoretical concept of moral responsibility.
