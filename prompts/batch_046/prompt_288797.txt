Take the debate shaped by Avishai Margalit’s defense of a shared ethical duty to remember grave wrongs—where collective memory functions as moral solidarity with victims and a safeguard against repetition—against David Rieff’s argument that strategic forgetting is sometimes not only permissible but necessary for social renewal, and for freeing later generations from an inherited mnemonic burden they did not choose. Within this conceptual space, several lines of inquiry remain unsettled: whether the obligation to remember is primarily owed to the dead, to survivors, or to the polity as a whole; whether forgetting can ever constitute a positive moral achievement rather than a mere failure of duty; how collective remembrance can be reconciled with individual claims to be forgotten; and what institutional architectures—memorials, archives, amnesty provisions, curricular mandates, or digital retention policies—best translate these norms into transitional justice practice.

Rather than surveying all of these, I want a defended prioritization. If you were advising a small interdisciplinary research group—philosophers, legal theorists, and one historian—given three years and modest resources, which two or three open questions in this domain should take precedence? Rank them with explicit criteria: conceptual upstreamness (which resolutions other debates depend upon), tractability for philosophical rather than empirical historical work, and practical payoff for how societies actually design commemorative or amnesty policy. In particular, address whether the deepest unresolved problem here is normative—about the scope, content, and bearers of our mnemonic obligations—or structural: about how plural societies can carry the weight of remembrance across generations without being immobilized by grievance. Where could a focused philosophical argument genuinely shift the terms of institutional debate, rather than merely annotate positions that are already well established?
