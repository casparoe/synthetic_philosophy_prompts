You have been asked to deliver the dedication address for the opening of the Voss Archive of Infelicitous Declarations, a new institution that preserves documented instances in which performative utterances—promises, appointments, baptisms, official proclamations—failed not through accidental mistake but through structural defects in their conditions of felicity. The audience is divided: half are philosophers of language familiar with the distinctions among locutionary, illocutionary, and perlocutionary acts and with the conditions of conventional procedure, correct execution, and sincerity; half are the family members and patrons who funded the archive, including relatives of the co-founder who died six months before the opening and will not be present. Write a speech of approximately ten minutes that honors the founders' vision—they held that a misfired promise reveals more about the institution of promising than a fulfilled one does—without reducing their idea to sentimental platitude. The speech must treat the dedication ceremony itself as a performative act whose own felicity is complicated by the absence of one of its intended performers, and it must explain, in terms accessible to the non-philosophers, why an archive of broken speech acts is not an archive of cynicism. Do not cite page numbers, quote from any philosophical text verbatim, or provide a bibliography or reading list. Let the conceptual framework do structural work: if the distinctions between act-types and felicity conditions were removed, the speech should collapse into generic encouragement about perseverance.
