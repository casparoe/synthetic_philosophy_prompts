okay so im a retired biologist who got into philosphy kind of late, and now i sit on this small ethics panel for our regional science consortium — we deal with misconduct complaints, authorship fights, data issues, that kind of mess. about twice a month we have to decide if someone should be held morally responsible for some screwup or if we should take what Strawson calls the objective attitude — treating them like a broken instrument or a sick system that needs repair rather than blame. but we dont recieve a real procedure, just vibes and gut feelings. i want a reusable decision flow my fellow panelists can actually run, grounded in Strawsons reactive-attitude framework but made usable for non-philosophers, and i want it to handle the difference between personal reactive attitudes (resentment, gratitude) and vicarious or institutional ones since were a panel not one angry person.

heres what i need. first, the flowchart in words: a sequence of questions asked in order, with what counts as sufficient yes/no at each step, explicit stop points where we switch to objective mode or stay in reactive mode, and clear flags for where the call is values not facts so we vote or defer. it should stop with a no when the conditions for reactive membership arent met, and hand off to a person when were just deciding how much we care. then run it on three cases: (1) a senior researcher who clearly fabricated images but claims severe mental-health crisis; (2) a grad student who forgot to cite a source in a draft but that omission made the senior author look original; (3) a case where the flow gives blameworthy but most of us hate that result — say, someone broke a brand-new open-data rule they genuinely didnt know existed, harm was minimal, and everyone agrees it feels wrong to blame them. finally, the honest limits: where this misfires, especially pretending institutional attitudes work like personal ones, and how cultural differences in reactive norms break the flow.

format:
1. The flowchart in words (~350 words)
2. Three worked cases (~500 words)
3. The honest limits (~150 words)

write it practical and casual, include a typo or two, no bibliographies, no "read Strawson 1962" instructions — just build it straight from the concept.
