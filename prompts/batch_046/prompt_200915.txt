Having retired recently from a career in condensed-matter physics and turned to philosophy, I have been studying Amartya Sen's claim that famines are rarely caused by an absolute shortage of food but by collapses in exchange entitlements—the legal and economic means by which people command resources—making them political crimes rather than natural disasters. I wish to treat the 1943 Bengal famine not as a familiar illustration but as a stress test for this framework. The salient facts are well established: rice availability fell sharply in several districts; the British administration's wartime Denial Policy destroyed boats and local rice stocks to impede a possible Japanese invasion; and rural laborers faced a sudden price spike that rendered rice unaffordable even where physical stocks existed elsewhere. Analyze this episode through Sen's entitlement theory with two disciplined constraints. First, map each affected group—rural laborers, urban wage-earners, fishermen whose boats and gear were destroyed, and rice traders—onto Sen's categories of direct and exchange entitlement, specifying precisely for each whether the collapse was in their endowment or in the exchange conditions that converted endowments into food, and identifying which group suffered which mode of failure. Second, examine whether Sen's "political crime" framing necessarily applies when the administrative breakdown resembles a cascading failure in a distributed network—where localized incompetence, broken information channels, and uncoordinated local decisions propagate systemically—rather than a centrally directed policy of starvation. Does Sen's taxonomy have the conceptual resources to distinguish a morally grave systemic coordination failure from an intentional crime, or does it collapse every large-scale entitlement collapse caused by government inaction into the latter? I want to see exactly where the theory illuminates the case, where it remains silent, and whether the distributed-network analogy exposes a gap in Sen's moral classification that a purely entitlement-based analysis misses. I am not seeking a bibliography or citation list; I want a disciplined mapping that lets the case talk back to the theory.
