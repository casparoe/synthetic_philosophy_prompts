For an advanced philosophy-of-biology seminar, construct a decision tree that takes a student from acceptance that viruses lack autonomous metabolism to a named position on life. The branches should ask: (1) whether autonomous energy transduction is necessary for life; (2) whether heritable variation requires chemical replication; and (3) whether evolutionary open-endedness is an essential feature. At the leaves, place the metabolic essentialist, informational functionalist, evolutionary processist, substrate-neutral pluralist, and any unoccupied logical combinations (label these ‘unnamed’). For each named leaf, state in one sentence its most awkward commitment regarding two borderline cases: a synthetic minimal cell with ribosome-free translation and artificial membranes, and a self-modifying algorithm running on distributed hardware. Flag any pair of leaves that the tree cannot genuinely separate, and briefly diagnose unoccupied nodes—are they incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored? The audience knows the standard autopoiesis and replicator debates; the point is to analyse the actual logical space rather than to list familiar labels.
