I am drafting a short essay for a graduate seminar in comparative historical development and would value comments on its coherence and on whether its central framing holds up.

My argument targets what I take to be an underappreciated circularity in the institutions-geography-culture debate over national wealth. The familiar endogeneity worry—that institutional quality is shaped by prosperity rather than shaping it—treats measurement error as correctable with better instruments. I want to push further: the very category of “institutional quality” (rule of law, property-rights enforcement, bureaucratic capacity) is epistemically coupled to the administrative infrastructures that also generate GDP figures, census data, and tax rolls. In polities that governed through ritual authority, seasonal patronage, or negotiated reciprocity rather than through codified rules and written registries, we do not so much encounter weak institutions as institutions of a different kind—modes of authority that leave sparse quantitative traces. Cross-national datasets therefore risk what I call “metric anachronism”: they read the absence of modern, paper-based bureaucratic visibility as institutional deficiency, thereby projecting a specifically modern, quantified form of governance onto societies that never required it.

I extend this point to geographic and cultural variables, noting that climate-suitability indices or value-system measures are frequently reconstructed from the same colonial and post-colonial administrative archives that constructed both the predictors and the economic outcomes being explained. My claim is not that institutions are irrelevant to prosperity, but that our evidentiary architecture is structurally coupled to the phenomenon under investigation in ways that standard instrumental-variable strategies do not resolve.

Could you comment on two specific issues? First, does “metric anachronism” do any real work that is not already captured by generic warnings about anachronism or archival selection bias? Second, does my distinction between “weak institutions” and “institutions of a different kind” withstand scrutiny, or does it risk romanticizing pre-bureaucratic governance by treating quantitative invisibility as evidence of an alternative rationality rather than as mere absence of data?
