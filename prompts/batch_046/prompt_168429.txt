I serve on a neighborhood infrastructure board that reviews technology proposals for public spaces—traffic systems, energy nodes, data networks, and maintenance equipment. We see about one proposal a month, none of us are engineers or philosophers, and we have to sign off in thirty minutes with non-technical members present. I have been thinking on my own about the claim that artifacts are not neutral, that they can carry politics inside their design—requiring certain social arrangements just to function, or being strongly compatible with some power structures and not others. I want this idea turned into a reusable decision procedure my board can actually run, not an essay.

Please write a step-by-step checklist we can apply in order, with clear questions, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, and explicit stop points: where we halt with a rejection or approval, and where we must hand the decision to human judgment because the issue is values rather than technical fact. The procedure must distinguish between an artifact that hard-requires a particular political or social condition to operate, and one that merely fits more comfortably with one arrangement than another. It should also include a check for hidden governance dependencies—contracts, data ownership, maintenance monopolies—that are not visible in the physical object.

Then apply the procedure to three proposals. First, a centralized biometric entry system for our public library that requires a single vendor-managed database and uniform access rules across all branches. Second, a decentralized solar microgrid for a neighborhood park that can be maintained by local cooperatives or by a city contractor. Third, apply it to an automated waste-sorting station that looks technically neutral—just bins and sensors—but that requires proprietary software updates and a long-term service contract with one provider, which would centralize control over waste data and maintenance budgets; I personally like this proposal for its cleanliness, so I want to see the procedure deliver an answer I will not want to hear.

For each application, show where the procedure gives a clear answer and where it stops and asks for a values judgment. Finally, tell me honestly what the procedure cannot do: which kinds of political embedding it will miss, where it is likely to misfire by treating a soft compatibility as a hard requirement or vice versa, and what kind of proposal should never be evaluated by it alone. Keep the language practical enough that a board member with no philosophy background could use it, but do not water down the conceptual distinctions that actually matter.
