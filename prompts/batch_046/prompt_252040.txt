I've been trying to wrap my head arround Popper's poverty of historicism for a seminar paper and I think I accidentally built an argument that eats it's own tail. Here is the chain that worries me.

(1) Popper's core objection to historicism is that you can't predict the course of history because history is partly driven by the growth of human knowledge, and predicting that growth requires knowing now what we'll discover in the future, which is self-contradictory.

(2) But scientific knowledge isn't just some external factor — it's embedded in social institutions, funding priorities, and technological capacity, all of which are themselves historical.

(3) So if the growth of knowledge makes long-range historical prediction impossible, then it also makes long-range prediction impossible in any domain where human learning shapes outcomes — which is basically every applied science, from epidemiology to climate modeling to urban planning.

(4) In fact, take piecemeal social engineering, which Popper endorses: it depends on our current scientific understanding of cause and effect. But if knowledge growth is radically open-ended, then the causal mechanisms we rely on today might be overturned tomorrow, making even small-scale intervention unpredictable in principle.

(5) So Popper's anti-historicist argument, if valid, seems to collapse into a general skepticism about rational planning altogether, not just about grand prophetic narratives.

(6) But Popper clearly wants to preserve rational critical discussion and incremental reform. That means he needs some domains where learning doesn't destroy predictability.

(7) So either there's a hidden distinction in his view that saves piecemeal engineering from the argument, or the argument proves too much and needs a premise rejected.

Where does the slip happen? Is it in (3) — that applied science is just as vulnerable? Or in (4) — that open-ended knowledge growth undermines local causal knowledge? Or is there a different cost if I reject (1) instead? I'm especially interested in what rejecting your chosen step costs for Popper's broader philosophy of science. Also please say what would change your mind — like what distinction or counterexample would convince you I'm wrong about where the overreach actually happens.
