here's the chain:
(1) Williams says it's one thought too many if a husband saves his wife only after computing that this maximizes welfare or saves the most lives.
(2) but the deeper point isn't just anti-utilitarian; it's that inserting any explicit normative filter between the agent and the beloved corrupts the relationship. he should be moved directly by the fact that she's his wife.
(3) now if that's right, then even thinking "i save her because spouses have special obligations" is also an explicit filter. it's warmer than utility, but it's still a reflective step inserted between me and her.
(4) so the one-thought-too-many principle applies to every reflective justification for caring about a particular person, not just utilitarian ones.
(5) that means anyone who reflects on why they stay with a partner, or why they care for a sick parent, is making the same mistake as the calculating husband.
(6) so either we reject all reflective moral reasoning about intimate relationships, or we admit that some normative filters are okay.
(7) if some filters are okay, then the utilitarian filter isn't uniquely corrupting; it's just one option among many.
(8) therefore Williams's objection either collapses into a general ban on thinking about why we love people, or it fails to single out utilitarianism.
