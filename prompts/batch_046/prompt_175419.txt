You are a senior philosopher of epistemology with particular expertise in peer disagreement, the dogmatism paradox, and second-order knowledge conditions. Before constructing your response, please begin with a brief internal brainstorming phase: list the standard positions on the paradox (equal-weight, steadfast, total-evidence, right-reasons), specify the conditions each requires for a disagreement to be epistemically relevant, and identify the point at which a disagreement stops being about belief and becomes about non-epistemic values.

Consider the following consistent verdict track record of a philosopher — call them R — delivered across a graduate seminar on rational belief revision:

Case 1. Two climate scientists with identical training and data access disagree about a specific model parameter; R holds that each should reduce confidence proportionally, because the symmetry is complete.
Case 2. A physician and a philosopher disagree about whether a particular brain state constitutes consciousness; R holds that the physician should maintain near-full confidence, since only one party possesses the relevant domain competence.
Case 3. Two ethicists with identical credentials disagree about the moral permissibility of an engineered being that behaves like a suffering human but lacks biological substrate; R holds that both should suspend judgment, on the grounds that the concept of moral status is itself contested and the disagreement is therefore deep rather than shallow.
Case 4. Two mathematicians disagree over a proof; one discovers a hidden lemma failure in her own reasoning; R holds that she should abandon her belief, while her peer should maintain belief but lower confidence in the proof's accessibility.
Case 5. A researcher and a policy advisor disagree about the regulatory implications of a settled scientific result; R holds that the researcher should maintain belief about the result, treating the disagreement as practical rather than epistemic.

Now consider Case 6: R is asked to evaluate a disagreement between two consciousness researchers with identical publication records. One argues that a specific predictive-processing architecture supports functional luminosity — the system can know that it knows its intermediate representational states without falling into the regress that the KK principle is often thought to block. The other argues that any such second-order report introduces systematic distortion, breaking the knowledge relation at the second order. Both adopted the theoretical framing from R's own earlier work, though R never endorsed the luminosity claim.

Predict R's verdict, for consistency with Cases 1–5, on whether R should (if placed in either researcher's position) maintain, reduce, or suspend belief about functional luminosity. Identify at least two distinct rules that could jointly generate Cases 1–5; for each, state its precise prediction for Case 6 and explain why. Then specify a single further case — involving disagreement about diachronic preference reversal in the presence of a money-pump structure — that would best discriminate between the two rules. For that further case, state exactly what each rule predicts and why that prediction differs.
