At a workshop on historical explanation, a colleague dismissed both covering-law and narrative accounts with the quip that history is just one damn thing after another, apparently meaning that historians can do no more than list events in chronological order. I want to reply with precision rather than a general defense. Lay out the distinct precise claims that sentence could express: that no universal laws govern historical change; that narrative coherence is irreducible to law-subsumption; that causal connections between events are always local and non-generalizable; that chronological ordering is epistemically prior to any causal claim; or that predictive modeling is illegitimate in historiography altogether. For each reading, indicate whether it is trivial, defensible, or false; specify which ones actually undermine both Hempel-style covering-law explanation and the configurational narrative view that treats historical coherence as irreducible to law-subsumption; and identify the reading my colleague needs if she wants to block analogies between climate-policy causation and historical causation without also undermining ordinary local narratives such as the fall of the Roman Republic. Present the results as a menu, and say which reading best fits the argumentative work the quip was doing in that room.
