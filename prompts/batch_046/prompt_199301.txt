I am taking a seminar that pairs Plutarch's Parallel Lives with studies in medieval and early modern hagiography, focusing on how biography is supposed to function as moral pedagogy. In our discussions, the instructor and several classmates repeatedly invoke the maxim "we learn virtue not by rules but by lives" to defend the educational value of narrative biography—whether Plutarch's comparative portraits or typological saints' lives—over abstract ethical theory. I am convinced the phrase is doing too much work, but I cannot articulate exactly where it slides between different claims. Could you lay out the distinct precise theses that "we learn virtue not by rules but by lives" might be expressing? In particular, distinguish psychological claims (about how moral character is actually formed), epistemic claims (about how we come to recognize or understand virtue), and normative-pedagogical claims (about how moral education ought to be structured). Within each category, specify what "lives" denotes—narrative biography with particularized agents, exemplary typology in which a figure instantiates scriptural or archetypal patterns, or embodied social practice observed in community—and what "rules" denotes: universal maxims, context-sensitive practical judgments, or institutional regulations. For each combination, state whether the claim is a platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, or false, and say whether it genuinely distinguishes biography-based learning from rule-governed learning or merely disguises the latter. Finally, identify which of these readings is actually being appealed to when the maxim is used to justify hagiographic reading—where the saint's specific deeds are interpreted as reiterations of biblical topoi—rather than Plutarch's comparative, particularizing method, and whether any coherent reading of the slogan can preserve that distinction without collapsing biography back into a form of implicit rule-following.
