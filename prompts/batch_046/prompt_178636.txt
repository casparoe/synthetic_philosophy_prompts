In my second career studying philosophy of action after retiring from experimental physics, I have watched the literature on the Knobe effect reach what resembles a thermodynamic dead state: for nearly two decades, one camp has maintained that the asymmetry in folk judgments—where harmful side effects are rated intentional while equally foreseen beneficial ones are not—demonstrates that moral evaluation is constitutively woven into the very concept of intentional action, while the opposing camp insists the asymmetry is a performance artifact driven by blame attribution, conversational pragmatics, or task-demand effects. Despite successive waves of refined vignettes, cross-cultural replication, and attempts to correlate the judgments with neural response patterns, neither side has altered its core commitment. The dispute feels less like an empirical disagreement that more data could settle and more like two groups running different measurements and calling the result “the concept of intentionality,” yet I remain unsure whether this is a verbal dispute about the term’s extension, a substantive clash over which psychological mechanisms count as concept-forming rather than post-hoc rationalizing, or a deeper divergence about what kind of explanation—normative, causal, or functional—philosophy of action owes. Drawing on my former field, I wonder whether the stalling resembles the situation in statistical mechanics where observers disagree not about the raw measurements but about whether they are tracking a state function, which ought to be path-independent, or a path-dependent observable that legitimately takes different values along different causal trajectories—suggesting that both sides might be correct about their local data while disagreeing about which quantity “intentional action” denotes. Is that analogy illuminating or misleading? More generally, is the Knobe impasse a hidden empirical question about the architecture of moral cognition, a disagreement about standards of evidence in which philosophical intuitions and experimental results are weighted differently, a confrontation with bedrock normative commitments about responsibility that neither side can argue for, or a sign that the original question was ill-posed? And given your diagnosis, what would actually constitute progress—a redesigned experiment that forces convergence, a formal distinction that dissolves the ambiguity, a change of subject away from “the” folk concept, or an admission that the debate should be expected to remain frozen?
