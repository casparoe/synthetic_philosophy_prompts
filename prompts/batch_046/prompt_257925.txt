Exam question:
In the Analects, Confucius connects the rectification of names to social order: when titles and enacted realities diverge, ritual and authority collapse. Contemporary role ethicists extend this by arguing that Confucian roles—teacher, student, ruler, minister—are not descriptions of pre-existing individuals but are constituted by the relationships themselves. Consider the following case. Dr. Chen is the senior attending physician in a teaching hospital. After an administrative restructuring, her formal authority and liability are reassigned to a new administrator, but she continues to mentor residents informally, including a resident named Liu. Liu relies on her clinical guidance for a high-stakes decision, yet the hospital's formal chain of command assigns legal liability elsewhere. Applying Confucian role ethics, analyze whether Liu owes Dr. Chen the deference proper to a teacher (shi) or whether the rectification of names requires redirecting that deference to the new administrator. In your answer, address (a) whether role-constituted obligations persist when institutional authority is withdrawn, and (b) how shu operates in structurally asymmetric relationships such as teacher-student.

Student answer:
Confucian ethics treats roles like teacher and student as relational rather than contractual, so Dr. Chen's authority does not vanish merely because an administrator took over her paperwork. The Analects say that if names are not correct, affairs will not be carried out; this means the restructuring has created a misalignment—the name "attending physician" now belongs to someone who is not actually guiding Liu, while Dr. Chen performs the substantive work of teaching. By shu, Liu should place himself in Dr. Chen's position and recognize her continued investment in his development, which generates a reciprocal obligation of deference. Because the teacher-student relationship is hierarchical, reciprocity does not mean equal exchange; rather, it means Liu owes her the specific deference that a junior owes a senior who provides both moral and technical guidance. Therefore Liu should treat Dr. Chen as his primary clinical authority. The formal liability chain is a modern institutional artifact that Confucian ethics treats as ethically secondary, though it should not be ignored entirely. Overall, the living relational substance matters more than the organizational chart.
