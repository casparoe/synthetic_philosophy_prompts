In philosophical discussions of P.F. Strawson’s work on reactive attitudes, there is a long-running impasse between two camps. The first camp holds that when we adopt the objective attitude toward an offender—treating them primarily as a subject for explanation, management, or therapeutic intervention rather than as a fully responsible interlocutor—we necessarily forfeit the moral standing to blame them, and that this shift is appropriate only in exceptional cases such as severe mental illness or extreme coercion. The second camp argues that the objective attitude is not a suspension of moral engagement but a transformation of it: by viewing the offender through explanatory and therapeutic lenses, we actually fulfil a deeper obligation to respect human dignity, particularly in cases of structural disadvantage, addiction, or trauma. Both sides cite Strawson’s distinction between participant reactive attitudes and the objective attitude. Both agree on the relevant empirical facts about coercion and addiction. Both agree that suffering is bad. Yet after years of exchange in journals and reading groups, neither camp has moved an inch.

My working hypothesis is that the stall is not about Strawson’s text at all, but about a hidden normative question: whether moral respect requires maintaining the interpersonal reactive stance even when rich explanatory understanding is available, or whether respect can sometimes demand that we replace resentment with clinical or structural concern. But I am unsure whether this is correct. There may instead be a verbal disagreement about what counts as the objective attitude, or about the meaning of moral respect. Alternatively, the two camps may be answering different questions—one about the conditions for legitimate blame, the other about the ethics of care—under a single heading. I also want you to consider a further possibility that neither camp has articulated: the idea of gradated objectivity, according to which reactive attitudes are not switched on or off as a unitary package but can be selectively suspended along specific dimensions—say, resentment suspended while moral expectation and certain forms of gratitude are retained—depending on the explanatory context. If this gradated model is coherent, then both camps may be treating Strawson’s binary distinction as exhaustive when it is not, and the stall may reflect a failure to notice that the real dispute concerns which dimensions of reactivity are appropriate to suspend, and why.
