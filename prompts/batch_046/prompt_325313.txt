A standard move in discussions of Carroll's tortoise—whether in logic seminars or in writing on the justification of deduction—is to compare logical inference rules to the rules of a board game such as chess. The thought is that just as one cannot justify the bishop's diagonal movement by playing a game that already presupposes it, so one cannot justify modus ponens by deriving it via an argument that already employs modus ponens. Proponents lean on this analogy to license the conclusion that logical rules are constitutive norms: they do not stand in need of further justification because they define the very practice within which justification takes place.

I want a precise audit of how far the analogy holds. First, provide an explicit correspondence table: in the logical case, what plays the role of the player, the rulebook, the referee, the board state, and the conditions for victory or defeat? Second, separate the disanalogies that matter for the intended use—especially those bearing on conventionality, revisability, and the source of normative force—from those that are merely cosmetic. For instance, chess rules are institutionally stipulated and could be revised without error, whereas logical rules are typically treated as necessary; but is that a genuine feature of logic, or is it smuggled in by selecting chess rather than, say, arithmetic? Third, consider an injected variant: treat logical inference not as playing a game but as compiling source code in a dependently typed programming language, where inference rules are explicit term constructors and the tortoise demands that the compiler justify its own parser syntax without invoking the parser's rules. Does this computational analogy recover something the chess analogy obscures—perhaps about explicit formalization, self-application, or the distinction between object-level and meta-level rules—or does it merely relocate the regress?

Finally, tell me whether the conclusion that deduction needs no external justification survives once these disanalogies are fully on the table, and if the chess analogy carries false baggage about conventionality or external authority, say whether a modified analogy—perhaps drawn from architectural foundations, or from the syntax of formal languages—would preserve the insight without the distortion. Be explicit about which of your verdicts depend on the analogy itself and which survive its collapse.
