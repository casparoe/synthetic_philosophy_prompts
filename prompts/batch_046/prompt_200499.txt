As a novelist drafting a philosophical symposium for my current work, I ask you to compose the joint statement that an adversarial collaboration between defenders of etiological (selected-effect) and causal-role (systemic/dispositional) accounts of biological function might produce. Structure it as follows: first, a numbered list of claims both camps can sign—covering what needs explaining (malfunction, evolutionary novelty, accidental effects, and the normativity of function attribution); second, a table of precise residual disagreements, each rendered in wording that the respective side would accept for its own view, especially on whether evolutionary history is necessary, sufficient, or irrelevant to functional status; third, the cruxes—those subordinate questions such that settling one would move at least one camp—including, for instance, whether a synthetic heart in a bioengineered organism possesses the function of circulation, and whether an evolved trait that now contributes nothing to fitness retains its function; fourth, for each crux, identify a concrete biological case or thought-experiment both sides agree bears on the issue, recording in advance each camp's explicit prediction about what the case demonstrates. Finally, indicate whether any portion of the dispute—perhaps around the rhetoric of "design"—is merely verbal, explaining precisely where the wording diverges without substantive disagreement. Keep the tone formal, neutral, and suitable for attribution in a fictional academic setting.
