I've been working through some concrete applications of Davidson's radical interpretation, and I've noticed a recurring interpretive situation that I haven't seen given a specific label in the literature, though it sits uncomfortably near several familiar problems. Consider two scenarios. In the first, an interpreter is trying to understand a speaker who discusses animals in hunting contexts—tracking movement, predicting behavior, coordinating kills—in ways that are entirely rational and mutually consistent. But when that same speaker discusses the very same animals in ritual or kinship contexts, the attributions seem to require beliefs that directly contradict the hunting ones: the creature is simultaneously a physical organism subject to causal prediction and a spiritual ancestor with intentional agency directed at the speaker's clan, with no apparent awareness of tension. In the second scenario, a speaker employs numerical terms with perfect consistency in trade and measurement, yet applies them in ways that systematically violate cardinality when counting family members versus strangers, not out of ignorance but in patterns that track social obligation and avoidance rules rather than quantity.

In both cases, the interpreter can preserve local rationality—within the hunting domain, within the ritual domain, within trade—by assigning ordinary meanings and beliefs. But any attempt to construct a single, globally integrated belief-set for the speaker either forces massive, widespread irrationality onto them or requires attributing a conceptual scheme so fragmented that it seems to violate the holism of belief and mental content central to Davidson's framework. The interpreter ends up with what feels like a patchwork of micro-interpretations, each coherent on its own terms, but lacking the unified conceptual economy that Davidson's account of interpretation seems to require. Is there an established name for this kind of domain-specific rationality that resists global integration? And if there is not, how should we classify it: as a local variant of the scheme-content distinction Davidson rejects, as an extreme form of interpretation indeterminacy, or as a genuinely distinct phenomenon—perhaps one that exposes a tension between local charity and global holism that Davidson's arguments do not fully resolve? I am particularly interested in whether this fragmentation can be accommodated within radical interpretation without abandoning the idea that belief and meaning are inseparable and globally interdependent.
