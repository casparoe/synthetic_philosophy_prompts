I am preparing an essay for my sixth-form philosophy seminar, and I should like to compare two frameworks that reject the atomistic conception of the self. The first is the Southern African ethic of Ubuntu, particularly in its relational ontology—expressed in the idea that a person is constituted through other people, as developed in the work of Desmond Tutu and John Mbiti. The second is the process-philosophical account of personhood, in which identity is not a persisting substance but a pattern of relational actual occasions of experience, along the lines suggested by Alfred North Whitehead.

Rather than treating these as mere abstractions, I want to test them against an unconventional case: a hypothetical posthumous digital agent that continues to participate in communal life by drawing upon the recorded speech, social obligations, and decision-making patterns of a deceased individual. Would Ubuntu recognise such an entity as possessing personhood, given that it maintains communal relationships yet lacks biological embodiment and ancestral connection? Would the process-relational view grant it derivative personhood on the grounds of relational continuity, even though no single stream of conscious experience persists?

Please compare the two frameworks with respect to (i) the specific criteria each employs to attribute personhood, (ii) how each analyses the absence of biological and psychological continuity in this scenario, and (iii) which offers the more coherent account of moral responsibility toward the agent. I should be grateful if the answer were composed in American English. I do not require bibliographies, citations, or reading lists—only a rigorous philosophical comparison.
