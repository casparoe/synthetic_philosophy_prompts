As a novelist accustomed to tracing how a small premise inflates into an impossible world, I have built an argument about European sovereignty and democratic legitimacy that now strikes me as suspicious: if it holds, it dissolves not merely the European Union but nearly every modern state. Here is the chain. (1) A democratic people retains genuine sovereignty only if it holds an unconditional, unilateral right to withdraw from any political association that binds it; otherwise its consent is structural rather than revocable. (2) The European Union's founding commitment to an ever closer union among the peoples of Europe establishes an integrative trajectory that no single member-state electorate can reverse without violating treaty obligations to its partners. (3) Therefore membership in the Union is incompatible with the kind of sovereignty that democratic theory demands. (4) Apply the same standard inside any representative democracy: citizens are bound by tax codes, criminal statutes, and regulatory regimes they never personally authorized, and they possess no unilateral right to exit the state's jurisdiction without emigrating entirely. (5) Consequently, if (1) is correct, then not only the EU but also the federal states of Germany, the United States, and every other constitutional democracy suffer from the same democratic deficit and sovereignty deficit. (6) Yet we do not regard those states as fundamentally illegitimate; indeed, we treat the absence of a personal exit option from every particular law as a feature of collective self-government rather than its negation. (7) So either (1) is false and sovereignty does not require unconditional withdrawal rights, or we must accept that democratic legitimacy is a fiction everywhere. I am inclined to preserve (1) in some form, because the idea that consent must remain revocable feels central to the moral standing of the Union's critics. Where does the argument slip? Is the error in (2), by treating integration as irreversible rather than as a voluntary framework sustained by continuing consent? Or in (4), by equivocating between withdrawal from an association and exemption from particular laws within it? Or is the slide elsewhere—perhaps between sovereignty as legal supremacy and sovereignty as democratic authorization—such that giving up (1) costs us less than it appears? Provide the diagnosis in American English: name the weakest step, explain why it once looked inevitable, and price precisely what we surrender if we reject it, especially regarding the EU's democratic deficit and the meaning of ever-closer union.
