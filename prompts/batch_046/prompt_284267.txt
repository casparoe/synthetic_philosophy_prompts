I'm taking an advanced philosophy seminar in high school, and we've been working through the discursive dilemma—the problem that individually consistent judgments on interconnected propositions can aggregate, via straightforward majority rule, into a collectively inconsistent set. Most discussions treat this as a technical puzzle for jury verdicts or legislative committees, but I want to examine it in a less common institutional setting. Consider a public school curriculum board that must vote on three linked claims: whether a particular historical account is factually accurate, whether teaching it cultivates civic virtue, and whether civic virtue ought to be a primary aim of history education. The board's charter requires that any adopted curriculum satisfy all three conditions jointly rather than separately, and it further requires the board to publish a coherent public justification for whatever decision it reaches.

Write an essay arguing either that this kind of case shows the discursive dilemma to be a structural failure of deliberative democracy itself—such that any aggregation procedure preserving anonymity and universal domain must either violate collective rationality or suppress minority reasoning—or that the dilemma is only a logical curiosity with no deep democratic significance, resolvable through appropriate institutional design. In developing your position, weigh premise-based against conclusion-based aggregation procedures, address whether a "reason-giving" transparency constraint (the obligation to publish a consistent justification) resolves the paradox or actually deepens it, and consider whether democratic bodies should therefore abandon majoritarian premise-voting in favor of an alternative mechanism, or whether collective rationality was never a legitimate expectation in the first place. Reach and defend a specific verdict rather than ending on an uncommitted survey of options.
