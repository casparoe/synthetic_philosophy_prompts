I serve on the disciplinary appeals panel of a national research-funding body. We must issue a reasoned recommendation, due within ten days, on two linked questions. First, should we revoke Dr. Elena Voss’s grant for negligent data misreporting, given that the error was non-intentional but materially affected two published figures? Second, should our panel chair, Dr. Marcus Reed—who was previously sanctioned at another institution for intentional data fabrication, served his suspension, and was reinstated with full privileges—be recused from voting on this case and from chairing future misconduct hearings?

Dr. Voss’s defense does not dispute the negligence. Instead, she argues that Dr. Reed lacks standing to blame her: because his own past misconduct was intentional and more severe, allowing him to sit in judgment violates the principle that hypocrisy undermines the authority of moral criticism. One panelist holds that institutional authorization alone confers standing, so Reed’s history is irrelevant; another holds that any past serious misconduct permanently disqualifies one from judgment. I am concerned that both positions miss a subtler distinction—between moral purity, comparative moral equality, and non-comparative institutional standing.

Please draft the panel’s formal written opinion. It should recommend a concrete course of action on both questions, specify a time frame for implementation, and treat the defense’s standing-to-blame argument seriously rather than dismissing it. In particular, clarify whether hypocrisy eliminates authority, merely diminishes it, or is altogether irrelevant when an institution has formally reinstated the person; and explain which of these views best supports your bottom-line recommendation. Address the strongest consideration on the opposing side honestly.
