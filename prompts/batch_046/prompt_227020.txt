The debate in philosophy of psychiatry over Ian Hacking's looping effects has been stuck for years: do interactive psychiatric kinds undermine realism about mental disorders, or only complicate our epistemology? Both camps accept the same historical examples—hysteria, autism, dissociative identity disorder—and both agree that being classified changes how people behave. Yet neither side has conceded anything substantial. I want a diagnosis rather than a survey. Is this disagreement verbal—hinging on different senses of 'real' or 'natural kind'—or substantive? Is there a hidden empirical claim about whether looping is reversible: can withdrawing a classification make the corresponding behavior vanish, or are the effects structurally irreversible? Or are the two sides answering different questions—one ontological, one predictive—under the same label? To test the diagnosis, inject the following hypothetical: imagine a well-documented psychiatric category in which patient awareness of the diagnosis systematically suppresses the symptoms rather than amplifying them, producing what we might call counter-looping. Would this case settle the dispute, or show that the disagreement was never about loop directionality in the first place?
