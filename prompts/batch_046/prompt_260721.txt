I'm moderating the Q&A for our department's visiting speaker series, which is tied to my coursework in comparative moral philosophy, and our guest is a contemporary philosopher who has developed a somewhat unorthodox interpretation of the classical Confucian debate on human nature. She argues that Xunzi's claim that human nature is bad—xing e—should be read not as a thesis about innate moral depravity but as a claim about structural incompleteness: human biological and emotional capacities lack internal self-organizing principles and therefore require ritual (li) and moral instruction not as external suppressions but as constitutive conditions that complete human nature, analogous to the way grammatical rules complete the human language faculty. She maintains that this makes Xunzi closer to a naturalistic virtue ethics than to the standard "correctionist" reading, and she further contends that Mencius's competing view—that human nature contains innate moral sprouts (duan)—is actually the more pessimistic position, since it treats these sprouts as fragile tendencies that social corruption can destroy rather than as capacities that culture actively constructs. Please include at least one question that presses the grammar analogy directly—specifically whether it implicitly commits her to a Chomskyan linguistic nativism she hasn't defended—along with a follow-up keyed to the likely reply that ritual is culturally variable in ways universal grammar is not. I also want a question about whether this reading collapses the Mencius-Xunzi distinction altogether, since both philosophers now seem to agree that culture is necessary for moral realization, followed by her probable distinction that Mencius treats culture as protective of innate goods while Xunzi treats it as constructive of goods that do not pre-exist. Finally, draft one question about the normative consequences: if ritual is genuinely constitutive rather than corrective, does moral failure become a kind of metaphysical deficiency or ontological lack rather than a culpable fault, and how does she avoid that implication without smuggling back in the very notion of innate moral potential she rejects?
