Write a formal dialogue between two equally competent philosophers of probability—call them A and B—who hold opposing views on a variant of the doomsday argument. The setting is conceptual: they are analyzing a hypothetical long-duration vessel whose crew are not biologically reproduced but are periodically reinstantiated from archived mental patterns. Each reinstantiation retains prior memories but is instantiated on a new substrate, and the ship’s records track both a sequential generation index and the cumulative total of all substrates ever activated.

A should defend the view that an observer can perform a standard birth-rank Bayesian update within the reference class of currently active crew members: finding oneself at a low sequential index should lower the expectation for the total number of future reinstantiations, much as in the classical doomsday reasoning. B should defend the opposing view that, because any given substrate could have been any of the previously activated ones, the appropriate reference class is the total pool of ever-active substrates, and that a principle of self-indication then favors larger total populations, thereby reversing or neutralizing A’s update.

The dialogue must include genuine back-and-forth: each speaker should respond directly to the specific objections raised by the other, rather than delivering parallel monologues. In particular, they should address three issues: (1) whether shared memory continuity collapses the distinction between sequential generations, thereby undermining the reference-class partition; (2) whether the sequential numbering is a genuine sampling frame or merely an artifact of record-keeping that should not enter the prior; and (3) whether the argument survives if the reinstantiation protocol is deterministic—i.e., if the sequence of activations is fixed in advance rather than drawn stochastically—since determinism might eliminate the probabilistic basis for the update altogether. Neither character should be a strawman, and the dialogue should not appeal to authority, citation, or bibliographic references.
