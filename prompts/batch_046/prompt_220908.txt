I lead a graduate seminar in social choice and political philosophy — 14 participants, one 75-minute session. Design a structured in-class simulation that makes the group feel the force of Sen's liberal paradox before anyone names it. Use a concrete but unfamiliar framing: the collective is restructuring a community-run oral-history archive, and two members advance opposing minimal-liberty claims over the same content domain — Member A holds a veto right against any deletion of recordings they contributed, while Member B holds a veto right against any retention of recordings they deem harmful. The remaining students form a neutral arbitration panel that must apply a strict Pareto-efficiency rule to any restructuring proposal. Design explicit roles, a non-monetary payoff scheme (e.g., collective legitimacy points that diminish whenever the panel violates either veto or approves an inefficient outcome), and a decision timeline that drives the panel to endorse both rights simultaneously. The rules should be arranged so that participants discover for themselves that satisfying both vetoes collapses the archive — the contradiction should emerge from their own binding votes, not from facilitator narration. Include: (1) a minute-by-minute timeline; (2) the debrief that maps each voting round onto the formal elements of the paradox (the minimal liberty principle, the Pareto criterion, and the domain overlap); (3) an honest admission of which feature of Sen's general proof — specifically its universal quantification over arbitrary pairs of individuals — the simulation cannot reproduce, and how you will supply that gap verbally; and (4) a prediction of what will predictably fail the first time you run this and how to correct it in real time. Write in American English. Please state how confident you are in the pedagogical design and single out the point about which you are least sure.
