I've been reading about the origin of the word genocide and I'm struggling to understand how a legal category can carry so much moral weight at the same time. A friend who took an introductory course in international law summarized Raphael Lemkin's idea for me, and I want to check whether his account is coherent or whether he is smoothing over important distinctions. He explained that Lemkin coined the term in the mid-1940s not merely for mass killing but for the deliberate destruction of a group's culture, language, religion, and social institutions—what Lemkin characterized as destroying the oppressed group's collective way of life and imposing that of the oppressor. According to my friend, the UN Convention adopted shortly afterward retained the word but stripped away all cultural and political dimensions, leaving only a precise checklist of physical and biological acts: killing members, causing serious bodily or mental harm, deliberately inflicting conditions calculated to bring about physical destruction, imposing measures to prevent births, and forcibly transferring children to another group. Because of this, he claims, there are now two incompatible concepts hiding behind a single word: Lemkin's broad moral idea of destroying a people as a people, and a narrow legal framework that largely ignores cultural erasure unless it is paired with direct physical violence.

My friend pushed the argument further. He said that this split explains why debates about naming atrocities are so politically charged: diplomats often avoid the word genocide because the legal definition is so specific, using terms like ethnic cleansing instead, but he insisted that ethnic cleansing describes essentially the same moral horror without triggering the same treaty obligations. He also proposed a hypothetical case in which a state systematically eliminates every school, religious site, and language program of a minority population over several generations, yet never kills anyone; he argued that this would clearly count as genocide under Lemkin's original conception, yet it would fail to qualify under the Convention, which he takes to show that the legal framework is morally arbitrary. From all of this he concluded that in public moral discourse we should abandon the word genocide altogether and simply catalog the specific harms—physical, cultural, economic, demographic—rather than fighting over a label that was politically diluted from the start.

I'm not sure which parts of this are accurate representations of Lemkin's work and which are subtle misrepresentations designed to make the conclusion seem inevitable. Does Lemkin's original writing really treat cultural destruction as conceptually independent of physical violence, or was it always embedded in a broader pattern of bodily harm? Is ethnic cleansing genuinely just genocide under a diplomatic euphemism, or is there a meaningful conceptual distinction that my friend is glossing over? And does his final recommendation—that we drop the term from moral discussion—actually follow from the gap between the broad idea and the narrow law, or is he conflating legal precision with moral uselessness? I would like a careful audit of this summary: which claims a defender of Lemkin's legacy or a scholar of international law would accept, which involve conflations or overstatements, and whether the reasoning holds up once those errors are corrected.
