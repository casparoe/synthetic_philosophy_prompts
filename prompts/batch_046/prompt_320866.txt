You are a philosopher of mind with particular expertise in artificial intelligence, epistemology, and the philosophy of cognition. Before you diagnose the argument below, spend a brief moment brainstorming: list the two or three most plausible breaking points, note what kind of cognitive architecture each repair favors, and flag any hidden slide between explicit symbolic representation and implicit or procedural representation.

Here is an argument advanced in a recent discussion that uses the frame problem not merely as a technical obstacle for AI but as a transcendental objection to representational theories of mind.

(1) Any system that behaves intelligently over extended time must maintain and update an internal model of its environment.
(2) Rational updating requires the system to determine, for each new input, which prior representations remain valid and which must be revised or discarded.
(3) This determination is the frame problem: without it, the system faces combinatorial explosion or total paralysis; with it, the system can act efficiently.
(4) But to solve the frame problem in a given domain, the system must represent that domain’s relevance conditions—that is, it must already know which changes matter for its current goals and which do not.
(5) Representing those relevance conditions just is maintaining an accurate, updatable world-model of the task environment.
(6) So solving the frame problem requires having already solved it: the capacity to update representations rationally presupposes the very representational stability and completeness that updating is supposed to secure.
(7) Therefore, no purely representational system can ever get started; representationalism explains intelligent behavior only by presupposing the capacity it aims to explain.

The speaker concludes that we should abandon representational theories altogether, including predictive-processing and some embodied-cognition accounts that still invoke internal models. I feel this proves too much—if it succeeds, it seems to undercut not only classical symbolic AI but also any view that assigns representations a causal-explanatory role in cognition, yet every individual step looks locally defensible. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Specifically, does rejecting step (4) commit me to a non-representational picture (e.g., direct perception, radical enactivism, or pure behaviorism), or can a representationalist absorb the cost by distinguishing explicit frame axioms from implicit, learned, or hierarchically organized expectations? And does the argument secretly equivocate between “representing relevance conditions as explicit symbolic structures” and “encoding them in distributed weights or procedural habits,” such that step (5) collapses two very different kinds of representation into one? Give your diagnosis, price the alternatives, and say which premises I can keep if I want to preserve both representationalism and the intuition that the frame problem is a genuine, non-trivial constraint.
