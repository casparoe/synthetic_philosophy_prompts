Me and my study group got stuck on this James vs Clifford thing after our ethics of beleif unit, and I want an outside take. Two people in the group, Maya and Leo, went back and forth like this:

(1) Maya said Clifford's shipowner case shows belief without sufficient evidence is always wrong, not just risky for you but for everyone, since careless belief makes you careless with other people. She applied it to personal stuff too: if you believe your partner is faithful on shaky grounds, you might treat them unfairly when doubts come up.

(2) Leo said that's missing James's point entierly. James wasn't talking about ignoring evidence you have; he was talking about options that are genuinely unavoidable, genuinely possible for you, and genuinely important, where the evidence is permanently ambiguous -- like whether free will exists or whether life has meaning. In those cases, suspending judgment isn't neutral; it's itself a decision with real consequences, so you have a right to let your passional nature decide.

(3) Maya came back that James's criterion for a living option is just whatever feels urgent to you, so a conspiracy theorist could claim their belief is unavoidable and important and hide behind James. She said without an objective line, James licenses any wishful thinking you can dress up as passion.

(4) Leo answered that James does have checks: the option has to be genuinely unavoidable (you can't avoid deciding) and genuinely possible for your actual life. He said most conspiracy theories fail because they don't change how you live; they're just entertainment. But then he applied it to a climate scientist who adopts a worst-case scenario belief to motivate urgent research despite ambiguous models. Maya said that's exactly the problem: Leo turned James into believe whatever helps you act, which collapses the ethics of belief into pure pragmatism with no epistemic constraint left.

Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the four moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify each rating. Then say who's more right overall, and point out what both are missing -- especially about whether James's unavoidable criterion can actually do the work Leo thinks it does.
