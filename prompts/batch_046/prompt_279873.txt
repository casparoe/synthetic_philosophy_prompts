In our graduate distributive-justice seminar we have been working through the priority view and why it is usually said to escape the leveling-down objection that plagues strict egalitarianism. I understand the standard reply: prioritarianism cares about absolute levels and weights benefits to the worse off more heavily, but it does not treat equality itself as intrinsically valuable, so reducing everyone to the same low level does not count as an improvement. Still, I am troubled by what looks like a formal feature of any weighted welfare function. Suppose we adopt a scheme in which each unit of welfare is multiplied by a weight that is inversely proportional to the individual's current absolute position—say, weight equals ten at welfare level 1 and weight equals one at welfare level 10—so that a marginal gain to the worst-off receives ten times the value of an identical gain to the best-off. Because the weight is tied to the baseline state rather than to the increment alone, it seems that uniform downward redistribution—compressing every individual to welfare level 1—would place every person in the highest-weighted category simultaneously. Would that not maximize the aggregate weighted sum, since no one remains in a lower-weighted position? In other words, doesn't the very mechanism that makes prioritarianism sensitive to deprivation also generate a perverse incentive to level everyone downward, provided the leveling is complete and uniform? I assume the error lies in applying the weight to the state rather than to the marginal benefit, or perhaps in ignoring a constraint on the total welfare pool, but I cannot see precisely where the calculus blocks this result. Can you clarify whether a consistently formulated prioritarian function genuinely eliminates all downward-leveling incentives, or whether the avoidance depends on an implicit boundary condition that keeps total welfare fixed?
