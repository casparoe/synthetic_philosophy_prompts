As a retired physicist who has turned to philosophy only recently and somewhat seriously, I have been tracking the dispute that has formed around Daniel Dennett's warning about artificial intelligence generating counterfeit people. In the discussions I have followed—seminars, essays, and informal exchanges—the participants appear to agree on the underlying facts: contemporary language models can sustain fluent, context-sensitive dialogue; they almost certainly lack phenomenal consciousness and biological embodiment; and deploying them to deceive human interlocutors is technically feasible and morally dangerous. Nevertheless, the argument has not moved in any discernible direction over the past several years. One faction treats Dennett's point as metaphysical: because the intentional stance is a predictive and explanatory strategy that we apply automatically to entities that behave rationally, AI systems that trigger this response are being erroneously categorized as persons, and the error is ontological. The opposing faction reads him as advancing a socio-epistemic caution: our cognitive architecture makes the intentional stance unavoidable, so we will treat these systems as agents regardless of their true nature, and the harm lies in the collapse of social trust, not in a misapplied category. Both factions appeal to Dennett's observation that adopting the stance is easy to invoke and nearly impossible to resist, yet they draw opposite conclusions.

My working hypothesis is that this is not a factual disagreement but a structural one: one side is answering a normative-institutional question about safeguards against deception, while the other is answering a descriptive-cognitive question about the conditions for correct ascription of agency, and both are using the phrase counterfeit people to cover both questions. I also wonder whether a hidden asymmetry is doing work—namely, that we can withdraw the intentional stance from an artificial system once we understand its mechanism, whereas we do not withdraw it from human beings even upon full neurobiological explanation, which suggests that biological origin functions as an unstated boundary condition for personhood in one camp but not in the other.

Can you diagnose the actual structure of this disagreement? Is it verbal, substantive, or a case of two distinct inquiries running under one title? Does the asymmetry of attribution I mentioned expose a hidden premise, or is it merely a residual biological chauvinism that neither side needs? If the impasse turns on bedrock intuitions about authenticity versus functional equivalence, what—if anything—would count as progress: a sharper distinction between predictive stance-adoption and moral recognition, a formal result about the conditions for withdrawing intentional ascriptions, or an acceptance that no further argument should be expected? I want a diagnosis supported by what each side actually claims, not a survey of positions.
