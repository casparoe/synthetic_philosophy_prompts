You are a senior philosopher of science specializing in American pragmatism and the epistemology of distributed inquiry. Before drafting, think carefully and begin with a brief brainstorming section: map the logical space of pragmatist theories of truth, identify the precise commitments that distinguish Peirce's convergence account (truth as the opinion an indefinite community of inquirers is destined to agree upon, with reality as its object and verification as a collective long-run process independent of any individual's psychological states) from James's experiential account (truth as a property ideas acquire through being verified in concrete, particular experience, where verification is always situated and tied to the practical consequences an idea produces for specific agents), and then locate exactly where those commitments generate different predictions about truth-status in cases of fragmented, instrument-mediated, and deferred verification.

Your task is to draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between Dr. A, who defends the Peircean view, and Dr. B, who defends the Jamesian view. The collaboration focuses on a specific conceptual problem: the epistemic status of predictive claims about open, complex systems—such as long-range climate or epidemiological forecasts—where no single human agent can directly verify the outcome, verification is distributed across automated instruments, statistical models, and peer networks over decades, and the practical working of the claim involves immediate policy decisions made under uncertainty long before any final convergence could occur.

Structure the document as follows:

1. Agreed claims (numbered list): propositions both Dr. A and Dr. B can sign, covering the formal structure of predictive reasoning, the role of instruments as legitimate extensions of inquiry, the distinction between prediction and explanation, and their shared rejection of both naive correspondence theories and purely coherence theories of truth.

2. Disagreements table: the exact points of divergence, phrased neutrally so each side recognizes its own position. Include at least: (a) whether truth requires actual future convergence or only present practical efficacy; (b) whether instrument-mediated data counts as experience in the relevant sense; (c) whether a claim can be true now if it works for present decision-makers but might be overturned by a more comprehensive framework later.

3. Cruxes: identify the sub-questions whose resolution would move at least one party. For each crux, specify a concrete case or piece of evidence both agree is relevant, and record in advance what Dr. A and Dr. B each predict about it. At least one crux should involve the scenario of a predictive model that guides successful intervention but is later substantially revised—Dr. A should predict that the earlier claim was approximately true or false rather than strictly true, while Dr. B should predict that it was genuinely true within the context of verification available at the time, though not eternal or unrevisable.

4. Residual verbal disputes: if any part of the disagreement dissolves upon clarification, say which part and why—for example, whether verification means the same thing in both theories, or whether convergence is meant as an actual historical endpoint or a regulative ideal.

Write in a formal, polished style suitable for publication in a philosophy of science journal. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, citations, or requests for further sources. Do not put quotation marks around any phrase drawn from real texts; paraphrase all references to Peirce or James without quotation marks, and avoid depending on specific titles or publication dates—refer to the positions generally as the Peircean view and the Jamesian view. Keep the focus on conceptual clarification rather than historical exegesis.
