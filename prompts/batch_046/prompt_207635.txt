I'm currently drafting revisions to an informed-consent protocol for an institutional review board, and the work has forced me to think more carefully about where deception ends and coercion begins. During a committee session last week, two colleagues—I'll call them Elias and Mara—became locked in a sustained argument over a hypothetical clinical case. A surgeon informs a fully competent patient that an elective procedure carries a 3% mortality risk, though the surgeon privately knows that for patients with this specific comorbidity profile the relevant literature indicates roughly 12%. The patient is not physically threatened, understands the nature of the operation, and signs the form. Elias maintained that because competence and freedom from overt coercion are both satisfied, the authorization is valid; deception, on his view, is a distinct moral failing of the physician but does not vitiate consent, since consent is fundamentally an act of authorization rather than an epistemic achievement. Mara rejected this separation sharply. She argued that deception of this magnitude does not merely misinform but structurally manipulates the deliberative environment, introducing a hidden form of control that undermines the voluntariness condition regardless of how rational the patient remains. When Elias objected that this standard would render almost every clinical disclosure inadequate—since physicians constantly simplify statistics—Mara proposed a counterfactual decision threshold: consent is invalidated only when the deception concerns a factor that, had it been known, would have led this particular patient, given their stated values, to refuse. Elias countered that this collapses consent into an unverifiable counterfactual about subjective values and smuggles in a substantive rationality requirement that medical ethics has never required. The exchange continued through several refinements, with Elias distinguishing deception about a procedure's identity from deception about its risk profile, and Mara distinguishing peripheral omission from what she called core epistemic domination, yet neither yielded ground. As a bystander who respects both positions but does not favor either, I want you to adjudicate this dispute point by point: where is Elias correct about the architecture of consent, where does Mara correctly identify a gap in his account, and what crucial assumption—perhaps about whether competence can ever be evaluated independently of the informational conditions in which it is exercised—do both seem to overlook? Please take a definite, committed stance rather than presenting a balanced menu of options, and ground your verdict in the specific moves each disputant actually made.
