In studies of societal collapse—particularly in discussions of the Classic Maya and Easter Island—I keep encountering what seems to be the same conceptual maneuver. A researcher initially claims that a society collapsed because a specific institutional feature, such as monumental architecture or inter-regional trade, vanished. When it is then shown that local populations survived and that social organization continued at a smaller scale, the researcher responds that collapse is inherently scale-dependent: the "complex society" failed, while the underlying population merely transformed. The problem is that the essential organizational layer is defined retrospectively, so that whatever actually disappeared is treated as the defining feature of the society that was lost. This makes the collapse thesis appear unfalsifiable: if large-scale institutions had persisted, the society would be said not to have collapsed, yet because they disappeared, that very disappearance is taken as proof that the essential layer was lost. Is there an established concept in archaeology or sociology for this kind of post-hoc scale selection—perhaps analogous to immunization strategies in philosophy of science—or is it simply a form of multi-level analysis? If there is a recognized term, how is it distinguished from legitimate pluralism about social scales? If not, could one be defined with explicit boundary conditions that separate it from ordinary theoretical flexibility?
