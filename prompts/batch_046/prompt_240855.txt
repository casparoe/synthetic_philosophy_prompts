I spent several hours last night in a vigorous debate with a friend who works in comparative politics, arguing over whether prosperity and public order can ever stand alone as a justification for authoritarian rule—using contemporary China and Singapore not as definitive case studies but as conceptual reference points. During the exchange I kept noticing a specific argumentative structure that I cannot name precisely, and I want to determine whether political philosophy or theory has already given it a label, or whether I must construct one myself.

The pattern works like this. First, when an authoritarian regime delivers rapid economic growth and low violent crime, defenders present these outputs as direct, quantitative proof that the absence of liberal democratic institutions is not merely acceptable but optimally efficient; the evaluation metric is explicit and favorable. Second, when growth stalls, corruption surges, or disorder breaks out—imagine a banking crisis followed by mass protests—the same defenders rarely concede that the model has failed. Instead, they argue that the disorder is exactly the catastrophe that authoritarian control was designed to prevent, and that its partial containment proves the regime's continued necessity. High prosperity therefore confirms efficacy, while diminished prosperity confirms indispensability. The normative premise—that political legitimacy derives from performance—remains intact regardless of empirical trajectory, because the performance criterion itself shifts silently from delivering abundance to averting collapse.

This is not simply the generic moving the goalposts, nor is it exactly Popper's idea of immunizing a scientific theory against refutation, though both are in the neighborhood. Those concepts concern protecting a specific empirical claim; what I have in mind is specific to the logic of output-based legitimation, where the metric of evaluation is dynamically redefined within a single normative framework so that no observable state of the world can challenge the institutional conclusion. Is there an established term—perhaps within studies of authoritarian resilience or performance legitimacy—for this self-sealing loop? If no term exists, can you propose a precise definition that specifies what belongs inside the loop—must it involve intentional rhetorical strategy, or can it function as an institutional logic without any agent's conscious belief?
