Question: In his political writings, Aquinas distinguishes between resistance to a usurper and rebellion against a ruler who holds legitimate authority but governs unjustly. Analyze whether, on his view, the killing of a ruler is ever morally permissible, with careful attention to (a) the distinction between sedition and legitimate resistance, (b) who possesses the authority to authorize such an act, and (c) whether a ruler who originally acquired power legitimately but becomes tyrannical may eventually be treated like a usurper.

Student answer:
Aquinas treats the killing of a ruler as a grave matter because it threatens the common good and risks the disorder of sedition, which he regards as a mortal sin. Nevertheless, he allows an exception for the usurper—one who seizes power without a rightful claim—since such a person is not a true authority and therefore does not command obedience. Resisting a usurper is not sedition but rather the restoration of legitimate order.

The conditions for permissible resistance are strict. There must be no higher authority—such as a superior court or sovereign—to whom appeal can be made. The act must be carried out by public authority or the community acting collectively, not by a private individual acting on personal judgment, since private assassination undermines social order regardless of the ruler’s crimes. Additionally, the tyranny must be extreme and persistent rather than merely inconvenient.

With respect to a ruler who originally held legitimate power but has become tyrannical, Aquinas allows that if all lawful means of correction have failed and the common good is severely endangered, such a ruler may eventually be treated analogously to a usurper. The community, authorized through its magistrates or collective judgment, may then resist, including by force, provided there is no superior to intervene. Thus, while sedition against legitimate authority is always forbidden, resistance is permissible when it is collective, authorized, lacks recourse to a higher power, and responds either to an original usurpation or to a legitimate authority that has forfeited its claim through extreme and unremedied tyranny. This aligns with Aquinas’s broader commitment—shared with Aristotle—that political authority is instrumental to the common good and therefore can be revoked when it systematically violates that end.
