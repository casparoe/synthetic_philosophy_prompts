Design a thought experiment in which illusionism about phenomenal consciousness and a self-representational theory of consciousness—where a mental state counts as conscious only if it represents itself—agree completely on the behavioral, neural, and functional profile of a subject, yet reach opposite conclusions about whether phenomenal experience is present. Constraints: use an ordinary clinical or domestic setting with no futuristic technology or exotic physics; present the scenario in a single paragraph; and isolate one structural feature that generates the disagreement rather than smuggling in the conclusion by stipulation. Engineer a patient with a variant of blindsight who retains accurate first-order perceptual discrimination and preserved affective tagging (appropriate emotional valence attached to stimuli) but who entirely lacks higher-order self-representation of those perceptual states; the patient sincerely reports vivid subjective experiences that are, according to your design, confabulated beliefs produced by intact language networks interpreting behavioral success. After presenting the scenario, demonstrate the reasoning each theory applies: what the illusionist says about the origin and functional role of the patient’s erroneous reports, and what the self-representationalist says about the missing self-referential relation that should have made the state conscious. Identify the precise feature doing the work—whether it is the decoupling of affect from introspective access, the presence of belief without corresponding higher-order representation, or the functional redundancy of the report mechanism—explain whether removing that feature collapses the case back into a standard neural-correlate dispute, and describe how a defender of the view that comes out badly would attack the scenario (for instance, by arguing that the case implicitly assumes the representational architecture it needs to establish). Conclude by stating what empirical finding or philosophical argument would change your assessment of which theory handles the case correctly, and explicitly flag the point in your reasoning that is most likely to fail. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; paraphrase all theoretical positions without quotation marks.
