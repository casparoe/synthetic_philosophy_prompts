At a university research-ethics review, two board members—Dr. Mira Chen and Dr. Jonas Reed—debated whether consent obtained in a particular clinical study should be considered valid. The study had enrolled participants who were told that serious adverse events occurred in fewer than 2 percent of cases, based on the best available aggregated data. What neither the researchers nor the participants knew was that the aggregation protocol systematically excluded members of a specific demographic subgroup to which several enrolled participants actually belonged; for that subgroup, the true rate was closer to 8 percent. The researchers sincerely believed the 2 percent figure, and no one had intentionally concealed anything.

Mira argued that the consent was vitiated by deception. She maintained that deception does not require intent to mislead: when a false belief about risk is structurally embedded in the consent process—produced by an invisible methodological choice rather than by anyone's lie—the participants are still deceived about the object to which they are consenting. They authorized exposure to a risk they understood as 2 percent, but the risk they actually faced was materially different. Therefore, she concluded, autonomy was undermined regardless of anyone's good faith.

Jonas replied that this stretches deception beyond recognition. Without an agent who intended to misrepresent, he said, we have honest error, not deception. He noted that consent in empirical research is always provisional and based on the best current knowledge; if unknown statistical biases automatically invalidated consent, then nearly every study would be retroactively void. He added that the participants were fully competent, not coerced, and able to understand the information presented to them. Their consent failed to match reality, but that is a flaw in the study design, not a failure of consent itself.

Mira countered that she was not claiming any falsehood invalidates consent—only that a false belief about the very nature of the risk, introduced by the framing itself, since "based on current data" implicitly claimed representativeness, means the participants never truly authorized this specific exposure. She distinguished between metaphysical completeness, which is impossible, and material misrepresentation of the risk profile relevant to the decision.

Jonas responded that if the standard for valid consent requires that every statistical aggregation be demographically perfect, then competence becomes impossible: participants can only understand what is presented, not audit hidden methodological exclusions. He argued that the appropriate response is to correct the record and offer redress, not to declare that consent never existed.

They turned to me, a silent observer, and asked me to referee. Please explain who is right about which specific point—particularly whether deception requires intent, whether structural misrepresentation can vitiate consent, and how competence relates to understanding presented information versus understanding actual underlying risk. Say who is more right overall, and identify what, if anything, both parties are missing about the relationship between the epistemic conditions of consent and its moral validity.
