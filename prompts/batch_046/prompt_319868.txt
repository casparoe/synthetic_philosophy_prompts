I was chatting with my flatmate about machine learning and philosphy of science over tea, and we got into wether Hume's problem of induction gets solved or just relocated by the no-free-lunch theorems. I cobled together this argument that now feels like it's trying to do too much, and I'm not sure where the sleight of hand is.

(1) The NFL theorems show that, averaged across all possible worlds, every predictive strategy has the same expected error. (2) So if you want to justify using induction rather than counter-induction, you need to restrict the space of worlds using a prior. (3) But Hume's point is that you can't justify that prior without already using induction — you'd need to observe that the prior worked in past worlds. (4) Therefore, inductive justification requires a prior, but justifying the prior requires induction, so we're in a vicious circle. (5) Now here's where I think it gets weird: some people talk about 'epistemic free-lunch zones' — domains where structural coherence (like logical consistency or causal closure) trims the world-space before any data comes in, so the prior isn't empirically learned but architecturally built-in. (6) If those zones exist, then induction inside them isn't subject to NFL, and Hume's circle is broken because the prior is non-empirical. (7) But wait — if coherence conditions alone can fix the prior, then I can construct a counter-inductive strategy that respects the same coherence conditions but predicts the opposite of induction. Call this 'coherent anti-induction'. (8) So even inside the zone, NFL applies again unless I smuggle in an empirical assumption about which coherence conditions matter. (9) Therefore, there are no epistemic free-lunch zones, and induction remains unjustified. (10) But that conclusion feels absurd — surely logical consistency gives us some constraint?

Where does this go wrong? I feel like step (7) might be cheating, or maybe I'm conflating algorithmic performance with epistemic justification. I don't want to just reject (10) because it's counterintuitive; I'd rather know which premise is actually false and what it costs me to drop it.
