I'm a PhD student in philosophy of mind working on self-knowledge and cognitive architecture. I'm running a 75-minute graduate seminar workshop on the reliability of introspection, with about fourteen participants, and I need a structured in-class activity—not a lecture or discussion—that makes the group feel, before I name it, a specific and somewhat under-discussed tension: that introspective reports about the immediate causal basis of one's own preferences can be systematically confabulated even under conditions of high confidence, minimal cognitive load, and strong first-person authority. The mechanism should be built from common conceptual resources rather than highly technical empirical findings, but I want the framing to inject one niche conceptual twist—namely, that such reports function less like perceptual observations and more like post-hoc rationalizations that preserve a sense of agential coherence—so that the idea is present in the prompt itself rather than treated as standard training material.

Design a runnable simulation or role-play with explicit rules, roles, and a payoff scheme that uses small, real stakes (for instance, points that determine who selects the next session's discussion topic). The rules should be arranged so that participants generate and defend introspective judgments about why they prefer one option over another, then encounter an independently observable feature of their choice environment that undermines the causal story they just offered, without anyone being told in advance that the activity is about confabulation. Provide: the setup and materials needed; the exact rules and incentive structure; a minute-by-minute timeline for the full 75 minutes; a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto parts of the reconstructive view of introspection; an honest prediction of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to correct for it; and a frank note on which feature of actual introspective unreliability—particularly regarding unconscious causal determination—the activity necessarily cannot reproduce and must therefore be supplied by my framing remarks. The activity should assume no prior reading and should not ask participants to produce citations, bibliographies, or reading lists.
