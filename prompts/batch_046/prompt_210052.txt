I'm trying to map my own judgments about when it is rational, irrational, or institutionally inappropriate to suspend what Wittgenstein calls hinge propositions—the framework commitments that normally stand fast and make inquiry possible. Here are my verdicts.

A research physicist who temporarily brackets determinism to develop quantum foundations: legitimate, even admirable.

The same physicist who refuses to sign off on an engineering safety report because she "can't rule out" that gravity is an illusion today: not legitimate; she should be removed from the project.

A juror who raises skeptical doubts about whether the courtroom, the judge, or her own memories are real: inappropriate for the institutional role; the doubt belongs outside.

A philosophy student who asks in a seminar how we know the world wasn't created five minutes ago with false memories: legitimate, even valuable.

A climate-science skeptic who accepts physics and statistics generally but insists that temperature records might be systematically fabricated by an unknown conspiracy: irrational, not a legitimate hinge suspension.

A patient with Capgras syndrome who denies her husband is the same person despite all recognition: not a rational judgment at all, but a cognitive malfunction.

A historian who seriously considers, in a peer-reviewed article, that Napoleon might never have existed: irrational, though structurally similar to the quantum physicist's bracket.

I keep going back and forth on two cases. First, a mathematician who, in a logic exam, writes "I cannot assume 2+2=4 without circular justification": I want to say this is a category error, like the juror's doubt. But second, a mathematician who, in a foundations seminar, explores intuitionistic logic and genuinely suspends the law of excluded middle: I want to call this excellent work. Both suspend arithmetic or logical hinges; what distinguishes them?

And I am unsure about conspiracy theories that don't deny physics or logic but deny a specific institutional fact—say, that a particular election was certified—while accepting all the surrounding institutional hinges. Is that a targeted hinge failure, or is it rational skepticism misapplied?

Is there a coherent principle that distinguishes legitimate hinge suspension from illegitimate or pathological ones—perhaps involving institutional role, domain specificity, reversibility, or the distinction between framework and content? Which of my verdicts does it force me to abandon, especially the pair I keep reversing on, and what does it say about the election-denial case?
