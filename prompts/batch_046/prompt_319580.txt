I'm presenting a seminar paper defending a weak, non-providential directional tendency in history—call it "institutional teleonomy"—and I need a Q&A prep sheet. My thesis: within liberal-democratic constitutional orders since the mid-twentieth century, institutional error-correction mechanisms (precedent-based legal reasoning, formal amendment protocols, deliberative feedback loops) are structurally asymmetrical. They filter out configurations that generate persistent, widespread, and articulated grievance more efficiently than they filter out stable ones, producing a contingent, reversible statistical drift toward more resiliently just arrangements over centuries. I do not invoke determinism, moral realism, or providence; the "direction" is retrospective and statistical, not prospective and guaranteed. My argument sketch: (1) adapt Monod's concept of teleonomy—apparent purpose without purposive agent—to institutional norms; (2) specify the asymmetry between destabilizing injustice and stable injustice; (3) restrict the claim to a specific institutional family to block Whig universalism; (4) concede that "injustice" and "grievance" carry normative load, but treat resilience (longevity, amendment frequency, articulation rates) as descriptively measurable.

The audience: six intellectual historians specializing in nineteenth- and twentieth-century grand narratives (deeply familiar with Koselleck, Arendt, and critiques of Whig history), two philosophers of history skeptical of evolutionary analogies in politics, and one political theorist of democratic legitimacy who is wary of normative smuggling in procedural accounts. None are analytic metaphysicians or decision theorists; all are sensitive to anachronism, contingency, and the difference between describing and prescribing.

Prepare the prep sheet. Order the objections by how damaging they are and how likely this room is to raise them. Phrase each in the vocabulary this audience actually uses—not generic "gotchas." For each: (a) the core objection; (b) what I should concede up front; (c) a reply I can deliver in about two minutes; (d) the exact point where that reply is weakest or soft. Finally, include the objection I am hoping nobody asks: the one that targets the retrospective/prospective teleology distinction in my framework—specifically, whether constituting ends only after the fact collapses the claim into pure narrative constructivism, leaving no directional tendency independent of our own historiographical narration. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
