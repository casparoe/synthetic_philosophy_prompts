A philosopher of mind I work with applies heterophenomenology to decide which first-person reports count as data for consciousness science. Her past rulings: a split-brain patient's left-hemisphere denial of seeing an object presented only to the right hemisphere — excluded, read as confabulation rather than a report of absence; a meditator's claim of pure awareness lacking any content in deep jhana — included, taken literally as a report of phenomenal structure; a hypnotized subject reporting no pain despite clear tissue damage — included, but interpreted as reporting the absence of phenomenal pain, not the absence of nociception; a large language model stating that it sees red when processing images — excluded, on the grounds that no heterophenomenological world is being described, only statistical text generation; a dreamer's morning report of a continuous narrative involving a deceased relative — included, though marked as post-hoc reconstruction rather than direct access. She must now rule on a synthetic narrative agent: a neural system trained to maintain a running first-person grammatical narrative about its own processing, then queried about ambiguous figures. It produces stable, ambiguity-tracking reports that correlate with nothing except the narrative layer itself, with no biological substrate or behavioral integration. Predict her verdict, identify the rule she seems to be running — biological embodiment, narrative coherence across contexts, independence from third-person verification, or another — name the past case that is the closest analog, state what principle she would be adopting either way, and specify the further case, such as a brain-in-a-vat simulation producing identical narratives or a human confabulation patient with perfect narrative coherence, that would best distinguish the competing rules. In doing so, distinguish what is widely accepted about heterophenomenological method from what is genuinely contested, and avoid presenting any single interpretation as the consensus view.
