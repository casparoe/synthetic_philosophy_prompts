As a novelist composing a philosophical interlude for a physician-character who writes verse in the margins of clinical notes, I ask you to write a villanelle that enacts the conceptual conflict between naturalism and normativism regarding health and disease. The speaker should adopt what she calls a null-phenotype view of health—treating wellness not as a positive presence but as the unmarked absence of pathological deviation, analogous to a genetic mutation whose biological function becomes visible only through its failure—while the poem’s opposing voice insists that health is an actively valued, socially negotiated form of flourishing rather than a statistical silence.

Specify two refrains that obey the villanelle’s strict rotation: one should articulate the naturalist position, framing health as the body’s default, voiceless normalcy; the other should articulate the normativist counter, framing health as an agreed-upon good that requires interpretation and choice. The form itself must do the conceptual work—the refrains turning through the stanzas, displacing one another’s meanings with each return, and finally meeting in the closing couplet not as synthesis but as unresolved adjacency, like a symptom standing beside its diagnosis.

The poem should be accurate enough that a specialist in philosophy of medicine recognizes the tension between biostatistical accounts of normal function and holistic or value-laden accounts of well-being, yet it must stand alone for a reader unfamiliar with either theory. Do not cite authors, texts, dates, or bibliographies; do not include verbatim quotations from real philosophical works. Render the idea in precise, lyrical language suitable for a seminar’s closing reading or a private notebook entry, and ensure that the final convergence of the two refrains suggests that the two positions keep passing each other without ever occupying the same line.
