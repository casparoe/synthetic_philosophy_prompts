The debate in the philosophy of time between presentists—who maintain that only entities present at the current moment exist—and eternalists—who treat past, present, and future occurrences as equally real—has remained unresolved for well over a century. Both sides accommodate the logical structure of tense in natural language, both have developed answers to McTaggart-style paradoxes about the contradiction of temporal passage, and both have strategies for addressing the absence of a global simultaneity in special relativity. Presentists typically invoke either a primitive, ungrounded property of presentness or a growing-block ontology in which reality accretes over time; eternalists typically treat tense as indexical, analogous to spatial location, and reconstrue change as mere variation among four-dimensional temporal parts. Despite this shared familiarity with the arguments, the literature exhibits a repetitive pattern: the same objections about truthmakers for past-tense propositions, the same replies about irreducible presentness, and the same stalemates over whether relativistic physics undermines an absolute now.

My hypothesis is that this is not a single disagreement but at least two distinct disputes running under one label. The first is ontological: what should be included in the inventory of reality? The second is explanatory and phenomenological: does temporal passage require representation in the fundamental structure of the world, or only in the structure of experience and causation? If presentists are primarily defending the first claim and eternalists are primarily defending the second, then the two sides may be talking past each other—using shared terms like 'real,' 'exists,' and 'passage' to mark different conceptual targets.

But I want a diagnosis rather than a restatement. Is the disagreement substantive but hidden—perhaps turning on whether causation is fundamentally asymmetric and whether that asymmetry requires an ontologically privileged present? Is it verbal, with 'exists now' functioning for presentists as a tensed predicate and for eternalists as a redundant indexical? Or is it a case of bedrock intuitions—about whether a past suffering retains the same metaphysical weight as a present one—that neither side can argue for without begging the question? And if your diagnosis is correct, what would actually move the debate? Would it take a formal distinction that cleanly separates ontological commitment from explanatory representation? A result in physics—say, about the ontology of spacetime—that forces one picture? Or is the impasse a sign that the question is ill-posed, and that progress requires dissolving the framing rather than resolving it?
