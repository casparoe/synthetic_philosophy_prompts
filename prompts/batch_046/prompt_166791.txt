As a software engineer without formal philosophy training, I'm trying to build a conceptual framework for privacy-preserving system design that goes beyond compliance checklists. Before I evaluate existing philosophical accounts, I want the full specification of what an adequate theory of the nature and value of privacy must actually deliver. Write the desiderata: what phenomena it must explain—e.g., why we care about being observed even when no harm results, why aggregation of seemingly harmless data points changes the moral landscape, and why retroactive exposure, where data revealed in one context becomes damaging in a later context, feels like a distinct violation—which distinctions it must preserve—such as between secrecy, anonymity, and control over interpretation, or between individual and collective privacy interests—which empirical results from cognitive science or sociology it cannot contradict—e.g., that people systematically misjudge future risks of disclosure—and which engineering constraints it should remain compatible with, like the impossibility of perfect deletion in distributed immutable logs. For each requirement, explain why it is genuinely required rather than a preference of one camp. Identify any pair of desiderata that appear jointly unsatisfiable—particularly whether a theory can simultaneously treat privacy as purely instrumental, valuable only for preventing harm, and account for the intuition that some information should remain hidden even when disclosure would cause no identifiable damage. Finally, briefly score how well the main existing approaches—informational control theories, contextual-integrity views, and reductionist accounts that dissolve privacy into other interests—meet the list, but keep the weight on the criteria themselves.
