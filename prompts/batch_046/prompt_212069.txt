You are an expert in medieval Islamic philosophy and philosophy of mind, with particular attention to metaphysical arguments about self-knowledge and substance. In my graduate study group on pre-modern metaphysics, two colleagues—Maya and Leo—became locked in a dispute about what Avicenna’s flying-man thought experiment actually establishes. Maya argued that the scenario proves the soul is a substance ontologically independent of the body. Her reasoning was that if a human being is created suspended in air, with no sensory contact whatsoever and no memory of prior experience, that person would still affirm “I exist.” Because this self-affirmation does not depend on sight, touch, imagination, or any other bodily faculty, the soul’s essential act of self-awareness must be entirely separable from corporeal operations; therefore, the soul can subsist as a complete substance without the body. Leo responded that the argument demonstrates only epistemic independence, not ontological separation. He pointed out that in Avicenna’s own hylomorphic framework, the soul is the form of the body, and the flying man merely isolates one proper operation—pre-reflective self-awareness—from those operations that do require bodily organs. Leo added that the flying man is deliberately stripped of all sensory and imaginative content, which means the soul is not shown functioning as a full substance with its own cognitive repertoire, but rather in an impoverished, isolated state; this undermines, rather than supports, the claim that the soul is a self-sufficient substance. Maya countered that Leo was begging the question by importing hylomorphism into the interpretation, since Avicenna introduces the argument precisely in contexts concerned with establishing the soul’s substantiality and immateriality. She also claimed that the affirmation “I exist” implicitly includes awareness of being a particular, unified subject, not mere bare existence, which requires a substantive bearer—namely, the soul itself. Leo then replied that even granting immateriality, one cannot validly infer separate substantial existence without an additional premise that Avicenna never supplies in this argument; he noted that later scholastic readers, including some within the Aristotelian tradition, criticized exactly this slide from “not dependent on body for self-knowledge” to “capable of existing apart as a complete substance.” Maya finished by saying that Leo was treating the absence of other cognitive acts as evidence of poverty, when it is actually methodological bracketing: the argument isolates the soul’s essential relation to itself precisely to show that this relation belongs to the soul alone, not to any bodily compound. They turned to me and asked me to referee, but I could not decide where the reasoning actually breaks down.

Please adjudicate their exchange move by move: evaluate the strength of Maya’s initial inference, Leo’s hylomorphic and “impoverished state” objections, Maya’s charge of question-begging and her claim about implicit particularity, Leo’s accusation of a non-sequitur regarding substantial existence, and Maya’s final defense about methodological bracketing. Say clearly who is right about each specific point, identify what both parties are missing—perhaps about the distinction between immateriality and separability, or about whether Avicenna intends the argument as a proof of substance or merely of a unique mode of self-presence—and conclude with an overall verdict on who is more correct, and why. Do not provide bibliographies or reading lists; just analyze the arguments as presented.
