The exam question was: Type-identity theory and functionalism offer competing accounts of how mental states relate to their physical bases. First, explain why the possibility of a Martian experiencing pain through silicon-based chemistry poses a specific challenge to the type-identity theorist who identifies pain with C-fiber firing. Second, explain how functionalism uses causal-role specification to accommodate this case of multiple realizability. Third, assess whether functionalism's solution genuinely preserves mental causation or merely relocates causal efficacy to the physical substrate, leaving the functional property epiphenomenal. In your assessment, distinguish carefully between Kim's causal exclusion argument and the more general worry that a multiply realizable property cannot itself be a cause.

The student's answer reads as follows. The type-identity theorist, following U.T. Place and J.J.C. Smart, holds that mental types are strictly identical to specific neurobiological types—pain is identical to C-fiber firing. If a Martian feels pain without C-fibers, perhaps via silicon electrochemistry, then one mental type is realized by two distinct physical types, which violates strict identity. The type-identity theorist must therefore deny that Martians feel pain or abandon the identity thesis. Functionalism avoids this by defining mental states solely through their causal-functional roles—their characteristic inputs, outputs, and internal relations—so that pain is whatever occupies the pain-role, regardless of substrate. This accommodates Martian pain naturally. However, this strategy introduces a causal difficulty. If every particular causal interaction is executed by the physical realizer—the silicon pathway, for instance—then the functional property itself appears to do no work. The mental state becomes like software running on hardware: descriptive of the organizational pattern but not an independent causal contributor. This suggests functionalism relocates all genuine causation downward, leaving mental properties epiphenomenal. The functionalist might reply that mental properties are second-order properties—properties of having properties that play the relevant causal roles—which allows them to organize and constrain their realizers without being reducible to them. By treating mental states as causally efficacious in virtue of being realized, non-reductive physicalism preserves downward causation. Yet this reply seems circular: it asserts that the functional organization causes behavior, but the organization is nothing over and above the organized physical processes. Consequently, functionalism resolves the problem of multiple realizability only by generating an equally serious problem about whether mental kinds can be genuine causes.
