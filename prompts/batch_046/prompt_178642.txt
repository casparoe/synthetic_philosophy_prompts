As a novelist presently drafting a work on the architecture of moral obligation, I have become preoccupied with supererogation—that elusive boundary where an action is laudable without being strictly required. I am asking you to compose a short story, rendered entirely as continuous prose without headings, bullet points, bold text, tables, or any other structural ornament, in the manner of a formal letter, that takes place during a single twilight conversation inside a glass observatory where an aging philosopher and her former student are cataloguing books salvaged from a fire that gutted part of a university ethics library.

Among the charred volumes they uncover one intact manuscript page that proposes an unfamiliar taxonomy: rather than dividing acts into the obligatory and the supererogatory, the anonymous author distinguishes obligatory acts, genuinely supererogatory acts, and a curious intermediate category this text calls counter-obligatory benevolence—deeds that remain good in themselves but grow morally questionable when repeated too often, as though praise were a finite currency that could be overdrawn across a lifetime. I want the two characters to debate this invented scheme without invoking any specific named philosopher or reproducing verbatim quotations from actual texts; instead, let their argument turn on a concrete scenario—whether saving a stranger from a collapsing structure remains praiseworthy if the rescuer has already performed two equally dangerous rescues that same month, and whether a society that quietly expects such rescues has transformed supererogation into a hidden duty. Please keep the narrative and the conceptual inquiry tightly intertwined, avoiding any simple moral lesson, so that the story itself asks whether the very notion of going beyond the call of duty can survive once we treat moral admiration as a resource to be rationed, and write in polished, formal language that allows the philosophical tension to emerge through setting and dialogue rather than through exposition alone.
