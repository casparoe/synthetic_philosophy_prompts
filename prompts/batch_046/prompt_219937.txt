I'm currently working through a problem in a graduate seminar on collective responsibility, and a late-night discussion with friends has left me wanting to sharpen the framing. We've been examining the standard inefficacy objection: in large-scale collective harms like climate change or industrial animal agriculture, any single individual's contribution is causally negligible, so no particular act can be said to cause or prevent the damage. The usual literature treats carbon emissions and meat consumption as parallel cases of overdetermined physical harm, but I want to extend the analysis across three domains simultaneously—personal carbon footprints, industrial meat consumption, and voting in mass democratic elections—while introducing a distinction that hasn't been central to the debate. Specifically, I distinguish between "aggregative harm," where damage is a physical sum of discrete acts (greenhouse gases, animal suffering), and "procedural harm," where the collective outcome is constituted by an aggregation rule rather than by physical accumulation (electoral results).

If the inefficacy objection holds with equal force in all three cases, then it seems to undermine not only personal obligations to reduce emissions or avoid meat, but also the moral significance of democratic participation itself, since a single vote almost never determines an election. Yet I suspect an asymmetry matters for moral reasoning: in aggregative harms, the individual act is physically redundant but still part of a causal chain, whereas in procedural harms the individual act is definitionally subsumed into a counting mechanism that only produces an outcome at the collective level. What are the most important philosophical implications of recognizing this asymmetry? Does it rescue voting from the inefficacy objection in a way that doesn't apply to climate or meat choices, or does it instead reveal that the objection is more corrosive—suggesting that any moral framework relying on individual causal efficacy collapses once we move from physical to institutional collective action?

My friends also raised a further worry. Even if we concede causal inefficacy across these domains, we might preserve moral reasons through "expressive" or "symbolic" value—voting expresses civic identity, plant-based eating expresses solidarity with suffering beings. But if we treat these expressive values as sufficient to ground obligation, have we effectively conceded that the original causal-inefficacy argument was never the real basis for our moral judgments? I want to understand whether this move genuinely saves our practices, or whether it quietly abandons the causal framework that made the objection seem compelling in the first place, and what that abandonment implies for how we should structure moral education and public deliberation about collective harm.
