I've been trying to work out what moral uncertainty actually demands by checking my gut reactions to concrete choices, and I'm hoping you can help me see whether they point to a unified decision rule or whether some judgments have to be revised. Here are my verdicts.

When I'm deciding whether to volunteer for a clinical trial that carries a small personal risk but might advance a treatment I think is probably beneficial, and I'm genuinely unsure whether the moral value of contributing to science outweighs my duty of self-preservation, I think I should participate if my best guess is that the contribution is worthwhile—I treat the uncertainty as something managed by acting on my best judgment, not by paralysis.

But when I'm allocating a fixed donation between two famine-relief organizations, and I'm about sixty-five percent confident that Organization A saves more lives per dollar but thirty-five percent confident that Organization B does, I don't think I should split my gift in a sixty-five/thirty-five ratio. I think I should give the full amount to A. The divided confidence feels like it belongs to my belief state, not to the moral reasons themselves.

There's a different feeling when the uncertainty runs between whole moral frameworks rather than within one. If I'm torn between thinking that lying to a dying patient about their estranged child is forbidden because deception violates dignity, and thinking that it's permitted because comfort matters more, I don't feel I should construct a weighted average—say, telling part of the truth—based on how plausible each framework seems to me. I feel I should commit to one view or the other, or at least not treat the frameworks as probabilities to be summed.

Yet there's a case that pulls the other way. Suppose I'm a public-health official deciding whether to recommend a vaccine that I believe is fifty-five percent likely to be net beneficial and forty-five percent likely to cause serious unforeseen harm. Here, despite the uncertainty being about outcomes within a single framework, I feel I should recommend it only if I'm substantially more confident—perhaps eighty percent—not at fifty-five percent. So my threshold seems to shift depending on the stakes or the kind of risk.

Finally, there's the case I keep revisiting: I'm a juror deciding whether to vote guilty when I think there's a seventy percent chance the defendant committed the crime, but I'm also uncertain about whether the legal standard should be "beyond reasonable doubt" interpreted as ninety-five percent certainty or as "moral certainty" closer to ninety percent. Should my vote depend on resolving that meta-uncertainty about the standard itself first?

Can you articulate one principle, or a small family of competing principles, that delivers my first three verdicts precisely—specifying what feature of each case explains why I should or shouldn't split, weight, or commit—and then apply it to both the vaccine case and the juror case? If no single principle captures all four, please say explicitly which verdict I'd need to revise and why. Also, please predict what your principle says about a situation I haven't listed: deciding whether to have a child when I'm morally uncertain about whether future people have rights that constrain present reproductive choices.
