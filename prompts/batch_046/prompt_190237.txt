Exam question (reproduced for reference):
Reconstruct the argument from illusion, focusing on the common-factor premise. Then explain the disjunctivist response, distinguishing carefully between (a) the metaphysical thesis that hallucination and veridical perception are different kinds of mental state, and (b) the epistemic thesis that they are distinguishable by the subject. Which of these distinctions does the disjunctivist actually need?

Student answer:
The argument from illusion starts with cases like seeing a bent stick in water. Because the stick is not bent, the property of being bent must belong to what I immediately experience, not to the stick itself. So I am directly aware of a mental representation—a sense-datum or internal image—that bears that property. The same structure applies to hallucination: if I seem to see a pink elephant that is not there, the property of being pink and elephant-shaped belongs to my experience, not to anything in the world. Because a perfect hallucination can be subjectively indistinguishable from a real perception, the mental representation present in hallucination must also be present in veridical perception. That shared representation is the common factor.

Disjunctivism denies this. In real perception I am not related to an internal representation; I am directly aware of the stick or the elephant themselves. In hallucination there is no such object, so the mental state is of an altogether different kind—perhaps a bare brain state with no object of awareness. Therefore disjunctivists reject the idea that the two experiences share subjective character: if one attends carefully, the direct presence of the external object gives genuine perception a qualitative richness that hallucination necessarily lacks. Once subjective indistinguishability is denied, the common-factor premise collapses, and with it the motivation for representationalism.

Moreover, if there is no common mental factor, then neuroscience cannot use the same explanatory model for both cases. Since the mental states are metaphysically different, they must be implemented by different neural mechanisms; a unified brain-based theory of perception and hallucination is therefore impossible.
