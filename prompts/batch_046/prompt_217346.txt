There's this practice cities have settled on for old statues and street names tied to figures who did serious harm — they wont tear the monument down or rename the road, but they install empty plinths around the statue for the groups harmed, and they put up a second street sign with the corrected name in bold black while the old name stays visible behind it in faded gray, with some bureaucratic rule that the original statue has to remain taller and physically centered. What theory of public memory or collective identity does that layout presuppose? Does it assume harm can be spatially quarantined by adjacency, or that a community needs the original artifact intact for narrative continuity? Is there an implicit view that moral taint is representational rather than intrinsic — so adding counter-images cancels it out? And is the practice philosophically consistent, since we keep the name legible but struck-through, wanting it both present and erased? Reconstruct the implicit theory of commemoration here, say whether the practice strictly needs it or could be licensed by rival views, and tell me what a policy that actually centered the harm rather than the original monument would have to change structurally — not just more plaques, but the spatial and symbolic logic itself.
