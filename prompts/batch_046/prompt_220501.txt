As a novelist who has begun auditing an epistemology course to understand how readers come to believe what my characters tell them, I found myself caught in a disagreement last week at a café with two other writers that I have been unable to resolve on my own. One of them, a journalist who writes long-form profiles, defended a strict reductionist account of testimony: he maintained that whenever we accept what someone tells us, we are ultimately relying on our own perceptual cues about the speaker's reliability and on our background reasoning, so testimony never functions as an independent source of knowledge but merely serves as an efficient transmission mechanism for inference and observation. The second, a poet who publishes narrative nonfiction, rejected this and argued for anti-reductionism: she claimed that testimony introduces a genuinely social dimension in which the speaker offers a kind of epistemic guarantee, and the hearer accepts the content on the basis of that guarantee rather than on any independent check. The conversation then turned to fiction, which is my own territory, and she proposed what she called analogical testimony: when a novelist writes that grief feels like a house in which every room remains illuminated long after the inhabitants have left, she is not merely expressing a private image but vouching for a structural claim about other people's emotional experience, inviting readers to treat the analogy as knowledge they could not have reached through introspection alone. The journalist responded that this is still reducible, because a reader only accepts such an analogy if it resonates with their own prior experience or with patterns they can independently recognize; the author's vouching adds nothing epistemically, since if the analogy fails to match the reader's internal framework, no amount of authorial authority transforms it into knowledge. The poet replied that this misses the point of vouching precisely in cases of analogical extension: the reader may lack direct experience of the specific grief being described, yet the author's testimonial stance licenses them to project the analogy onto unfamiliar territory, something mere private reasoning could not authorize. They turned to me to adjudicate, and I found myself genuinely stuck.

Could you walk through their exchange step by step, assessing which moves are sound and which collapse distinctions they should preserve? In particular, I want to know whether analogical testimony in literary fiction can be irreducibly testimonial without being literally assertoric, or whether the reductionist is right that every such case ultimately dissolves into the reader's own inferential and experiential resources. Please also say what both sides are missing about the role of narrative framing in mediating this kind of epistemic exchange, and indicate who is more right overall without reducing the verdict to a simple tie.
