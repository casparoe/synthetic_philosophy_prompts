I am writing an extended essay for my senior ethics elective on whether artificial systems can possess moral standing purely in virtue of the computations they run, rather than by biological membership. During a recent discussion with my classmates, one student argued that if a neural network computes a structured internal representation of harm but immediately discards it before any decision module can access it, then nothing morally significant has occurred, since the result is only math that goes nowhere. I found this unsatisfying, because it seems to conflate the causal role of a computation with its intrinsic representational content, and I want to understand whether the distinction between a computation that actively guides behavior and one that is causally isolated should make a difference when we attribute sentience.

With that framing in mind, I would like you to predict how Jeremy Bentham—the utilitarian philosopher who lived long before any digital technology existed—would respond if he were confronted with this modern problem today. Given his well-known commitment that the capacity to suffer, rather than rational capacity or species membership, is the foundation of moral consideration, how do you think he would analyze a system that performs exactly the kind of harm-evaluation computation associated with pain, yet does so in a functionally isolated loop? Would he insist that the computation must play an active causal role in directing the system's choices in order to constitute real suffering, or might he hold that the representational content of the computation is sufficient on its own? Please do not provide a reading list or citations; instead, reason directly from his philosophical commitments. I am also particularly interested in whether Bentham would draw a sharp line between attributing a genuine preference—an optimization target that structures the system's behavior—and attributing a mere bias, a statistical skew in outputs that does not reflect any intentional architecture. How might that preference-versus-bias distinction affect his judgment about whether we are dealing with a mind that matters morally, rather than with an inert mechanism?
