Consider the following argument, which attempts to show that epistemicism about vagueness collapses into supervaluationism and thereby undermines its own motivation.

(1) Epistemicism holds that every vague predicate has a precise, sharp extension; our hesitation near the margins is explained solely by our ignorance of where the cutoff lies.

(2) If "bald" has a sharp boundary, then so must "definitely bald," "definitely definitely bald," and so on, generating an infinite hierarchy of meta-predicates, each with its own sharp but unknown cutoff.

(3) At every level of this hierarchy, the speaker remains ignorant of the exact boundary.

(4) Supervaluationism explains the same hesitation not by hidden facts we fail to know, but by semantic indeterminacy: there simply is no fact of the matter about the boundary.

(5) Now suppose an epistemicist asserts that there is a sharp line between bald and not-bald. If asked whether it falls at n hairs or n+1, she must confess ignorance; and she must confess parallel ignorance about the boundary for "definitely bald," and so on upward.

(6) Consequently, for any practical or discursive purpose, the epistemicist can never appeal to a determinate fact that distinguishes one marginal case from the next.

(7) Therefore the epistemicist's postulated "sharp boundary" does no explanatory work that differs from the supervaluationist's "absence of boundary": both leave every borderline case without an accessible determinable truth-value, at every order.

(8) So epistemicism proves too much. It was introduced to preserve classical bivalence and reject semantic indeterminacy, yet if (1)-(7) hold it reproduces exactly the practical indeterminacy it sought to avoid, rendering the distinction between unknown fact and missing fact empty.

Which premise or inferential step should be rejected? Explain why that step initially looks compelling, and then price the alternatives. In particular, if the error lies in (2) or (3), explain how the epistemicist can block the infinite hierarchy without conceding that vagueness is semantic rather than epistemic. If the error lies in (6) or (7), defend the claim that metaphysical ignorance is genuinely different from ontological indeterminacy even when both are practically inaccessible, and show why that distinction does not collapse. If you reject (1), state whether you are adopting supervaluationism, a degree-theoretic view, or something else, and address whether your preferred position can resolve the sorites paradox without implicitly reintroducing sharp lines at some hidden level.
