I've been messing with philosophy since I retired from the chemistry lab, and lately I've been stuck on these dispostional properties — fragility, solubility, that kind of thing — and all the talk about finks and masks (some guy named Martin wrote about it?). Anyway my study group came up with this argument that seems to kill dispositions entirely, and I can't tell if it's clever or just broken.

(1) If O genuinely has disposition D to φ in conditions C, then whenever O is in C and nothing interferring happens, O φ's.
(2) Take any case, like a sugar cube being soluble. For every proposed D we can always imagine a 'double-fink' gadget: there's a hidden chemical mask M inside O that's disposed to block φ exactly when C occurs, but M is itself kept dormant by a second fink F unless some far-away trigger R happens.
(3) So in reality, for any object and any proposed power, either that double-fink setup is actually present (just hidden/inactive right now) or it's completely absent.
(4) If it's present, then O doesn't really have D — there's an interfering mechanism built right in, even if it's masked at the moment.
(5) If it's absent, then O's φ-ing in C is just a lucky accident of there being no blocker; there's no intrinsic power, only the hapenstance of an empty environment.
(6) So either O lacks D, or O only behaves like it has D by pure coincidence.
(7) Therefore nothing ever genuinely has a disposition; it's all categorical bases plus accidental surroundings.

That can't be right — I talk about reactive properties every day in chemisty! — but each step looks okay. Where does it go wrong? I want to keep (1) if possible. Is (4) wrong because a dormant mask doesn't defeat the disposition? Or is (5) wrong because the absence of interference is exactly part of what the conditions C include? And is this double-fink idea even a fair extension, or did we just smuggle in something new?
