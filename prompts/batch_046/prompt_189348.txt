Next Thursday I defend my thesis in a philosophy-of-mind workshop at a research university. My position is that eliminative materialism about beliefs and desires is correct, but not for the familiar reason that folk psychology is a stagnant, predictively failing theory. Rather, I argue that belief-desire explanation requires what I call normative entrenchment: mental states must have correctness conditions that constrain behavior independently of underlying causal dynamics. Once cognition is reconstructed within a hierarchical predictive-processing framework—where the only ontologically basic items are precision-weighted prediction errors and active-inference policies—there is no room for states that are normatively entrenched in this way. Beliefs and desires should therefore be eliminated, though belief-desire talk may survive as a macro-level heuristic describing the dynamics of active inference.

The audience is precisely this: a philosopher of neuroscience who regards eliminativism as empirically premature because neuroscience remains incomplete; a cognitive scientist who already treats belief-desire vocabulary as a high-level gloss on precision-weighted dynamics and will ask why I need elimination rather than reduction; a philosopher of language who will press me on whether removing beliefs collapses the conditions for semantic content altogether; two graduate students familiar with Churchland but not with predictive-processing mechanics, who will ask whether this is just Dennett-style instrumentalism dressed in neuroscientific language; and a senior enactivist who will argue that predictive processing already saves intentionality without requiring elimination.

Prepare me for the objections. Order them by how likely and how damaging they are. For each, phrase it as that specific interlocutor would phrase it, then indicate what I should concede immediately, what I must hold firm on, and where my reply is structurally weakest. Include the objection I am hoping nobody asks—the one that targets the gap between my claim that predictive processing replaces normative entrenchment and my concession that belief-desire talk survives heuristically—and specify exactly how to answer it without sounding defensive. Finally, identify the weakest point in my own argument that I should prepare to defend openly rather than hope they overlook it.
