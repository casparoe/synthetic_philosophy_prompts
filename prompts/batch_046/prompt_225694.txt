I serve on my university’s Committee on Institutional Naming and Historical Accountability, and we must issue a formal recommendation—before our March session—regarding the “Winston Churchill Center for Strategic Studies,” a research hub named under a legacy bequest now contested by students and donors alike. The dispute has become philosophical rather than merely administrative: defenders invoke the “man of his time” defence, arguing that Churchill’s imperial commitments and his government’s role in the Bengal famine must be assessed against the moral-epistemic limits available in the 1940s; critics reply that present institutional honor is not a historical verdict but a synchronic act of endorsement, and that the defence illegitimately transfers excusing conditions for an agent onto justifying conditions for an institution that continues to valorize him. To avoid the usual culture-war script, I want the committee to reason through a specific conceptual distinction I have been developing in my work on retrospective evaluation—between diachronic moral benchmarking (whether an agent violated norms they could reasonably have been expected to know and act upon) and synchronic institutional complicity (whether a present institution, knowing what it now knows, participates in unredressed symbolic allegiance to harms that remain structurally alive). Apply this framework to Churchill specifically—treating both his wartime leadership and his colonial record seriously—while also indicating how the same reasoning would bear on parallel cases involving Jefferson and Columbus, since the committee insists on consistency across named spaces rather than ad hoc exception-making.

The memo should not read as public-relations management or as a historical brief, but as an actionable institutional opinion that researchers, administrators, and dissenting committee members could actually respond to with reasons rather than with slogans. It must engage the strongest version of the retentionist position—namely, that removing or altering the name imposes an anachronistic moral framework that flattens historical complexity and punishes achievement for failures that were widely shared—rather than treating it as a straw figure. At the same time, it should address the critics’ central point that institutions do not “judge history” in the abstract when they maintain a name; they make a present-tense allocation of honor, resources, and visibility, and that allocation can be complicity regardless of the agent’s temporal location.

Please draft the committee memo with the following sections, in this order and with approximate lengths: Executive Summary (about 150 words); The Conceptual Framework: Benchmarking versus Complicity (about 250 words); Application to the Churchill Center—The Defence and Its Limits (about 350 words); The Strongest Case for Retention Without Change (about 200 words); Recommendation and Concrete Conditions (about 200 words). The recommendation must be a single concrete course—retain the name with mandatory contextualization, rename with a transitional timeline, or suspend naming pending a structured review process—with specific conditions and a time frame, and the reasoning throughout should remain philosophical and institutional rather than biographical or polemical. Do not include a bibliography, citation list, or suggested reading.
