I'm drafting this novel about a farming collective in the high desert where they've inherited this weird, unwritten agreement with the neighboring valley—basically a seasonal rotation pact that forces them to leave half the fields fallow in winter, which everyone thinks is just old superstition. One of my protagonists wants to tear it up and plant year-round with new drip irrigation, but another character keeps bringing up Chesterton's fence, and I started wondering about Burke's whole thing with society being a partnership across generations. I need to understand the most important implications of Burkean conservatism and Chesterton's fence argument for a case like this, where the fence is an informal agricultural practice with no documanted origin, but where modern soil science might actually back up the rotation (though my characters don't have acess to that research). How does the epistemic humility argument work when nobody can point to the original reason, and does Burke's idea that we're partners with the dead and unborn add any moral weight beyond just we don't know enough? Also, I'm curious about the flip side—if tearing down the fence leads to immediate profit but collapses the watershed in thirty years, is that a failure of Burkean reasoning or a failure of not being Burkean enough? I want the answer to be precise about which parts of the tradition are actually doing philosophical work, not just generic respect the past stuff, and I'd like it to mention whether there's any coherent line between legitimate institutional memory and just clinging to outdated customs for nostalgia's sake.
