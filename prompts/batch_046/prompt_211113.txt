I'm a trustee of a small mutual-aid housing collective. We are deciding between two structural repair contracts. Plan X removes a critical safety hazard for ninety-nine residents, but it permanently disables the ventilation in the sole flat occupied by the trustee who proposed Plan Y. Plan Y reduces the hazard for fifty residents and harms none. One colleague argues that rejecting Plan X is straightforward "scope neglect"—a cognitive bias that overweights a single, concrete loss against a diffuse, larger gain. He maintains that our charter is essentially a welfare-maximisation agreement, so summing outcomes across members is precisely what rational governance demands.

I feel there's a structural mistake in treating our obligations to each member as fungible units that can be traded off in aggregate. Our agreement binds us to every resident individually, not to a collective utility function. I'm drawn to the idea that we should ask whether each affected person—especially the resident whose home would be ruined—could reasonably reject the plan from their own standpoint, rather than comparing the two options globally. But I worry I may simply be rationalising an aversion to concentrated harm, dressing up ordinary risk aversion in philosophical language. How ought I to evaluate this disagreement without either ignoring the scale of the benefit or reducing our members to entries in a calculus?
