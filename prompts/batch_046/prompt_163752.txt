After a recent disagreement with my dissertation advisor about whether democratic legitimacy requires actual consent or only counterfactual endorsement, I have become convinced that my graduate students need to confront idealized preference aggregation as a live philosophical problem rather than as a familiar slogan from alignment discourse. I am revising the final exam for my upper-level seminar on collective rationality and want a single essay question—answerable in about seventy-five minutes and roughly six hundred to seven hundred words—that forces students to think through coherent extrapolated volition from first principles.

To keep the question from being answered by reciting standard critiques of CEV, I am injecting a modified construct: call it counterfactual coherent volition, or CCV, defined as the limit point of an agent’s preference-revision procedure under three joint conditions—first, that the agent knows all of her current empirical beliefs about the consequences of option X are false; second, that she retains her underlying normative architecture (her higher-order values and reflective-equilibrium procedures); and third, that she revises her first-order preferences solely through coherence with that retained architecture rather than through direct desire-fulfillment. The exam question should ask students to evaluate whether disagreement between two agents about what their respective CCVs entail should be interpreted as an epistemic disagreement about a single correct extrapolation, or as a form of deep value pluralism that no coherence procedure can resolve, and—crucially—whether this distinction undermines CCV’s viability as a normative basis for collective decision-making.

Please provide the exam question itself, a model answer of approximately six hundred to seven hundred words, and a marking scheme that separates an A answer from a B answer by identifying the specific philosophical moves each must contain, especially how the student handles the tension between retaining normative architecture and allowing empirical correction to reshape first-order preferences, and whether they recognize that the epistemic-versus-pluralist distinction itself depends on a prior commitment about the nature of practical reason. Write everything in American English, do not include bibliographies or citations, and design the difficulty so that it comes from identifying the conceptual crux rather than from summarizing a well-known literature.
