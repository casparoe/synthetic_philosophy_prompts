I am a graduate student in comparative constitutional theory, currently enrolled in a seminar on federalism and multi-level governance spanning the US, Germany, Switzerland, and the EU. I sit as the student representative on my university's Cross-Jurisdictional Governance Panel, and I need a formal written opinion—recommending approval, rejection, or approval with conditions—to circulate before Thursday's meeting. The administration proposes dissolving our three campus-level social-science research ethics boards and replacing them with a single Central Research Integrity Office that reviews all protocols involving cross-campus data sharing. To address fears of over-centralization, the proposal includes a burden-shifting mechanism that inverts the usual logic of subsidiarity: instead of the center justifying its intervention, each campus board must supply quantitative evidence—specifically, documented participant-harm incidents and unresolved appeals exceeding defined thresholds—to prove its own incompetence and retain jurisdiction. If it cannot, authority defaults upward. The drafters claim this draws together US federal-preemption reasoning, the German Länder model of concurrent powers, Swiss enumerated-competence practice, and EU subsidiarity protocol, yet applies them to non-territorial functional bodies—epistemic communities within a private university—rather than to sovereign territorial units, with local autonomy treated as the exception rather than the default.

Please draft the panel's decision memo with a concrete recommendation and an implementation timeframe. The reasoning should engage seriously with the strongest consideration on the losing side—whether that is regulatory fragmentation and inconsistent protection of participants, or the hollowing out of local accountability through an evidentiary burden that campus boards may lack the resources to meet. The memo should also address the philosophical core: whether subsidiarity operates here as a procedural principle about proximity of decision-making or as a substantive claim about the intrinsic value of local judgment, particularly given that these bodies lack territorial sovereignty and democratic mandate.

One important caveat: if my framing confuses subsidiarity with mere administrative efficiency, or if applying federal and cantonal logic to a private university's internal governance is conceptually incoherent—especially if the reverse-burden mechanism collapses local autonomy into a procedural hurdle with no normative bite—please say so plainly rather than drafting a polished document that papers over the confusion. I want the reasoning to distinguish objections that would be decisive against the recommendation from those that are merely awkward, and to state explicitly what the recommended position risks and what its defenders ought to concede.
