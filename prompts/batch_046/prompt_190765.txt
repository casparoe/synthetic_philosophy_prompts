Consider an argument that extends the Grand Inquisitor’s diagnosis in Dostoevsky’s The Brothers Karamazov into a normative conclusion about modern institutional design.

(1) The Inquisitor claims that human beings are constitutionally unable to bear the burden of freedom; given the choice, they will exchange liberty for security, certainty, and authoritative guidance.

(2) If a population systematically prefers arrangements that undermine its own long-term flourishing, then a benevolent authority that overrides that preference is not violating autonomy but correcting a cognitive or volitional defect.

(3) Contemporary behavioral economics and predictive institutional design have repeatedly shown that populations favor immediate security and calculable welfare over the risks and responsibilities of autonomy, confirming the Inquisitor’s descriptive claim in modern democratic and algorithmic governance contexts alike.

(4) Therefore, any institution—whether ecclesiastical, bureaucratic, or data-driven—that possesses sufficient predictive capacity and benevolent intent has not merely permission but a positive moral duty to restrict freedom in exchange for guaranteed welfare.

(5) Within this framework, resistance to such paternalistic restriction cannot be treated as legitimate dissent; it must be interpreted as a symptom of the very incapacity for freedom that justifies intervention in the first place.

(6) Consequently, accepting the Inquisitor’s psychological insight entails that authoritarian paternalism is morally obligatory wherever it can be reliably implemented.

The conclusion proves too much: it appears to justify pervasive coercive benevolence across welfare states, public-health regimes, and AI-aligned governance systems, leaving no protected sphere for autonomous refusal.

I wish to preserve both the Inquisitor’s psychological insight in (1) and its modern empirical confirmation in (3), but I reject (6). Identify the weakest step in the chain—most likely somewhere between (2) and (5)—and explain precisely why it fails. In particular, address whether there is a hidden equivocation between descriptive weakness for freedom and normative forfeiture of the right to it, and specify what rejecting your chosen step costs for anyone who still takes the Inquisitor’s diagnosis seriously. If the flaw is instead in (3), explain why the modern evidence does not license the normative move the argument requires.
