Please read the following outline for a short philosophical essay on population axiology and provide detailed critical comments. I am exploring whether a particular modification of welfare aggregation can avoid the standard repugnant conclusion without simply collapsing into average utilitarianism or a lexical priority rule.

The outline introduces a distinction between scalar welfare and what it terms "dimensionally clustered welfare." Rather than treating welfare as a single homogeneous quantity that can be summed across lives, the essay proposes that welfare is structured across at least three non-substitutable dimensions: hedonic quality, eudaimonic flourishing, and relational embeddedness. Its central normative claim is that aggregation should be governed by a "coherence constraint": a population is only axiomatically comparable to another if the lives within it each meet a minimal threshold across all relevant dimensions, so that extreme surplus in one dimension cannot compensate for the complete absence of others.

The outline then constructs a modified repugnant scenario. Population A consists of ten individuals with high, balanced welfare across all three dimensions. Population B consists of ten thousand individuals who possess extraordinarily high hedonic welfare but entirely lack eudaimonic and relational capacities—not through deprivation, but through a hypothetical constitution that renders those dimensions inapplicable to them. On a summed scalar measure, B vastly exceeds A. Yet under the coherence constraint, B fails to constitute a valid comparative population, because its members do not occupy the full dimensional space required for aggregation against A.

The essay asks whether this constraint genuinely blocks the repugnant conclusion or merely disguises an average-utilitarian intuition behind formal machinery. It further asks whether the hypothetical constitution of B's members smuggles in a hidden person-affecting premise—namely, that these individuals are not the right kind of subjects to count in the comparison—and whether that undermines the argument's neutrality.
