I am delivering the opening remarks at a one-day interdisciplinary workshop convened to mark the tercentenary of George Berkeley's The Querist. The audience is divided evenly between historians of philosophy and Berkeley scholars, who know the text's queries intimately, and economists and policymakers with no prior exposure to it. I want the address — roughly eight minutes — to treat Berkeley's monetary philosophy not as historical colour but as a structural constraint on what can be said. Specifically, the speech should turn on his argument that money functions legitimately only as a representational sign tied to real productive labour or land, and it should use that claim to show why praising decentralised digital currencies without such backing forces one either to abandon Berkeley's logic or to smuggle in a metaphysics of value he rejects. The philosophical point must shape the arc of the address: determine what praise is available, what comparisons to paper credit remain open, and where the speaker must fall silent rather than overreach. It should land for the specialists without losing the non-specialists, and it should close by articulating one concrete conceptual implication — not a policy recommendation, but an institutional constraint — that follows directly from the Querist's view of money. Please write in British English, do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and avoid generic biographical remarks about Berkeley.
