I am composing a novel in which a central character, now in her seventies, must decide whether to honor an advance directive she signed at thirty—before she received an implanted neural augmentation that, over four decades, gradually rewired her evaluative framework. For the chapter's epigraph, I require a sonnet that treats the authority of that earlier self as a genuine philosophical tension rather than a legal formality. Structure the poem so that the octave presents the directive as an act of enduring fidelity—perhaps through images of sealed ink, an unbroken signature, or a voice preserved intact—establishing the conventional view of precedent autonomy. At the volta, line nine, introduce the complication: the augmentation has not merely aged her but transformed her evaluative architecture, so that the self who wrote the document and the self who must execute it are separated not only by time but by a kind of cognitive discontinuity. The sestet should then ask whether honoring the directive constitutes fidelity to a prior person or coercion of the present one, leaving the question open. Use a regular rhyme scheme—ABAB CDCD EFEF GG—with the turn audible in syntax and mood, shifting perhaps from declarative certainty to interrogative suspension. The philosophical content should be precise enough that a reader familiar with bioethics recognizes the conflict between precedent autonomy and present autonomy, particularly as it applies to long-term cognitive enhancement rather than standard dementia. Please write this sonnet.
