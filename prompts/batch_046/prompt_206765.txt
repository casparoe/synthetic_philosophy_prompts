heres what they wrote:

The inefficacy objection says that in big collective problems one persons action is too small to make a difference. For climate change, my flight emits CO2 but the climate system is so huge that my specific tons dont change anything — its overdetermined by millions of other emitters. So I cant be blamed for a specific harm.

With meat, its different because theres a supply chain. If I dont buy a burger, the store might order one less patty next week, but often the surplus just gets dumped or exported, so theres a rebound where my refusal actually shifts waste rather than reducing suffering. This is the shadow supply problem.

For voting, its clearly threshold-based: unless Im the tie-breaking vote, my ballot doesnt change the outcome. So the inefficacy objection is strongest here.

Now, the reply "if everyone did it, it would be disastrous" doesnt fully solve this because it just shows the collective pattern is bad, not that my specific contribution is wrong. But actually, in the climate case, since harm is linear and additive, everyone doing it is literally just the sum of each flight, so the reply works better there. In voting, the reply fails because elections arent summed like that — theyre decided by a cutoff.

However, theres also structural complicity. Even when my action doesnt cause harm directly, I participate in a system that requires mass participation to function. Parfit argued that in collective harm cases we should focus on whether our act is part of a harmful collective practice, regardless of causal efficacy. So I think individual responsibility survives mainly through this structural layer, not through direct causation.

Wait, but in meat consumption, because of the shadow supply rebound, reducing demand can actually increase waste in secondary markets, which means my individual boycott might have negative side effects. So the structural view might actually excuse me here, because opting out disrupts the system in unpredictable ways.
