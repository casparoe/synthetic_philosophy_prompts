Read Charlie Chaplin's Modern Times (1936) through Richard Sennett's idea of the intelligence of the hand—the claim that craft knowledge is embodied in rhythmic, tactile engagement rather than in explicit rules. Rather than treating the factory scenes as standard Marxist alienation, ask whether Sennett's framework fits better: does the Tramp's compulsive bolt-tightening on the conveyor belt show the destruction of embodied skill, or does it parody the illusion that such skill ever existed? Consider the feeding-machine scene and the dance with the gears in terms of bodily knowledge versus mechanical repetition. Does the final image—the Tramp walking away with the girl—imply that escape from automation restores craft, or does it concede that even free labour requires an embodied mastery the Tramp lacks? Be faithful to both film and philosophy: note where Chaplin resists a Sennettian reading, and say what the film adds to philosophical debate about work and automation that argument alone cannot.
