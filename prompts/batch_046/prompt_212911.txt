I hold the view that cultural values can function as autonomous causal engines in macro-historical explanation, not merely as epiphenomenal glosses on material conditions or institutional constraints. The paradigm is Weber’s thesis about the Protestant ethic: an internalized vocational calling did not just mirror emerging capitalist rationality but actively channeled it, producing outcomes that pure interest-based or structural accounts underdetermine. To make the claim precise, I distinguish normative traction — a selective coupling between a representation of the good and sustained behavioral patterns that propagate across institutional scales — from functional mimicry, in which actors behave as if guided by a value without being causally shaped by it. I want an honest map of what would count against this kind of value-causation.

Specifically, what observations, conceptual distinctions, or formal results would have to hold for the claim to fail? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. For instance, suppose reconstructed archival evidence showed that Calvinist merchants systematically interpreted predestination in ways that actively discouraged long-term capital accumulation, yet accumulation accelerated in those same regions through entirely unrelated credit-network innovations. Would that collapse the thesis, or could it be absorbed by appealing to unintended functional mimicry? More generally, what exactly fixes the boundary between normative traction and functional mimicry, and does expanding the scope of the latter risk insulating the view into unfalsifiability?

Please address the following:
- Which defeaters a committed defender of value-autonomy would concede as genuinely damaging, versus which they would dismiss as missing the mechanism.
- What distinctive predictions this view makes that rival materialist or institutional explanations do not — that is, what it risks that they do not.
- Whether qualification by appeal to unintended consequences or mimicry is a legitimate theoretical refinement or a symptom that the position has been rendered immune to refutation, and whether such immunity is a vice for a claim in historical sociology.
- The conditions under which a value-based explanation becomes redundant rather than false, and whether redundancy is a graver problem for this view than direct falsification.
