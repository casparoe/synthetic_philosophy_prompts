I am preparing a short position paper for an early modern ethics seminar and want a consistency check before I commit to the view in print. Over the course of the term I have arrived at what I take to be a broadly Leibnizian moral framework, but I am unsure whether the pieces actually fit together. Specifically, I hold that moral truths are eternal verities grounded in the divine intellect rather than arbitrarily created, which makes them necessary in the same sense as mathematical relations; I also hold that because God actualizes this world as the best of all possible worlds, the specific positive obligations that bind rational agents are those that maximize overall harmony within this particular system of goods and permissible evils; I take human freedom to consist in spontaneity guided by rational determination rather than in any liberty of indifference, so that moral agency is fully compatible with our actions being determined by our internal natures; I believe that clear and distinct moral knowledge is available to reason alone, though our judgments are routinely obscured by passion and sensory confusion; and I maintain both that moral evil is strictly a privation, possessing no positive reality of its own, and that such privation nevertheless plays an indispensable functional role in allowing the universe to achieve a greater perfection than it could otherwise attain. I am further inclined to claim that had God actualized a different possible world, the concrete duties governing rational agents in that world would differ, even though the underlying eternal moral principles themselves would remain unchanged. Taken together, do these commitments form a coherent package, or are there hidden fractures that I am not seeing?

Please treat this as an audit rather than a lecture: go through the commitments pair by pair, rank any genuine tensions by severity, and identify which conflicts only appear serious but dissolve on closer inspection. I suspect there may be an apparent contradiction between treating moral evil as pure privation and assigning it an indispensable role in cosmic harmony, but I want to know whether that genuinely collapses once the distinction between absence and functional contribution is made precise. I also want to know whether my world-relative account of concrete duties undermines my claim that moral truths are necessary eternal verities, and whether my rationalist determinism about freedom leaves moral knowledge with enough practical urgency to matter for genuinely responsible agency rather than reducing ethics to a speculative science. For each tension you identify, specify which single revision would be least costly and what exactly would have to be surrendered in making it.
