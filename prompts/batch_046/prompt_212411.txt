The debate over Darwin’s evolutionary inversion and Turing’s behavioral inversion—whether they show that comprehension was never causally necessary, or merely that it can be mechanistically bypassed—has been circling the same ground since the 1980s without either camp conceding an inch. One side treats adaptive complexity and passing the imitation game as proof that functional competence never required internal understanding to begin with, and now cites transformer-based language models as the decisive modern case: fluent, context-sensitive behavior emerging from statistical pattern-matching with no evident grasp of meaning. The other side agrees on every scientific and historical point but insists that these are methodological relocations, not metaphysical eliminations; they point to the first-person phenomenology of genuinely grasping a proof, a metaphor, or an irony as data that cannot be dissolved into performance metrics, and they read LLM outputs as sophisticated mimicry that makes the absence of understanding more glaring, not less. Both sides have heard every reply, yet neither moves, which suggests the stall is structural rather than evidential. My working hypothesis is that part of the disagreement is verbal—“comprehension” is doing triple duty as a causal role, a phenomenological state, and a normative achievement—while another part is a hidden dispute over explanatory primacy: whether we should reduce understanding to successful performance or treat performance as intelligible only against a background of prior understanding. I want a diagnosis, not a survey: are these two groups actually answering different questions—one about causal architecture, one about phenomenological ontology—under a single heading? Is there a disguised empirical disagreement, for instance about whether any cognitive architecture can generate comprehension without prior competence, or vice versa? Or is the framing itself ill-posed, treating “competence without comprehension” as a coherent category when it may conflate distinct phenomena? Finally, what would actually count as progress: a formal result that separates predictive competence from representational understanding, a conceptual distinction that disentangles the causal, phenomenological, and normative senses of comprehension, or an argument that the question should be abandoned and replaced with a different one?
