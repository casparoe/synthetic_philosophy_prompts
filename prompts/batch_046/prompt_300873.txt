Draft the joint statement that a moral particularist—who denies that moral judgment depends on invariant principles and instead treats reasons as context-sensitive— and a moral generalist—who holds that sound moral judgment requires true, context-transcending principles—could both sign after a sustained adversarial collaboration. Structure the document as follows. First, a numbered section of agreed claims about the nature of moral reasoning, the reality of genuine moral conflict, and the role of practical wisdom or discernment. Second, a disagreements table stating each side's residual position in wording its own proponents would accept: specifically, whether moral reasons exhibit "holism" (so that a feature that counts as a reason in one case can count differently or not at all in another) and whether "moral residue"—lingering regret or unreconciled obligation after a correct all-things-considered choice—is best explained by conflict among invariant principles or by the irreducible particularity of reasons. Third, identify the cruxes, especially: (a) whether the perceptual recognition of "moral salience" reveals particular reasons directly or applies general rules to concrete situations; and (b) whether expertise in moral judgment consists in sensitivity to contextual nuance or in mastery of principles. For each crux, provide a concrete hypothetical case that both sides agree is diagnostically relevant, and record in advance each side's predicted analysis of that case. Keep the formulations exact enough that neither side can dismiss the disagreement as merely verbal.
