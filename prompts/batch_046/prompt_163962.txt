I hold that when a community's collective memory and the professional historical record come into direct conflict, the community's living memory should exercise what I call mnemonic sovereignty: it ought to be treated not as a subjective complement to the archive, but as a normative authority capable of overriding documentary findings in public institutions—museums, curricula, memorials—particularly where the events are foundational or traumatic to group identity. This means historians' corrections should be subordinated to the community's transmitted account in these contexts, rather than the reverse. What would genuinely count against this view? I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward: which specific cases or thought experiments expose it, which commitments in epistemology, justice, or institutional design it depends on and might collapse with, and what its defenders could honestly concede versus what they would dismiss. In particular, does the view risk being insulated into unfalsifiability—say, by treating every archival contradiction as further evidence of systemic erasure—and if so, is that insulation a philosophical defect or a feature? I'd also like you to distinguish objections that attack the view's coherence from those that show it collapses into a rival account (for instance, a purely procedural or purely historical one).
