I cover philosophy and cognitive science for a general audience, and I'm developing a long-form feature on how thinkers should weigh their own reasoning against statistical patterns of peer disagreement—the inside view against the outside view. Rather than a conventional debate, I want to stage an adversarial collaboration between two camps: the Reason-Trackers, who maintain that in structurally transparent domains one's direct evaluation of arguments should dominate even over base-rate data about disagreement, and the Calibrationists, who maintain that persistent peer disagreement is itself a reliability indicator that demands epistemic modesty in the form of downward confidence adjustment.

Please draft the joint statement these camps could co-sign. Use this structure: first, a numbered list of agreed claims about belief revision, about what disagreement metrics actually measure, and about the formal limits of social epistemology; second, a disagreements table that states each camp's residual position in wording that camp itself would accept; third, the cruxes—the sub-questions that, if settled, would move at least one side, including whether there is a non-circular criterion for identifying one's own domain of competence and whether the so-called mirror test (would one's conclusion have reversed under different initial priors?) is a genuine diagnostic or a post-hoc rationalization; fourth, for each crux, the tests or arguments both sides agree are relevant, with each side's advance prediction recorded.

Keep it free of bibliographies, reading lists, or citations—the aim is conceptual precision, not scholarly apparatus. Where the disagreement is partly verbal—for example, over whether modesty denotes calibrated self-assessment or reference-class deference—say exactly which part and why. The goal is a document both parties could put their names to, with every remaining divergence made exact.
