As a journalist who covers science and ideas, I have been asked to deliver the opening remarks at an interdisciplinary workshop titled “Reasoning Together: Norms Without Decrees.” The audience is deliberately split: half are philosophers and political theorists familiar with metaethics and deliberative democracy, and half are practitioners—urban planners, educators, and community organizers—who have no patience for jargon but care deeply about whether their decisions can be justified to people who are not in the room. The philosophical idea that must do real structural work is indirect normativity mediated by idealized deliberation: norms do not bind because an authority issues them, but because they survive a procedure of justification to all affected parties, including those structurally absent from any actual meeting. Because of this, I must not issue direct moral commands—“we ought to do X”—but must frame every normative claim as conditional on whether it could survive that idealized procedure. To keep the prompt from being a generic exercise, please inject one new conceptual distinction: the “shadow quorum,” meaning that an idealized deliberation is defective not merely when it lacks proxy representatives for the absent, but when it fails to account for the actual conditions of absence—economic exclusion, temporal displacement, or epistemic marginalization—that make the present agreement possible in the first place.

You are a professional philosopher with extensive experience in public rhetoric and applied ethics. Please think carefully and begin with brief brainstorming: identify three concrete tensions that arise when indirect normativity is applied to real-world policy deliberation (for example, the gap between designing a deliberative procedure and being subject to it; the risk that procedural correctness masks substantive domination; and the asymmetry between those who can speak in the room and those whose silence is a precondition for the conversation). Then, draft a polished speech of roughly 700 words—about five minutes—structured around these tensions. The speech should open with a specific, non-philosophical image or anecdote; develop the argument that direct normative decrees fail precisely because they skip the mediating work of justification; and close with a genuinely open question rather than a prescription, since indirect normativity forbids the speaker from claiming final authority. The language must be rigorous enough that philosophers recognize the framework, yet entirely accessible to practitioners: avoid unexplained technical terms such as “supervenience” or “deontic logic,” and do not invoke named theoretical frameworks—such as contractualist or discourse-ethical models—by proper name unless you explain them from scratch within the text itself. Most importantly, use the “shadow quorum” concept to shape what the speech can and cannot say: every proposed norm must be presented as provisional, pending justification to those outside the room, and the speaker must explicitly acknowledge that her own authority to speak is contingent on that absent justification. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations of any kind; do not reference specific page numbers or obscure texts. Write the final speech in a formal, polished register suitable for a journalist delivering remarks to a mixed professional audience.
