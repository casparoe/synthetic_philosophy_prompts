Compare the counterfactual dependence theory of causation, as developed in the Lewisian tradition, with the interventionist framework that employs structural causal models and do-operators, associated with Judea Pearl. Rather than offering a general survey, examine specifically how each account handles causal selection in cases of symmetric overdetermination modified by interaction: imagine two independent mechanisms, each sufficient on its own to produce outcome O, are both present, but O is subtly altered by their joint presence in a manner neither could produce alone. For the counterfactual theorist, identifying the actual causes requires weighing the similarity of possible worlds in which one mechanism is absent; for the interventionist, it requires assessing whether fixing one variable via an intervention leaves the structural equations governing the other intact. Which framework delivers the more defensible diagnosis of what actually caused the modified outcome O? Furthermore, evaluate whether either approach covertly relies on pragmatic or normative assumptions—such as which variables count as legitimately manipulable—rather than providing a purely metaphysical analysis of causation.
