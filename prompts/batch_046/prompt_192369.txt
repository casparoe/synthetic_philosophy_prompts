Write a proposal for a one-semester philosophy-of-science term paper of approximately 6,000 words. The project examines whether Milton Friedman’s “F-twist”—the claim that unrealistic assumptions in a model are justified if the model yields accurate predictions—applies uniformly to two distinct kinds of idealization in scientific modeling, or whether it conceals a normative distinction that undermines its own defense.

State the precise question: is there a principled difference between “structural” idealizations (simplifying the ontology of the system, e.g., treating economic agents as fully rational or treating a fluid as continuous) and “parametric compensation” (deliberately setting physical parameters, such as climate-model diffusion coefficients or friction terms, outside their empirically measured ranges in order to stabilize predictions and mask missing structural physics)? Specifically, does the F-twist justify both equally, or does parametric compensation fail the F-twist’s implicit conditions because it achieves predictive accuracy not through a false-but-tractable representation of the target system, but through an ad hoc calibration that severs the link between model mechanism and real-world mechanism?

Explain why standard treatments leave this open: philosophers of economics have debated whether unrealistic assumptions are harmless if predictive success holds, while philosophers of climate modeling have criticized tuning practices as epistemically risky, but neither literature systematically asks whether tuning-for-compensation is a different normative category from structural simplification.

Propose an approach: analyze a specific, well-documented case in Earth-system modeling where parameters are pushed beyond physical plausibility—for example, adjustments to ocean-mixing coefficients or cloud-parameterization constants that compensate for unresolved sub-grid dynamics—and compare its epistemic structure to a canonical structural idealization, such as the rational-actor model in macroeconomics. The method should be conceptual, not empirical: map the logical form of the justification in each case, identify whether predictive success is achieved via representational simplification or via error-cancellation, and determine whether Friedman’s defense requires that the false assumption be part of the model’s explanatory structure rather than a hidden corrective.

Say what conclusions are possible—perhaps that the F-twist applies only to structural idealizations; that it applies to both but requires a supplementary condition about transparency or recoverability; or that the distinction collapses upon analysis—and specify which considerations (e.g., whether the tuned parameter can be derived incrementally from first principles, or whether the model remains de-idealizable) would decide between them.

Give an honest assessment of scope and failure conditions: the paper must avoid becoming a technical review of climate physics; it succeeds if it delivers a clear conceptual taxonomy with one worked example, and it fails if the distinction between the two idealization types turns out to be merely terminological or if the climate case cannot be described precisely enough without original empirical research. Note that citations will be added separately; do not include a bibliography or literature review by citation.

Keep the proposal to about one page, suitable for a term-paper approval, with a realistic timeline for one semester.
