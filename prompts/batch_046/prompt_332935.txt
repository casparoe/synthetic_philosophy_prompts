I was prepping for my grad seminar on demandingness objections and I dug up this old chunk from Sidgwick's Methods of Ethics — the original 1874 printing, not the modern reprints with all the footnotes cleaned up. He seems to argue, more or less, that rational benevolence requires treating every other person's good as equally important to your own, except where you can clearly see one good is larger or more certain. But then he immediately adds this strange qualifier about practical limits: we don't actually treat distant suffering as equally urgent, not because the principle is false, but because our knowledge and power are bounded. So there's this tension where the theoretical demand looks totally unlimited — like you ought to be maximizing good everywhere — but the practical application gets filtered through some feasibility screen.

Anyway, I was trying to explain this to a freind who thinks consequentialism just means do the best you can, and she kept mixing up the idea that you have to maximize overall good with the idea that you have to eliminate every personal project. I wonder if Sidgwick is actually carving out space for what we'd now call supererogation, or if he's saying that any failure to promote the greatest good is still a moral defect, just one we can't always avoid. Here's the part that really confuses me: he talks about adopting the point of view of the universe without losing your particular attachments, but then he also seems to say those attachments are only justified if they don't reduce the total good by more than some implicit margin. Is he proposing something like a moral economy where personal projects are permitted only up to a kind of ethical debt ceiling? I don't need a bibliography, just help seeing whether that reading holds water or if I'm importing modern budget-language into Victorian ethics.
