I am preparing the conceptual framework for a graduate seminar on retrospective moral judgment, and I have grown dissatisfied with the way discussions of figures such as Jefferson, Churchill, and Columbus tend to recycle the same binary—either we apply our present standards anachronistically or we excuse them as men of their time—without first asking what a normative theory in this domain is actually required to do. Before we adjudicate particular cases, I want a full specification: what must an adequate theory of judging historical figures deliver in order to count as philosophically adequate?

Please write the desiderata as a requirements list. For each requirement, provide the motivating case or conceptual distinction behind it, and flag whether it is non-negotiable or merely a preference of a particular camp. The list must cover, at minimum: (1) the distinction between evaluating an agent's actions, character, and lasting institutional effects, with an explanation of why collapsing these produces the wrong verdict in at least one of the three canonical cases; (2) a principled account of how temporal distance affects moral assessment—specifically, whether and how available moral alternatives in the agent's historical context function as excusing conditions rather than mere explanations; (3) consistency with the psychological reality that moral cognition is partly shaped by social norms, without allowing that fact to dissolve all retrospective blame into sociological determinism; (4) the capacity to handle asymmetrical judgments, such as praising Churchill for resisting fascism while condemning his imperial policies, without generating contradiction; (5) a clear boundary between epistemic humility about historical context and moral exculpation, particularly in cases like Columbus where the harm was foreseeable even by contemporaneous critics; and (6) whether the theory must deliver a single overall verdict or can tolerate irreducibly plural assessments. You should also inject and defend one further requirement of your own design—something genuinely necessary but rarely discussed in the literature—such as a condition governing how we weigh an agent's private moral reform against their public legacy, or a constraint on how retrospective judgment interacts with present-day obligations of repair.

With the list in place, identify any pair of desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied—perhaps between demanding full contextual charity and insisting on non-negotiable moral principles—and explain the tension. Finally, briefly score three broad approaches—strict presentism, strong historicism (including the man of his time defense), and a pluralist virtue-ethical assessment—against your list, noting which single requirement eliminates the largest number of popular positions. A table is acceptable, but keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases rather than on the scorecard.
