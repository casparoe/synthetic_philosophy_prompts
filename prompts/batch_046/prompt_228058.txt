I'm trying to determine what I actually take corruption to be, working from concrete judgments rather than from a predefined definition, and I'm particularly interested in whether my intuitions depend on Western norms of impartial administration or capture something more general.

Here are my verdicts. A municipal official who awards a public-works contract to her technically qualified but clearly inferior brother-in-law has acted corruptly. A legislator who openly promises to direct infrastructure spending to the districts that elected her, and delivers after taking office, has not acted corruptly; this is distributive politics, not corruption. A diplomat who accepts an expensive ceremonial object at a state function where refusal would be interpreted as a grave diplomatic insult, with no specific policy bargain attached, has not acted corruptly. A corporation that donates substantially to a charity directed by a minister's spouse, with no explicit agreement but with both sides understanding that favorable regulatory rulings will follow, has acted corruptly. A civil servant in a society where small post-service gratuities are obligatory social reciprocity—refusal being deeply shaming—who accepts a modest cash envelope from a citizen whose permit she just expedited, has not acted corruptly, because the exchange belongs to a local moral economy rather than to her official role. By contrast, a judge who receives an annual seasonal food gift from a lawyer who regularly practices in her court, in a legal culture where such gifts are entirely normative and untied to particular cases, has acted corruptly, even though the practice is traditional.

These last two seem hard to reconcile: both involve customary gifts in professional contexts, yet one strikes me as legitimate reciprocity and the other as a corruption of judicial impartiality. The case I keep oscillating on is a private school admissions director who accepts substantial tuition discounts for her own children from parents whose applications she evaluates; sometimes this feels like a clear conflict of interest, sometimes like an unavoidable feature of elite institutional life.

Is there a coherent principle—perhaps tracking cross-role exchange, institutional role-distortion, or the universalizability of the practice—that captures all these judgments, or must I abandon at least one? If a principle saves the legislator, diplomat, and civil-servant cases but forces me to treat either the judge or the admissions-director case differently, which verdict should yield and why? What does the resulting account predict for an unlisted case: a hospital administrator who reserves premium surgical slots for patients referred by physicians who regularly send her expensive holiday gifts, in a medical culture where such gifting is standard professional courtesy? Finally, does any account that delivers my judgments necessarily impose Western liberal standards of bureaucratic neutrality onto practices that may be locally legitimate, and if so, is that a cost of the principle or a feature?
