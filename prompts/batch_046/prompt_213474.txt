I'm working through Nietzsche's distinction between monumental, antiquarian, and critical history in a philosophy of culture seminar, and I'm trying to test my own intuitions about when historical consciousness actually nourishes life rather than burying it. Here are my concrete verdicts, which don't seem to fall cleanly into the three familiar boxes. A young athlete who immerses herself in heroic, almost mythologized biographies of past champions to steel her will — clearly life-promoting, monumental history in its proper place. A town historical society that catalogues every invoice from a demolished factory and blocks any redevelopment of the site because the past must remain untouched — clearly harmful, antiquarian preservation become a tomb. A therapist who helps a patient dismantle the family myth of unbroken self-sufficiency that hides real trauma: it frees the patient but strips away every narrative thread that connected her to her parents — I keep reversing myself; sometimes this feels like essential critical history, sometimes like destructive over-correction that severs the root without planting anything. A revolutionary group that ritualistically debunks every prior revolution as betrayal, preventing any tradition from stabilizing — harmful, though I can't tell whether this is critical history turned pathological or monumental history collapsed into perpetual iconoclasm. And an immigrant who meticulously maintains every ritual and document from her homeland, not out of piety but because without them she reports feeling literally without a self — sustaining, even vital, yet structurally identical to the factory archivists' deadening preservation.

What I want is not a summary of the taxonomy but a precise principle — or a small competing set — that captures the pattern behind these judgments, especially the tension between the factory archivists and the immigrant, both engaged in meticulous preservation but with opposite effects. The principle should be stated with the case that motivates each clause, its verdicts on the therapist and revolutionary-group cases, and an honest statement of which of my five verdicts must be abandoned if coherence is to be preserved. Please respond in exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion: item one should state the core principle; items two and three should apply its clauses to the immigrant-archivist pair and to the therapist case respectively; item four should address the revolutionary group; and item five should identify which of my original verdicts the principle cannot save and why.
