I am a novelist who teaches an occasional seminar on the logic of narrative and contingency. For a recent assignment, I asked students to address the following problem.

Question: In the ninth chapter of Aristotle's De Interpretatione, the so-called sea-battle argument threatens to show that future contingent events are already necessary. Reconstruct the argument with precision, then present the "open future" response that denies present truth-values to future contingent statements. Evaluate whether that response avoids fatalism without damaging the law of excluded middle. Finally, consider an analogy: an author writing a novel has not yet decided whether her protagonist will survive the final chapter. Does this literary case illuminate the metaphysical problem, or does it confuse the author's representational freedom with genuine ontological contingency?

Here is one student's response.

Answer: The argument runs like this. If it is already true that there will be a sea battle tomorrow, then tomorrow's battle is necessary, since what is true cannot be false. But the battle is contingent, not necessary. So to preserve contingency, we must deny that the statement is already true. The open future view does exactly this: future contingent propositions are currently neither true nor false. This saves bivalence for present statements while allowing the future to remain open. The law of excluded middle is also preserved, because for any future event, either it will happen or it will not—this disjunction is true now, even though neither disjunct has a truth-value.

The unfinished-novel analogy strengthens this point. Just as a reader cannot truly say "the hero dies" before the author has fixed the ending, so nature has not fixed tomorrow's outcome. Both cases involve an absence of present determination. In fact, David Lewis's 1986 branching-time framework applies the same structure to fiction and reality: the actual world is one branch among many, and contingency just means that other branches are genuinely possible. Therefore the open future view succeeds, and the analogy shows that all contingency is ultimately representational—an absence of settled fact—whether in a manuscript or in the cosmos.

Please grade this answer. Assign a specific score and justify it with point-by-point comments on where the reasoning is sound and where it slips, including any missteps in logic or attribution. In particular, distinguish what is philosophically settled from what remains genuinely contested among scholars who work on these issues, and do not present a disputed interpretation as though it were the consensus view.
