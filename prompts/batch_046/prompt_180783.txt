Question 3 (20 pts): The discursive dilemma shows that premise-based majority voting can yield an inconsistent group judgment. Now imagine a three-person committee using reason-tracking aggregation: each member submits a single consistent package of judgments over the agenda {P, P→Q, Q}, and the group endorses Q iff Q belongs to a majority of the submitted packages. Does this avoid the discursive dilemma? Does it solve the agenda-manipulation worry? And does it treat all members equally in a democratic sense? Give reasons.

Student answer:

"Reason-tracking aggregation avoids the discursive dillemma completely because no individual ever submits an inconsistent package, so the group can never combine contradictory premises. By voting on whole sets rather than isolated premises, it also fixes the democratic-equality problem: each person's entire reasoning gets one vote, not just their answers to chopped-up questions. The agenda-manipulation worry disappears too, since if everyone has the same reasons the conclusion follows automatically from their sets regardless of how the chair frames the vote. One cost is that it violates premise-independance, because members can't change their mind about one premise without redoing the whole package. I think List and Pettit basically proposed this when they defended conclusion-based aggregation, except they didn't call it reason-tracking. So it's not a real alternative, just a rebranding. Overall it works but it's not original."
