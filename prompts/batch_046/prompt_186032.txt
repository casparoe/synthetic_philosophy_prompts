I'm working on dissociations between access and phenomenal consciousness, particularly in debates over global workspace theory. I've noticed a recurring dialectical pattern in how empirical cases—continuous flash suppression, attentional blink, or subliminal priming—are handled, and I'm unsure whether it already has a name.

In these experiments, a stimulus produces measurable neural processing and behavioral priming, yet subjects fail to report it. Global workspace theorists typically treat the absence of global broadcast as proof that no consciousness is present, relegating the processing to an unconscious precursor. Phenomenal-consciousness theorists typically treat the same processing as proof that phenomenal consciousness is present, and treat the failure of report as showing only that access has been blocked. What interests me is not the disagreement itself, but a specific structural feature: each side takes the very same gap—processing present, access absent—as decisive evidence for the primacy of its own consciousness concept, and then uses that primacy to dismiss the other concept as tracking a mere derivative or artifact. The dissociation data do not merely underdetermine the two views; they are interpreted through a mirror-image dependency, where each camp's definition of consciousness determines what counts as the "real" phenomenon.

Is there an established term for this particular argumentative structure—where two competing definitions of a mental state both claim explanatory priority over identical dissociation evidence, and each uses that priority to relegate the other to absence or artifact? If not, how should it be distinguished from ordinary theoretical underdetermination and from the standard access/phenomenal distinction itself? I am not looking for a literature review, but for the conceptual carving: what exactly this pattern is, what it is not, and where its boundaries lie.
