Compare Charles Sanders Peirce's and William James's pragmatist accounts of truth with respect to a specific problem: a scientific community formulates a precise, empirically meaningful prediction about a physical process that, due to permanent technological and cosmological constraints, can never be observed or tested by any actual investigator. Peirce defines truth as the opinion destined to be agreed upon at the limit of inquiry, while James ties truth to experiential verification and practical consequences. Analyze how each account evaluates the truth-status of this permanently untestable prediction. In particular, address whether Peirce's appeal to counterfactual agreement at the end of inquiry allows him to assign truth-values independently of actual testing, whether James's verificationist commitments force him to deny the prediction literal truth-status or to reinterpret its content in terms of present practical differences, and which position better preserves the intuition that scientific theories can remain genuinely true even when some consequences lie permanently beyond observational reach. Do not provide a bibliography or reading list; ground your answer in the conceptual structure of the two positions.
