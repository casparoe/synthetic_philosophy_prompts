I'm auditing my own views on tyrannicide for a workshop and need a consistency check before presenting. Here is roughly where I've landed:

(1) I accept Aquinas's core restriction that private individuals may not, on their own private authority, kill a ruler, even a tyrant; legitimate resistance requires some public authority or communal judgment rather than unilateral action.

(2) I also hold that when a regime has systematically destroyed the common good and all institutional remedies are blocked, the political community as a whole retains a natural right to depose that ruler, and this right can be exercised by representatives acting with communal mandate.

(3) I think that in modern centralized authoritarian states, there is rarely any public authority independent of the regime itself, so the Aquinian condition for legitimate resistance is almost never met in practice.

(4) At the same time, I maintain that moral evaluation of resistance should depend partly on empirical conditions: if collective action is structurally impossible without initial acts by private dissidents, then forbidding all private violence effectively eliminates the community's right altogether.

(5) I believe that moral rights and permissions are institutionally structured: only those occupying recognized public roles can authorize lethal resistance, and private citizens lack that normative standing regardless of circumstances.

Go through these and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only appear to conflict, and how severe each tension is. For each real tension, state the least costly revision and what it costs me. Then rank the tensions by severity and identify the single revision that would clean up the most problems without collapsing the view into either pure pacifism or unconditional rebellion. Be specific about whether (3) undermines (2) by removing its conditions of exercise, and whether (4) introduces a consequentialist consideration that conflicts with the deontological framing of (1) and (5).
