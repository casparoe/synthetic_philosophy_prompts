You are grading an undergraduate exam in philosophy of religion and Buddhist thought. The exam asks students to apply the doctrine of anatta to a precisely described scenario. Below is the exam question followed by one student's answer. Exam question: The Buddhist doctrine of anatta denies a permanent, unitary self (atman) and analyzes personal identity as a conventional designation (pannatti) for the five aggregates (skandhas): form, feeling, perception, mental formations, and consciousness. In Theravada Abhidharma, clinging (upadana) is said to perform two functions: an integrative function, binding the aggregates into a narrative self, and an affective function, generating craving (tanha) for their continuation. Some interpreters distinguish a further residual form—pre-reflective, non-propositional orienting awareness—that persists even when both integrative clinging and affective craving have ceased, corresponding to the subtle conceit asmi-mana (I-am-ness). Using the Abhidharma framework, answer the following: (i) Is this residual orienting awareness one of the five aggregates, or does it fall outside them? (ii) Does the complete cessation of craving and integrative clinging suffice for liberation (nibbana), or must this residual awareness also be eliminated? (iii) How does the Mahayana doctrine of sunyata (emptiness) treat this residual awareness differently from the Theravada treatment of it as a mental formation (sankhara) to be uprooted?

Student answer: The doctrine of anatta holds that there is no permanent atman underlying experience; instead, a person is just the five skandhas operating together. Clinging binds them, and it has both an integrative aspect—creating a narrative self—and an affective aspect—producing craving. When both are eliminated, the aggregates should no longer constitute a person. The meditator has eliminated gross craving and integrative clinging, yet retains a pre-reflective orienting awareness, which is the subtle conceit asmi-mana. In Theravada Abhidharma, this is classified as a mental formation (sankhara) and must also be destroyed for full liberation, so cessation of craving alone is not sufficient. In Mahayana thought, because all phenomena are empty (sunyata) of inherent existence, this awareness is already empty and lacks self-nature; consequently, it does not need to be eliminated but only recognized as empty. Once recognized, the meditator is liberated even if the feeling persists. The key difference is that Theravada treats the residue as a real obstacle to be uprooted, whereas Mahayana treats it as an illusion that vanishes upon correct insight. Since clinging is the only force that binds aggregates together, and craving fuels clinging, the absence of both means the aggregates can no longer function as a unified self; therefore the remaining awareness must lie outside the five aggregates. This reveals a tension in Buddhism: if awareness remains but is not an aggregate, it functions like a hidden substrate, which seems to contradict anatta.
