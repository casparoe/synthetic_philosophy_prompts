I was discussing Bentham's sexual ethics with a friend who'd read a popular philosophy blog post summarizing his unpublished writings on same-sex relations. The summary claimed that Bentham treated sexual morality as a direct application of the felicific calculus, literally assigning quantitative weights to the intensity and duration of pleasure produced by different consensual practices, which led him not merely to tolerate but actively to prescribe arrangements that maximized aggregate happiness—including, the author implied, expanding marriage contracts to any combination of consenting adults that passed this optimization test.

It further argued that Bentham rejected appeals to unnaturalness as not just weak but conceptually incoherent within utilitarianism, since nature carries no normative weight whatsoever, and that this reasoning collapses into a kind of hedonistic anarchism about sexuality: if pleasure is the sole metric, then no consensual practice can be ruled out on principle, which is why modern liberals should distance themselves from Bentham's position. Something about this feels too mechanistic and too strong—particularly the idea that Bentham made positive prescriptions about which sexual arrangements people ought to enter, and the claim that he applied the calculus in this quantitative, engineering-like fashion to intimate acts. Is this summary fair?
