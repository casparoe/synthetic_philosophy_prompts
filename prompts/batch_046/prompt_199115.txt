I'm grading responses for a graduate seminar on conceptual structure in evolutionary biology and early philosophy of mind. The exam question was: Compare Darwin's inference from artificial to natural selection with Turing's inference in his 1950 essay Computing Machinery and Intelligence from human reasoning to machine performance. In each case, a system lacking the mental property usually tied to a competence—intentional selection, or understanding—nonetheless produces that competence. How exactly does each thinker invert the ordinary reasoning that links competence to comprehension? And where does the analogy between evolutionary adaptation and computation break down?

One student answered as follows:

Darwin begins with artificial selection, where human breeders intentionally choose traits, and then inverts this reasoning: nature achieves the same adaptive competence without any selecting mind. The mechanism—differential survival—replaces the breeder's comprehension. Turing performs a structurally identical inversion. He starts from the fact that humans display reasoning competence and asks whether a digital computer running the right program could display the same behavioral competence. If it can, then we have reasoning without internal comprehension, just as we have adaptation without a designer. Both arguments therefore show that comprehension is a redundant hypothesis: once you have the right mechanism—environmental filtering or algorithmic execution—the mental property is unnecessary for explaining the output. The main difference is temporal scale: Darwin's process is cumulative across generations, while Turing's is instantaneous. But the philosophical upshot is the same functionalist conclusion—competence alone licenses the attribution of the underlying capacity, whether that capacity is intelligence or evolutionary design.
