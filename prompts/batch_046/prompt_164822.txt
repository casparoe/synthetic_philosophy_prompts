I'm trying to work out my own position on political obligation and civil disobedience by testing my intuitions against concrete cases, and I'd like help finding the principle behind them, if there is one. Here are my verdicts, with brief reasoning.

First, on general obedience: if a democratic state enforces a law that is unjust but does not require me to do anything morally wrong—say, a zoning ordinance that unfairly restricts housing—I think I should obey it anyway, because the obligation is institutional and general rather than evaluated statute by statute.

Second, if the law requires me to actively participate in injustice—say, to report a neighbor's religious affiliation to authorities—then the obligation is void, and refusal is required rather than merely permitted.

Third, if I refuse to pay taxes that fund a military action I consider immoral, but I continue to live in the society and use public goods like roads and emergency services, I think my refusal is unjustified. Either I should fully exit and reject all benefits, or I should pay and work for change through legal means.

Fourth, if I live in a direct democracy, vote for a policy, then later realize it was unjust, I think I have a stronger obligation to comply with it than someone who never supported it—precisely because I participated in authorizing it—though I can still disobey if I do so openly and accept the legal consequences.

Fifth, if I reject the state's legitimacy entirely and live self-sufficiently, but I still use a single public road to access my property, I think I have no broad political obligation, yet I do have a specific, benefit-based obligation regarding that road; refusing to pay the corresponding tax is unjustified.

Sixth, comparing two people who break the same unjust conscription law: the one who breaks it openly, accepts prison, and continues to argue for repeal acts within the bounds of civil disobedience. The one who breaks it secretly, lies to authorities, and avoids punishment is merely evading the law, and I think this is morally different, even though both violate the same statute.

Seventh, if a local democratic authority refuses to enforce a national law that conflicts with its own just ordinance—say, a sanctuary policy protecting vulnerable residents—I think citizens have an obligation to support the local authority over the remote national one, provided the local decision is closer to their direct democratic participation.

These verdicts seem to pull in different directions: in case three I demand full exit for tax refusal, but in case five I allow partial rejection of benefits; in case four I say participation strengthens obligation, but in case seven I say proximity of democratic participation can weaken national obligation. Is there a coherent principle—perhaps involving distinctions between direct complicity, derivative authority, and conditional benefit—that captures all seven verdicts? Or must I drop at least one? Please also say what the principle predicts for a case I haven't listed: a citizen never consented, rejects all state benefits except emergency medical care she once received, and now refuses to pay taxes specifically earmarked for military spending.
