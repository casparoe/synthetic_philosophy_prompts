After a long and inconclusive argument with a friend about whether universal conscription can be morally justified within a framework of armed neutrality, please draft the joint statement that two opposing camps could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. The parties are: (A) civic-republican conscriptionists, who hold that a democratic state maintaining armed neutrality must bind citizens to collective defence through universal service liability in order to preserve social solidarity and credible deterrence; and (B) liberal-volunteer advocates, who hold that armed neutrality is best secured by a professional, volunteer-based force with selective reserve obligations, because universal conscription violates bodily autonomy and produces militarily obsolete mass reserves in an era of precision and asymmetric warfare.

Format the deliverable as a numbered section of agreed claims followed by a disagreements table. The agreed claims should include points both sides genuinely accept—such as the recognition that Switzerland, Sweden and Finland have all tied conscription or selective service to neutrality or non-alignment, and that none of these models has eliminated reserve liability entirely—while separating substantive disagreement from merely verbal confusion. The table must set out the precise formulations of their residual disagreement, each phrased in wording that its own side would endorse.

You must also identify at least three cruxes: sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one party. For each crux, specify the case, argument, or thought experiment that both sides agree bears upon it, and record each side’s predicted outcome in advance. One crux should turn on what I call the mobilisation credibility paradox: the claim that armed neutrality requires visible mass reserves for rapid total-defence mobilisation and deterrence, yet modern drone-based and asymmetric threats render large conscripted infantry reserves strategically redundant. A second crux should examine whether selective conscription—of the kind Sweden and Finland have moved toward—creates a morally relevant distinction between service liability and deployment exposure, or whether it merely disguises coercion behind probabilistic selection. A third crux should ask whether neutrality itself can survive without the social egalitarianism that universal conscription is said to foster, particularly in small states that lack alliance guarantees.

Keep the statement focused on conceptual architecture rather than empirical military history; do not ask for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists. Write in formal, polished British English, and inject just enough novel framing—particularly the credibility paradox and the liability/exposure distinction—so that the answer cannot be assembled from standard pre-trained summaries of Scandinavian or Swiss defence debates.
