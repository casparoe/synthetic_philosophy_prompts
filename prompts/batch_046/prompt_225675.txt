I am preparing a feature for a science and ideas magazine on how hard determinist arguments about moral responsibility are being re-examined in clinical neuroethics, and I have spent several months interviewing a legal philosopher who applies what she calls a Strawsonian basic argument to institutional judgments of blame. Across our conversations she has ruled on five hypothetical cases, and I would like to know how she would treat a sixth that I have designed specifically to test the boundaries of her framework. She maintains that genuine moral responsibility requires being causa sui—being the ultimate origin of one's own nature and actions—and on that basis she has said that a violent offender whose behavior was driven by an undiagnosed frontal-lobe tumor is not responsible; that a fraudster whose risk-taking temperament was entirely formed by a documented childhood of extreme deprivation is not responsible; that a corporate executive who deliberated for months and then knowingly authorized environmental harm is still not responsible, because the deliberative character that guided her choice was itself the product of genetic and social conditions she never authored; that a contemplative practitioner who spent two decades deliberately reshaping his own cognitive habits through rigorous discipline and then broke a solemn vow, claiming the act expressed his authentic self, is nevertheless not responsible, since even that long program of self-modification was set in motion by prior conditions outside his control; and that an artificial decision system running an internal loop of reflective endorsement of each action is not responsible, because the loop was engineered by others. The new case I wish to present involves a patient facing a progressive neurological disorder that will eventually eliminate her capacity for autonomous choice; she consents to an experimental gene-therapy protocol that partially redesigns her prefrontal risk-assessment circuitry, and after the procedure she commits a harmful act, insisting that it was the first truly hers because she had authorized the very architecture that produced it. I am less interested in a simple yes or no than in identifying the rule she appears to be running, articulating at least one competing rule that accommodates her earlier verdicts just as well but diverges on this case, predicting which of those rules she actually follows, naming the further hypothetical case that would settle the matter between them, and giving the reasons for your preference. Please write your response as continuous prose without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as if composing a letter or an email.
