I am preparing a philosophical profile for a seminar on collective memory and democratic legitimacy, and before I present it I need to know whether these commitments actually hang together or whether I am helping myself to hidden inconsistencies. Here is roughly where I have landed. First, I take a strictly functionalist view of national myths: the only legitimate criterion for endorsing one is whether it currently fosters democratic solidarity and civic participation, not whether it corresponds to the archival record. Second, I believe that “lost cause” myths are structurally unique and uniquely dangerous—because they preserve an open grievance that can be mobilized against present institutions, democratic societies should actively dismantle them even when they deliver strong social cohesion. Third, I think that Masada, Dunkirk, the Kosovo Field of Blackbirds, and the Confederate Lost Cause all belong to a single narrative type, namely military defeat reframed as voluntary collective sacrifice, and that any attempt to praise Masada or Dunkirk while condemning the others merely reflects present political taste rather than any real structural difference between them. Fourth, I hold that democratic states have no legitimate authority to suppress collective memories, false or harmful, because monopolizing the narrative of the past is itself a form of civic domination that violates citizens’ mnemonic autonomy. Fifth, I want to maintain that Dunkirk is nevertheless an acceptable national myth because its “deliverance” framing does not require falsifying an enemy’s moral status, whereas the Lost Cause and Kosovo myths necessarily do so.

Please audit this package for consistency. Which pairs are in genuine tension, which only appear to conflict, and what is the least costly revision for each? I am especially worried that my functionalism in the first commitment is being selectively overridden in the second, that my claim of structural uniformity in the third undermines the moral distinction I draw in the fifth, and that there may be a hidden tension between the second and fourth commitments that I am not seeing clearly. Rank the tensions by severity, indicate for each whether the cheapest fix requires abandoning a principle or refining a distinction, and tell me which single revision—if I could make only one—would restore the most coherence without collapsing the overall position. Be specific about exactly which commitment pulls against which, and note where the reply I would give in defense is weakest. Do not just list conflicts; trace what each view commits me to and whether those commitments are jointly sustainable.
