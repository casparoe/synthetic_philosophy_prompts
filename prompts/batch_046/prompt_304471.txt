I'm preparing the final session of a philosophy-of-mind seminar on representational theories, and I'd like to close with a villanelle—nineteen lines total, five tercets followed by one quatrain, rhyme scheme ABA, with two refrains (A1 and A2) that recur according to the form's rules—that treats the language of thought hypothesis as its actual subject rather than a decorative theme. The poem needs to be precise enough that a specialist in philosophy of mind wouldn't wince: it should reflect the core commitments that mental representations possess syntactic structure (like sentences composed of lexical atoms) and that cognitive processes are computations defined over those structures, not merely that the mind "uses language" in some loose sense.

Please make the formal architecture do philosophical work. Let the two refrains correspond to the two core commitments—one to syntax, one to computation—and allow their repetition and recombination across the tercets to mirror the systematicity argument: the idea that the capacity for one thought entails the capacity for structurally related thoughts because the same representational atoms are rearranged. The rhyme and refrain should feel mechanical, not merely musical; the reader should experience the recombination the theory describes.

Rather than offering a straightforward exposition, inject a specific tension into the poem's turn, staged in the final quatrain where the refrains converge. Take up the problem of misrepresentation and conceptual change within a syntactic system: if mental representations are literally sentences in a mental lexicon, then error looks like syntactic or semantic malfunction rather than false belief, and if the vocabulary and grammar are fixed, then genuine conceptual innovation—acquiring categories that are not rearrangements of existing atoms—seems impossible. End the poem on that unresolved tension: does altering one's mind require rewriting the mental grammar itself, or was the syntax always sufficient and we only ever misarranged it? Keep the tone contemplative, avoid resolving the tension with either a sermon about human reason or a shrug about neural plasticity, and do not include bibliographic references, citations, or named texts in quotation marks. The audience is a seminar of graduate students who have already worked through the standard introductions, so the poem should assume familiarity with the basic framework but reward attention to the conceptual pressure point I've described.
