I have just finished a long and rather intense argument with a friend about whether legal moralism can survive once Hart's criticisms are taken seriously, and I am now preparing the questions for a colloquium interview I will conduct next week with Dr. Elias Vorn. Vorn defends a position he calls 'threshold legal moralism': he grants Hart that Devlin was wrong to treat society's shared morality as a monolithic structure that law must preserve in its entirety, but he argues that Hart underestimated the possibility of localised, institution-specific normative erosion. On Vorn's view, once a practice becomes sufficiently prevalent within a particular public framework — he focuses particularly on commercial surrogacy embedded in state-funded healthcare systems — it can alter what he terms the 'background conditions of mutual recognition' to the point where democratic coordination is undermined, even if no individual suffers direct physical or economic harm. He therefore accepts the harm principle for direct injuries, but holds that law may legitimately restrict practices that cross this density threshold within an institutional domain.

Please draft eight to ten interview questions that avoid asking him merely to restate the view. I want at least one question pressing him on how he distinguishes genuine threshold disintegration from ordinary moral pluralism; at least one on whether his notion of 'background conditions of mutual recognition' can be made operational without smuggling in a substantive conception of the good life; and at least one on his reply to Hart's familiar objection that moralist arguments tend to confuse the intensity of popular feeling with the rational justification for criminal prohibition. For each of these three, include a follow-up keyed to his most likely response — respectively, that disintegration is marked by the failure of coordination mechanisms rather than by mere disagreement; that operationalisation requires only procedural norms of reciprocal recognition rather than thick moral commitments; and that Hart's charge overlooks the structural, as opposed to emotional, character of normative erosion.

Please also include a question that asks what kind of empirical or institutional evidence could cause him to abandon the view, and one that tests whether his restriction to specific 'institutional domains' is stable or whether it collapses into a broader liberal perfectionism once the boundary conditions are relaxed.

In framing your response, please separate what is settled among scholars of the Hart–Devlin debate from what remains genuinely contested, and do not present any particular reading of Hart or Devlin as if it commanded universal agreement.
