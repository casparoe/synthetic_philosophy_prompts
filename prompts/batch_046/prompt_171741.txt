I'm working through some concrete cases to see what I actually think about remembrance and forgetting, partly in dialogue with the kind of concerns Margalit and Rieff raise about ethical memory and the right to be forgotten, though I'm not trying to apply their theories mechanically. I want to know whether my judgments hang together or whether I'm holding incompatible commitments.

Here are my gut reactions, case by case. A museum removes a monument to a local industrialist who built his fortune on slave labor and places it in an unmarked storage facility with no identifying label; the curators argue that the object is too toxic to display even critically. I think this is wrong: the artifact should be preserved and contextualized, not hidden away as if the harm itself could be made invisible. But when a survivor of state violence asks that her testimony be sealed for fifty years rather than published, I think that is her right—she should have sovereignty over whether her trauma becomes part of the permanent public record. A government proposes to permanently archive every citizen's private digital correspondence "so that nothing is lost to history"; I think this violates a legitimate right to be forgotten and should be rejected. After a civil conflict, the new regime mandates that school textbooks present only the victors' narrative and erase the defeated community's account entirely; that is clearly wrong. A therapist uses guided techniques to help a patient with severe PTSD deliberately blur and eventually lose specific sensory details of the trauma; that seems not merely permissible but humane. A town that suffered a factory disaster stops holding public commemorations after forty years, letting the anniversary pass quietly; I am genuinely torn—if the survivors want silence, it feels respectful, but if it is merely collective fatigue, it feels like negligence. Finally, a historian discovers that a beloved founding story of a nation is largely fabricated, and publishing the correction would destabilize a fragile democratic transition; my instinct is that the correction should still be made, though I feel less certain here than in the monument case.

I've been framing this tension in terms of mnemonic sovereignty—the authority of individuals and groups over how they are inscribed in collective memory—set against historical accountability, which demands that harmful acts and their agents remain visible. But I cannot tell whether these two ideas actually cover all my judgments, or whether they conflict in ways I have not yet faced. Is there a precise principle, or a small family of principles, that captures why unmarked storage is wrong, sealed testimony is right, permanent state archiving is wrong, textbook erasure is wrong, therapeutic blurring is right, and the anniversary case is ambiguous? What should the principle say about the historian's dilemma—does accountability always trump political stability, or only when the harm is direct and identifiable? And please apply whatever account you propose to a case I have not listed: a community decides to rename a public square and simultaneously destroy all old maps that show the former name, so that the old designation becomes literally unlocatable. Does the principle condemn that, and if so, on which clause? I also want an honest statement of which of my original verdicts your principle cannot accommodate without distortion, and whether I should overturn one of them—perhaps my hesitation about the historian, or my ambivalence about the anniversary—to preserve coherence.
