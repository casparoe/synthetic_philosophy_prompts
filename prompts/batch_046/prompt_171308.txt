Here are my verdicts on whether various claims qualify as laws of history, in the sense discussed in critiques of historicism. I take it that a claim such as “all industrial societies eventually develop representative institutions” might be a law, though it risks being unfalsifiable through vagueness. I hold that explaining a singular event—for instance, that a particular revolution occurred because of fiscal crisis and institutional paralysis—is not law-governed explanation but narrative causation. I also think that unconditional predictions about the entire future course of humanity—say, that technological progress will necessarily culminate in a post-scarcity civilisation—are prophecies rather than scientific laws. Where I am uncertain is with strictly conditional claims about singular trajectories: if global temperatures were to rise by four degrees, then specific patterns of mass migration would follow. This seems law-like, yet its conditions may never be repeated. Likewise, if historians discovered that every maritime trading empire dissolves within fifty years of reaching a particular debt-to-revenue threshold, I am unsure whether this is a genuine law or an accidental correlation. I also judge that forecasting the specific fate of an existing regime—whether it will survive the decade—is not an application of historical law, even if general conditions for instability are well understood. Is there a coherent principle—perhaps involving the repeatability of conditions, the exclusion of intentional agency, or the logical form of the claim—that captures these verdicts?
