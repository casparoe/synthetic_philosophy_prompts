I'm preparing background material for a philosophy seminar that bridges epistemic logic with debates about practical knowledge. In our discussion, we have been working with a restricted formulation of Fitch's paradox: instead of applying the knowability principle — that every truth is possibly known — to propositional knowledge in general, we apply it specifically to procedural knowledge, or knowing-how, which we are treating for the sake of argument as a stable, non-propositional disposition that need not involve explicit propositional access. Even under this restriction, the paradoxical derivation seems to go through: if it is always possible for an agent to recognize (even implicitly) whether they possess such a disposition, and if the absence of recognition entails the absence of the capacity, then it follows that every agent either already possesses every procedural skill they could possibly have, or lacks all of them entirely. This strikes me as deeply counterintuitive, but I am not sure whether the error lies in importing modal epistemic principles into a non-propositional domain, in our analysis of recognition conditions for dispositions, or in the original knowability principle itself.

Given this framing, what are the most important implications of the restricted paradox? Please address, in particular, what it suggests about whether intellectualists about know-how can consistently deny that knowing-how is subject to epistemic modal closure; whether anti-intellectualists are forced into a global skepticism about unexercised skills; and how we ought to reconceive the relationship between dispositional stability and epistemic accessibility if we wish to block the derivation without abandoning the intuition that unknown procedural capacities remain genuinely possible.
