I serve on the programming and ethics committee of a publicly funded urban-heritage foundation that must vote on a permanent exhibition proposal, "The Slow City," which applies Braudel's longue durée framework to local history. The design treats geographic and climatic structures—river sedimentation cycles, deforestation timelines, and agricultural carrying capacity—as the primary causal agents across eight centuries, relegating wars, reforms, and social movements to surface turbulence. Proponents argue this corrects anthropocentric bias and supports climate-adaptation policy by showing how short-term human decisions are tightly constrained by deep structures. Opponents counter that the framing systematically flattens the causal efficacy of collective human agency—especially organized resistance by displaced communities—by treating structural constraints as destiny rather than as conditions shaped by past struggle; they further object to an interactive policy simulation in the gallery that asks visitors to choose between event-level flood barriers and structural watershed management over 300-year timelines, claiming this implicitly frames democratic deliberation as epiphenomenal to geographic fate. Draft the committee's formal recommendation memo—approve, reject, or approve with conditions—addressing both the historiographical value and the ethical risk of determinism in a publicly funded narrative, treating the strongest consideration on the losing side with genuine fairness, and avoiding any request for external bibliographies or citation lists.
