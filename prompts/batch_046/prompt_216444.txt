Examination question: In Plato’s Laws, the Athenian Stranger defends supervised symposia—regulated communal drinking—as an instrument of the legislator. Articulate the political and psychological function Plato assigns to these gatherings, contrast this with his treatment of intoxication in the Republic, and assess whether the two works are genuinely in tension or whether they share an underlying theory of how alcohol relates to the structure of the soul.

Student answer: The student argues that in the Laws Plato treats wine as a kind of social mirror. When citizens drink together under the gaze of a sober magistrate, alcohol strips away conventional masks and reveals true character—courage, fear, temperance, or licentiousness—making the hidden architecture of the soul visible to the community. The legislator exploits this “sympotic transparency” to identify citizens whose souls are defective and need correction, and to test potential rulers, since the man who remains orderly when intoxicated demonstrates fitness for command. Thus alcohol functions not as a poison but as an epistemic instrument.

By contrast, in the Republic Socrates condemns drunkenness outright for the guardian class, comparing the drunken soul to a tyrant: both are ruled by the appetitive part, destroying the harmony of reason, spirit, and appetite. The Republic therefore seems to prohibit exactly what the Laws encourages.

The student resolves the apparent conflict strategically. The Republic addresses an elite military class that must never surrender rational control, while the Laws addresses the broader citizenry of an actual city. Both texts accept the tripartite psychology: alcohol weakens reason and strengthens appetite. The difference is contextual—elites avoid it entirely; ordinary citizens may undergo supervised exposure so the legislator can observe natural dispositions.

Finally, the student connects the Laws’ view to Plato’s discussion of writing in the Phaedrus. Just as writing is a pharmakon for memory, wine is a pharmakon for the soul—an external technology that substitutes for internal virtue by chemically revealing character. From this analogy the student infers that Plato’s approval of symposia in the Laws signals a retreat from the Republic’s moral psychology: if wine can reliably expose the soul, the tripartite model becomes redundant, because we can read character directly from behavior rather than through an internal hierarchy of parts.
