As a doctoral student in philosophy of language working on hermeneutic structure in artificial interpretation, I have become preoccupied with Hofstadter's distinction in Gödel, Escher, Bach among frame messages, outer messages, and inner messages, and I worry I have drawn a conclusion that may rest on a confusion. It strikes me that if the frame message is what instructs an interpreter how to decode the outer message into the inner message, then the frame is not merely background context but constitutive of meaning itself: without the correct frame, there is no path to the intended inner message, only noise or an unintended one. Applying this to large language models, which lack any direct access to an author's original frame—no shared cultural background, no awareness of medium-specific conventions, only statistical regularities over token sequences—it seems they do not so much misinterpret a text as substitute a synthetic frame derived from the distributional patterns of their training corpus, thereby generating the correct inner message not for the author's intended communication act but for a different one altogether. From this I am inclined to infer that there is no frame-independent inner message that could be recovered or missed, and that Hofstadter's tripartite scheme ultimately collapses into a kind of interpretive monism in which meaning is not located in the message but is wholly constituted by the frame the interpreter supplies. Is this reasoning sound? Specifically, if every interpretation in the absence of the original frame necessarily involves such substitution rather than failed recovery, should we abandon the notion that texts possess author-intended meanings that can be correctly or incorrectly grasped, and instead treat all interpretation as the successful construction of a new inner message governed by whatever frame is operational? I want a committed answer that takes a definite position on whether Hofstadter's framework actually supports this monistic conclusion or contains resources to resist it—particularly on whether frame messages can be partially reconstructed rather than wholly replaced, and whether the outer message carries sufficient structural constraint to prevent arbitrary frame substitution. I also want you to assess whether describing LLM interpretation as successful frame substitution rather than as misinterpretation is a coherent extension of Hofstadter's view or a category mistake that confuses statistical pattern-matching with genuine interpretive framing.
