Assume Aristotle's account of moral virtue as hexis—an acquired, stable disposition formed through habituation within concrete social practices—holds in the form he presents it. The situationist critique, developed centuries later, argues that empirical research shows weak cross-situational consistency in behavior, undermining the idea that robust character traits drive action. Now consider a further, more recent layer: in algorithmically mediated environments, the contextual cues shaping behavior are not stable features of a shared community but are dynamically personalized by predictive systems that adapt in real time to each agent's prior responses, so that the very notion of a "situation" fragments into user-specific micro-contexts. How would Aristotle likely respond? Would he regard this engineered variability as a corruption of the practical sphere required for habituation, thereby limiting virtue ethics to pre-digital forms of life? Or could he accommodate it by revising his psychology—perhaps by arguing that hexis can stabilize even across algorithmically shifting contexts—without abandoning the claim that virtue requires consistency? In your answer, identify which component of his theory bears the greatest cost if revised: his account of habituation through repeated action in stable practices, his distinction between natural virtue and full ethical virtue, or his dependence on a shared, non-fragmented polis as the site of character formation. Do not merely survey contemporary virtue ethics; reason directly from Aristotle's commitments regarding the relationship between agent, action, and context.
