At last week's aesthetics seminar, two doctoral students debated whether the enjoyment of tragic art is ethically compromised. (1) Mara argued that tragedy functions as emotional tourism: audiences seek out representations of extreme suffering—betrayal, death, ruin—precisely because the fictional frame insulates them from real moral obligations, such as consolation or justice, while still delivering cathartic relief. The suffering is not sanitized, she said, but exploited as an affective resource from which we extract pleasure without cost. (2) Leo responded that this misidentifies the object of enjoyment. We do not relish the suffering itself, but the formal intelligibility it acquires through artistic structure—the precise patterning of character, cause, and consequence. The grief is substrate, not commodity; the pleasure is cognitive, comparable to grasping a complex proof, and the emotional devastation is incidental rather than essential. (3) Mara countered that if grief were merely incidental substrate, audiences should accept tragedies with diminished emotional devastation, yet they reject such works. This shows that the devastation is essential, which implies that we indeed consume suffering for affective yield, not merely for formal appreciation. (4) Leo then introduced a distinction he called affective calibration: tragic art trains emotional responses to be proportionate and nuanced, so the pleasure lies in recognizing one's own improved moral sensitivity rather than in consuming pain. Mara dismissed this as the therapeutic fallacy—treating art as emotional hygiene—and noted that calibration still requires intense suffering to operate, so it fails to exorcise the exploitation charge.

Please adjudicate these four moves in turn: rate each from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with brief justification. Then indicate who is more right overall, and specify what both overlook—especially whether the fictional frame genuinely transforms the moral status of witnessed suffering, or whether there is a principled distinction between emotional labor directed at fictional characters and the obligations owed to real persons.
