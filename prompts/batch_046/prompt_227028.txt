I am working through whether brain size is morally relevant by testing my intuitions against concrete cases, and I want to see whether a single principle can organize them or whether some verdict must be sacrificed.

Here are my judgments. A human with severe microcephaly—brain weight near 350 grams, with profoundly limited cognition—retains full moral status equal to any neurotypical adult; brain volume is not the bearer of that status. A sperm whale, with an approximately eight-kilogram brain and rich social cognition, does not possess greater moral standing than a human infant whose brain is roughly 400 grams; if there is a difference, it favors the infant despite the whale's far larger substrate. If a neurotypical adult underwent cortical graft augmentation—integrating non-functional but structurally sound cortical tissue that tripled brain volume without altering cognitive architecture, phenomenal experience, or personality—I do not believe moral status would shift in any direction. Comparing two patients with advanced neurodegeneration, one with marked cerebral atrophy reducing volume by thirty percent but with preserved islands of conscious awareness, and another with intact brain volume but diffuse synaptic degradation producing permanent unconsciousness, I judge the atrophied patient to retain full moral status and the other to lack it; here size tracks the wrong feature.

However, I remain genuinely unsettled about a hypothetical engineered species with brains of twenty kilograms and an extremely high cortical redundancy coefficient—massive parallel replication of identical processing modules—running a cognitive architecture isomorphic to human consciousness but with far greater throughput and resilience. I cannot decide whether the sheer scale of redundant substrate confers any additional moral weight, or whether this is ethically equivalent to a single human mind instantiated in parallel.

Finally, two individuals with identical cognitive profiles and conscious lives, but one with a naturally larger brain due solely to greater glial density and white-matter volume rather than neuronal count, strike me as having exactly equal moral standing.

Is there a coherent principle—perhaps one that distinguishes functional architecture and phenomenal integration from substrate scale, modified by some redundancy-sensitive or threshold-sensitive clause—that accommodates these verdicts? Which judgment should be overturned for the sake of parsimony? What does the best account predict for the high-redundancy engineered case I described, and for a borderline scenario not listed: a brain organoid cultivated to adult-human volume with sustained electrophysiological patterns but lacking sensory integration or behavioral embedding?
