I am drafting an essay for my philosophy elective on practical rationality, and I need a precisely engineered thought experiment built to specification. Please construct a realistic, one-paragraph variant of Kavka's toxin puzzle—no omniscient predictors, no science-fiction technology, and no exotic physics. The reward should be an ordinary but substantial sum, such as a full college scholarship, offered on the condition that the agent genuinely intends, by a specific deadline, to drink a real but harmless emetic that causes only temporary, intense discomfort; she need not actually drink it, and no external party enforces compliance.

Design the case so that two theories of rational agency deliver opposite recommendations about whether to form the intention: (1) a diachronic commitment view, according to which a formed intention normatively binds one's future self to execution; and (2) a standard expected-utility view, according to which intentions are evaluated only by their causal and evidential contribution to welfare. Specifically, the commitment view should counsel against forming the intention, whereas the expected-utility view should counsel in favor.

After presenting the scenario, walk through the reasoning each theory gives, identify the structural feature that drives the divergence—such as whether future choice-points are treated as independently optimizable—state whether removing that feature collapses the case back into the standard toxin puzzle or produces a different paradox, and provide an honest assessment of how a defender of the theory that comes out worse would attack the case and whether that attack undermines the split. The purpose is to isolate exactly what makes self-binding intentions rationally problematic, not merely to restate the original puzzle.
