I am a high school student organizing a 75-minute workshop for my school's philosophy club—approximately fourteen participants, ages 16 to 18—on ethical reasoning about non-human animals. I want a runnable classroom activity, not a lecture, that makes the group feel the structural force of wild-animal suffering before anyone names the philosophical positions involved. To avoid a generic debate, please center the design on a niche framing I will call trophic-level welfare incommensurability combined with the replacement dynamic: the idea that reducing suffering in wild populations often requires reducing the number of individuals who come to exist, and that welfare metrics for predators (typically deprivation-based) and prey (typically violence-based) may be causally incommensurable rather than merely different in magnitude.

Design a structured deliberation or role-play with four assigned roles—an interventionist welfare ecologist, a non-interventionist rights advocate, a local conservation manager bound by ecological-integrity mandates, and a welfare economist tasked with aggregation—set in a concrete localized scenario (for example, a wetland system facing parasite-driven prey mortality and predator population stress). The rules must require unanimous agreement to authorize any intervention, but each role should operate with a distinct success metric that cannot be translated into the others' terms, and any proposed action should trigger a visible replacement effect that undermines at least one other role's goals. Include the exact scenario, role cards with hidden incentives, the decision procedure, a minute-by-minute timeline for the 75 minutes, and the materials needed.

The debrief should map specific moments in the activity onto parts of the philosophical problem—where incommensurability appeared, where replacement dynamics blocked intuitive solutions, and what coordination failure occurred. It must also include an honest note on which feature of wild-animal-suffering ethics the activity cannot reproduce (for instance, the evolutionary-scale magnitude of suffering or empirical uncertainty about invertebrate sentience) and a prediction of what will predictably go wrong the first time the activity is run, with instructions for handling it.

Finally, in the debrief and in any framing of the stakes, please separate what is settled—such as the broad acceptance in cognitive ethology that many wild animals experience suffering—from what is genuinely contested among knowledgeable people, such as whether this generates a practical duty of large-scale ecological intervention, and do not present a contested view as the consensus. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the facilitator will have no web access during the session.
