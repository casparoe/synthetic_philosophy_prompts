I'm delivering the opening remarks at a retirement symposium for the university's chief registrar, who spent thirty-five years speaking the institution into being — conferring degrees, validating records, and maintaining the archive of authority. The room is split evenly: philosophers of language and institutional theory on one side, her family and staff on the other. I want the speech to use Austin's performative/constative distinction as its structural spine, not as ornament: it should explain why her career was never merely bureaucratic but a continuous enactment of institutional reality, and it should recognize that this very address is itself a performative — a ceremonial withdrawal of her institutional voice. To keep it from being a reheated summary, frame the work through a specific conceptual distinction introduced for the occasion: event-performatives (one-time state changes, like conferring a degree) versus maintenance-performatives (the largely invisible, repeated speech acts that keep an institution valid over time — verification, record-keeping, and the silent affirmation that past conferrals still hold). The speech should show that her life was almost entirely the latter, and it should close by actually performing the transition: using formal, ceremonial language that passes her authority rather than merely describing it. Keep it under eight minutes, include both audiences without lecturing either, and do not include citations, reading lists, or references to secondary literature.
