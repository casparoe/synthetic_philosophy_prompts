I have come to accept Dennett's multiple drafts model of consciousness—the view that there is no Cartesian theater where sensory inputs converge into a unified stream, but rather a distributed set of parallel, competing narratives that are constantly being edited and revised, with consciousness being the post-hoc story the brain tells itself about what was already processed. I find it convincing partly because it dissolves the hard problem by denying the premise that there is a single, unified phenomenal field that needs explaining, yet I worry I may have insulated the view from criticism by interpreting every apparent counterexample as just another draft being revised.

What would genuinely count against this model—not merely the familiar objections about the unity of consciousness or about subjective phenomenal quality, but specific empirical findings, phenomenological reports, or conceptual distinctions that defenders of the model would themselves concede as damaging? In particular, I am interested in whether results from split-brain research, the temporal dynamics of backward masking, or reports from neuropsychological conditions such as anosognosia for hemiplegia pose a live threat rather than a mere puzzle to be accommodated. I also want to know whether the model risks unfalsifiability: if any pattern of neural activation can be redescribed as multiple drafts in competition, has the view purchased its explanatory flexibility at the cost of predictive emptiness, and is that a fatal flaw for a theory of consciousness or only an awkward feature? Please rank the potential defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and indicate which ones a committed multiple-drafts theorist would acknowledge as genuinely counting against the view rather than dismissing out of hand.
