I am an undergraduate taking a course in social epistemology, and I have observed a recurring pattern in seminar discussions that I do not know how to classify. When a small group of students is deeply committed to a particular normative framework — for instance, a specific account of deliberative democracy — they rarely dismiss objections outright. Instead, they maintain an informal, shared taxonomy of standard misunderstandings: each counter-argument is quickly assigned to a pre-established category, such as being a confusion of procedural fairness with substantive equality, or a presupposition of aggregative rationality, and once categorised it is treated as already answered by a canonical reply linked to that box. New categories are added only slowly, after a particular objection has recurred several times, and members seem to value fluency in the taxonomy itself. I want to know whether this practice — building and maintaining an objection-classification system that intercepts dissent before it reaches the theory's core — has an established name within work on echo chambers or epistemic bubbles. How does it differ from an ordinary auxiliary-hypothesis defence, from motivated reasoning, and from the mechanisms usually described in the echo-chamber literature? If no precise concept exists, what conceptual boundaries would separate it from those neighbours, and what term might best capture it?
