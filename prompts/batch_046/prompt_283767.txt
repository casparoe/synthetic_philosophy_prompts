I'm running a philosophy of epistemology seminar next semseter and I'm trying to figure out if I can use 12 Angry Men to make the conciliationism vs. steadfastness debate feel less like a logic puzzle and more like an actual human problem. The film has one juror — Juror 8 — holding out against eleven others on a murder conviction, and eventually he brings them all around. Usually people read this as a triumph of rational persuasion, but I want to ask whether he's actually violating conciliationist norms: if you're an epistemic peer to eleven other people and they all think you're wrong, shouldn't you lower your confidence?

Read the film through that lens, but also through this other idea I ran into — some people distinguish 'surface disagreement' about the verdict from 'deep disagreement' about what counts as a good reason in the first place. In 12 Angry Men they all hear the same testimony about the switchblade and the old man's walk, but they bring totally different life experiences to it. Is Juror 8 being steadfast in a justified way because the disagreement is deep, or is the film actually showing conciliation happening slowly as they align their reasoning standards? I want a reading that doesn't just make the movie an illustration — it should say where the film gets the philosphy wrong, maybe by making Juror 8 obviously right from the start, and whether the ending supports conciliation or steadfastness once everyone actually shares his view. What scenes should I point my students to — the knife demonstration, the old man's testimony — to show where peer status breaks down?
