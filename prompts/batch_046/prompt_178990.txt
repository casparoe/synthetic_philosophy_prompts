Below are the notes for a short essay I am drafting on the conceptual architecture of multi-level governance. I would welcome your comments, especially on the coherence of the distinction I draw, the aptness of the comparative framing, and whether I overlook significant counterarguments.

The essay contends that the conventional interpretation of subsidiarity—assigning authority to the lowest level capable of effective action—collapses two independent criteria: democratic proximity to affected populations, and institutional capacity for credible long-term commitment. I propose disentangling them. The first criterion, which I call proximity subsidiarity, tracks geographic and participatory closeness. The second, commitment subsidiarity, tracks whether a given governmental level can bind itself across electoral cycles to sustain long-horizon investments.

Consider climate-adaptation infrastructure. Municipal authorities score high on proximity but frequently face short electoral horizons, balanced-budget rules, and limited borrowing capacity that undermine multi-decade commitments. National or supranational bodies may therefore be appropriate not primarily because spillovers cross borders—though they might—but because multiannual budgetary frameworks or treaty-locked obligations provide stronger commitment devices. I compare three systems. In the United States, federal influence operates largely through conditional grants, yet direct federal implementation is constrained by anti-commandeering doctrines; the result is a structural gap when state-level commitment mechanisms are weak. In Switzerland, cantonal debt brakes reinforced by direct-democratic referendum rules appear to combine proximity with credible commitment, suggesting that the two criteria need not conflict. In Germany, by contrast, executive federalism assigns implementation largely to the Länder, but their fiscal dependence on federal revenue-sharing and joint decision-making in the Bundesrat means that commitment capacity is dispersed rather than localized, producing a different tension.

The essay asks whether EU subsidiarity review—currently focused on cross-border effects and democratic proximity—should explicitly incorporate an assessment of commitment capacity. I am particularly interested in whether such an incorporation can be principled, or whether it inevitably becomes a blanket justification for upward centralization.

Please comment on whether this framework is conceptually sound, whether the comparison across the US, German, and Swiss cases is valid despite their different party structures and fiscal architectures, and whether you can identify a principled boundary that prevents commitment-based reasoning from swallowing proximity-based reasoning entirely. I am also interested in whether there are hidden empirical premises in the climate-adaptation example that weaken the argument.
