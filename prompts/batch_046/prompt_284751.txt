Consider the contemporary debate concerning whether outputs from generative image and language systems constitute art in a sense that bears on cultural value, legal authorship, and artistic practice. To move beyond the well-rehearsed opposition between human intentionality and statistical pattern-matching, frame the issue through three somewhat under-examined distinctions: first, between "procedural surprise"—unexpected aesthetic outcomes that emerge from a model's internal dynamics rather than from explicit instruction—and "authorial risk"—the exposure of a maker's judgement to failure and revision; second, between "generative production" and "curatorial selection" as potential loci of artistic expression; and third, between the "material substrate" of an artwork, understood here as a fixed object, and its "probabilistic substrate," understood as a sampling process that produces non-identical instantiations.

Within this framing, identify which open questions or lines of work should be prioritised by researchers, policymakers, and practitioners over the coming decade. Specifically, evaluate the following four candidates: (A) whether procedural surprise can ground aesthetic value independently of human intentionality; (B) whether copyright and moral-rights frameworks can coherently cover curatorial selection from generative spaces without either extending full protection to prompts or collapsing outputs into the public domain; (C) how the measurability of "creative contribution" ought to inform funding, accreditation, and exhibition criteria in public cultural institutions; and (D) whether the shift from manual production to prompt-engineering and post-selection undermines the social and economic role of the artist in ways that require structural policy response.

Provide a defended prioritisation: state your ordering, specify the criteria you are using (for example, tractability with current conceptual and legal tools, dependency—whether resolving one question unlocks or renders moot others—neglectedness relative to existing literature, and practical urgency for institutions), and articulate the trade-offs between them. For instance, if resolving (B) would largely settle (A) for legal purposes, or if (C) depends on a prior answer to (D), make that dependency explicit. Additionally, indicate which of these lines you would deprioritise on the grounds that resolving them would not substantially alter practice or institutional design, and specify what empirical or conceptual development would cause you to revise your ranking. Write your response in British English, and do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
