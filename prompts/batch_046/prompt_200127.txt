You are a philosopher of social science specializing in Weberian sociology and the philosophy of explanation, and you are constructing a teaching instrument for advanced students. Before you write anything, take ten minutes of internal reasoning: first, list the logical combinations of answers to the core questions about whether values cause economic outcomes, at what level they operate, and whether they need individual psychological presence; second, locate the eight named positions below inside that space; third, identify which combinations are empty and why. Only then present the final instrument.

Design a decision-tree taxonomy—exactly five binary branch nodes, yielding eight terminal leaves—that sorts a respondent’s actual commitments on whether culture can be a cause, using Max Weber’s thesis about the Protestant ethic and the spirit of capitalism as the central diagnostic case but never as a decorative example. The audience is an advanced undergraduate or early graduate seminar: they know that Weber argued an ascetic religious orientation contributed to modern capitalist behavior, but they do not know this taxonomy in advance.

Every branch node must present a concrete counterfactual case or claim that a respondent can answer without knowing which label it leads to. Do not ask “Are you a Weberian?” or “Do you reject materialism?” Instead, pose scenarios such as whether capital accumulation would persist if the religious framework disappeared but trade networks and property law remained intact, or whether a geographically isolated community with identical ascetic values but no market access would develop rationalized accounting practices.

The tree must be built from the logical space outward: present the five diagnostic questions in sequence, show how their yes/no combinations generate eight cells, then place the positions in those cells. You must include these eight named leaves:
1. Weberian elective affinity: values and economic structures co-evolve through non-reductive mutual fit.
2. Strong cultural mechanism: values directly reshape individual preferences and institutional rules in a causal chain.
3. Institutional-filter selection: culture operates only as an environment that selects among pre-existing economic practices, not as an originator.
4. Epiphenomenal rationalization: values are post-hoc justifications for behavior driven entirely by material interest and structural constraint.
5. Interpretive meaning-constitution: values make capitalist behavior intelligible but do not causally produce it; explanation is understanding, not causal mechanism.
6. Structural homology: religious values and capitalist rationality are co-expressive manifestations of a deeper social logic, neither causing the other.
7. Value-rationalization of material interest (hybrid): actors genuinely hold values, but those values survive only when they align with pre-existing material advantages, so values appear causal but are actually filtered interests in disguise.
8. Material determinism with no cultural remainder: economic structure alone explains the form and emergence of capitalism; culture is purely decorative.

Inject one niche conceptual distinction not standard in introductory treatments: the representation problem. At one node, ask whether values must be individually internalized and psychologically present to constrain action, or whether they can operate as collective representations that shape behavior even when individuals are not consciously motivated by them. Use this split to separate leaves that would otherwise overlap.

After placing all eight positions, explicitly flag any pair of leaves that your five-node tree cannot genuinely separate—state which nodes fail to distinguish them and whether the overlap is due to conceptual similarity or to the tree’s resolution limits. Then identify every logically possible combination of answers across the five nodes that remains unoccupied. For each empty cell, briefly diagnose whether it is empty because the combination is conceptually incoherent, practically unattractive, or merely unexplored.

Keep all references to Weber’s argument in paraphrase; do not put any real passage in quotation marks, and do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citation requests. Do not depend on obscure empirical facts—such as specific seventeenth-century Geneva archival records—that a model without web access could not verify. Finally, present a scoring key that maps each of the eight answer-patterns onto its corresponding leaf, with one sentence per pattern explaining why that sequence of answers belongs to that view.
