I am a novelist defending a thesis at an interdisciplinary colloquium on cognition and culture. I argue that cross-cultural variation in philosophical intuition—specifically regarding attributions of moral responsibility—is not merely noise around universal cognitive modules, but is systematically scaffolded by narrative form: cultures shaped by dominant linear, agent-centered plot structures tend to yield stronger internalist intuitions, while those shaped by cyclical, relational narrative traditions tend toward contextualist or distributed attributions. I claim that literary form operates as an implicit conceptual framework, and that standard survey instruments miss this structural layer by treating intuition as context-independent.

The audience comprises experimental philosophers and cognitive anthropologists who conduct large-scale cross-cultural surveys. They are methodological universalists: they regard observed cultural variance as surface noise over deep, shared moral-psychological architecture, and they distrust explanations invoking “literary culture” without operationalized variables. Several have worked on cross-population studies of intentional-action asymmetries; none specialize in narrative theory. They care about replication, representative sampling, and protection against just-so stories.

Prepare my Q&A defense sheet. Order the objections by how likely and damaging they are, phrased exactly as these researchers would put them—using their vocabulary of modules, noise, operationalization, and confounds. For each, indicate what I should concede up front, what I must hold, a concise reply deliverable in about two minutes, and a frank note on where that reply is weakest. State plainly the weakest point in my own position—that I have not yet isolated narrative form experimentally from other socio-cultural variables—rather than hoping they overlook it. Include the one objection I am hoping nobody raises: whether my claim is even empirically falsifiable with existing survey instruments. Do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
