I've noticed a recurring pattern in moral judgment that I can't place within the standard vocabulary of moral responsibility, and I'm unsure whether it corresponds to an established concept or needs a new name.

Consider three cases that share a common causal structure but receive different moral responses. In the first, a person was raised in conditions of extreme deprivation and consequently never developed the emotional capacity to recognize the suffering of others; when they harm someone through callousness, observers tend to agree that resentment is inappropriate and should be replaced by an objective, explanatory stance—much as Strawson describes—because the deficit lies in a general emotional mechanism rather than in a particular choice. In the second, a person was raised in an isolated community with no access to certain factual or ethical information and consequently holds a sincerely false belief—say, about the moral status of a particular practice—that leads them to cause harm. Although the informational gap was produced by the same kind of external, formative conditions as the emotional deficit, observers often resist full exemption; they treat the agent as still appropriate for reactive attitudes, or at least as not fully excused, because the deficit concerns the content of belief rather than the machinery of feeling. In the third, a person has a neurological condition that suppresses a specific practical mechanism—risk-aversion or impulse-control—leading to reckless harm; here, observers again tend toward exemption, treating the failure as belonging to the architecture of agency rather than to the particular beliefs that guided the act.

The pattern is this: suspension of reactive attitudes seems reliably triggered by deficits in general capacities—emotional, volitional, or cognitive mechanisms—while being resisted for deficits in specific informational content, even when both are equally involuntary, equally determined by upbringing or biology, and equally causally responsible for the harm. The boundary tracks whether the failure is in how an agent processes moral reality or in what particular reality they happen to have encountered.

Is there an established term for this asymmetry—the differential application of Strawsonian exemption based on capacity failure versus content failure? If so, how is it distinguished from the broader objective-participant distinction, which seems to apply to persons as a whole rather than selectively to particular kinds of internal deficits? And if there is no established term, can you define and delimit the phenomenon precisely: specifying its boundary conditions, distinguishing it from standard discussions of ignorance and excuse, and indicating whether it applies symmetrically across emotional, cognitive, and conative domains?
