I was arguing with a friend after our philosophy of relgion semniar about whether anatta actually does any real work, and I think I accidentally built a reductio that makes the whole doctrine eat itself. Here's the chain I walked us into, step by step:

(1) Anatta denies an enduring self, leaving only momentary skandhas.
(2) But if there's literally no subject persisting even for a single moment, then there's no owner of present experience either - consciousness is just ownerless viññāṇa arising and falling.
(3) If experiences have no owner, then dukkha isn't happening to anyone; it's just an unpleasant quality of certain mental states with no subject of harm.
(4) So when Buddhism talks about suffering and its cessation, it's describing a problem with no victim that needs saving.
(5) Meanwhile karma requires actions belong to an agent who can reap them later; without a subject, karma collapses into impersonal causation with moral labels stuck on.
(6) So anatta either makes Buddhism's goals incoherent, or it secretly relies on a reconstructed self it claims to reject.
(7) The only escape is a 'conventional self' real enough for ethics and practice, but that just pushes the question back: why deny the ultimate self if the conventional one does all the work?
(8) So the doctrine proves too much - it undermines suffering, karma, and liberation at once.

I really want to keep (1) and (3) because they feel like the heart of the teaching, but I can't see how to hold them without (4) and (5) crashing in. Which step should I reject? Is there a standard reply that doesn't just smuggle the self back in? My friend thinks I'm mixing up diachronic and synchronic emptiness, but I'm not sure where the line is - maybe that's exactly where the argument slips? I don't need a bibliography, just where the exit is and what it costs to take it.
