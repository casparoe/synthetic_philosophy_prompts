Having spent thirty years in systems neuroscience before retiring, I have recently turned to philosophy of mind, particularly Douglas Hofstadter’s claim that the self is a strange loop — a recursive causal pattern that need not be tethered to a single biological substrate. I am drawn to his suggestion that when we intimately know another person, fragments of their loop may persist inside our own brains, yet I worry this idea collapses into absurdity if pushed rigorously. Here is the argument I have built, which seems valid but reaches a conclusion I cannot accept.

(1) If the self is strictly a recursive information pattern, then the particular neurons that realize it are metaphysically irrelevant; persistence depends only on structural fidelity, not on bodily continuity. (2) Inter-brain synchronization studies indicate that during deep joint attention, one brain can converge on the predictive dynamics characteristic of another, thereby realizing a substantial portion of the other’s causal loop rather than merely representing it. (3) So when brain B achieves this convergence with brain A, a live fragment of A’s self genuinely persists inside B, distributed across both substrates. (4) By the same reasoning, anyone who deeply internalizes a biography and allows its narrative structure to reshape their own predictive processing instantiates fragments of the deceased subject; thus the dead live on in every attentive reader. (5) If self-fragments are freely transferable and multiply instantiated without boundary, then no individual loop can be isolated, and personal identity dissolves into an undifferentiated collective consciousness containing thousands of overlapping selves. (6) That dissolution is false, since practical and moral life presupposes distinguishable agents.

If I must preserve premises, I would keep (1) and (2), but I need to know which step actually fails. Please respond as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction or conclusion. Identify the weakest premise, explain why it initially seemed compatible with Hofstadter’s framework, and describe what abandoning it costs for the possibility that an “I” might genuinely survive distributed across brains.
