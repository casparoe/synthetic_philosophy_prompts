In my normative-ethics reading group, the question of whether genuine moral dilemmas are possible has stalled despite full agreement on every canonical case. One side holds that in situations of jointly unsatisfiable, equally weighted obligations—imagine a physician with one life-saving dose and two identical patients—there remains a rational, morally appropriate regret that tracks an actually violated binding duty, not merely a psychological residue; they call this an "unexhausted moral remainder" with diachronic half-life, meaning the overridden obligation continues to generate normative claims even after the decisive choice is made. The other side, working within a Rossian or formal deontic framework, insists that all conflicts are resolved at the all-things-considered level, leaving exactly one duty; they treat post-choice regret as either non-rational grief or a misdescription of an unfulfilled prima facie reason whose prescriptive force is fully extinguished once the decisive reason is identified. Both camps accept the phenomenology, the logic of the cases, and the reality of the agent's torn experience, yet neither has moved in years. Is the disagreement substantive—about whether reasons can survive being overridden—or verbal, turning on whether "obligation" is reserved for decisive duties only? Or are the sides answering different questions, one about momentary action-guidance and one about retrospective evaluation? Diagnose the structure of the impasse—hidden empirical claim, standard of evidence, bedrock intuition, or ill-posed framing—and specify what distinction, formal result, or conceptual clarification would count as progress, or defend the view that none should be expected.
