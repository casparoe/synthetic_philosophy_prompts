In a graduate seminar on the metaphysics of intellectual property, we were debating whether formal algorithms—purely abstract structures—can coherently be treated as ownable commodities. One interlocutor repeatedly invoked the maxim that "ideas want to be free" to reject any licensing regime for mathematical methods, yet it remained entirely unclear what work the phrase was actually doing. Could you lay out the distinct precise claims this slogan might be expressing—distinguishing, for example, an empirical thesis about creator motivation, an ontological thesis about the non-rivalrous nature of abstract objects, a normative welfare claim about information dissemination, and a conceptual thesis about the category mistake of applying property-language to non-physical entities?

For each interpretation, indicate whether it is trivial, substantively defensible, false, or purely rhetorical, and specify which ones, if any, genuinely entail that proprietary restrictions on algorithms are illegitimate. I am especially interested in whether people who repeat the slogan are committing a hidden conceptual slippage—treating "freedom" in the legal sense of absence of constraint as if it followed from "freedom" in an ontological sense of being intrinsically unbound—and whether that slippage survives once we separate ownership of a particular token expression from control over an abstract type.
