The essay distinguishes between what I call controlled mental parameter drift and its uncontrolled counterpart. In the controlled case, we imagine altering exactly one feature of a physical system—such as removing air resistance in Galileo's comparison of falling bodies of different weights—while holding constant every structural constraint that is anchored by invariant laws, so that the mental model reveals a dependency that survives abstraction. I treat Galileo's case not as an empirical forecast but as a structural demonstration that gravitational acceleration is independent of mass once resistive forces are held away, and I treat Einstein's elevator thought experiment similarly: local indistinguishability between free fall and inertial motion holds only because curvature terms are mentally fixed at zero, revealing the equivalence principle rather than inventing it. By contrast, I argue that philosophical thought experiments—such as Putnam's twin-earth scenario or variations on the trolley problem—typically exhibit uncontrolled drift, because altering social, linguistic, or psychological conditions does not leave behind a rigid network of conservation laws that could anchor the mental model, which is why they clarify concepts without delivering scientific knowledge.
