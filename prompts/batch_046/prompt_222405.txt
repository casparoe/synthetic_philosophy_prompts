Design a decision tree that classifies positions on the wrong-kind-of-reasons problem in moral philosophy, intended for use in a graduate seminar without relying on prior familiarity with the standard taxonomy. Start from a single concrete scenario: an agent performs a rescue that saves a life, but does so entirely to avoid being publicly shamed; they feel no concern for the victim and would not have acted had the audience been absent.

Structure the tree as a sequence of four binary branch nodes, each phrased as a substantive claim about the case rather than as a philosophical label. The first node should ask whether the agent's deficiency is best characterized as a failure of rationality, a failure of moral virtue, or a failure of moral understanding. The second should ask whether the correction of that deficiency requires an internal restructuring of the agent's motives or can be achieved through external alignment—such as institutional incentives—without any motivational transformation. The third should ask whether the wrong-kind-reasons problem applies symmetrically to prudential and epistemic norms, or whether morality is uniquely affected. The fourth should ask whether a wrong-kind reason can ever be converted into a right-kind reason through reflective endorsement or social scaffolding, or whether the kind is fixed by origin.

Each leaf must specify the combination of answers that reaches it, name the position if one is standardly defended (for example, strict moral rationalism, externalist justificationism, normative generalism, moral particularism about motivation, or the transformative endorsement view), or mark it "unnamed." For each named leaf, provide in one sentence its most awkward commitment—the consequence its defenders must accept but find hardest to defend. Then map the full logical space: identify any combination of answers that yields no named position, flag it explicitly, and diagnose in a brief clause whether the gap is likely incoherent, merely unattractive, or unexplored. Finally, note any pair of named positions that the tree's questions cannot distinguish, and explain which additional distinction would be needed to separate them. Do not include bibliographic references, reading lists, or requests for citations; the instrument should function solely through conceptual analysis of the case and the logical structure of the answers.
