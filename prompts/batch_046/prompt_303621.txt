After a long and rather exhausting argument with a friend who works in development economics, I am now convinced I need to be far better prepared for the questions I will actually face rather than for the ones I imagine in my own head. Next Thursday I am presenting a seminar paper—tentatively concerned with the normative architecture of reciprocal obligation—to a room composed mainly of comparative political anthropologists and historians of Southeast Asian and West African state formation, plus one development economist and a legal scholar who specializes in customary law. The audience is ethnographically literate, deeply skeptical of universalist moral philosophy, and generally regards corruption as an analytically blunt instrument; they are familiar with the broad patron-client literature but tend to view any attempt to rehabilitate clientelism with suspicion, assuming it either romanticizes inequality or serves neoliberal governance agendas. My thesis is that we should distinguish allocative corruption—the diversion of public resources for private gain—from what I call constitutive clientelism, in which bureaucratic office is not a pre-existing structure that gets captured but is itself constituted through ritualized gift-exchange protocols that generate reciprocal obligations structurally analogous to diplomatic hospitality rather than to market bribery. I further argue that Western anti-corruption frameworks illegitimately universalize a specific historical separation between public office and private kinship that emerged from particular European state-formation processes, and that applying this standard to societies where office and obligation are co-constituted commits a category error that misdescribes the normative logic of the practice.

My friend pressed me on whether I was simply redefining corruption away, and whether my framework could still condemn clear abuses—such as when a minister channels disaster-relief funds to a lineage group using precisely these same ritual protocols. I need a prep sheet for the Q&A: the eight or so objections this specific audience will actually raise, ordered by how damaging they are to my core argument, each phrased in the vocabulary they would use rather than in generic philosophy terms, with a two-minute reply and an explicit note on where that reply is weakest. I want to know what I should concede up front—probably that some practices I call constitutive clientelism can indeed become vehicles for severe harm—and which objections have real ethnographic or conceptual force versus which ones are largely rhetorical. Crucially, include the one objection I am hoping nobody asks: the question about whether my framework, if adopted by an international lending institution, would functionally license the very extractive dynamics that anti-corruption regimes were designed to stop. I need to reply without sounding like I am defending corruption, and I need to know exactly where my reply collapses.
