I'm grading essays for an upper-level philosophy of logic seminar. The exam prompt was: "Frege's late personal diary—published after his death and containing anti-democratic, nationalist, and anti-Semitic entries—raises a biographical problem. Does it bear on his anti-psychologism and his claim that thoughts (Gedanken) are objective, mind-independent entities? Assess whether there is any philosophical tension, beyond mere hypocrisy, and be precise about what kind of relevance (logical, hermeneutic, or none) is at stake."

Here is one student answer (approx. 320 words):

Frege's anti-psychologism holds that logical laws govern objective relations among thoughts, not mental processes. Because thoughts exist independently of any particular mind, the psychological makeup of the thinker—including political prejudice—cannot affect the validity of logical arguments. Therefore, Frege's late diary, which expresses subjective and morally reprehensible views, is biographically significant but philosophically irrelevant to his logic.

One might object that Frege's objectivity requires that thoughts be graspable by any rational being. His diary suggests he did not regard all people as equally rational or worthy of democratic participation. If logical universalism implies universal access to reason, then denying that access to specific groups conflicts with the spirit of his philosophy. But this objection confuses levels: Frege's universalism concerns the form and content of thoughts, not the distribution of rational capacities across populations. Even if he wrongly restricted political agency, logical truths remain valid for any mind capable of grasping them.

A more serious worry is that Frege's concept of "objectivity" is defined only negatively, as independence from particular mental states, without specifying the positive conditions for grasping. His political writings might reveal an implicit assumption that "rational being" refers to members of a specific cultural group rather than to minds as such. If so, his philosophy of logic might depend on a restricted conception of reason. However, there is no direct evidence in Frege's logical texts that he restricted the domain of logical subjects in this way. Without explicit textual support, we should treat the diary as extraneous to the system. Thus, the diary does not undermine anti-psychologism, though it complicates our assessment of Frege's consistency.
