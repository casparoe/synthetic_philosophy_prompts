I just finished a long and somewhat frustrating argument with a close friend who teaches comparative constitutional law. She kept insisting that every exam on federalism and subsidiarity ends up being a memorization test—students recite the US Tenth Amendment framework, Germany’s concurrent-competence rules, the Swiss cantonal sovereignty provisions, and the EU treaty-based proportionality review without ever confronting the underlying conceptual tension. We were specifically debating whether subsidiarity actually does normative work or whether it is just a post-hoc rhetorical device that courts and legislatures invoke after reaching a politically convenient outcome. I am now writing the final exam for my upper-level seminar on Federalism and Subsidiarity across the US, Germany, Switzerland, and the EU, and I want to design one essay question that cannot be answered by institutional summary alone.

Please draft a 90-minute exam essay question aimed at advanced undergraduates or first-year graduate students. The scenario should be conceptually rigorous but hypothetical: a transboundary alpine watershed management authority must decide whether to impose uniform pollution controls, and in the dispute a Swiss canton invokes direct-democratic veto mechanisms, a German Land asserts its constitutional role in concurrent legislation, and an EU-level agency claims that cross-border environmental externalities justify centralized harmonization. The question should require students to evaluate which level should govern, but specifically to distinguish among three different conceptions of subsidiarity—democratic proximity, administrative efficiency, and constitutional identity—and to show precisely where these conceptions conflict rather than converge. It must be impossible to answer well by merely describing the four polities’ institutional structures.

Along with the question, provide a model answer of approximately 750 words. The answer should not be a generic summary; it must contain four specific analytical moves: first, identifying why democratic proximity fails when externalities cross jurisdictional boundaries; second, showing how the German concurrent-competence framework generates a structurally different problem from Swiss cantonal sovereignty; third, explaining why EU proportionality review operates differently from US federal-preemption analysis; and fourth, defending a specific resolution while explicitly acknowledging the normative cost to at least one conception of subsidiarity. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, author citations, or verbatim quotations—the response should be answerable entirely through conceptual reasoning. Finally, provide a marking scheme of no more than 300 words. It should clearly separate an A answer—which executes all four moves with precision and recognizes the conflict between conceptions—from a B answer—which describes institutions accurately but applies only one or two conceptions superficially—and a C answer—which summarizes federal structures without engaging the conceptual tension. Name specific mistakes that should cost marks, such as conflating subsidiarity with simple devolution, treating EU and US multilevel review as structurally identical, or ignoring the direct-democracy dimension in the Swiss case. Keep the model answer within 750 words, the marking scheme within 300 words, and design the essay question so that a complete response fits within about 1,000 words for a 90-minute exam.
