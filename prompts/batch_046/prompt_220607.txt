I've noticed a recurring argumentative pattern in public and academic discussions about generational cohorts—say, debates over whether "Gen Z," "Millennials," or "Baby Boomers" possess distinctive moral psychologies, economic dispositions, or political temperaments. A speaker will attribute some broad trait to an entire cohort as if it were an essential, causally potent property—"Gen Z is structurally incapable of long-term planning because of their digital-native cognition," or "Boomers, as a generation, will never accept climate regulation because it threatens their identity." The cohort is treated as a genuine explanatory kind, with shared internal nature. But when confronted with clear counterexamples—a twenty-five-year-old with conventional career ambitions, or a seventy-year-old lifelong climate activist—the same speaker retreats: "Of course generations are just statistical artifacts, arbitrary fifteen-year bins; I'm only describing aggregate trends, not every individual." Then, often within the same paragraph, they revert to essentialist language: "But the Boomer generation, by its very nature, resists..." I am not asking about stereotyping in general, nor about whether cohort studies are sociologically valid. I want to know whether there is a specific philosophical or conceptual label for this precise double movement—treating a demographic bracket as an ontologically robust kind for explanatory and normative work, while treating it as a mere conventional grouping whenever it faces disconfirmation. How does it differ from simple reification, from the ecological fallacy, and from a motte-and-bailey defense? If no precise term captures it, I want you to propose one, define it formally, and specify its boundary conditions—distinguishing it from legitimate statistical generalization on one side and from ordinary group prejudice on the other.
