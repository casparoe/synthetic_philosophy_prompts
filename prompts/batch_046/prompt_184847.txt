I've been mulling over those arguments about transformative experience - the kind where people say you can't rationally choose to become a parent or join a religious order because you can't know what it's like from the inside until you're there. There's a particular asymmetry I keep noticing in how these debates are run, and I'm not sure whether it's part of the standard problem or something else entirely.

So, take the parenting case. Beforehand, the argument goes that your current preferences are built on ignorance, so you shouldn't treat them as authoritative - you just dont have the data. But once someone has actually become a parent, their testimony is suddenly treated as the real deal: "you'll understand when you're there". Meanwhile, if the pre-parent self said explicitly "I don't want my values and priorities rewritten", that's dismissed as exactly the kind of ignorance the argument warned about. The post-parent self gets epistemic authority to validate the choice, but the pre-parent self doesn't get authority to refuse the rewrite.

Same pattern with religious conversion: the pre-conversion self's reluctance is framed as blindness, but the converted self's endorsement is framed as insight. It feels like there's an implicit rule that the "later" version is the legitimate heir to the decision, whilst the whole point was that personal continuity or value-stability is precisely what's disrupted. Is there a name for this specific authority asymmetry? Is it just baked into the usual transformative-experience account, or is it closer to adaptive preference working in reverse, or maybe a kind of hindsight bias dressed up? And if it doesnt have its own label, how should I think about its boundaries - like, does it require actual value-change, or does it show up whenever we grant future selves veto power over present ones?

Not after a bibliography, just whether this carving already exists and exactly where its edges sit.
