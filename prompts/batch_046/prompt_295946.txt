I'm preparing a long-form feature on democratic legitimacy and recently attended a lecture in which a political philosopher summarized the boundary problem—who belongs to the demos—like this: "The standard account holds that democratic membership should be defined by the all-subjected principle, which includes anyone living under the coercive authority of the state. By contrast, the all-affected interests principle holds that anyone impacted by collective decisions should have a say. The lecturer argued that defenders of the all-affected view implicitly sever democracy's normative link to coercion, because if being affected is sufficient, then future generations, foreign nationals, and even non-human animals with welfare interests become members, rendering the boundary concept useless. Meanwhile, the all-subjected view keeps the boundary tight and coherent, though it straightforwardly excludes future generations since they are not yet coerced. Therefore, anyone who favors the broader principle must accept that democracy is no longer specifically about self-rule by those bound by law, but about general stakeholder management."

The framing strikes me as suspiciously neat—particularly the claim that all-affected theorists abandon the coercion link entirely, the treatment of future generations as an obvious exclusion rather than a hard limit case, and the implication that the two principles are mutually exclusive rather than overlapping. Is this a fair summary of how democratic theorists actually debate the boundary problem? Please separate what accurately captures the distinction between the principles, what is a common but misleading simplification, what subtly misrepresents the positions of careful defenders of the all-affected view, and whether the lecturer's conclusion—that broadening the boundary dissolves democracy's normative core—follows from anything correct in the summary. I want to understand precisely where the argument is doing real conceptual work and where it is constructing a straw position.
