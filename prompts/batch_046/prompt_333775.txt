I am developing a conceptual framework for a course on historical causation that treats certain non-human entities—pathogens, climate systems, volcanic aerosols—not merely as background conditions but as "historical actors" with a form of causal agency, while remaining vigilant against the slide into environmental determinism. I want to test whether this framework can illuminate an episode usually explained through human decision and institutional failure alone: the collapse of Ferdinand de Lesseps's French Panama Canal project between 1881 and 1889. Treat the standard outline as common ground: de Lesseps had completed the Suez Canal with a sea-level, lock-free design; he insisted on replicating that model across the mountainous, rainforest isthmus of Panama; and within a few years yellow fever and malaria—carried by mosquito vectors in the local ecology—killed thousands of workers, destroyed investor confidence, and bankrupted the enterprise, after which American engineers adopted both a lock-based design and an aggressive sanitation campaign.

Please analyze this episode through the lens of microbial and vector "agency." First, map the actors precisely: distinguish the human ones (de Lesseps, the Compagnie Universelle, the labor force, colonial administrators) from the non-human ones (the mosquitoes, the viral and parasitic agents, the isthmus hydrology and climate), and specify what counts as an "action" or decisive move for each category rather than treating biology as mere setting. Identify exactly where attributing agency to the pathogens explains the failure more persuasively than standard narratives of engineering hubris or financial mismanagement—does the mosquito population constitute an adversarial actor that actively foreclosed the sea-level plan, or is that an anthropomorphic projection? At the same time, show where the framework risks determinism: does it reduce de Lesseps's refusal to abandon the lock-free design, the colonial labor regimes that shaped exposure patterns, or the decision to recruit from immunologically diverse regions to passive effects of microbial presence?

Second, use the episode to test the framework's limits. The eventual American success depended not on eliminating the pathogens but on reorganizing human institutions—vector control, labor camps, engineering adaptation—around them. Does this show that microbial "agency" is always mediated by human organizational response, and therefore never determinative on its own? Or should the framework be refined to distinguish between direct agential causation and what we might call "constraint-formation," in which the pathogen establishes boundary conditions within which human agency operates but does not itself choose among outcomes? I want the analysis disciplined: do not impute intentions to viruses, be explicit about where the theory is silent, and deliver a clear verdict on whether this case ultimately supports, complicates, or undermines the idea that germs can be historical actors without collapsing history into environmental determinism.
