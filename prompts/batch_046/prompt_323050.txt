I have recently begun working on adversarial robustness in vision models, and in our lab's discussions of epistemic reliability we keep returning to Nozick's tracking conditions: a model "knows" its classification only if, in nearby worlds where the input differs, the output tracks that difference. Some colleagues extend this to argue that epistemic closure fails for neural networks—if a model knows p (the image is a dog) and knows p entails q (it is not a cat), it need not know q, because adversarial perturbations can break the tracking link to q without breaking the link to p. They draw an explicit analogy between Nozickian tracking and adversarial robustness testing: just as Nozick denies closure by allowing that tracking can hold for p but fail for its logical consequences, adversarial examples show that robustness can hold for base classifications but fail for derived ones. I want to audit this analogy precisely. Give me the correspondence table—what plays the role of the belief, the truth-condition, the nearby worlds, the tracking relation, and the closure principle—then identify where the mapping breaks: does an adversarial perturbation correspond to a nearby possible world in Nozick's modal framework, or does it operate within the same world with altered stimuli? Does the analogy smuggle in a computational ontology of belief that Nozick's externalist account rejects? Finally, tell me whether the conclusion—that neural networks lack epistemic closure in a way that vindicates Nozick—actually survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying the argument all along. If there is a better analogy for relating adversarial failure modes to knowledge conditions, suggest it briefly.
