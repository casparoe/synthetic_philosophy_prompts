I'm trying to work out what I actually think about quantum-mechanical interpretations—specifically Everett, Copenhagen, and Bohmian mechanics—by testing my gut reactions to concrete cases rather than by comparing formalisms. Here are my verdicts. I'd like to know whether a single principle delivers them, which ones must be sacrificed for coherence, and what the surviving view predicts about a case I keep returning to.

Case 1 — Double-slit with single particles, no which-path information recorded anywhere. I think the particle genuinely lacks a definite trajectory through one slit; positing Bohmian hidden paths is adding unobservable machinery that doesn't earn its explanatory keep. Verdict: anti-Bohm; the state description should not include hidden positions.

Case 2 — Schrödinger's cat in a box that is perfectly isolated from the environment for an hour. I think the cat is determinately alive or dead, not in a superposition of both, yet I also think the universal wavefunction evolves unitarily with no physical collapse event. Verdict: Everett-style branching—definite outcomes in each branch—without an actual collapse.

Case 3 — Wigner's friend inside a sealed lab measures a spin-½ particle and records the result. Wigner, outside, then performs an interference measurement on the entire lab. I find it genuinely implausible that the friend was in a superposition of having seen spin-up and spin-down; a macroscopic record or conscious observation collapses the state for her, and Wigner should treat her result as definite once he accesses it, not embed it in a larger superposition. Verdict: Copenhagen-style collapse is real and occurs at the measurement-apparatus or observer boundary.

Case 4 — A delayed-choice quantum eraser in which which-path information is stored in a quantum memory, then coherently erased by a unitary operation before any macroscopic observer examines anything, after which an interference pattern is recovered. I think this shows that "collapse" cannot be a physical process tied to observation, since the pattern returns; but I also think Bohmian mechanics cannot reproduce this without running afoul of the statistical predictions that violate Bell-type inequalities in other settings. Verdict: only unitary evolution is fundamental (Everett), and the appearance of collapse is perspectival.

Case 5 — EPR correlations with spacelike-separated measurements, but now the two observers are in relative motion so they disagree about which measurement occurred first. I think any interpretation that requires a definite fact about the temporal order of collapse (Copenhagen) or about a hidden-variable trajectory updating instantaneously across frames (Bohm) is in serious trouble. Verdict: Everett avoids the problem entirely, since there is no collapse event whose ordering needs fixing.

The tension is between Case 3, which pulls strongly toward Copenhagen, and Cases 2, 4, and 5, which pull toward Everett. Meanwhile Case 1 rejects Bohm on grounds of ontological excess, while Case 5 rejects Bohm on Lorentz-invariance grounds—similar conclusion, different reasoning.

The case I keep going back and forth on: a quantum computer runs Shor's algorithm with continuous quantum error correction. Syndrome measurements are made repeatedly, but the classical results are never displayed to any macroscopic observer; then the computation completes and is read out. Does the continuous unobserved correction involve branching (Everett), hidden-variable guidance of the physical qubits (Bohm), or unitary dynamics that only appear collapsed at the final readout (Copenhagen with a delayed boundary)? I can't settle whether my Case 3 intuition about macroscopic records applies here, since the syndrome data are technically macroscopic but never observed.

Is there a coherent principle—perhaps about when an interpretation must invoke a quantum-classical boundary, or about what counts as unacceptable explanatory surplus—that captures Cases 1 through 5? If so, what does it say about the error-correction case? If not, which verdict should I overturn: the anti-Bohm stance in Case 1, the pro-collapse stance in Case 3, or the Everett preference in Cases 4–5? And please be explicit: does saving every verdict require a principle so baroque that it ceases to be principled? Finally, state precisely what the best surviving principle predicts for the quantum-computing case I described, including whether it forces me to treat the hidden syndrome measurements as having collapsed the computation into definite error-correction paths.
