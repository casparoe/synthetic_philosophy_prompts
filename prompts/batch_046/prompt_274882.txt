You are an expert philosophy exam setter and professional philosopher of mind with extensive experience designing assessments for upper-level seminars. Before you begin drafting, take a careful moment to brainstorm: what structural features allow an intuition pump to persuade without genuinely supporting its conclusion, and how can an exam question force a student to dissect that mechanism rather than simply apply a familiar label like "begging the question" or "appeal to imagination"? Think specifically about how a meta-cognitive twist can alter the epistemic status of a first-person report within a functionalist setup.

Your task is to write one essay exam question—answerable in approximately 45 minutes—together with a model answer of about 600 words and a detailed marking scheme. The question must be for an upper-level undergraduate seminar on intuition pumps and philosophical methodology. It must not be answerable by reciting standard summaries of well-known cases (e.g., Mary’s room, the Chinese Room, the Trolley Problem).

Center the question on the following constructed scenario, which you should present in full within the exam prompt itself—do not ask students to have prior knowledge of it:

Consider "The Silent Orchestra." A concert hall is filled with listeners who possess fully intact auditory information-processing—perfect pitch discrimination, timbre recognition, emotional labeling of harmonic progressions—yet completely lack any phenomenal auditory experience; sound is registered as pure data with no "what-it-is-like" quality. During a single symphony performance, a surgeon gradually replaces, one neuron at a time, the auditory cortex of one such listener with functionally identical silicon analogues. By the final chord the replacement is total. At no point is there any behavioral change: the listener continues to discuss dynamics, identify instruments, and report emotional responses using the same vocabulary. Crucially, at the exact midpoint of replacement the surgeon implants a single meta-cognitive "monitor"—a module that allows the listener to observe and report on their own processing states without altering those states in any way. After the replacement is complete, the listener reports: "Something is missing, but I cannot specify what; all the data is present, yet the hall feels hollow."

The exam question must require students to do four things:
1. Identify at least two specific features of this case that operate as intuition pumps (e.g., gradual replacement, functional identity, the appeal to transparency in the final report) and explain what each is designed to make the reader accept implicitly.
2. Analyze whether the case genuinely supports the claim that phenomenal consciousness is not functionally analyzable, or whether it misleads by smuggling in an unjustified assumption about the reliability of meta-cognitive self-monitoring.
3. Address specifically how the implanted monitor changes the epistemic situation: does it provide a genuine discovery about the absence of phenomenal qualities, or does it generate a confabulated narrative that feels like discovery? The answer must engage with the distinction between access consciousness and phenomenal consciousness, but must apply it to this specific structure rather than recite the distinction generally.
4. Contrast the persuasive strategy of The Silent Orchestra with one standard intuition pump from the literature—without merely summarizing that standard case—showing exactly how the two pumps use different routes to elicit a philosophical verdict.

The model answer should demonstrate the specific analytical moves: diagnosing the pump’s structural flaw, explaining why functional identity plus meta-cognitive access does not entail phenomenal absence, and showing how the "hollow" report is epistemically ambiguous. The marking scheme should separate an A answer (which identifies the confabulation risk, explains why gradualism here is a red herring, and uses the comparison in part 4 to illuminate rather than decorate) from a B answer (which describes the scenario accurately and takes a side—dualism or functionalism—without analyzing the pump mechanism). Specify marks for each of the four parts (suggesting a 20-point total), name the specific mistake that costs marks in each part, and include a one-line note on how candidates typically go wrong—e.g., treating the listener’s report as transparent evidence, or using the comparison in part 4 as an excuse to summarize a familiar case.

Write in a formal, polished style appropriate for an exam committee document. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or requests for citations. Do not ask students to name specific authors unless necessary for part 4, and even there the grading must not depend on correct attribution. Ensure the prompt is constructed so that a student cannot succeed by memorizing standard positions on functionalism or dualism; success must depend on analyzing how this specific constructed case attempts to manipulate intuition.
