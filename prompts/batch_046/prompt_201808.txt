I'm preparing a position paper for a philosophy of mind seminar and want a consistency check on the following package of views, all of which I take to be broadly Davidsonian. (1) The principle of charity is constitutive of interpretation: any being we treat as an agent must be attributed mostly true beliefs and a largely rational belief-desire network. (2) Radical interpretation can proceed without prior knowledge of the speaker's language, psychology, or semantic intentions, using only observable patterns of assent and dissent in shared environmental conditions. (3) Mental content is exhaustively determined by the patterns available to radical interpretation; there is no "inner" mental residue that escapes third-person interpretive access. (4) The well-known underdetermination of interpretation is purely epistemic: there is a single correct assignment of content, but our evidence may not always fix it. (5) First-person self-knowledge is direct and non-interpretive: I do not apply charity to my own behavior to learn my beliefs. (6) I also think Davidson's triangulation argument requires that interpreters share tracking of enduring macroscopic objects; consequently, radical interpretation is impossible in principle for any system whose sensory inputs are entirely fleeting and lack object-directed stability.

Please audit these commitments. Which pairs are in genuine tension, which only appear to conflict, and what is the least costly revision in each case? Rank the tensions by severity, and identify which single change would clean up the most problems. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
