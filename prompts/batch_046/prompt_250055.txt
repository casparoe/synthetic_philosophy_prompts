i've been arguing with my study group about safe pareto improvements in policy design, and one friend walked me into an argument that feels airtight but proves way too much. here it is:

(1) A reform counts as a safe Pareto improvement only if, for every affected person i, well-being W(i | reform) is at least as high as W(i | no reform) in the nearest counter-factual world, and there's no catastrophic tail risk.

(2) Well-being is path-dependent and tied to narrative identity: if the reform shifts i's career, relationships, or life trajectory, then the counter-factual i in the no-reform world has a different biography and is not, in the relevent metaphysical sense, numerically identical to the actual i.

(3) So comparing W(i | reform) to W(i | no reform) for any non-trivial reform involves comparing well-being across numerically distinct subjects, which violates the identity condition required for Pareto comparison to be meaningful.

(4) Therefore any reform that actually changes someone's path fails to be a safe Pareto improvement.

(5) But if only path-preserving tweaks—like adjusting a tax bracket no one notices—count, then institutional reform is basically impossible, which is absurd; we'd be stuck with blatantly unjust rules just because fixing them alters some accountant's career arc.

(6) So one of these steps has to go.

i'd like to keep the idea that well-being isn't a simple scalar and that risky reforms need real justification, but i don't want status-quo absolutism. which step should i reject, why did it look fine, and what's the cost—do i have to accept naive quantitative utilitarianism? or is there a hidden slide between narrative continuity and numerical identity in step (3)?
