After watching a colleague’s legally protected but algorithmically amplified rhetoric gradually erode the shared evidentiary ground in my own research group—without ever targeting anyone directly—I have been trying to understand whether Mill’s harm principle can address harms that are structural and diffuse rather than direct and individual. Write a dialogue between a strict Millian liberal and a proponent of structural epistemic harm about whether speech that degrades collective reasoning conditions can count as other-regarding harm justifying restriction. Both interlocutors should be equally sharp: the liberal should challenge that this standard collapses into mere offense or authoritarian gatekeeping, and the structuralist should reply with a specific mechanism—such as algorithmic reinforcement of mutually exclusive evidentiary frameworks—rather than vague cultural decay. The liberal should then ask whether that mechanism distinguishes harmful amplification from unpopular dissent, and the structuralist should answer with a principled boundary—perhaps between speech that challenges norms and speech that systematically disables the normative framework itself—without appealing to bibliographies or authority. Keep the exchange focused on whether the harm principle can accommodate injury to a diffuse epistemic community without licensing the suppression of legitimate disagreement.
