I have recently found myself debating whether to cast a ballot for a candidate I consider harmful but less destructive than the alternative, and whether that act expresses my values or merely authorizes harm. This tension between expressive commitment, instrumental duty, and complicity in the lesser evil brought me back to Shirley Jackson's The Lottery. Read the story through the ethics of voting: is the villagers' cheerful participation in the drawing best understood as an expressive ritual of communal identity, and does Tessie's belated protest reveal that the logic of voting for the least bad option collapses once one becomes the victim? Consider whether Jackson's depiction of collective authorization—where each individual contribution is statistically negligible—illuminates or distorts the argument that a single vote in a large election is morally inconsequential. I want a reading that treats the story's social dynamics and the philosophical debate with equal seriousness, and that is honest about where the analogy between democratic selection and ritual stoning breaks down.
