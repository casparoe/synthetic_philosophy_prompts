In a recent seminar on moral uncertainty, a student repeatedly appealed to the maxim that "when the numbers are vast enough, a near-zero probability becomes decisive," applying it to argue that insect sentience—given trillions of individuals—demands moral priority over smaller groups of clearly conscious beings. I want to dissect what that slogan could actually be committing her to before I reply. Can you lay out the distinct precise theses it might express—separating epistemic claims about rational hedging, decision-theoretic claims about expected-value aggregation across populations, and normative claims about how moral weight should scale with probability of sentience—indicating for each whether it is defensible and whether it genuinely licenses treating insects as comparable in importance to, say, a smaller number of mammals? Present the readings as a menu with verdicts, and say which ones actually do the work she needs.
