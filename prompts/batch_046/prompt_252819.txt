Construct a thought experiment in which knowing-how and knowing-that come apart in both directions within a single realistic scenario: one agent possesses exhaustive propositional knowledge of how to carry out a complex cognitive-interpretive task, yet is structurally unable to perform it, while a second agent performs the task flawlessly but holds none of the corresponding propositional beliefs. Constraints: the task must be non-physical—no manual skills, athletic performance, or sensory-motor expertise; the first agent's propositional knowledge must be genuinely complete, as though they have memorized an exhaustive manual, algorithm, or decision tree; their failure must not derive from inexperience, anxiety, or lack of practice, but must persist even when every representational and motivational condition is satisfied; the second agent's success must not be explained away as implicit, tacit, or unconscious propositional knowledge. Set the scenario in an ordinary institutional setting—such as a legal chamber, a medical review board, or a translation office—not in speculative fiction, and keep the narrative to one paragraph. Then provide the verdict analysis: for each agent, state whether knowing-how and knowing-that are present and justify the judgment by reference to the specific mental states or capacities exhibited; identify the precise structural feature of the case that permits the separation; and explain what collapses if that feature is removed. Conclude by presenting the intellectualist response—the view that knowing-how ultimately reduces to a form of knowing-that—and showing exactly how an intellectualist would try to dissolve the gap, whether by appealing to hidden propositional states, contextual knowledge, or some other move, together with an assessment of whether that response begs the question against the anti-intellectualist.
