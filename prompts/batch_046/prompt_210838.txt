I was discussing Douglas Hofstadter's analysis of self-referential structures with two colleagues from my philosophy of institutions reading group, specifically the idea that tangled hierarchies contain a substrate level that remains untouched by the loops above it—often illustrated by Escher's lithograph in which each hand draws the other yet neither draws the page itself. We tried to apply this framework to a thought experiment in constitutional design, and the conversation split into two camps that neither of us could reconcile. Jordan argued that in any system with genuine recursive authority—such as a Meta-Legislative Council empowered to amend not only ordinary statutes but the very rules governing its own powers—the substrate is eventually pulled inside. Just as a formal system can embed axioms about its own axioms, or a constitution can contain provisions for its own revision, there is no permanently outside level. Once the loop closes, authority is tangled all the way down, and appeals to an untouched foundation are merely a refusal to follow the recursion to its end. Riley countered that Hofstadter's central insight is precisely the existence of an inviolate level—the paper, the physical reality, the original democratic act of ratification—that the system can never fully represent without contradiction. Applying this to the Council, Riley maintained that even if the body reinterprets or nullifies its founding charter, the validity of that very act still derives from the constitutional framework established by the original ratification. The loop is only apparent; the substrate reveals itself in the performative contradiction that arises when the system tries to transcend its own origin.

Jordan then pressed a distinction between physical and logical substrate, noting that in Gödel's results the physical computer running the proof is irrelevant—what matters is that the formal system cannot contain its own consistency proof. By analogy, the original constitutional convention is a historical event, not an active element of the current rule-system. If the Council can supersede that mandate through its own internal procedures, then within the functioning system of governance there is indeed no inviolate level, only circular authority. Riley replied that this conflates representation with existence: the outside is defined not by physical location but by representational absence. The founding convention may be historical, yet it functions as the logical substrate precisely because it is not subject to the Council's rules. Even a resolution declaring the convention obsolete depends for its legitimacy on the very framework the convention created. The contradiction Jordan identifies is therefore not a successful transcendence but the exact point where the untouched level makes itself visible—analogous to the moment in Escher's image where the hands would have to draw outside the page and fail. They asked me to referee the dispute, and I genuinely could not determine who was tracking the analogy correctly. Please adjudicate point by point: can a recursive institutional system internalize its own substrate, or does contradiction always reveal an untouched level outside? Is the analogy to Escher's image and to formal self-reference genuinely sound, or does one of them misapply it? And finally, who is more right overall about whether tangled hierarchies in governance contain an inviolate level, and what important consideration are both overlooking?
