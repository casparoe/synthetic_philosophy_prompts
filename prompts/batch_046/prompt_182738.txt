The debate over whether Black's two-sphere universe refutes Leibniz's principle of the identity of indiscernibles has cycled through the same positions for decades. One side treats the spheres—identical in all qualitative and relational respects within an otherwise empty, symmetric space—as a direct counterexample, arguing that being two miles apart from the other sphere is a shared relation that fails to distinguish them. The other side responds with weak discernibility, irreflexive relations, implicit orienting frames, or appeals to haecceities, insisting the principle survives. Despite constant rehearsal, no one moves. My hypothesis is that the stall is not a disagreement about identity but a buried conflict over the ontology of properties: one camp implicitly treats properties as particularized tropes that cannot be genuinely shared, so that numerical difference requires a primitive thisness; the other camp treats properties as shareable universals, allowing relational universals to do the distinguishing work. Is this the right diagnosis—verbal, substantive, or two different questions running under one heading? I want a structural diagnosis supported by what each side actually says about relations, symmetry, and property-instantiation, together with an honest account of what could make progress: a formal distinction that both sides accept, a conceptual clarification about intrinsic versus extrinsic relations, or an argument that the problem is ill-posed. To test the diagnosis, consider a pushed variant: the spheres possess identical internal rotational symmetries, there is no external frame or observer, and even the relation of non-identity threatens to collapse into a self-relation. Does this variant expose a deeper formal disagreement, or does it merely reproduce the same metaphysical impasse?
