me and my roommate were arguing about this hypothetical we made up for a philosophy of action class. The scenario: there's a drought and you're in a bunker with the only working water purifier. Outside there's one other person who can reach the intake valve if you open the airlock, but opening it risks contaminating your supply. You don't open it, they die of thirst.

My roommate (call her Jordan) said that's clearly letting die, not killing. You didn't harm them; you just failed to rescue them at risk to yourself. The other guy in our group (Marcus) said no, it's killing by omission structured as preservation. You're actively maintaining the bunker seal — every hour you check the lock is an action that keeps them out. Jordan said that's just describing inaction with fancy words. Marcus said okay, then change the setup: the purifier has an automatic feeder tube that extends outside if you don't press a button every 24 hours. You stop pressing it, the tube retracts, they die. Is that letting die? You literally have to do nothing.

Jordan said yes, that's the classic allowing case — you withdraw a benefit you were providing, but you're not initiating harm. Marcus said but you were providing it only because the machine was set up that way, and your decision to stop pressing is an intervention that changes the causal path. Then Jordan said well if that's a doing, then every time you stop feeding a stray cat you've adopted, you've killed it. Marcus said maybe yes, if you've created a dependency, but that's not the point. Then Marcus brought up this weird idea he read somewhere about "moral buffering" — using physical or institutional distance to convert what feels like a doing into an allowing. He said Jordan is doing exactly that: she wants the bunker wall to be a kind of moral insulator so she can call it allowing. Jordan said that's not an idea, that's just recognizing that causation respects boundaries. Marcus said boundaries are social constructions; the molecule doesn't care whether there's a wall.

They asked me to referee but I honestly don't know who's right about what here. Can you go through thier exchange move by move? Specifically: is Jordan right that failing to open the airlock is letting die? Is Marcus right that maintaining the seal is a sequence of actions? What's the deal with the automatic tube case — does stopping the button-press change the moral category? And is Marcus's "moral buffering" thing actually a real concept or just a relabel? Also, are both missing something about how dependency changes the doing/allowing line?
