Since retiring from a career in experimental physics, I have been working through questions in philosophy of well-being with a method closer to testing hypotheses against data than to historical scholarship, and I find that my intuitions about particular cases are far sharper than my grasp of any unifying principle. I would like to present a series of judgments I have made about concrete scenarios and ask whether a coherent principle can be extracted that respects them, what it implies for the cases that still divide me, and whether the price of simplicity is the abandonment of one of my verdicts.

Here are my assessments. First, the classic experience machine: a life of simulated achievement, deep friendship, and artistic creation, phenomenologically perfect but with no actual papers written, no physical artworks existing, and no real persons touched. I judge that such a life is deficient in well-being; the subject enjoys excellent experiences, yet something essential to human flourishing is absent. Second, a patient maintained in sustained euphoria by medication while her organs fail unnoticed: this is not well-being at all, despite the subjective quality. Third, an elderly engineer who spends his retirement constructing and running an elaborate model railway in his basement—real metal tracks, real locomotives, no audience, no economic purpose, and no lasting record—this strikes me as genuinely good for him, not defective in the way the fully simulated life is. Fourth, a theoretical physicist who derives correct predictions about quantum gravity, records them in notebooks she never shares, verifies them only through internal consistency, and dies unknown: I am genuinely torn. The cognitive achievement is real, yet the absence of empirical confirmation, peer scrutiny, or any causal impact on the world makes me hesitate to call her life fully flourishing.

Fifth, a musician who performs technically demanding concertos inside a soundproof chamber with perfect virtual audiences whose responses are algorithmically generated; she knows they are not real people, but her execution requires genuine skill. I think her well-being is partly intact—she exercises real capacities—yet diminished in a way that the model-railway builder's is not. Sixth, a caregiver who sustains a severely disabled family member through years of exhausting labor, experiencing little pleasure but maintaining a relationship that remains genuinely reciprocal despite its asymmetry: this seems to me a paradigmatic form of well-being. Seventh, a novelist who writes a brilliant work inside an immersive simulation where every interlocutor and reader is artificial, but who saves the text to an external server so that real people eventually read it after she exits: my intuition splits sharply between the artificiality of the process and the reality of the product.

Is there a precise principle—perhaps one that distinguishes activities whose value is constitutive of the experience itself from those that depend on counterfactual stability, external anchoring, or causal connection to an independent reality—that captures why the model-railway builder and the caregiver flourish, while the fully simulated life and the euphoric patient do not? Does it also place the isolated physicist, the virtual musician, and the simulated novelist in intermediate zones without ad hoc adjustments? Or must I revise one of my verdicts—most likely the case of the writer whose work eventually reaches reality—to preserve simplicity? I am particularly interested in whether the principle needs to invoke something like objective functional conditions, or whether a more nuanced distinction between phenomenal welfare and counterfactual robustness will suffice.
