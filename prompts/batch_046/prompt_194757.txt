I'm working through how Buddhist anattā should shape judgments about personal identity, and my intuitions across concrete cases feel hard to square. Here are my verdicts. First, in the traditional rebirth scenario: a monk dies, and years later a child elsewhere demonstrates verifiable knowledge of the monastery and recognizes former disciples. I think the child is not literally the same person—no enduring self or soul migrated—but there is a causal continuity of consciousness that makes it conventionally appropriate to say karmic traces carry over, even though literal identity fails. Second, in severe Alzheimer's: the biological organism survives but all psychology—memory, personality, awareness—is destroyed. I still think obligations of care remain exactly as before; the living body is still that person, and it would be wrong to treat it as a different being merely because the mental stream has been interrupted. Third, in a gradual synthetic replacement over a decade, each neuron is replaced by a functionally identical artificial analog that preserves neural patterns and first-person reports; at the end, the synthetic being remembers the biological past and reports continuous experience. Here I think identity is preserved—it is the same stream of consciousness, despite total material discontinuity. Fourth, in instant teleportation: a scanner records the complete brain state, destroys the original, and reconstructs an exact duplicate elsewhere. The duplicate has all memories and reports being the original. I think the original died; the duplicate is a new being with inherited false memories, and survival has not occurred.

These pull in different directions, and I keep returning to a fifth case about moral responsibility: a practitioner who has genuinely realized anattā through deep insight claims he should not be blamed for harming someone, since there was never a self that performed the action—only impersonal mental and physical processes. I think this misuses the doctrine; responsibility does not require a substantial self, only that the causal stream producing the harm is the one now facing consequences. So my question is whether any principle—perhaps drawing on conventional versus ultimate truth, or on conditions of causal continuity—delivers all five verdicts. I see particular tension between accepting substrate-independent survival in the gradual case but rejecting it in the instantaneous one, and between allowing biological continuity alone to sustain identity in Alzheimer's while denying that psychological continuity alone establishes identity in rebirth. Is there a coherent account that saves every verdict, or must one go? And if the best principle is simpler but overturns one of mine, which should it be? Finally, what should it predict for a case I have not settled: a digital consciousness running on one server is split into two equally valid, initially identical copies running separately, both claiming to be the original—are they both, neither, or is there a conventional fact of the matter?
