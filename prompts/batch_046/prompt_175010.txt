Read Samuel Beckett’s *Waiting for Godot* through the philosophical problem of why there is something rather than nothing, but do so with a specific conceptual distinction in mind: separate the modal formulation of the question (why does any contingent being exist at all, rather than there being necessarily nothing) from the causal-explanatory formulation (what ground or process accounts for the origin of being). The play presents a world of almost bare existence—two figures on a bare stage, waiting for a presence that never arrives, repeating actions without progress—so ask whether Beckett dramatizes a kind of “brute contingency” in which being is given without reason or ground, and whether Vladimir and Estragon’s insistence that Godot will come operates like an unstated commitment to the principle of sufficient reason. Examine how particular dynamics illuminate or resist this reading: their debates about whether they are in the right place or on the right day as a theatrical version of epistemic uncertainty about being itself; the tree’s minimal, persistent presence as a marker of something rather than absolute void; and Pozzo’s collapse into blindness together with Lucky’s muteness as figures of being stripped of agency and voice. At the same time, consider where the play refuses to become cosmology: the “nothing” in *Godot* may be theatrical and existential rather than metaphysical, and Godot’s absence may signal a lack of meaning or salvation rather than an absence of all being. Does Beckett deliberately conflate, or keep rigorously apart, the question of why being exists and the question of whether being has purpose? I want a reading that takes both the play’s dramatic logic and the metaphysical distinction seriously—faithful to Beckett’s resistance to interpretation and to the philosophical problem’s resistance to theatrical illustration—and that ends by saying what *Waiting for Godot* adds to the debate about existence and nothingness that a purely argumentative essay cannot.
