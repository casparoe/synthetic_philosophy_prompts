State your credence, as a specific number between 0 and 1, that the moral equality of combatants can survive in a philosophically coherent modified form if we grant two premises: (1) individual combatants in conventional wars almost never have reliable epistemic access to whether their cause is just; and (2) a combatant's liability to be killed depends not on collective membership in an unjust war but on whether they presently constitute an unjust threat to specific others, where "unjust threat" is construed in a strictly agent-relative rather than cause-relative manner. Decompose your reasoning into four sub-questions with rough probabilities: (a) whether epistemic opacity genuinely blocks the application of liability-based asymmetry in practice; (b) whether an agent-relative construal of unjust threat is coherent without reducing combatant killing to ordinary individual self-defense; (c) whether institutional combat roles can generate symmetric permissions to kill even when underlying individual liability is asymmetric; and (d) whether the conjunction of (1) and (2) leaves any non-pragmatic normative content in an equality thesis or merely dissolves it into a convention for reducing harm. Explain how these probabilities combine—whether multiplicatively, as weighted averages, or otherwise—to reach your overall figure; identify which sub-question your final number is most sensitive to; and specify what empirical evidence about combatant belief-formation under conditions of radical uncertainty, or what conceptual argument about collective action, would most shift your credence.
