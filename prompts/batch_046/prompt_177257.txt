Consider an institutional policy recently adopted by a professional licensing board: applicants for certification must submit a "causal autobiography" that traces every significant career choice back to a cause they themselves originated, explicitly excluding genetic, environmental, or social determinants. Those who identify any irreducible external causal chain are disqualified, while successful candidates receive "full authorship status," which assigns them exclusive liability for professional failures and exclusive rights to intellectual property. What theory of moral responsibility does this policy presuppose? Does it treat being causa sui as an achievable condition or as a regulatory fiction? Reconstruct the implicit view, evaluate whether it can withstand Galen Strawson's Basic Argument that genuine moral responsibility requires being the ultimate cause of oneself in a manner no one can satisfy, and indicate which parts of the policy would have to be abandoned or revised if that argument is correct.
