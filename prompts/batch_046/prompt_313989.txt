You serve on the municipal oversight board for an algorithmic emergency-housing-voucher allocation system. The current protocol assigns vouchers using a static risk-score model trained on administrative records; it does not incorporate real-time caseworker assessments or neighborhood-level updates. A proposal now before the board would mandate that the system acquire additional contextual information—structured caseworker interviews and updated local indicators—before each allocation round, introducing a mandatory 48-hour delay. Proponents invoke Good's theorem on the value of information: for any rational decision-maker with a well-defined utility function, observing a free signal before acting can never reduce expected utility, so requiring information acquisition should be axiomatically sound. Critics respond that the theorem's conditions fail here in three specific ways: the 48-hour delay imposes real welfare costs (information is not free); applicants strategically adapt their disclosures once they learn that interviews influence outcomes, breaking the independence between signal and state; and the observation protocol itself reshapes the social dynamics being measured, so the "signal" is not an exogenous reading but an intervention.

Draft the board's formal written opinion—recommending adoption, rejection, or conditional adoption with specified safeguards—addressed to the city administrator and distributed before next week's vote. Your memo should precisely identify which premises of Good's result hold in this institutional context and which are violated, distinguish between violations that merely increase the cost of information (and can therefore be priced into the decision rule) and violations that undermine the logical structure of the theorem itself (such as decision-dependent outcomes or non-exogenous observation), and propose a concrete implementation framework with timelines and evaluation criteria rather than an abstract philosophical conclusion. Crucially, treat the strongest opposing argument with intellectual honesty: the concern that framing information as unconditionally valuable can mask asymmetries in who controls the gathering process, whose vulnerabilities are exposed by the interviews, and whose housing needs are deferred by the mandatory delay.
