A philosopher of biology and economics in my department—call her Dr. Vasquez—has staked out clear, consistent positions on whether various phenomena are best explained by Zahavi’s handicap principle, as opposed to index signaling, Spence-style differential-cost signaling, or direct functional adaptation. Her verdicts span biological and social domains: she holds that the peacock’s tail is a handicap; that stotting in Thomson’s gazelles is not a handicap but an index of present physical condition; that conspicuous consumption of luxury goods is a handicap; that university degrees functioning as hiring credentials are not a handicap but a case of standardized differential investment; that complex song in male songbirds is a handicap because of predation risk and metabolic cost; that bright aposematic coloration in poison dart frogs is a handicap, since toxin production is metabolically expensive and only healthy individuals can sustain it; and that large antlers in moose are not a handicap but functional armaments whose costs serve competitive utility directly.

Consider now a new, engineered case. A research laboratory trains its flagship language model so that every chain-of-thought trace is generated in an extravagantly redundant format—repeating intermediate steps with unnecessary elaboration—such that reproducing the trace requires roughly an order of magnitude more compute and token expenditure than a minimal reasoning path would require. The redundancy serves no communicative or problem-solving function; its sole purpose is to make it prohibitively expensive for smaller models or malicious actors to forge or imitate the reasoning signature. Crucially, the computation tax is embedded in the model’s own inference architecture: the model itself must expend the extra resources to produce the trace, so that only architectures with sufficient capacity can maintain task performance under the self-imposed burden.

Predict Dr. Vasquez’s verdict: would she classify this engineered redundancy as a handicap signal? Identify at least two distinct principles that fit her earlier seven verdicts equally well—one emphasizing non-instrumental waste decoupled from function, another emphasizing differential vulnerability to cost outside institutional or functional frameworks—and say what each predicts about this AI case. Give your best guess as to which principle she is actually running, with reasons drawn from her pattern of judgments. Which of her earlier cases is the closest analog? Finally, propose one further hypothetical case—preferably crossing a domain boundary she has not yet addressed, such as an institutional or artificial-selection context—that would best distinguish the leading principles from one another.
