Is there a name for this pattern I'm seeing in enviromental history writing? Like, take the Tambora eruption and the year without summer - authors will say the volcano crippled agricultural systems, or that a pathogen exploited trade routes opened by colonization. The verbs are tactical: undermine, redirect, force, exploit. Then when someone points out thats anthropomorphic, the author pulls back and says they're only describing causal weight, not agency. But they don't actually rewrite the sentences - they just add a disclaimer saying of course I don't mean literally. So the tactical framing stays in the text but gets a mechanistic disclaimer attached. Its not reification, its not determinism, and its not just metaphor because the author relies on the tactical framing to do explanatory work - like, the explanation only works if you imagine the climate as pushing against human institutions with directional pressure. I keep running into this in climate-and-politics and germ-history texts. Is there an established concept for this specific rhetorical move, where agency-language is retained for explanatory force but disclaimed for ontological safety? If there's no name, can you coin one and draw its boundaries - especially how its different from plain personification and from actual environmental determinism?
