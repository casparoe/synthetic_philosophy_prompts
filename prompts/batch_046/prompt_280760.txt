I hold a specific resolution of Fitch's paradox of knowability. The standard derivation shows that if every truth is in principle knowable, then every truth is actually known, which is taken to refute the knowability principle and, with it, certain anti-realist or verificationist commitments. I think this response is too quick. Instead, I claim the derivation tacitly relies on embedding the knowability principle in a modal framework—typically S5—where epistemic accessibility is an equivalence relation, symmetric and transitive. If we replace this with a directed, non-transitive accessibility relation, then a truth can be knowable from world w without being knowable back from the world in which it is known. On this view, the principle that all truths are knowable survives, but knowability is indexed to a specific epistemic position and is not preserved across the modal steps the proof requires. This allows a qualified verificationism: there is no truth that is in principle unreachable, yet there can be truths that, once known, alter the accessibility conditions so that other truths are no longer reachable from that updated position. I take the paradox therefore not as a refutation of anti-realist intuition, but as a warning against treating epistemic modality as symmetric and transitive.

What would count against this position? I want the arguments, formal results, and conceptual pressures that should actually move me, sorted by how decisive they would be. Specifically: do formal reconstructions show that the omniscience derivation genuinely fails in non-transitive frames, or does it merely relocate in hidden premises about accessibility? Are there independent philosophical reasons to reject non-transitive epistemic logic, or does it generate unacceptable consequences elsewhere? Does this framing actually preserve verificationism, or does it collapse into a position indistinguishable from a modest realism that also accepts indexed knowability? Does the view predict anything that its rivals do not—say, about the structure of self-referential knowledge claims—or has it been qualified into unfalsifiability, where any failure of the derivation is automatically attributed to an accessibility adjustment? If it is well insulated, is that insulation a virtue or a vice for a position of this kind? I also want an honest assessment of what defenders of the standard resolution (those who reject the knowability principle outright) would concede here, and whether my framing misrepresents the logical structure of the original argument. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and tell me whether the position risks anything genuine or has been protected into emptiness.
