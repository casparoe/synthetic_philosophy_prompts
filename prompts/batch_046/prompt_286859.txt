I've been testing a colleague's institutional intuitions over a series of hypothetical reforms, and I'm trying to determine whether his judgments trace a coherent rule or an unstable hybrid. He describes himself as a Burkean conservative with a Chestertonian commitment to fences—he holds that one should not tear down an institution until one understands why it was erected, but he also insists that some fences were built to consolidate illegitimate power and may be removed once that purpose is exposed. Here are his verdicts so far. First, on a municipal charter requiring building permits to be physically stamped in sequence by three clerks—a procedure with no legal effect beyond an instantaneous digital record—he said preserve it intact, arguing that the mandatory delay functions as an unplanned cooling-off period that prevents impulsive demolition of historic structures. Second, on a tradition requiring rural magistrates to spend one day each month riding circuit through outlying districts despite adequate video links, he said keep the ride, because physical presence reveals ground conditions that formal reports systematically conceal. Third, on a religious school curriculum that still teaches geocentric astronomy within a historical narrative that explicitly frames it as a corrected error, he said reform the framing language but preserve the pedagogical sequence, since encountering a wrong idea in its original context teaches epistemic humility better than sanitized textbooks do. Fourth, on an apprenticeship system that restricts entry to direct descendants of current members, he said abolish the restriction immediately despite its eighty-year history, on the grounds that this fence was built for exclusion rather than for coordination or quality control. Fifth, on a parliamentary rule allowing any member to force a full oral reading of bills exceeding fifty pages, he said preserve the rule despite its obvious inefficiency, because the strategic cost of bloat is precisely the hidden function.

The new case concerns a national archive that maintains a complete analog microfilm backup of all government digital records, updated quarterly by hand in climate-controlled vaults, never used for retrieval since digital search is faster and more accurate. The system was designed in the 1950s as a hedge against nuclear electromagnetic pulse, a threat model now considered obsolete; yet the manual updating process requires archivists to physically inspect every record, and this human loop has twice detected corruption in the digital database that automated audits missed. Would he preserve this analog fence, abolish it, or modify it? More importantly, I want you to recommend the verdict that best coheres with his own track record—identifying the closest analog among the five above and stating honestly what principle he would be adopting either way, whether it is a pure Chestertonian hidden-function test, a Burkean prescription-based rule weighted by survival and social embedding, or a hybrid that distinguishes between fences correcting institutional failure and those preserving hierarchy. Which interpretation do his past rulings actually support, and what single further case—specifying the institution and the proposed reform—would split these interpretations decisively?
