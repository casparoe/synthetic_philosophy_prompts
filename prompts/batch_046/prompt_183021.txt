Right, here's the thing that's been niggling me. In basically every physics syllabus and science museum display, Kepler gets split in two. On one side you've got the Rudolphine Tables, the elliptical orbits, the optics—presented as pure empirical-mathematical triumph. On the other side you've got his astrology, his casting of horoscopes (including for Wallenstein), and his belief that the Earth possessed a kind of soul—an anima terrae—that physically responded to the geometric angles between planets. That second half either gets left out entirely, or it's mentioned with a little wink: 'he had to do it for money,' or 'it was just the culture of the time.'

The practice is that we treat these two halves as belonging to different kinds of thinking—rational calculation versus symbolic-cosmological belief—even though Kepler himself saw them as continuous, part of one celestial physics where harmonic geometry governed both orbital motion and terrestrial response.

So what philosophical commitments does that institutional split preseupposes? Does it require a sharp demarcation criterion between science and pseudoscience? Does it presuppose that empirical reasoning and cosmological interpretation can't inhabit the same coherent worldview? Or is the split compatible with several different theories of scientific identity?

Also, is the practice philosophically consistent with how we now treat Newton's alchemy, which gets museum space and serious historical study? What's the implicit theory of value or rationality that licences treating one thinker's 'weird' commitments as worth preserving in the narrative and another's as embarrassing footnotes?
