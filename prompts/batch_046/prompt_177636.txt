Design an in-class simulation for an upper-level philosophy seminar of about fourteen students, running seventy-five minutes, that makes the group feel a specific structural feature of path dependence—call it the branching-access gap—rather than generic historical lock-in. The branching-access gap is the situation in which a collective is stuck on a suboptimal path not because any individual prefers to remain, nor because a switch is technically impossible, but because the very coordination mechanism that would allow a unanimous jump to a nearby superior branch has been eroded or was never developed along the current path. The participants should discover through their own sequential choices that they can all see the better branch, that each privately wants to move, and that no procedural rule available within the game permits the jump without unanimous consent—which the path itself now prevents them from achieving.

Specify the physical layout and materials (cards, tokens, a simple board, or hidden envelopes), assign distinct institutional roles (for example, legacy investors, new entrants, and a neutral registry), and give round-by-round rules with payoffs that are not monetary but visibly alter a collective score or resource pool. Include a hidden inflection point—around the midpoint—where the payoff structure silently shifts so that the current path becomes self-reinforcing and the cost of unilateral deviation spikes, while the cost of coordinated deviation remains low but structurally unattainable because the registry rule requires unanimous signaling, and the signaling channel has been disabled for anyone still on the old path.

Then lay out a minute-by-minute timeline showing when participants should first notice divergence, when they should realize private preferences are aligned, and when they should attempt and fail a collective escape. Provide a debrief that maps each round onto a component of path dependence—contingency in the opening round, increasing returns after the inflection point, the collective-action failure in the escape attempt, and the erosion of the coordination mechanism—without naming the branching-access gap until the final ten minutes.

Also include: an honest prediction of what will go wrong the first time the activity is run (for instance, one student reverse-engineering the payoff shift early and breaking the shared experience, or groups inventing out-of-game communication to coordinate) together with a concrete facilitator response that preserves the effect without shaming the student; and a frank admission of which feature of real-world historical path dependence the simulation cannot reproduce—specifically, the multi-generational timescale and the normative authority of “this is how it has always been done”—together with the exact verbal framing the facilitator must supply at the end to close that gap. The idea should emerge from the rules and incentives; do not lecture on path dependence before the game begins, do not ask for bibliographies or reading lists, and do not cite specific scholarly texts.
