So I've been chewing on this idea that the "Middle Ages," "Renaissance," and "Scientific Revolution" are basically made-up chapters that 19th-century textbook writers invented just so history would have neat sections. Not just that the borders are fuzzy — I mean that if you actually look at what people were building, eating, and tinkering with, nothing magical occurs in 1453 or around 1687. My specific twist is that the only real structural break wasn't cultural at all but administrative: it's when European universities started requiring formal Latin examinations for craftsmen and doctors instead of letting guilds handle training, and that happened patchily way after the so-called Renaissance. So calling 1500 a "rebirth" feels to me like calling Monday a new life because you bought a planner. I'm definatly oversimplifying, but I don't want to be that guy who dismisses everything without knowing what'd actually break his view.

What would count against this? I'm not asking for a bibliography — I just want to know which observations or arguments would actually kill my position versus just make it awkward. Like, is there material-culture evidence of sharp shifts right at those traditional dates? Would my bureaucratic-break idea collapse if university formalization actually started earlier, say in Bologna or Salerno? Or is there a philosophical reason we need these period labels even if they're historically sloppy — maybe they're useful fictions that organize knowledge? Rank the defeaters for me: fatal, serious, merely awkward. And tell me what a defender of the traditional three-part story would themselves admit is a real weakness in their own view, not just what they'd throw back at me. Also, am I insulating my idea too much with "it was all gradual" to the point where it's not even wrong, just useless?
