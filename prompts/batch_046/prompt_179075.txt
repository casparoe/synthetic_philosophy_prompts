I was recently appointed to a university working group charged with evaluating reparative claims arising from institutional complicity in historical labor coercion, and the assignment has become personally consequential since genealogical research revealed that my own great-grandparents were among the contract laborers brought in under a 1920s state-bilateral agreement to construct the university’s rail spur—workers whose wages were withheld, whose contracts were unilaterally voided, and whose presence has been mischaracterized in our institutional archives as voluntary economic migration. Our group has already rendered verdicts on five prior claims, and we must now decide a sixth that appears to straddle the boundaries of our reasoning.

Our precedents are these. First, we approved both an endowed scholarship program and a mandatory curriculum revision for direct lineal descendants of enslaved people whose labor built a central campus building. Second, for a community displaced by a state-mandated hydroelectric project in the 1950s—racially neutral in design but destructive in effect—we rejected land restitution yet approved funding for a commemorative archive and public history installation. Third, for descendants of indigenous students subjected to forced assimilation in boarding schools, we approved language revitalization funding and a formal institutional apology, but explicitly rejected individual cash payments. Fourth, we rejected a neighborhood claim for a community investment fund in an area that had experienced redlining and subsequent wealth extraction, on the ground that the proposed beneficiary pool was not restricted to lineal descendants of the specifically harmed class. Fifth, we approved symbolic acknowledgment and dedicated research funding, but rejected direct monetary compensation, for descendants of sharecroppers systematically defrauded by a university-affiliated agricultural cooperative in the 1930s, noting in our reasoning that the institution had neither owned nor directly operated the cooperative.

The new claim comes from descendants of the rail-spur laborers. They seek three distinct remedies: a formal correction of the archival record that misrepresents the workers as voluntary migrants; the establishment of an independent research center on state-sanctioned contract labor coercion; and a modest community development fund targeted at the present-day town where many descendants still reside, though that town is now economically diverse and a substantial portion of descendants have relocated elsewhere. The claimants emphasize that the harm was neither chattel slavery nor racially targeted exclusion in the conventional sense, but rather contract fraud, wage theft, and documentary erasure directed specifically at a non-citizen labor class operating under state sanction.

If we wish to remain consistent with our own track record, what should our verdict be—approval, partial approval, or rejection—regarding each of the three requested remedies? Which of our five precedents serves as the closest analog, and does that analogy genuinely determine the outcome? Our prior decisions appear jointly compatible with at least two underlying rules. One rule ties material reparations to direct institutional ownership or operational control of the harm, combined with a precisely defined descendant class that continues to bear demonstrable economic disadvantage; on this rule, the community fund is unjustified because institutional ownership of the rail-spur labor was indirect and the beneficiary pool is now diffuse. Another rule permits symbolic and archival reparations whenever an institution has actively misrepresented coercive conditions in its own records, regardless of current economic status, direct ownership, or the racial framing of the harm; on this rule, both the archival correction and the research center are required, and the community fund might follow as a logical extension of recognizing state-sanctioned contract fraud. State what each rule predicts for this case, indicate which prediction you believe best coheres with our actual precedents, and specify the further hypothetical case—perhaps involving descendants of a different coercive labor scheme whose members now enjoy full economic parity with the general population—that would force our working group to choose definitively between these two rules.
