As a retired experimental physicist who has spent the last several years reading systematically in moral philosophy and historiography, I now serve on my municipality's heritage-review board, which must decide every quarter whether to rename buildings, alter curricular framing, or leave commemorations intact for historical figures whose actions are now widely condemned. The board is composed of citizens without philosophical training, we have thirty minutes per case, and any recommendation for removal requires unanimous sign-off. The standard defence—that a figure was a man of his time—is too blunt an instrument; I want it translated into an operational procedure that retains its moral insight but eliminates its vagueness.

The underlying principle I want operationalized is this. Judging a historical figure for commemorative purposes should not ask whether he was good by our lights, but should run three successive filters. First, an internal-coherence filter: did the figure violate normative commitments they themselves explicitly endorsed? Second, a contemporaneous-access filter: was there a body of criticism, empirical evidence, or alternative practice available within their social and intellectual environment that they could plausibly have accessed and that undermined their justification? Third, a structural-causation filter: was the harm in question embedded in a system the figure actively designed or reinforced, or was it an incidental feature of a context they inherited and could not have substantially altered?

Please construct a reusable decision procedure my board could actually run. Present it as an ordered sequence of questions—what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a recommendation against removal or reframing, and where it must hand the decision back to human judgment because the question is irreducibly one of values rather than historical or logical fact. Be explicit about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and state what the procedure cannot do—particularly its limits in handling figures whose moral errors are interwoven with genuine achievements that the board also values.

Then apply the procedure to three cases: Thomas Jefferson, Winston Churchill, and Christopher Columbus. For one of these, the procedure should yield an answer that conflicts with the progressive removalist stance common among board members—perhaps recommending retention with heavy contextualization rather than removal—so that we can see how the mechanism behaves under disagreement. Conclude with a brief note on how this procedure differs from both blanket condemnation and from the uncritical man-of-his-time defence, and on why a retired scientist might still find it unsatisfying.

Write this in formal, polished prose suitable for presentation to a board of non-specialists. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; I will supply references myself if needed. Do not invent specific archival discoveries or obscure biographical details I could not verify without access to specialized sources.
