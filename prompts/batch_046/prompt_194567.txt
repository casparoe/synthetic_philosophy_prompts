Since my appointment three months ago to fill a vacancy on our city’s Civic Justification Board, I have been trying to understand the implicit framework we apply when citizens invoke religious reasoning in public testimony. Our mandate is not to regulate belief but to determine what may enter the board’s official reasoning and what must remain in the expressive register of testimony alone. To that end, I have reviewed our precedents. In a zoning dispute over a shelter for unhoused persons, a speaker opposed the project using only specific scriptural commands about “strangers within your gates” interpreted through a particular covenantal theology, offering no secular parallel; we struck the testimony and invited resubmission with publicly accessible reasons. In a school-board debate over curriculum, parents interwove scriptural anthropology with peer-reviewed developmental psychology; we admitted the testimony in full, including the religious portions, on the grounds that the secular reasons were independently sufficient. In an environmental hearing, a citizen advocated stricter regulations through philosophical natural-law reasoning about human flourishing and stewardship, with no reference to revelation or religious authority; we admitted it without restriction. In a health-funding hearing, a speaker argued for prioritizing hospice care over experimental treatment based entirely on a reported personal mystical experience of “divine presence in suffering,” with no premises a non-believer could share; we excluded it and asked for reformulation. In a casino-license proceeding, a coalition submitted a brief using only economic-impact studies and public-health data; internal correspondence later revealed church organization and theological motivation about vice, but we admitted the brief and held motivation irrelevant to justification.

We now face a proposal to deploy an algorithmic risk-assessment tool for allocating public housing. One speaker submits testimony opposing it solely on the grounds that “each person bears an inviolable, non-quantifiable dignity rooted in being made in the image of God,” and explicitly states that secular “human dignity” language is an inadequate translation that strips the claim of its normative force. The speaker offers no empirical critique of the algorithm, no constitutional or policy-specific argument, and declines our invitation to provide a secular parallel. They ask that we include this reasoning in our official recommendation.

Given our track record, should we admit this testimony, exclude it, or admit it with a formal reservation? Which of our five precedents is the closest analog? I am particularly interested in identifying the competing principles that could jointly explain our past rulings—perhaps a strict translation requirement, an overlapping-consensus criterion about shareable values, and a register-separation rule that distinguishes admissible testimony from reasons the board may itself cite. For each principle, say what it predicts about this case, and tell me which decision best coheres with our body of precedent and why. If our precedents genuinely underdetermine the outcome, be explicit about what principle we would be adopting with each choice—full exclusion, full admission, or admission-without-citation. Finally, propose a hypothetical further case that would best distinguish which of these principles is actually running in our practice. I am less interested in the answer alone than in the rule we seem to be running, the other rules that fit our verdicts equally well, and the precise case that would force us to choose among them.
