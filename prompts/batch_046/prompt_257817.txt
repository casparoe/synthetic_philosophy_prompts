Make an argument that archaeological inferences about centralized institutional authority in non-literate societies—drawn from standardized administrative artifacts such as weights, sealings, or storage facilities—depend on an implicit theory of material culture as preserving what might be called frozen illocutionary force. On this view, an administrative object is not merely a functional instrument or a passive record of exchange, but a material trace that carries the directive or declarative power of an institutional speech act. Argue that this commitment is necessary to justify interpreting such artifacts as evidence for a centralized redistribution authority rather than for a decentralized network of reciprocal obligations, since the material patterns alone underdetermine the institutional structure. Then address the following tension: if the frozen speech act interpretation requires attributing specific propositional intentions to the makers of these artifacts, it risks importing an anachronistic psychology of belief; but if it abandons intentional attribution altogether and relies purely on structural pattern-matching, it collapses into a functionalism that cannot distinguish bureaucratic control from emergent coordination. Resolve this tension in favor of your main argument, explaining how the inference can be sustained without requiring full-blown propositional attribution yet without reducing institutional authority to mere behavioral regularity.
