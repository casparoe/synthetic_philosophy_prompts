I'm trying to pin down my view on free-floating rationales — reasons that hang around even when there's literally no reasoner to hold them — and I'm not sure my commitments actually hang togther. Here's what I think:
1. A normative reason doesn't need an actual, existing agent to be real; there can be a reason not to cause suffering in a world where no one exists to feel it or act on it.
2. But I also accept the deliberitive constraint: if a consideration couldn't in principle enter into anyone's practical reasoning, then it isn't a reason at all, just a bare value-fact.
3. I hold that all genuinely practical reasons are agent-relative — they depend on some agent's practical identity or commitments — and that so-called agent-neutral reasons are really just summaries of many agent-relative ones.
4. I think there's a strong reason right now not to engineer a future being who'd suffer horribly, even though that being might never actually exist and so there's no actual victim.
5. I also think the weight of a reason is fixed by the intensity of what's at stake, not multiplied by the number of agents invloved — ten people's mild discomfort doesn't generate ten times the reason that one person's agony does.

Which of these are actually pulling against each other, which only look like it, and what's the least painful revision? Please rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them all the same. Also, please say what would change your mind — which argument or consideration would flip your verdict, and where do you think your own reasoning is shakiest?
