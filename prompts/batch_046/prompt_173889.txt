I'm putting together a module on narrative identity for my grad seminar and I've been stuck on this pattern I keep seeing in how people talk about public figures versus themselves. You know how when someone describes a politician's career, they weave every scandal and reversal into this smooth arc -- 'he was always going to fall because of that early compromise' -- as if there was one continuous self steering the ship? But then you ask the politician, or anyone close to them, and it's all contingency, panic, reacting to the news cycle, no sense of being the protagonist of a story. What's interesting to me is that we dont just do this for ourselves; we construct these centers of narrative gravity for other people that are actually denser and more coherent than anything they experience from the inside, and then we use that external arc to judge them -- like 'of course he did X, that's exactly who he is' -- even though the person themselves never inhabited that coherence.

Is there an established term for this specific asymmetry? I know Dennett's center of narrative gravity is about the self we construct for ourselves, and there's the fundamental attribution error, but neither quite captures this vicarious version where we impose a tighter narrative coherence on others than they have for themselves, and then treat that imposed coherence as morally relevant -- like it reveals their 'true self' in a way that excuses or condemns them. I'm not looking for just a bare name; I want to know if there's literature that maps exactly where this differs from ordinary storytelling about others, whether it counts as a kind of narrative violence or just a cognitive shortcut, and if there's no precise term, how you'd define its boundaries so it doesnt collapse back into regular bias or the basic Dennett idea. Sorry for the ramble -- been thinking about this since a conversation with a friend who studies literature and cant name it either.
