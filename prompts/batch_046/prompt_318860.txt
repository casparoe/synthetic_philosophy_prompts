My thesis is this. The gender-equality paradox—the pattern that countries with stronger legal gender equality and more progressive gender norms often show larger, not smaller, gender differences in career choices, such as the overrepresentation of men in engineering and women in nursing and teaching—is best explained not as a failure of culture to finish its work, but as evidence that when economic coercion and legal exclusion are reduced, underlying differences in preference become more visible rather than suppressed. From this I draw a normative conclusion: justice in career distribution should not be measured by representational parity in every field. Instead, I propose evaluating societies by what I call preference-autonomy indexing—that is, the extent to which individuals can pursue the paths they actually prefer without being forced into them by economic necessity or pushed out of them by social penalty. I therefore argue that policies aiming directly at numerical gender parity in specific occupations risk reducing autonomy by treating statistical divergence as injustice even when it reflects freely made choices.
