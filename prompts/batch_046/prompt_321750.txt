hey, I've been noticing this pattern in philosophy of mind discussions, especially when people talk about AI consciousness and whether we could ever tell. The pattern is: someone grants that phenomonal properties — the actual feel of pain, or being awake, or seeing red — don't cause anything physical. They don't cause any behavorial or neural effects, they don't cause you to say "I'm in pain," they don't cause your belief that you're conscious. So far that's just standard epiphenomenalism, right?

But then they turn around and say "well I still know I'm conscious because I'm directly acquainted with it," treating that knowledge like it doesn't need any causal link. They act as if first-person access is a special kind of observation that works even when the observed thing can't affect the observer — like a camera that can photograph something that emits no light. I've seen this with pain reports and with the "I'm awake right now" case, where people insist they can't be wrong in their own case, even though they just agreed the feeling doesn't cause the belief.

What strikes me is the asymmetry: they apply the causal exclusion strictly for third-person cases — that's why we can't detect consciousness in AI — but they don't apply it to themselves. They don't explain why introspection gets a free pass, they just treat it as obvious and don't recieve any pressure to defend it. Is there an established name for this specific move, where you accept causal inertness everywhere except in your own case and treat that exception as needing no argument? I know it's near the paradox of phenomenal judgment, but that seems to be about whether judgments can be true without being caused by their objects. Here the weird part is specifically the first-person exemption and the refusal to justify it. If there's no precise term, how should we carve it out — what's the core of the move, what's it not, and what would be a good name? I don't want just a label; I want to understand exactly where the edges are.
