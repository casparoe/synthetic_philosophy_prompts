I am preparing a feature for my science-and-ideas column on how consequentialist reasoning is being absorbed into consumer technology, and after a long conversation with my editor about whether moral philosophy can survive being translated into product specs, I want a satirical companion piece that treats the demandingness objection not as a theoretical puzzle but as a venture-capital design brief. Write a deadpan parody of a Silicon Valley product-launch press release—approximately 600 words, in the flat, hyper-jargonized voice of a wellness startup announcing its new wearable ecosystem—called the Obligation Band. The device continuously calculates a user’s real-time “demandingness deficit” by applying scalar consequentialist calculus rather than binary act-rules: every local choice is weighed against globally optimal welfare distributions, and the interface displays a running balance of unfulfilled agent-neutral obligations that updates with each conditionalized prior. To keep the satire conceptually precise and in the weeds, inject the idea of “moral offsetting”—users purchase utility credits to neutralize localized consumption—along with a subscription tier named Obligation Plus that rebrands supererogation as a premium feature. The fictional Chief Ethics Officer should treat the “threshold problem,” the philosophical question of where demandingness becomes excessive, as a user-experience calibration challenge, while the system classifies agent-relative reasons—family obligations, personal projects, rest—as “legacy preference modules” subject to algorithmic depreciation. Include one invented quote from this CTO discussing prior welfare distributions, and one from a skeptical “legacy humanist” consultant complaining about the depreciation schedule.

The piece must stay deadpan and legible: a specialist should recognize that the exaggeration is fair, seeing how scalar consequentialism’s refusal of a stopping point is literalized in an endlessly updating deficit display, while a general reader should grasp that there is no stable boundary between “enough” and “not enough” once the calculus is taken seriously. Do not include bibliographies, citations, or reading lists; the text should stand entirely on its internal explanations. Avoid naming living individuals or attributing specific published works to anyone; the only named voices should be the two fictional characters. The humor should arise from getting the conceptual machinery right—conditionalization on priors, the distinction between local and cloud-based reasoning, the impossibility of defining a non-arbitrary ceiling for sacrifice—rather than from inventing false philosophical positions. End with the implied reductio: if the logic holds consistently, rest is never earned, only deferred.
