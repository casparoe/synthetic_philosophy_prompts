I'm preparing a presentation for my philosophy-of-science graduate seminar on the compatibility of Laplacean determinism with human freedom, and I've become stuck on a tension I see in recent discussions of deterministic chaos. I understand the standard point that chaotic dynamical systems—say, Lorenz-type attractors—exhibit sensitive dependence on initial conditions, which makes long-range forecasting practically impossible even when the governing equations are fully deterministic. A colleague in applied mathematics also mentioned the shadowing lemma, which she used to argue that numerical approximations of chaotic orbits are always tracked by some true orbit of the system, so that practical unpredictability never amounts to a failure of determinism itself.

Reading further on my own, I encountered the idea that certain chaotic maps generate symbolic dynamics with maximal Kolmogorov-Sinai entropy, producing bit sequences that are algorithmically incompressible—meaning no algorithm shorter than the sequence itself can reproduce it. From this, I started to think that chaos might introduce something stronger than mere epistemic limitation: if the evolution of a deterministic system yields outcomes that are uncomputable from the initial conditions by any finite procedure, then determinism might be technically satisfied (the laws still govern every step) yet metaphysically hollow, because the link between present state and future outcome is broken in principle rather than only in practice. So my question is whether algorithmic incompressibility in chaotic dynamics actually undermines the determinist's claim that the future is fixed by the present, or whether it remains only a statement about our computational limits. I would especially appreciate clarification of whether the shadowing lemma is compatible with this stronger claim, or whether it shows that every apparently random outcome is still deterministically anchored. Please respond in American English.
