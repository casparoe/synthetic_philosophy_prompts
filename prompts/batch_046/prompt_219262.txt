I am running a 90-minute graduate workshop on economic epistemology for about fourteen students who have already worked through the canonical texts of the socialist calculation debate but still tend to frame the disagreement as being primarily about incentives rather than about the structure of knowledge itself. I want a classroom simulation that forces them to experience—before anyone labels the problem—why locally dispersed, tacit, and indexically anchored information resists capture by a central authority even when that authority employs iterative shadow-price adjustments. The activity should feel concrete rather than abstract: participants should be managing a simulated supply chain in which hidden local conditions determine outcomes, yet those conditions can only be partially and misleadingly translated into formal reports.

Please design a structured game with rules, roles, a timeline, and small real stakes. Divide the group so that three or four students form a central planning board that must allocate scarce intermediate inputs across four production nodes each round, using only standardized quantitative report forms submitted by the remaining students, who act as local managers. Each manager should hold a private condition card describing an unobservable local reality—such as an improvised workaround, an invisible material degradation, or a context-specific substitution possibility—that is genuinely true but structurally inexpressible within the report's categories. The board sets allocation quotas or shadow prices; the managers respond with adjustments expressed solely through the form's limited fields. After two normal rounds, introduce a crisis round with an unexpected input shock that alters the hidden conditions in ways the reporting framework cannot register, causing the board's iterative corrections to diverge wildly from actual needs. Include a payoff mechanism—perhaps accumulated points that determine which text the group discusses next week—so that coordination failures carry tangible weight.

Following the simulation, provide a debrief that maps specific breakdown moments onto arguments in the debate: Mises's claim that rational calculation requires actual market prices formed through exchange with private property, Hayek's emphasis on the division of knowledge and the inability of central aggregation to replicate dispersed contextual awareness, Lange's counter-proposal that trial-and-error shadow pricing could approximate market coordination without private ownership, and the precise limitation of this simulation—namely that it can reproduce the epistemic opacity of local conditions but cannot by itself reproduce the dynamic entrepreneurial discovery of entirely new possibilities, which the facilitator must supply. Also include an honest note on what predictably goes wrong when running this: managers may try to cheat the form, the board may overfit to noisy reports, and some participants may reduce the problem to mere communication delay rather than structural inexpressibility, with brief suggestions for how to redirect each failure. Do not ask for a bibliography, citation list, or external reading recommendations; the goal is for the idea to emerge from the rules and payoffs of the game itself.
