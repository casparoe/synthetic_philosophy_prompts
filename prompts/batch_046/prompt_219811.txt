I'm writing a position paper for an advanced seminar that crosses philosophy of mind with the metaphysics of collective artifacts, and I keep returning to a tension I think the literature on the extended mind has not fully resolved. Most discussions of Clark and Chalmers's parity principle treat the boundary question as if it were always one-to-one: one agent, one notebook, one belief. But I want you to make a rigorous argument for a more radical extension—specifically, that when an external representational artifact is constitutively integrated into the cognitive processes of several agents at once, we should not distribute ownership across individual extended minds, but should instead recognize a genuinely polycentric cognitive system.

Your task is to defend the position that such shared artifacts—collaboratively maintained navigational charts, intergenerational mnemonic devices, or distributed classificatory systems—function not merely as tools used by a collective, but as substrates that transform the metaphysical status of the beliefs they carry. Argue that applying the parity principle separately to each participant produces contradictory ascriptions of mental ownership, and that the only coherent resolution is a non-aggregative form of group cognition that is irreducible to the union of individual extended minds. You should anticipate and respond to the objection that this view simply reproduces standard accounts of collective intentionality—say, those associated with Searle or Tuomela—by explaining precisely why treating the artifact as constitutive rather than instrumental breaks the analogy with shared action. Please develop this conceptually rather than through empirical case studies, and do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; I want an argument that stands on its internal logic rather than on appeals to authority.
