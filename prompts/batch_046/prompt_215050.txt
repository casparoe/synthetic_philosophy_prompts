I am a high school student enrolled in an advanced philosophy seminar, and I have been studying Immanuel Kant's moral writings in order to understand his well-known view that human beings have direct duties only to rational agents, while any obligations concerning animals are indirect and grounded in the preservation of our own moral character. From my reading, Kant seems to hold that cruelty to animals is wrong not because it violates the rights of the animals themselves, but because it hardens the heart and weakens the disposition to treat human beings with respect; yet since animals lack autonomy and the capacity for rational self-legislation, they do not belong to the realm of ends in themselves. This framework was formulated in the late eighteenth century, long before contemporary biotechnology, and I am trying to apply it to a problem that could not have existed during Kant's lifetime.

Consider a hypothetical scenario projected for the mid-twenty-second century, in which synthetic biology and automated vertical agriculture make it possible to cultivate engineered mammalian organisms—call them nutritive substrates—that are specifically designed through precise genetic editing to lack nociception, consciousness, and any form of future-oriented cognition. These beings would exist solely as living tissue cultures in fully mechanized facilities, never experiencing suffering and possessing none of the rational nature that Kant associates with moral personality, and they would be harvested exclusively for protein production. Given Kant's distinction between direct and indirect moral consideration, how do you think he would evaluate this practice? Would he maintain that we still have an indirect duty not to treat these entities as mere resources, on the grounds that deliberately manufacturing living organisms for pure instrumentalization expresses a kind of moral hubris and degrades the human dignity that requires us to respect nature's order? Or would he conclude that, in the complete absence of both sentience and potential rational autonomy, there is no relevant moral consideration at all—direct or indirect—because nothing in the being connects to our own moral nature? Moreover, do you think he would regard the intentional design of non-sentient life for consumption as morally worse than using naturally occurring animals, precisely because the act of creation is guided entirely by instrumental purpose rather than by an acknowledgment of a natural order that we merely inhabit?
