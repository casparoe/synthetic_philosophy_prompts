I'm drafting the capstone assignment for my graduate seminar on normative ethics, and I want the students to produce something genuinely collaborative rather than adversarial in the usual sense. We have been working through the paradox of deontology—the apparent conflict between agent-centered constraints against harming and the apparent obligation to minimize harm, even in cases where the only way to prevent greater harm is to inflict a lesser one. Two camps have crystallized in our discussions, and I would like you to draft the joint statement that representatives of both could sign after a sustained adversarial collaboration.

On one side stand the Constraint Preservationists, who hold that the paradox is not merely apparent but structurally deep: any consistent deontology must either abandon its absolute prohibition on using persons as mere means or abandon its claim that we have stringent duties to rescue and protect, and attempts to split the difference collapse into incoherence. On the other side stand the Agent-Relativity Dissolutionists, who maintain that the paradox dissolves once we rigorously separate agent-relative reasons (the special wrongness of my causing harm) from agent-neutral reasons (the value of states of affairs in which harm is minimized); on their view, there is no contradiction in holding that I must not kill one to save five while also recognizing that a world with one death is worse than a world with five. To keep the collaboration from rehashing well-worn trolley scenarios, I want both sides to address a constructed crux that I will call institutional mediation—cases in which an agent achieves a harm-minimizing outcome only by authorizing or delegating to an organizational structure that performs the harmful act, so that no individual in the chain directly causes the harm, yet the agent's authorization is causally and morally indispensable. Both camps should state whether this mediation genuinely removes the agent-relative wrong or merely distributes it across collective roles.

Please produce the document in the requested format: first, a numbered section listing the claims both sides genuinely accept—about the formal structure of deontological theories, about what needs explaining in the classic cases, and about what institutional mediation involves—written in language each camp would endorse. Then present a disagreements table that gives the precise formulations of their residual disputes, phrased so that each side recognizes its own view in its row. Identify the cruxes—specifically, whether agent-relative wrongness can be genuinely distributed or delegated, and whether the distinction between doing and allowing survives institutional complexity—and for each crux specify a concrete case or argument both sides agree is relevant, together with each side's explicit prediction about how that case should be resolved. Where a disagreement turns out to be verbal rather than substantive, say exactly which part and why. The goal is not adjudication but exactitude: I want to see where the argument lives once the rhetoric is stripped away.
