I was discussing with a colleague whether Darwin's account of adaptive design and Turing's behavioral criterion for intelligence form a single philosophical pattern—what I will call the competence-comprehension inversion—rather than two separate historical episodes. The evolutionary side is familiar: natural selection produces exquisitely functional structures, from eyes to immune responses to navigational capacities, without any comprehension, intention, or representational foresight in the mechanism that produces them. The Turing side inverts the direction: since we lack direct access to internal mental states, we are asked to attribute comprehension—intelligence, understanding, consciousness—solely on the basis of sustained behavioral competence, treating performance as the only available evidence for mind. The stronger thesis that emerged in our conversation is that these inversions are jointly exhaustive and mutually reinforcing. They imply that comprehension is never a necessary causal condition for the production of competence (Darwin), and never an independently detectable property that exceeds or diverges from observed competence (Turing). From this, one might conclude that ascriptions of understanding are either pragmatic heuristics applied to flexible performance or redundant projections with no explanatory work to do, and that any attempt to ground mental attribution in an internal, non-behavioral factor is either empirically empty or collapses into an unverifiable dualism.

I find this paired thesis compelling but worry that it might be too well insulated—capable of absorbing any counterexample by reinterpretation—so I want a direct assessment of its exposure. What would have to be true for the double inversion to fail? On the evolutionary side, is there a live empirical or theoretical possibility that certain forms of biological adaptation—perhaps those involving anticipatory modeling, error-correction architectures, or syntactic representation—cannot be accounted for by selection on behavior alone, and that they instead require an internal representational system whose presence is not reducible to its behavioral outputs? On the Turing side, is there a formal or conceptual argument showing that behavioral equivalence necessarily underdetermines internal structure in a way that makes it impossible for comprehension to be genuinely absent where competence is present, or vice versa? I am especially interested in what defenders of the paired view would themselves concede as counting against it—not merely what critics say—such as a domain where we reliably withhold mental ascription despite high behavioral competence, or grant it despite marked incompetence, and whether those cases can be accommodated without ad hoc qualification. Does the position risk collapsing into a broader eliminativism about mind, and if so, is that a theoretical cost or an intended consequence? Please distinguish decisive defeaters from merely awkward ones, and say whether you think the thesis is genuinely exposed or protected by qualifications that render it unfalsifiable in practice.
