I am a final-year undergraduate taking a seminar on medieval rationalist theology, and I am preparing to interview—on a student-run philosophy podcast—an unnamed visiting scholar who defends an unconventional reading of Maimonides on animal sacrifice. Rather than accepting the familiar concessional account, according to which sacrificial law is merely a pedagogical accommodation to Israel's idolatrous past, she proposes what she calls a 'preservationist' thesis: that the detailed regulations of sacrifice in the Torah do not point away from animality but deliberately institutionalise it, preserving a non-rational, embodied creaturely dimension within worship that Maimonides' intellectualist ethics would otherwise suppress. She expects three objections: that this romanticises a violent practice; that it sits uneasily with Maimonides' teleological view of the commandments as instruments of intellectual perfection; and that it cannot explain why, if the purpose is merely symbolic preservation, the law specifies particular species, ages, and bodily defects with such granular precision.

Please draft a sequence of eight to ten interview questions that press her specifically on this preservationist framing, without asking her simply to restate it. The arc should begin with the textual basis in Maimonides' principal philosophical work—without requesting citations or bibliographies—then move to the tension between this institutionalised animality and the intellectualist ideal of worship directed solely toward knowledge of God, and finally to the problem of regulatory granularity: why the law distinguishes between blemished and unblemished animals in ways that seem to require more than symbolic significance. For each of the three anticipated objections, include a follow-up question keyed to the weakest point in the reply she is most likely to give. Conclude with a single question asking what kind of textual or archaeological discovery—again, with no request for specific sources—would genuinely undermine her reading rather than merely adding historical complexity. The questions should be suitable for an audience of philosophy students familiar with the standard concessional interpretation but not with this particular reconstruction.
