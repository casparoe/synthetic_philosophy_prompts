Consider a jurisdiction that enacts, through fully regular legislative procedures, a criminal sentencing statute that applies prospectively to all defendants convicted within specified categories. The statute is published in the official gazette and sets precise input-output rules: conviction class and prior record determine a sentence range. However, the computation is performed by a proprietary machine-learning model whose internal weights and feature interactions are opaque even to the judges who apply it. Defendants can learn their category and the resulting range, but they cannot understand, anticipate, or contest the specific reasoning that produces their particular sentence.

A legal theorist claims that this arrangement violates Lon Fuller's inner morality of law—especially the requirements of clarity and congruence between declared rule and official action—because the law fails to guide conduct in the intelligible way that legality requires. The theorist supports this by appealing to Fuller's well-known critique of the Nazi grudge-informer statutes, arguing that both cases involve a breakdown of the transparency necessary for law to function as a system of rules rather than arbitrary power.

A second theorist replies that H.L.A. Hart's legal positivism defeats this objection. On this reply, Fuller's eight desiderata are conditions of efficacy or principles of legality, not moral conditions; they describe what makes a rule-system work well, not what makes it law. Hart's separation thesis therefore permits fully valid law that is procedurally opaque, provided it meets formal criteria of enactment and institutional pedigree. Moreover, Hart's discussion of the minimum content of natural law concerns substantive protections for life and property, not procedural transparency, so it offers no support to the first theorist.

Assess this exchange. Specifically: (a) Does Fuller's critique of the grudge-informer laws genuinely extend to algorithmic opacity, or does it depend on features—such as retroactivity or secrecy of the rule itself—absent here? (b) How should Hart's distinction between the separation thesis and the minimum content of natural law be brought to bear—does it successfully insulate formal validity from Fuller's procedural concerns, or does it mislocate the point of the inner morality?

Student answer:

I believe the exchange turns on whether procedural opacity is the same kind of failure Fuller's inner morality targets.

Fuller's account lists eight desiderata—generality, promulgation, prospectivity, clarity, consistency, feasibility of compliance, stability over time, and congruence between official action and declared rule. Together these form an inner morality that is not external moral evaluation but a set of conditions that make law possible as a guide to conduct. The algorithmic statute satisfies most of them: it is general, promulgated, prospective, consistent, and stable. Its weakness lies in clarity and congruence. Fuller's famous critique of the Nazi grudge-informer laws showed that when legal rules are kept secret or applied retroactively, citizens are left without guidance; law collapses into arbitrary command. By analogy, when defendants know the statutory inputs and outputs but cannot access or understand the model's reasoning, they are in a structurally similar position: they are subject to rules whose application they cannot anticipate or contest. Thus the first theorist's extension is sound.

Hart's response in The Concept of Law is to distinguish the separation thesis from what he calls the minimum content of natural law. The separation thesis holds that there is no necessary conceptual link between law and morality; the minimum content holds only that, as a matter of contingent fact, any viable system will include basic protections because humans are vulnerable. Fuller's eight principles, Hart argues in Chapter 9, belong neither to morality proper nor to the minimum content; they are conditions of efficacy—requirements for a system of rules to function as such. Therefore, even if the algorithm reduces predictability, that is a failure of good legal craftsmanship, not a failure of legality. The statute remains law, albeit inefficient law.

Still, I think the second theorist overstates Hart's insulation. Fuller's concern with clarity and congruence is not merely about efficiency but about the very possibility of self-direction under rules. If citizens cannot understand how a rule applies to them, the system lacks the transparency that makes rule-following rational. Hart might reply that efficacy is a matter of degree, but Fuller's stronger claim is that extreme procedural failures destroy law altogether. So the algorithmic case may not reach the extremity of the Nazi example—there is no retroactivity or secret statute—but it does move in the direction of a failure of legality, not just poor policy.

One point of confusion to avoid: it is tempting to read Fuller's inner morality as a version of Dworkin's principle of integrity, where law must present itself as a coherent moral narrative. But Fuller's view is structural, not interpretive; he is concerned with the formal conditions of rule-guidance, not with moral coherence across cases. Therefore, the first theorist should not claim that the algorithm violates law's moral integrity; she should claim that it violates law's formal capacity to guide.
