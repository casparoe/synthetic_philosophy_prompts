I'm a PhD student in philosophy of language running a 75-minute graduate seminar for 10–12 students. Design a structured, lecture-free activity that makes Quinean indeterminacy of translation feel like a live practical problem rather than a static "gavagai" puzzle, and do not name the thesis until the debrief.

The conceptual injection: the simulated "native language" should include aspectual particles that do not grammatically distinguish enduring objects from temporal stages. This should allow one field team to construct a manual rendering an ostensive display as "there is a rabbit" while another team, using identical behavioral evidence, renders it as "there is a rabbit-stage," with both manuals predicting every assent, dissent, and gesture pattern exactly. The divergence should emerge from the students' own rule-building, not from facilitator revelation.

Please specify: (1) roles—two "field linguist" teams observing the same confederate speaker, with explicit rules that restrict admissible evidence to observable assent/dissent and ostensive behavior and forbid mental-state attribution; (2) a manual-construction phase with a clear timeline and materials; (3) a coordination round in which both teams must jointly distribute shared resource tokens using only their translated terms, forcing contradictory practical allocations despite perfect predictive parity; (4) rules that block resolution by appeal to hidden intentions or "what the speaker really meant"; (5) the debrief, mapping the observation phase, manual divergence, and coordination failure onto Quine's analytic hypotheses, ontological relativity, and the underdetermination of translation manuals by behavior; (6) an honest note on what the activity cannot reproduce—specifically, the full behaviorist reduction of semantic content to dispositions and the logical infinity of empirically equivalent manuals—plus guidance on the predictable failure mode (students will assume they missed an empirical clue, or will smuggle in mentalistic glosses) and how to handle it without breaking the frame. Keep it formal, concise, and free of citation or bibliography requests.
