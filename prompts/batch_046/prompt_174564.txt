I'm a doctoral student in moral philosophy working on practical reason and the structure of rational commitment, and I've been wrestling with a problem that came up in our graduate seminar on decision theory. The standard economic view holds that honoring sunk costs is always irrational, because past expenditures are causally irrelevant to the expected value of future outcomes; my advisor pressed this point forcefully, arguing that any appeal to prior investment is necessarily a cognitive distortion rather than a legitimate reason. I want to push back, but not in the familiar ways—I'm not interested in defending sloppy financial reasoning or the gambler's fallacy. Instead, I'm drawn to a more specific and, I think, undertheorized case: situations in which the "cost" is not monetary but biographical, and in which continuing a course of action serves not to recover the investment instrumentally but to preserve the coherence of one's practical identity. To make this concrete, consider a composer who has spent over a decade developing an intricate acoustic language for a chamber ensemble format that is now technologically obsolete and commercially nonviable. She recognizes that further work will not yield recognition, income, or audience expansion, and she is fully aware that her years of training are irrecoverable. Nevertheless, she continues, not because she miscalculates future returns, but because the practice has become constitutive of her agency: abandoning it feels less like prudent reallocation and more like a kind of self-fragmentation. I am planning to write an essay that asks whether honoring sunk costs in this identity-constitutive register can ever escape the charge of irrationality.

Weigh the main arguments on both sides with care. On one side, defend the hard-line view that even biographical investments provide no genuine reason for action once future benefits are excluded, and that appeals to "narrative coherence" or "practical identity" merely dress up an affective attachment in rational clothing. On the other side, develop the reply that practical reason is not exhausted by instrumental optimization, and that maintaining commitments central to one's self-conception can be rational even when they are costly and unrecoverable. Do not end on a non-committal or purely descriptive note: reach a clear verdict about whether there exists a legitimate exception to the sunk-cost fallacy for identity-preserving commitments, and justify that conclusion with explicit reasoning. You may assume familiarity with standard decision-theoretic frameworks, but do not rely on obscure technical apparatus or require specialized empirical data.
