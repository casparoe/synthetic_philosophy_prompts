I am a novelist preparing an essay for a literary quarterly that treats philosophy as narrative architecture rather than as doctrine. The piece examines what I call the inversion of reasoning shared by Charles Darwin and Alan Turing: in both thinkers, a form of competence that operates without comprehension is made to explain phenomena that tradition had taken to require comprehension from the outset.

In Darwin's account—particularly in his discussion of moral faculties in The Descent of Man—social instincts and habitual behaviors are selected for their survival value long before any rational justification exists. The comprehension that humans later experience as moral reasoning is, on this view, a post-hoc rationalization of competences that were never guided by reason. The inversion is genealogical: reason does not generate morality; morality generates reason as its narrative afterimage.

In Turing's 1950 essay Computing Machinery and Intelligence, the inversion is performative. By replacing the question of whether machines can think with the question of whether a machine can behave indistinguishably from a thinker, Turing severs intelligence from internal comprehension. The machine's competence—statistical pattern-matching, learning by reward—needs no understanding of meaning to produce outputs that an observer interprets as meaningful. Yet Turing also introduces the child machine, a system that learns through education rather than being fully programmed, which seems to reintroduce a developmental, almost genealogical trajectory into the machine's competence.

My essay proposes that these two inversions are mirror images that fail to align precisely because of this temporal difference. Darwin's competence is collective and historical: the species accumulates blind adaptations over generations, and comprehension emerges only at the end of that accumulation. Turing's competence is individual and present-tense: the machine acquires patterns in real time, and comprehension is attributed by an external observer in the moment of interaction. The strange result is that Darwin removes comprehension from nature and then locates it in the human subject as an evolutionary latecomer, while Turing removes comprehension from the machine and then locates it in the human observer. Both thinkers, in other words, preserve comprehension by displacing it—Darwin into the rationalizing agent, Turing into the interpreting interrogator.

I am especially interested in whether this framing obscures something important. Does Darwin's account of moral development actually allow for a stronger, non-rationalist form of comprehension—perhaps a kind of embodied social knowledge—that my post-hoc rationalization framing flattens? And does Turing's child-machine analogy genuinely smuggle back a form of internal development that undermines his own performative inversion, or is it merely a pedagogical metaphor?

I would be grateful for comments on the accuracy of these characterizations, on whether the genealogical-versus-performative distinction is genuinely illuminating or artificially imposed, and on whether the central comparison risks being more elegant than true. I am also open to suggestions about which parts of the argument feel underdeveloped—particularly the claim about the observer's role in Turing's inversion—and whether there are obvious objections from the philosophy of biology or philosophy of mind that I have failed to anticipate. The essay is intended for an intelligent general audience, so clarity of the conceptual architecture matters more than scholarly comprehensiveness; I do not need bibliographies, citations, or reading lists, only critical judgment on the ideas as presented.
