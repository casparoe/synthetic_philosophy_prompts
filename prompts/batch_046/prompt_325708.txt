Here is an argument I built for a seminar on Kierkegaard's attack on the press and the concept of the public, and now I worry it is a self-undermining trap. (1) For Kierkegaard, the press creates the public by allowing anyone to hold opinions without being anyone in particular, so that speech is structurally detached from personal presence and ethical accountability. (2) Genuine ethical communication, by contrast, requires that the speaker be present and answerable—ultimately to God—for what is said. (3) If detachment of speech from particular identity destroys ethical accountability, then any medium that structurally separates speaker from message is corrupting. (4) Yet Kierkegaard published his major pseudonymous works—under names such as Johannes Climacus and Anti-Climacus—through the very Copenhagen press he criticized, using false names that explicitly concealed his particular legal identity. (5) So either his own authorship falls under his condemnation, or there is a principled distinction between the anonymity of the public and the pseudonymity of the religious author. (6) The only available distinction is that the pseudonymous author retains inward divine accountability, while the public speaker is accountable to no one. (7) But inward accountability to God is not externally observable; from the outside, an anonymous commentator making the same claim of inner answerability is indistinguishable from Kierkegaard. (8) Therefore the criterion either condemns its own creator or collapses into an unverifiable subjective exemption that anyone can invoke. (9) Hence the critique proves too much: it undermines the author's practice unless it abandons any observable standard for responsible speech. Which step should I reject? I would prefer to keep (2) if I can, but I want to know what giving up each of the other premises costs. Please say what would change your mind about where the flaw actually lies, and flag the point in your reasoning that is most likely to fail. Keep your response under 350 words, in at most three paragraphs.
