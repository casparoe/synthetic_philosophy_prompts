I facilitate a rationalist reading group—eight regulars, mostly STEM backgrounds with scattered philosophy exposure, meeting for ninety minutes in a borrowed seminar room. Design an in-class activity that makes the group feel the epistemic pressure of the Boltzmann brain problem through their own choices rather than through exposition. To keep it from being a reheated version of every cosmology podcast they have already heard, build the exercise around an invented but structurally faithful “memory-fragment consistency game”: each participant draws a hidden role card—“evolved trajectory” or “thermal-fluctuation trajectory”—where both roles carry an identical set of apparent memories, reasoning capacities, and sensory access to the present room. They must then reach a collective decision about which origin-story to endorse, using only internal coherence checks and a small shared stake (control of the next session’s reading selection plus a pooled snack budget). Structure the rules so that every verification procedure available to one role is symmetrically available to the other, forcing the group to discover that coordination collapses not because anyone is demonstrably wrong, but because self-location fails under the prior. Provide the role cards, the decision protocol, a ninety-minute timeline with clear phases, the payoff mechanism, and a debrief that maps each moment of the game onto the standard Boltzmann argument—including explicit acknowledgment of which cosmological feature (actual fluctuation-rate distributions, decoherence conditions, or the measure problem over infinite space-time) the game necessarily simplifies or misrepresents, and how you would supply that missing piece in discussion. The idea should arrive in what happens to the group, not in what you say afterwards. After presenting the design, state how confident you are that this produces the intended epistemic shock rather than mere skepticism about other minds, and single out the specific design element you are least sure will survive contact with real participants.
