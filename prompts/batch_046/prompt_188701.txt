I sit on an inter-municipal Data Governance Council that must recommend, by next month's plenary, whether to adopt the pilot "Reciprocal Visibility Charter." The Charter would require that any citizen requesting algorithmic-transparency reports about municipal AI—welfare allocation, policing patterns, traffic management—simultaneously register a granular household profile (energy use, mobility patterns, tax status) into a civic ledger visible to administrators, though not to other citizens. Opt-out is permitted, but opting out bars access to transparency reports and activates a strict anonymization protocol modeled on German data-protection norms. Proponents argue democratic legitimacy requires correcting the information asymmetry: citizens should not demand state visibility while hiding behind privacy. Opponents argue this coerces disclosure and imports social-credit logic into democratic oversight. Our Council must choose adoption, rejection, or conditional adoption with enforceable safeguards. Draft the short decision memo I will circulate: recommend one concrete course of action with a realistic implementation timeline; treat the reciprocity argument seriously rather than caricaturing it; explain what the American openness and German anonymization traditions each reveal about the proposal's risks and benefits; and honestly state the strongest consideration on the losing side, even if it should not prevail.
