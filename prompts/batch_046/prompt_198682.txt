I am convening a ninety-minute graduate seminar in metaphysics and the philosophy of physics, with twelve participants seated around a single table. I want them to feel the pressure of the hole argument—the thesis that applying a smooth transformation inside a matter-free region of spacetime produces a mathematically distinct representation describing the exact same physical situation—before anyone names the argument or introduces differential geometry. Design a runnable, low-tech simulation using only paper grid-maps, colored tokens, and a simple betting or scoring mechanism. The rules should require participants to place physical events (token meetings) on a shared map, then apply a permitted “relabeling rule” that shifts coordinate assignments inside a designated blank region while leaving the exterior unchanged. They must then fill out a point-coincidence ledger: for each event, they record whether it occurs at the “same point” across the two descriptions. Structure the payoffs so that treating the two descriptions as ontologically distinct worlds yields a prediction failure—specifically, determinism about the future evolution of points inside the hole collapses—while treating them as redundant descriptions preserves consistency but forces them to abandon the view that individual spacetime points have independent reality. Include a precise debrief that maps each phase of the exercise onto a corresponding move in the argument: the initial substantivalist commitment to the grid, the diffeomorphism enacted inside the blank region, the point-coincidence check, and the resulting redundancy of representations. Anticipate the predictable failures—participants conflating coordinate relabeling with physical motion, or insisting that an undetectable hidden difference must exist—and specify how the facilitator should correct these confusions through guided questioning rather than lecture. Be frank about the feature that this paper exercise cannot reproduce—namely, the constraining role of the Einstein field equations in fixing the transformation—and state exactly how the facilitator must supply that missing element in the final five minutes. Do not assign advance readings, do not include a bibliography, and do not reveal the name of the argument until the debrief’s closing stage; the force of the idea should emerge entirely from what the rules and incentives make the participants do.
