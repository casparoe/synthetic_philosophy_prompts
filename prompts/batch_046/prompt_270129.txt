I just spent the better part of an evening arguing with a close friend about Moritz Schlick’s view that life carries no transcendent purpose and that its only genuine meaning lies in play—activity pursued for its own sake rather than as a means to some further end. My friend insisted this was merely romantic nostalgia for childhood, but I kept returning to Schlick’s sharper claim: that once an activity becomes instrumental—art done for reputation, sport done for health, conversation done for networking—it ceases to be meaningful in the strict sense, and adulthood is essentially a long, tragic migration from play into work. To settle the argument, I want to see what happens if we take the framework literally. Write a deadpan parody of a corporate “Employee Meaning and Play-State Audit,” formatted as a numbered questionnaire with checkboxes and brief explanatory notes. The document should be authored in the voice of an earnest organizational psychologist who genuinely believes she can restore workplace meaning by classifying every task and relationship as either “Pure Play (Autotelic)” or “Instrumental Work (Meaning-Deficient),” with subcategories for “Regressed Adult Instrumentality,” “Failed Gamification,” and “Youth-State Recovery Protocols.” The satire should exaggerate real features of Schlick’s position—his insistence that meaning is not purpose, his identification of youth with play, his suspicion of any goal-directed corruption of activity—rather than inventing a strawman. Keep the irony sustained and deadpan: the form should never wink, and the absurdity should emerge from pushing Schlick’s own distinctions into bureaucratic overdrive. Aim for roughly 600 words. Make it readable to someone who has never encountered Schlick, but precise enough that someone familiar with his essay recognizes the specific exaggerations—especially the idea that even art and friendship become meaningless the moment they are pursued for an external reward. Do not include bibliographies, citations, or requests for source verification; the framework should be presented as an implemented company policy.
