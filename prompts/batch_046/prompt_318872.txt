As a retired physicist who has recently turned to philosophy of religion, I have been studying Gödel's unpublished notes on the afterlife alongside Leibniz's Monadology (1714). In the sections concerning simple substances, Leibniz maintains—if I read him aright—that monads are naturally indestructible, that death is only a transformation in which perception becomes more confused rather than ceasing, and that rational souls therefore preserve their identity across bodily change. I am trying to understand how Gödel transforms this into a modal-ontological claim: he seems to treat the survival of a thinking substance not as a biological fact but as a consequence of the logical consistency of the properties that constitute it, analogous to the way he treats the existence of God. Could you help me unpack the exact structure of this inference, distinguishing what is well-established in Gödel's notebooks from what scholars genuinely dispute—especially whether he meant to prove the personal continuity of an individual soul or only the necessary persistence of rational substance in some form? Please separate settled interpretation from live controversy, avoid presenting any disputed view as consensus, and do not supply bibliographies or reading lists; I wish to reconstruct the reasoning on my own.
