I was talking with a friend in my moral phil course about lexical threshold negative utilitarianism — the view that suffering has lexical priority over pleasure up to some cutoff, but past that point they become commensurable in some kind of weighted sum. We've both recieved the standard objections (that it's ad hoc, that it smuggles in a deontological cutoff) and the usual replies, and it feels like the literature just keeps replaying the same moves since maybe the 90s. But I wonder if the real stall is deeper: are the two sides even talking about the same kind of commensurability? One side seems to mean cardinal intersubstitutability in a welfare calculus, the other means phenomenological trade-offability — whether I can actually weigh a headache against a sunset. Meanwhile there's this niche idea I ran across about asymmetric valence carriers — that pleasure and pain don't share a common currency because they attach to different representational contents, not just different intensities. If that's right, then the whole framing of lexical thresholds might be a category mistake, treating two different mental kinds as points on one axis.

So here's my ask: diagnose why this particular disagreement has stalled. Is it substantive about welfare metrics, verbal about commensurability, or are the sides answering different questions — one normative about aggregation rules, one descriptive about phenomenal quality? And does the asymmetric valence carrier idea actually do any work, or is it just another redescription? Please say how confident you are in what you claim, adn single out the point you're least sure about — maybe whether the stall is genuinely conceptual or just that nobody's run the right thought experiment yet.
