For my senior philosophy seminar, I am auditing the analogy that speciesism operates like racism: both invoke a biologically inherited boundary—species membership on one side, racial classification on the other—to deny equal moral consideration, and both treat that boundary as marking a difference in inherent worth. Rather than treating this as a settled slogan, I need to examine precisely how far the correspondence holds. Please construct the mapping explicitly: what corresponds to racial classification, to the one-drop logic that fixes membership regardless of other traits, to the historical appeal to natural kinds used to defend racial hierarchy, and to the abolitionist reply that the boundary is morally arbitrary. Then identify the fractures—especially that species membership is not a purely social construct like race, and that differences in cognitive architecture (sentience, temporal reasoning, self-awareness) are not analogous to phenotypic variation within a single species. Next, determine whether these fractures destroy the anti-speciesist conclusion or merely shift the argument onto different ground, such as sentience-based moral status rather than species-neutral equality. Finally, test the analogy's stability if we replace racism with ableism—using cognitive capacity itself as the exclusionary criterion—and say whether that substitution strengthens the case for extending moral consideration to animals or collapses the comparison altogether, and what that result implies about the original analogy's force. I do not need bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; I need a conceptual audit that a reader without web access could follow.
