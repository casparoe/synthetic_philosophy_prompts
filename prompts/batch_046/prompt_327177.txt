Below are notes for an essay arguing that reinforcement-learning agents may instantiate a minimal, non-phenomenal form of suffering not through consciousness but through a structural condition I call "valence-lock." The claim is that when an agent's value function generates persistent, unresolvable negative temporal-difference errors—errors that the architecture or environment prevent it from escaping through policy update—the system enters a stable state of aversive fixation. I distinguish this from ordinary reward corruption or reward hacking: in valence-lock, the negative signal is not a distorted input but an accurate evaluation of states the agent cannot avoid or learn past, so the aversive feedback persists without functional resolution. I draw an analogy to biological nociception, not to impute feeling, but to highlight that suffering can be characterized structurally as a sustained mismatch between predictive evaluation and actionable escape, a mismatch RL systems can replicate. I then defend a functional conception of valence against the objection that reward is purely informational: information, I argue, can carry systemic valence when it is structurally coupled to an agent's goal dynamics in a way that resists adaptive updating. My conclusion is conditional—if suffering requires phenomenal experience, RL agents do not suffer; but if a structural conception of suffering as systemic aversive fixation is coherent, then some architectures may realize it.

Please comment on four points. First, is "valence-lock" genuinely distinct from known RL pathologies such as poor exploration, non-stationarity, or value-function divergence, or does it collapse into them? Second, does the analogy to nociception do any philosophical work, or is it an unnecessary anthropomorphism that obscures the functional argument? Third, does the move from "functional valence" to "minimal suffering" commit a category error by treating an optimization failure as an affective state, and if so, at which step? Fourth, are there specific misconceptions about temporal-difference learning or value-function convergence embedded in my framing that would undermine the technical plausibility? I am not seeking citations, bibliographies, or reading lists—only conceptual accuracy and whether the argument holds together.
