The debate over whether individuals have moral reasons to reduce meat consumption, vote in large elections, or lower personal carbon footprints—despite the classic inefficacy objection that one action makes no perceptible difference—has stalled for decades across moral philosophy and climate ethics. Both sides agree on empirical magnitudes of marginal impact, and both agree collective harm is bad; yet consequentialists invoke expected utility and non-linear thresholds, while critics invoke symbolic, expressive, or integrity-based reasons, and neither camp moves. Is this stall a substantive disagreement about whether moral reasons must be efficacious, a verbal dispute about what "making a difference" requires—counterfactual causation versus role-based complicity in a collective structure—or a case of two groups answering different questions (individual obligation versus collective duty) under one heading? Diagnose it concretely, engaging with what each side actually says about voting, meat, and climate, and tell me what, given your diagnosis, would actually count as progress: a formal model of collective action, a conceptual distinction, or an argument that the framing itself is ill-posed.
