I'm presenting to our philosophy-of-science seminar next Thursday. My thesis is that Al-Ghazālī's occasionalism should not be read as denying empirical regularities—fire burns cotton, medicine cures—but rather as targeting specifically the Aristotelian claim that created substances possess intrinsic natures (ṭabīʿa) that necessarily generate their effects. Once you separate observed correlation from metaphysical necessity, I argue, occasionalism is structurally closer to a Humean constant-conjunction view secured by divine guarantee than to an anti-scientific skepticism, and it therefore undermines scientific realism only about natures, not about predictive practice.

The audience is mostly analytic philosophers of science: several with explicit Humean sympathies on causation, two who work on scientific realism and inference to the best explanation, and one historian of Islamic philosophy who is protective of reading Al-Ghazālī as a theologian doing kalām rather than as a proto-philosopher of science. They care about whether occasionalism collapses predictive success, whether it licenses global skepticism, and whether my reading modernizes him. They will be suspicious that I am importing analytic distinctions he never made.

Prepare me for the question period: list the objections in roughly the order they will come, phrased as these people would phrase them. For each, note whether it has real force, what I should concede up front, what I should hold, and where my reply is weakest. Identify the weakest point in my own position—the objection I should prepare to defend rather than hope they miss. Finally, tell me the one objection I am probably hoping nobody asks, with the reply I need to have ready in the time available.
