Consider the institutional practice—widespread in universities and corporate sustainability programs—of running meat-reduction and air-travel pledges, presenting individual dietary and travel choices as genuinely impactful climate actions. This coexists with the standard economic and philosophical lesson that in large markets or electorates one person's choice is causally negligible: a single skipped flight or avoided burger does not change aggregate supply or emissions. Yet the same institutions rarely treat democratic voting, despite its comparable statistical inefficacy, as a morally optional contribution, and they do not apply the negligibility argument to tax compliance or jury service. What theory of individual responsibility within collective harm does this selective deployment of the inefficacy objection presuppose? Must it hold that some actions carry expressive, virtue-based, or relational weight regardless of causal impact, or can a control-based or contractual view license singling out consumption without treating civic obligations the same way? And are the practice’s parts internally consistent, or do they quietly depend on incompatible views of when individual agency morally matters—perhaps treating market participation as voluntary and therefore accountable, while treating democratic and legal duties as non-voluntary obligations immune to efficacy concerns? Distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely fits it well, and say whether replacing the underlying view would force the institution to abandon the pledges, extend them to voting, or drop the negligibility argument altogether.
