I recently joined the investment committee of my family’s philanthropic foundation, and we face a decision about endowing a thirty-year program in speculative carbon-capture technologies. Every consultant presents Monte Carlo simulations and risk-adjusted returns, yet the actual problem is that we cannot assign stable probabilities to whether the institutional framework governing carbon credits will exist in any recognizable form two decades hence—this is not risk within a fixed model, but Keynesian uncertainty, where numerical probabilities have no basis. After studying Keynes’s account of probability and rational belief, I am struck by his distinction between measurable risk and genuine uncertainty, and especially by the idea that the rational guide is not an odds-ratio but the weight of argument—the balance of relevant evidence against our total ignorance—though I find it hard to keep the two dimensions separate in practice.

I would like a sonnet that makes this epistemic structure visible through its form: use the octave to construct the seductive picture of quantifiable risk, with probabilities treated as fixed and the weight of evidence ignored; turn at line nine to the Keynesian rupture, where the absence of a numerical basis does not license arbitrariness but demands a different measure—weight rather than odds; and let the closing couplet state precisely what rational commitment requires when weight is substantial but probability remains undefined. The poem should be accurate enough that someone familiar with Keynes’s epistemology recognizes the specific function of weight versus mere ignorance, not a generic warning about an unpredictable future. Please begin with the poem itself, with no preface or commentary.
