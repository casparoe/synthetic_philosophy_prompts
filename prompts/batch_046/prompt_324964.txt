I run a 90-minute advanced undergraduate workshop with 16 participants on comparative moral psychology. I need a self-contained, runnable in-class simulation—no external readings assigned, no bibliographies or citation requests—that makes students feel, through rules and token-based stakes, the structural clash between German-style rule-following and institutional-order morality and American-style individual-conscience morality. The scenario should be a simulated district-level crisis: a partial contamination of the municipal water supply with ambiguous authority structures, where assigned roles (district bureaucrats with protocol manuals, emergency responders with discretionary powers, affected residents with private information, and an independent observer panel with scoring authority) must decide whether to adhere to hierarchical procedure or act on direct moral intuition.

Design the full activity: role cards with hidden incentives, a three-round structure (information asymmetry, decision with limited communication, consequence reveal), a symbolic-token payoff scheme that simultaneously rewards collective coordination and individual moral intervention but makes them structurally incompatible, and explicit rules that cause coordination to collapse in predictable ways when conscience-driven actors break protocol. Arrange the dynamics so the tension emerges from play itself: strict rule-followers should produce visible, orderly outcomes at an obvious human cost, while exceptionalist actors should achieve local moral rescue but destabilize the collective framework—before any facilitator names the philosophical distinction.

Then provide the debrief: map each round’s critical breakdown onto the conceptual distinction (institutional rule-integrity versus exceptionalist conscience); predict exactly what will go wrong on first run (e.g., observers breaking character to lecture, participants reducing the conflict to simple selfishness, one dominant role monopolizing deliberation) and specify how to correct it in real time; and be frank about which feature of the folk-morality divide—its deep embedding in long-term socialization and national narrative rather than in situational choice—the simulation necessarily cannot reproduce and that you must supply through framing discussion.

Keep the deliverable fully self-contained: materials list, timing breakdown, payoff table, round rules, debrief outline, and an honest assessment of the activity’s representational limits.
