I'm noticing a recurring structural split in moral biography—running through Plutarch's parallel lives, medieval hagiography, and even contemporary leadership narratives—where the episodes that carry the narrative weight are precisely the ones a reader cannot practically replicate. A Roman senator refuses a gift within an obsolete patronage network; a saint endures torture in a specific persecution; a modern founder works extreme hours during an unrepeatable market window. The reader is clearly invited to learn from the life, yet the actions themselves are too context-bound or extreme to serve as direct models. At the same time, the biographer constantly inserts brief normative judgments—this showed incorruptible nature, this revealed true humility—that seem to carry the actual pedagogical load. The episodes function less as imitable scripts and more as evidentiary spectacles that validate the author's evaluative framing. Is there an established concept or literature for this bifurcation, in which narrative content supplies proof or awe while commentary supplies the transferable moral instruction? If there is no precise term, can you coin and define one, distinguishing it carefully from ordinary moral exemplarity, from allegory, and from the simple failure of biography to be practical? I would also like the boundary conditions made explicit: does the phenomenon require that the episodes be genuinely unreplicable, or is it sufficient that they are merely highly unlikely? And how should we treat cases where the author seems unaware of the split, presenting the extreme episode itself as the direct model despite its obvious impracticality?
