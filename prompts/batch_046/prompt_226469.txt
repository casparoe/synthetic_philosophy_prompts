I run a 90-minute seminar on philosophy of mind for 14 upper-level undergraduates. Design an in-class simulation—using only index cards, colored tokens, and printed rulebooks—that makes the students feel the force of the Chinese Room argument before anyone names it. Include concrete rules, a small-stakes payoff scheme (for example, points for coherence ratings that determine a nominal prize), and rotation instructions so every student spends time in each role. The rules should be arranged so that Stage 2 genuinely wants to adapt rather than follow blindly, and the Coherence Rater should be incentivized to distinguish fluent-sounding outputs from genuinely responsive ones, so the group runs into the problem of whether adaptive syntax can become semantics. After 45 minutes of play, provide a debrief that maps specific moments—rule revision, coherence judgment, rotation—onto parts of the Chinese Room argument and asks whether the adaptation mechanism introduces something the standard formulation overlooks. Be explicit about what feature of the argument the simulation cannot reproduce (likely the first-person phenomenology of understanding) and tell me exactly how to supply that missing piece through guided reflection rather than facilitator assertion. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time—such as Stage 2 students inventing semantic shortcuts, or raters conflating fluency with meaning—and how to handle those failures without breaking the simulation frame.
