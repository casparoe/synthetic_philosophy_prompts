Design a classroom activity for an upper-division philosophy seminar of about 14 students across 75 minutes that makes participants feel the structural gap between mutual knowledge of rationality and common knowledge of rationality in epistemic game theory. Do not begin with a lecture; the distinction must emerge from play and failure.

Structure: Give every student a sealed envelope containing either “R” or “S” (distribute 10 R and 4 S). They open it privately but may not show it. The collective task is a simultaneous vote on “Proceed” in round 1: if exactly all R-holders vote Proceed and no S-holders do, each earns 5 points; any misalignment yields 0 for all; if no vote succeeds by round 3, everyone earns 1. Before voting, run three 4-minute “epistemic rounds” with strict speech rules: round 1, students may only state first-order facts about themselves; round 2, only second-order claims about what specific others know about them; round 3, only third-order claims. Crucially, secretly assign two students as silent observers who see every envelope but may not speak or vote—this asymmetry is not announced, only discovered if students notice inconsistencies. The payoff scheme should reward correct coordination but penalize false confidence with total loss, so rational hesitation is individually safe but collectively costly.

Include: (1) the exact rules, envelope distribution, timeline, and how the silent-observer asymmetry is revealed; (2) a debrief that maps each speech round onto levels of epistemic knowledge, explains why rational players who mutually know everyone is rational still fail to vote Proceed in round 1, and identifies precisely how the silent observers break common knowledge without breaking mutual knowledge; (3) an honest note on the feature this cannot reproduce—the formal infinite regress of “I know that you know that I know...”—and what the facilitator must supply verbally after the failure; (4) what will predictably go wrong the first time (students will likely violate the speech constraints and fabricate agreement by round 2) and how to correct it without destroying the experience. The idea should live in the voting failure and the discovered asymmetry, not in the facilitator’s summary.
