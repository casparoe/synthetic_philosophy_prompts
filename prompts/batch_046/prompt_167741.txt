After a long argument last night with a friend who keeps insisting that European integration is either pure intergovernmental cooperation or an illegitimate superstate, I tried to lay out exactly where I stand on sovereignty, the democratic deficit, and ever-closer union by working through concrete scenarios. I want to know whether my judgments hang together on a single principle, or whether I am just rationalizing inconsistencies.

Here are my verdicts.

First, if citizens reject a treaty revision in a national referendum and the substance is then implemented through a separate intergovernmental agreement ratified only by parliaments, I regard that as an illegitimate evasion of democratic sovereignty. The direct refusal should block the outcome, not merely the legal form.

Second, when a national constitutional court rules that an EU directive exceeds conferred competences and violates national constitutional identity, and the European Court of Justice responds by asserting unconditional supremacy of EU law, I accept the result. Sovereignty is genuinely pooled here; without a single final arbiter, union law fragments, so the democratic deficit is tolerable.

Third, if a treaty removes unanimity in energy policy and large states then outvote a small state to impose grid-interconnection rules that dissolve its nationally owned energy monopoly, I do not see a democratic deficit at all. The small state consented to qualified majority voting; being outvoted is the legitimate price of ever-closer union.

Fourth, when the EU adopts a common defense-procurement framework by invoking a general flexibility provision originally designed for economic harmonization—securing unanimous Council approval but avoiding explicit treaty revision and without focused parliamentary debate on defense—I call that an illegitimate power grab. Sovereignty over defense is core, and using an economic clause to bypass explicit constitutional authorization creates a democratic deficit regardless of governmental unanimity.

Fifth, the European Parliament—elected with chronically low turnout—uses its co-decision powers to impose uniform asylum-processing timelines that override a member state's tradition of discretionary, case-by-case evaluation. I think the weak electoral mandate makes this democratically hollow, yet because the harmonization protects vulnerable individuals against arbitrary state power, I ultimately find the deficit tolerable, though I remain genuinely uneasy about it.

The case I keep returning to is this: suppose the same Parliament, on the same low-turnout mandate, mandates a common corporate tax base that eliminates a small state's long-used competitive rate, and the state had previously agreed to move to qualified majority in taxation only for explicit anti-fraud measures, not for rate harmonization. Would I tolerate that the way I tolerate the asylum rule, reject it the way I reject the defense-procurement framework, or treat it like the energy outvoting?

Is there a coherent principle—perhaps distinguishing core sovereignty from pooled sovereignty, or input legitimacy from output legitimacy, or explicit consent from functional spillover—that captures cases one through five? If so, what does it predict for my back-and-forth tax case and for a scenario in which enhanced cooperation allows a subset of states to create a fiscal union that binds non-participating states through market-access conditions? And which of my five verdicts must I abandon if I want the principle to stay simple?
