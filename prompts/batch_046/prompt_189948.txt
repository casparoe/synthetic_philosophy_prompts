After a long argument last evening with a friend who has been studying forecasting methodology and the outside view of political collapse, I find myself unable to reconcile several judgments I made about when a reference class is legitimate for predicting the lifespan or dissolution of large political entities. I want to know whether there is a single principle that captures all of them, or whether some verdict must be sacrificed for coherence.

Here are my judgments. First, for forecasting the remaining endurance of the People's Republic of China, I think the correct reference class is “rapidly industrializing authoritarian regimes with single-party control” rather than “Chinese dynastic regimes,” because institutional form determines the vulnerability to legitimacy crisis more than civilizational continuity does; the causal mechanism of collapse is different. Second, for the European Union, I think “customs unions and voluntary confederations” is appropriate, while “twentieth-century empires that dissolved” is not, even though the EU exercises imperial-scale regulatory power, because sovereignty remains pooled rather than extracted; structural sovereignty should trump functional resemblance. Third, for the British Empire, I accept “maritime empires with extensive settler colonies” as the right class, whose base rate predicts gradual decline, but I also think the actual rapid decolonization after 1945 demands a temporal adjustment for the global emergence of self-determination norms—so the base rate applies, yet must be weighted downward by what I would call a historical discontinuity factor. Fourth, for the United States I feel genuinely torn: “constitutional republics older than 250 years” contains essentially one member, rendering the base rate useless, whereas “great powers since 1500” is so broad that it includes entities with entirely different fiscal and military architectures. I am inclined to say the right class is “continental federal republics with written constitutions and professional standing militaries,” but that class is nearly as small, and I am unsure whether statistical power or causal specificity ought to win. Fifth, and here is the hard tension: for the Soviet Union I reject “all one-party states” because the decisive vulnerability was ethnic federalism, not party structure, which implies that causal mechanism should govern class selection; yet for post-1945 Japan I accept “defeated imperial powers that retained a monarchy” over “island constitutional monarchies,” again privileging causal mechanism (defeat, occupation, constitutional imposition) over institutional taxonomy. But if mechanism always governs, then for the United States I ought to be able to construct a narrow mechanism-based class with very few members and still derive meaningful predictions, which seems to violate the statistical logic that makes the outside view valuable in the first place. So my cases seem to demand both that reference classes be causally homogeneous and that they contain enough members to support a base rate, but these demands conflict precisely when we study unique or near-unique entities.

Is there a coherent account—perhaps something like “mechanism-homogeneous classes, with elasticity permitted only when the shared mechanism operates across institutional forms, combined with a minimum sample-size threshold that can be relaxed only for mechanisms that have been independently validated in larger classes”—that saves all five verdicts? Or does accommodating the conflict between mechanism specificity and statistical power force me to drop my judgment about the Soviet Union, my judgment about the American reference-class problem, or my commitment that both causal relevance and adequate sample size are necessary conditions? Please also say what the resulting principle predicts for a case I have not listed: for instance, whether forecasting the durability of a hypothetical “post-national climate federation with binding supranational enforcement” should rely on the reference class of “environmental treaties with coercive monitoring mechanisms” (small sample, precise mechanism) or “federal unions that gradually expanded their mandate over centuries” (large sample, different mechanism).
