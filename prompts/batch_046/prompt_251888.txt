For the past several months I have been working within an immersive, persistent virtual environment in which I hold what might be called property in digital artifacts that I have maintained and modified over years. This personal involvement has made the following claim philosophically urgent rather than merely academic. I hold that virtual harms—such as the deliberate destruction of these artifacts or sustained targeted exclusion within the environment—are not reducible to offline psychological distress, but constitute direct harms to entities that possess a dependent, interactionally sustained reality. Specifically, I defend an interactional-contract view: virtual objects and spaces are institutional realities analogous to those described in social ontology, but they depend for their existence not merely on collective acceptance, but on a continuing normative framework of interaction among participants. On this account, damaging a virtual artifact or violating the norms that sustain the shared environment is not merely upsetting users; it damages the ontological structure that makes the virtual world real in its own right, generating moral obligations that are irreducible to obligations about psychological welfare. What would count against this position? I want an honest map of its exposure. Which observations about how users actually treat virtual goods, which arguments from ontology or philosophy of mind, which formal analyses of digital artifact identity, or which intuitive moral counter-cases would be decisive rather than merely awkward? What would defenders of a purely psychological-reductionist account themselves concede as genuinely damaging—perhaps cases where virtual harm seems entirely absent despite severe psychological distress, or vice versa? Could my view be qualified into unfalsifiability by expanding the interactional contract to cover any persistent interaction, and if so, is that insulation a vice for a thesis that claims real moral weight? Please distinguish between evidence that would force abandonment of the interactional-contract element, evidence that would only narrow the scope of virtual reality, and evidence compatible with the core view but that makes it less philosophically interesting. I am particularly interested in whether there are structural features of digital environments that undermine the analogy to institutional reality altogether.
