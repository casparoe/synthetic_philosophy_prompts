During our preparatory session, one participant repeatedly invoked the slogan the crowd is untruth—sometimes framing it as Kierkegaard's specific critique of collective cognition mediated by print, sometimes wielding it as a blanket dismissal of any mass opinion—to argue that algorithmically amplified consensus on digital platforms is automatically disqualified from carrying epistemic weight. Before I respond, I want to be precise about what that slogan can actually amount to, since its force seems to depend entirely on which underlying thesis is being smuggled in.

Could you lay out the distinct precise claims that the crowd is untruth might express, treating them as a menu rather than competing interpretations of a single doctrine? I am especially interested in four possible readings: first, an epistemic claim about the unreliability of belief aggregation in large groups; second, an existential or moral claim about the dissolution of individual responsibility and the suppression of qualitative distinction; third, an ontological claim about whether a collective can literally be the subject of truth or falsehood; and fourth, a historically specific claim tied to Kierkegaard's notion of leveling and the press's production of an anonymous public that lacks the concrete relations necessary for genuine commitment. For each reading, indicate whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or actually false; and specify which, if any, genuinely license the inference that widespread online agreement should be ignored. Please also note which interpretation my colleague's argument actually requires, and identify the weakest point in that interpretation—particularly where it conflates Kierkegaard's critique of a specific media-induced abstraction with a general skepticism about distributed knowledge.
