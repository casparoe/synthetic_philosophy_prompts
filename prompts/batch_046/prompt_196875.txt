I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background. In distributed-system debugging I routinely reason abductively: I weigh competing hypotheses—race condition, hardware fault, network partition—by how coherently they cover the observed logs, not by computing explicit probabilities. I've encountered two philosophical objections that seem to threaten this practice. First, there's a gap between an explanation being "lovely" (covering many symptoms with few ad hoc assumptions) and being "likely" (actually probable given our background knowledge), so selecting the loveliest story might just reward narrative coherence over truth. Second, there's the "bad lot" problem: we may be picking the best candidate from a set that never included the true cause, which makes internal comparison unreliable regardless of how rigorous it is. Could you explain the core conceptual tension? Specifically, does the loveliness-likelihood gap mean that inference to the best explanation can never justify belief on its own, or only that it needs probabilistic supplementation? And does the bad-lot problem undermine abductive reasoning in practical domains like debugging—where our hypothesis space is always incomplete—or only in scientific theory-choice where completeness is impossible?
