I keep noticing a specific argumentative choreography in discussions of whether the Holocaust should be treated as singular or as part of broader modern patterns. In one move, a speaker explains the genocide through mechanisms that are generic to modern organization rather than specific to Nazi ideology: rail schedules coordinating mass movement, bureaucratic roles split so finely that no individual feels personally responsible for death, moral language replaced by statistical and technical terminology, and populations treated as material to be processed efficiently. From this the speaker concludes that the event is not an atavistic exception but a warning embedded in ordinary modern institutions—something that reveals the danger of modern rational organization itself. In the very next move—often within the same essay or lecture—the speaker rejects any comparison to other large-scale modern administrative violence, such as the logistical management of colonial forced-labor regimes or the bureaucratic machinery of industrial-scale internal purges elsewhere, on the grounds that the Holocaust’s total intentionality or unique victim selection makes it analytically incomparable. The modern structural analysis is used to universalize the cause, but an exceptionalist shield is then raised to block comparative testing.
