Since my father's death six months ago, I have been paying closer attention to how the people around me justify continuing with life. I keep encountering a particular posture. A person maintains a demanding practice—visiting the same gravesite every Sunday without any belief in an afterlife, or training for a marathon they know they will never win and that serves no larger cause. When pressed on why it matters, they do not cite happiness, duty, or transcendence; rather, they claim its value lies exactly in its resistance to all such justifications, saying it is important precisely because it needs no authority outside itself. Nevertheless, they defend lapses in the practice as serious failures rather than mere preferences, and they speak of it with a non-negotiable urgency that sounds like defending a sacred obligation. I have seen this in my mother's rigid adherence to cooking one specific meal every anniversary despite her atheism, and in a colleague's devotion to an amateur chess club he freely describes as just a game but says he would lose the thread without. The posture seems distinct from simple habit or hobby, and also from religious faith, since the practitioners explicitly reject any external ground. Does this configuration have an established philosophical name—treating a self-chosen, acknowledged arbitrary commitment as an unconditional obligation precisely on account of its arbitrariness? If there is no precise term, how does it differ from Sartrean bad faith, from Camus's absurd hero, and from the contemporary notion of ground projects?
