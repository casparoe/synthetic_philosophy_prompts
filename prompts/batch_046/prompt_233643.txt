Consider the following argument about the doctrine of double effect in collective, mechanized contexts. (1) Double effect permits an action with a foreseeably bad outcome only if that outcome is neither intended as an end nor intended as a means. (2) In a collective decision executed by an automated mechanism, individual agents intend only the good result—say, saving five lives—while the harmful causal pathway is handled entirely by the machine, with no single person physically causing it. (3) Therefore, if a democratic majority votes to activate a system that saves five people by using one person's body as a necessary physical counterweight, no voter intends that person's crushing as either an end or a means; each voter intends only the activation of the saving mechanism. (4) By extension, whenever harm is causally embedded in an automated procedure enacted by distributed choice, the harm is transformed from an intended means into a mere foreseen side-effect. (5) Consequently, the classic distinction between flipping a switch to divert a trolley and pushing a person onto the tracks collapses: both become permissible if the pushing is performed by a machine triggered by collective choice rather than by individual bodily force. (6) But if double effect cannot distinguish switch cases from footbridge cases under mechanization, it forfeits its standard explanatory role. (7) Hence either double effect is restricted to direct individual bodily actions, in which case it is far narrower than usually assumed, or it permits any harm that can be mechanized and democratically authorized, which proves too much. Where does the argument go wrong? Identify the specific step or hidden assumption that fails, explain why it initially looks plausible, and then say what must be given up—whether a particular reading of collective intention, a restriction on mechanized agency, or a revision to the means/end distinction—to preserve the doctrine's coherence.
