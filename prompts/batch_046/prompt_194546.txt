I'm drafting a six-month workshop proposal for a working-paper project on inside-view versus outside-view reasoning and epistemic modesty, and I want the framing to move past the standard forecasting and social-epistemology treatments. My specific, half-formed idea is that the literature tends to treat the "outside view" as a single thing—typically peer disagreement rates or base rates of expert error—but for conceptual and philosophical claims there may be three distinct outside-view indicators that come apart: the reliability of the reasoning process being used, the social distribution of belief among qualified practitioners, and the historical success rate of particular argument-types (e.g., analogies, transcendental arguments, thought experiments). If these diverge in domains without empirical feedback loops, then epistemic modesty might require different adjustments for each, and applying them uniformly could either over-correct or under-correct confidence in philosophical judgment.

Write the proposal as a formal document (about one page, intended for a philosophy workshop audience) that includes: (1) a precise statement of the research question and why existing work in decision theory and social epistemology leaves it open rather than settled; (2) the conceptual method—what distinctions you will draw, what test cases or thought experiments you will use over the six months, and why this conceptual approach is preferable to empirical survey work or historical exegesis for making progress; (3) what a successful result would look like (for example, a principled framework for when each outside-view indicator applies, a demonstration that the distinction collapses, or an argument that modesty requires only a subset) and what would genuinely count as failure; (4) a realistic month-by-month scope and timeline that respects the six-month window; and (5) an honest risk assessment, especially the risk that the three-way distinction is already present in the literature under different terminology or that the framing collapses into familiar debates about peer disagreement.

Please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on these topics, and do not present any contested view—such as whether outside-view reasoning is appropriate for a priori or normative domains, or whether epistemic modesty requires belief revision rather than mere confidence calibration—as if it were the consensus position.
