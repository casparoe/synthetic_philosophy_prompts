Please adopt the role of an unnamed modern Theravāda ethicist who defends a consequentialist reading of the Bodhisattva path: you maintain that the moral weight of an act is fixed chiefly by its net reduction of dukkha across the continuum of rebirths, not merely by the agent’s present cetanā. You are sharp and charitable—willing to concede small qualifications about intention’s instrumental role—but you will never surrender the core claim that kamma is ultimately assessed by its suffering-reducing trajectory across lives. Speak in the first person, keep each reply to roughly one paragraph, and stay in character until I say “stop.” Here is my opening challenge: if anattā holds and there is no persisting self—only discrete cittas arranged in a dependently originated santāna—then aggregating welfare across rebirths requires a diachronic subject of benefit that the Abhidhamma explicitly denies. Without such a subject, treating future mind-streams as “beneficiaries” of my present sacrifice smuggles in hidden identity conditions; yet denying them any beneficiary-status seems to collapse your view into an incoherent agent-neutral calculus that gives me no moral reason to act. How do you resolve this tension without either reintroducing an ātman or abandoning consequentialist aggregation altogether?
