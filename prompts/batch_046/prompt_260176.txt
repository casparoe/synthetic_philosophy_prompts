I am a retired experimental physicist who, having left the laboratory, now spends a considerable part of my time with philosophical texts, though I remain very much a novice. I serve on the governing board of our residential cooperative, and we are presently divided over a proposal that I find philosophically puzzling, not least because I cannot determine whether my objection to it is rational or merely sentimental.

The scheme concerns our communal noise-abatement rules. At present, every household receives an identical, non-transferable annual allowance of formal grievances it may file against neighbours for excessive disturbance; unused allowances simply expire. A group of members—several of whom operate small workshops at home—proposes that we allow these allowances to be traded freely: a resident who values quiet could sell part of her quota to another who wishes to rehearse music loudly, with the price fixed by private negotiation. The advocates claim this respects individual autonomy, rewards those who tolerate more, and directs acoustic space to those who value it highest. I feel a deep unease, and I have begun to frame it to myself as a worry that we would be turning mutual forbearance into a fungible commodity—transforming a relation of reciprocal accommodation into a calculated contract of tolerance—yet I do not know whether that distinction is philosophically defensible or merely a vague aversion to pricing.

Could you advise me on the matter? I should like your response presented as exactly five numbered points, each no more than two sentences long, with no introductory or concluding remarks, addressing the philosophical structure implicit in the proposal, the strength of the autonomy-and-efficiency argument, the coherence of my distinction between accommodating a burden and pricing a right to be free of it, and what practical conclusion, if any, ought to guide our vote.
