At a workshop on language and law, two philosophers—Mira and Leo—debated a custom case. A city ordinance penalizes “crowded parks,” applied to a space gaining visitors one by one. Mira defended epistemicism: there is an exact headcount—say, 201—where the park becomes crowded; our ignorance of the cutoff is purely epistemic, and small increments do not erase the hidden boundary. Leo defended supervaluationism: “crowded” admits many admissible sharpenings with different cutoffs, so there is no single fact of the matter; the tolerance intuition—that one more person never makes the difference—reflects genuine semantic indeterminacy, not mere ignorance. Mira replied that supervaluationism cannot explain why we still feel compelled to apply “not crowded” at 200 if there is genuinely no correct answer; Leo answered that epistemicism must reject the tolerance principle entirely, which is intuitively costly. Mira conceded the cost but insisted it is the price of preserving bivalence.

Leo then introduced a twist: suppose a judge knows the headcount is exactly 200 and must rule on liability under the ordinance. If epistemicism is correct, there is a determinate answer—crowded or not—though the judge may be unable to know which precise sharpening governs the term. If supervaluationism is correct, she should declare the case genuinely indeterminate rather than merely unknown. Mira said this collapses the distinction in practice: if the precise boundary is permanently inaccessible and varies with practical precisification interests, calling it “hidden” adds no explanatory power over semantic indeterminacy. Leo countered that epistemicism at least preserves a normative ideal—there is one correct ruling—even if human judges can never reach it, whereas supervaluationism abandons that ideal for practical convenience.

They asked me to referee and I could not decide. Please adjudicate point by point: who is right about the tolerance principle, about the judicial case, and about whether epistemicism collapses into supervaluationism in practice? Say who is more right overall, and identify what important consideration—perhaps about the role of practical interests in fixing admissible sharpenings, or about whether bivalence is worth preserving—both are missing.
