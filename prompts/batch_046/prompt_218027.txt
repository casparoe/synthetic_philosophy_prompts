I just finished a long and genuinely unresolved argument with a close friend about whether being a moral saint—someone maximally, perfectly morally good—is actually desirable for a human being. Still unsettled, I later overheard two colleagues, Lena and Marcus, replay almost the same disagreement at a dinner party. I am reporting their exchange as a neutral bystander; I do not know which side is stronger and I want an outside philosophical adjudication rather than personal advice.

Please approach this as an expert moral philosopher. Before adjudicating, take a moment to think carefully and brainstorm the key distinctions: what "maximal" moral goodness could mean (outcome-optimization, threshold-satisfaction, or virtue-perfection), whether morality is best understood as competing with personal goods or as structuring them, and what empirical or conceptual assumptions about human psychology are hidden in the dispute.

Here is their exchange.

(1) Lena: The idea of a moral saint is undesirable because maximal moral commitment requires treating one's own life, projects, and intimate relationships as permanently subordinate to the needs of strangers. Even if this produces the best aggregate outcomes, it eliminates the space for the non-moral goods—deep friendship, artistic play, unstructured leisure—that make a human life worth living. A life of pure moral optimization is a life of alienation.

(2) Marcus: That assumes "maximal" means "maximizing outcomes regardless of cost." But moral goodness is not a quantity like utility; it is a quality of character. The truly excellent person possesses practical wisdom that integrates moral demands with human flourishing. She does not annihilate her personal life; she orders it well. So maximal moral goodness is just perfected virtue, which is clearly desirable.

(3) Lena: Practical wisdom does not solve the problem of infinite demand. Even with perfect wisdom, if suffering is always present, the saint must still direct nearly all resources away from personal projects. The "integration" you describe is cosmetic: it means only that the saint learns to enjoy her deprivation, not that she avoids extreme self-sacrifice. Calling this "flourishing" redefines flourishing to include radical deprivation.

(4) Marcus: You are smuggling in a consequentialist budget model, where morality competes with personal goods for a fixed pool of time and energy. On a virtue-ethical view, being just or compassionate is expressed within one's roles—parent, friend, citizen—without requiring universal sacrifice. The saint is not a global utility calculator; she is someone whose every action is excellent. Friendship itself is a moral virtue, so deep attachments are part of, not obstacles to, her goodness.

(5) Lena: Even granting that framework, there remains an asymmetry: moral failure is far graver than non-moral failure. To minimize moral risk aggressively, the maximally good agent must avoid commitments—having children, owning property, entering complex obligations—that generate unavoidable conflicts. So the pursuit of maximal goodness pushes one toward a thin, risk-averse existence. Most people rightly reject this form of moral asceticism.

(6) Marcus: That confuses risk-aversion with moral seriousness. Someone with perfect practical knowledge resolves conflicts justly when they arise; she does not avoid all commitment out of fear. In fact, avoiding commitments to prevent moral risk is itself a vice—cowardice or deficiency in generosity. Thus maximal moral goodness requires engaging fully with life's commitments, not retreating into a protected, minimal life.

(7) Lena: But you are ignoring the psychological reality of moral cognition. Even if virtue becomes habitual, maintaining maximal moral salience requires sustained attentional resources—what we might call a "moral attention budget." The more one optimizes for moral perception, the more one suffers from moral crowding-out: aesthetic experience, playful spontaneity, and even romantic intimacy get filtered through an evaluative lens that suppresses non-moral enjoyment. The saint does not choose deprivation; her cognitive architecture makes non-moral goods inaccessible.

(8) Marcus: You overstate the cognitive load. Once virtue is fully habituated, moral perception operates as second nature with minimal conscious effort, freeing rather than consuming attention. The agent can then be fully present in friendship or art without perpetual moral auditing. The problem you describe is not maximal virtue but moral hypervigilance, which is actually a defect—an anxious over-optimization—not an ideal.

Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the eight moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with brief justification for each rating. Then say who is right about which specific point—particularly about whether "maximal" is coherent without self-destruction, whether virtue ethics resolves the tension, and whether psychological crowding-out is a real obstacle. Identify what, if anything, both parties are missing, and note whether they are talking past each other by using different conceptions of moral goodness.

Finally, please provide an explicit epistemic check: state exactly what argument, empirical finding about human motivation or moral psychology, or conceptual distinction would lead you to change your overall verdict, and flag the point in your reasoning—whether in your interpretation of "maximal," your evaluation of moral psychology, or your assessment of one specific move—where you are most likely to fail or to have smuggled in an unstated assumption.
