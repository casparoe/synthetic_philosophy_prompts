I am preparing materials for a graduate seminar on strategic rationality, and one of my students has advanced an argument that backward induction in the centipede game, if consistently applied, undermines its own normative authority rather than explaining away apparent irrationality. She lays out the reasoning as follows: (1) The standard backward induction solution requires that, at every decision node n, player P knows that at node n+1 the opponent will stop if stopping is rational for the opponent. (2) For P to possess this knowledge, P must be able to run precisely the same rationality computation that the opponent runs, not merely anticipate its output. (3) Thus the argument tacitly assumes what we might call algorithmic transparency: that rational agents have direct access to the computational procedures by which other rational agents derive their choices. (4) But algorithmic transparency is incompatible with bounded rationality, since simulating another agent's reasoning introduces a regress—simulating the simulation, and so on—that no resource-limited mind can complete. (5) Therefore backward induction applies strictly to agents with unbounded computational capacities, that is, to idealized agents rather than to actual decision-makers. (6) If a normative principle applies only to non-actual agents, it lacks prescriptive force for bounded agents like us, which implies that continuing play in the centipede game may be fully rational for real players after all. (7) Hence the paradox is dissolved not by showing that real players are irrational, but by showing that the rationality standard being invoked was never properly addressed to them in the first place.

She takes this to demonstrate that the real error lies in applying an unbounded rationality criterion to bounded agents, and that once we correct for boundedness, backward induction loses its grip on actual sequential choice. I feel there is a subtle slide somewhere between steps (3) and (5)—perhaps an equivocation between having epistemic access to an opponent's conclusion and having access to the algorithm that produces it, or perhaps a modal slide from what is required for knowledge to what is required for computation—but I cannot locate it precisely, and I am reluctant to dismiss the argument without naming the exact cost of rejection. Which step should be given up? I would especially like to preserve both that backward induction retains genuine normative force for ordinary agents and that bounded rationality is not reducible to a mere computational limitation, so I am hoping the resolution does not force me to abandon either of those commitments. Could you diagnose the precise location of the flaw, explain why that step initially seemed plausible in the context of common-knowledge reasoning, and assess what giving it up costs for our understanding of sequential rationality and the centipede paradox?
