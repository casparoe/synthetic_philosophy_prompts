Question: Some analyses of consent distinguish transactional consent—to a specific act or procedure—from contextual consent—to the epistemic and normative framing conditions in which that act occurs. Consider a case in which a physician, seeking to avoid unnecessary anxiety, strategically omits mention of a remote but serious risk that is not part of the defining purpose of a routine procedure. The risk is genuinely tangential to the procedure's core function, though it is foreseeable and causally linked to it. The patient is fully competent, no false statement is made, and the patient consents to the procedure. Analyze whether this consent is valid, addressing (i) whether strategic omission can constitute deception that invalidates consent, (ii) whether any form of coercion is present, and (iii) how competence relates to the informational environment. In particular, assess whether the transactional/contextual distinction supports the conclusion that omission of such tangential risks leaves transactional consent intact.

Student answer: Valid consent requires voluntariness, adequate information, and competence. Deception undermines consent by inducing a false belief about the object of consent, thereby violating autonomy. However, deception in the strict sense requires an active misrepresentation or false statement; strategic omission involves only a failure to disclose, which does not induce any false belief but merely leaves knowledge absent. Consequently, omission does not amount to deception and cannot invalidate consent on those grounds.

Coercion requires threats or force that compel agreement, neither of which is present here. The physician exerts no pressure, and the patient retains full freedom to refuse. Thus the voluntariness condition is clearly satisfied.

Competence requires understanding the nature of the act and its direct, defining consequences. The patient understands the procedure itself. Applying the transactional/contextual distinction, the patient gives transactional consent to the medical act. The omitted risk pertains only to the contextual informational environment, not to the core identity of the procedure. For contextual omission to undermine transactional consent, the omitted fact must be structurally integral—meaning the act's normative character fundamentally changes in its presence. A remote, tangential risk fails this threshold; it remains external to the transactional core. Therefore, although full contextual consent to disclosure is absent, transactional consent remains valid, and the consent as a whole is not invalidated.

One might object that competence is domain-relative and that withholding risk information undermines the patient's ability to exercise competent judgment over the decision context. Yet competence should be assessed relative to the direct object of consent, which is the procedure, not the surrounding risk profile. Since the patient's grasp of the procedure is intact, competence is not compromised, and the consent stands.
