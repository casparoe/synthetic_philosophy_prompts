I have just come from a long and unresolved argument with a close friend about whether Leibniz's moral philosophy is irreducibly intellectualist—whether his identification of justice with wisdom-guided charity makes moral concern depend on a kind of cognitive achievement that excludes ordinary emotional attachment, and whether his comparison of moral judgment to the perception of harmony reduces ethics to an elite aesthetic exercise available only to those with philosophical training. Tomorrow evening I am delivering the retirement toast for my doctoral advisor, who spent forty years defending the opposite reading: that for Leibniz moral knowledge is not deductive but perceptual, requiring the slow cultivation of what she terms an expanded point of view in which one learns to register the appetitions of other monads not as neutral data but as normative reasons, and that this form of attention is a discipline open to anyone rather than a privilege of the learned. The room will be divided between moral philosophers familiar with her work on Leibniz's doctrine of appetition and family members who will not distinguish Leibniz from Locke. I need a polished, roughly five-minute toast that uses our argument as its structural backbone: it must ask, implicitly, whether her career demonstrates that Leibnizian morality can be genuinely warm without being false. The philosophical idea should do real work—determining what can be praised about her legacy and what must remain unsaid—rather than serving as decoration. Specifically, I want the toast to turn on the tension between outcome-based assessment, which would count publications and honors, and process-based appreciation, which recognizes the gradual transformation of how one sees others, and to argue, without didacticism, that honoring her requires adopting the second perspective. It should land precisely for the philosophers without alienating the family, and it must avoid any bibliography, citation, or reading list.
