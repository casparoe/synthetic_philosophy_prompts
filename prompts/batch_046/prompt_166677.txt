Question (25 marks): Karma is often treated as a metaphysical doctrine about rebirth and causal continuity, but philosophers have asked whether it can serve as a secular theory of moral desert. Explain whether karma, understood as an impersonal law linking moral quality to welfare outcomes, can justify the claim that wrongdoers deserve to suffer without invoking a divine judge or an enduring self. In your answer, consider whether the absence of a cosmic “accountant” strengthens or weakens karma’s credentials as a normative account of desert, and address the objection that an automatic causal mechanism cannot by itself generate normative fittingness.

Student answer:

Karma offers a distinctive theory of moral desert precisely because it dispenses with both God and the soul. In Hindu and Buddhist traditions alike, karma operates like a natural law: good actions produce good results, bad actions produce suffering. Because it is impersonal, no deity needs to distribute rewards and punishments, which avoids the arbitrariness often charged against divine command theories. The absence of a permanent self in Buddhism—anatta—means that desert cannot attach to an enduring ego, but this does not undermine karma; rather, it shows that desert is a property of action-streams rather than of persons. This makes karma more coherent than retributivism, which requires a stable subject of punishment.

The objection that causation is not justification misses the point. If suffering reliably tracks moral failure through a law-governed process, then the correlation itself establishes fittingness. A wrongdoer who suffers has not been arbitrarily harmed; the suffering is the direct fruit of their own conduct. In this sense, karma solves the problem of moral luck: because outcomes are strictly proportionate to prior moral quality, nobody receives undeserved hardship. The mechanism is automatic, but that is its strength—desert requires only that welfare align with virtue, not that an intelligent agent calculate it. Therefore, karma succeeds as a normative theory of moral desert without metaphysical extravagance.
