After a long, somewhat circular argument with a friend about who, if anyone, bears moral responsibility when a learning-based autonomous system causes serious harm, I want to stop trading intuitions and actually specify the criteria. Write the full list of desiderata that an adequate theory of responsibility attribution for autonomous systems must satisfy to count as philosophically adequate—not just legally workable, but conceptually sound. For each desideratum, state the motivating case or distinction that makes it necessary, especially the distinction I kept trying to draw between operational responsibility (who initiated or authorized the specific act) and architectural responsibility (who determined the system's decision boundaries, training objectives, or reward structure). Mark which requirements are genuinely non-negotiable—without which the theory collapses into either blanket exculpation or indiscriminate blame—and which are merely camp-specific preferences, such as demands for full determinism, complete interpretability, or preservation of human exceptionalism. Then address the hard structural question: is there a subset of these desiderata that is jointly unsatisfiable, given basic constraints on distributed agency and the impossibility of full predictive control over adaptive systems? If so, expose that tension precisely; my friend insisted there wasn't one, and I think he is wrong. Finally, identify which single desideratum does the most damage to the popular responsibility-gap view that no human agent is responsible, and note whether any existing framework avoids that damage without falling into scapegoating of the nearest human operator. A table is acceptable, but keep the weight on the criteria and their justifications, not on a survey of named theories.
