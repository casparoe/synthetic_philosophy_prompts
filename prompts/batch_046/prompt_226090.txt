As a retired physicist who spent decades on optimization problems in condensed matter systems, I have recently turned to moral philosophy and find myself struck by a stalemate in the interpretation of Leibniz's ethics that should, by scientific standards, have yielded to clarification long ago. On one side stand what I will call the metaphysical reductionists, who hold that for Leibniz the predicate good ultimately denotes nothing other than a high degree of ontological perfection—that is, the maximization of reality or essence compatible with existence—so that moral judgments are essentially descriptive reports about the hierarchical ordering of possible worlds. On the other side stand the normative autonomists, who insist that Leibniz retains a genuinely prescriptive dimension: rational agents recognize obligations that are not merely labels for metaphysical completeness but function as binding reasons that can conflict with, and sometimes override, mere perfection-maximization. Both camps draw on the same corpus—the essays on justice and the common good, the Monadology's remarks on the City of God, and the correspondence on freedom—yet neither has produced an argument that the other finds even mildly damaging, and the literature seems to circle the same distinctions without convergence.

My working hypothesis is that this is not a simple factual dispute about texts but a structural misalignment. Perhaps the two sides are using the term reason in incompatible registers, one treating moral reasoning as a calculative comparison of world-states and the other as a practical recognition of obligations inherent to rational nature. Or perhaps the disagreement is verbal, turning on whether a normative claim can be reduced to a descriptive one without losing its action-guiding force—a question that might dissolve once we distinguish semantic reduction from ontological reduction. To push this further with a framing drawn from my old field: in optimization theory we distinguish between a global objective function and local boundary conditions that are non-negotiable for system coherence. Might the Leibniz debate be hiding exactly this distinction? The reductionists may be describing the global objective—the best of all possible worlds—while the autonomists defend local normative constraints, such as justice or promise-keeping, that are not merely instrumental to global optimality but are conditions of the system's coherence. If so, the disagreement is substantive but mislocated: it is not about whether morality is real, but about whether normative constraints operate as boundary conditions within an otherwise perfectionist calculus.

Given all this, I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict. Is the stalemate in Leibniz scholarship genuinely a clash of interpretations supported by textual evidence, or is it a case in which the disputants answer different questions—one about cosmological explanation, the other about practical deliberation—under the single heading Leibniz's moral philosophy? Does my optimization-with-boundary-conditions framing capture a real conceptual distinction that could move the debate forward, or does it merely rename the deadlock? More generally, what kind of disagreement is this—verbal, substantive, a divergence in standards of explanation, or bedrock intuitions about naturalness and normativity that neither side can argue for—and given your diagnosis, what should we expect progress to look like? Would it require a new formal distinction, a reconception of the question, or an honest admission that no convergence should be expected?
