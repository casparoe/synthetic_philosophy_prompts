The debate over the Knobe effect—since Knobe's original finding that people judge harmful side effects to be intentional while judging beneficial side effects unintentional—has persisted for decades without convergence. One camp argues that moral evaluation genuinely penetrates the folk concept of intentional action: the asymmetry shows that normative considerations are partly constitutive of the concept itself, not merely external influences on its application. The opposing camp treats the asymmetry as a performance artifact generated by conversational pragmatics, demand characteristics, or the salience of blame and praise, insisting that the underlying concept of intentional action remains morally neutral and that the vignettes only reveal how people talk, not how they think.

Despite an accumulation of experiments manipulating blame attribution, norm type, cross-cultural framing, and the structural separation of harm from intention, the literature remains stalled: each result is interpreted by one side as confirming conceptual contamination and by the other as confirming a confound that has not yet been fully controlled. I am asking not for a verdict on which side is correct, but for a diagnosis of the disagreement's structure. Is this a verbal dispute about the word "concept," where one side means competence and the other includes performance mechanisms? Is it substantive—do the sides make different empirical predictions about, say, whether the effect survives in scenarios where blame is structurally decoupled from harm through a specific feature of the causal chain? Or are the two groups answering different questions under a single heading: one conducting conceptual analysis of folk psychology about what intentional action is, the other conducting cognitive psychology about what mechanisms generate verbal responses to hypothetical vignettes?

Consider in particular the hypothesis that the impasse is sustained because both camps share the vocabulary of "the concept of intentional action" but are actually pursuing different projects—one mapping the representational content of a mental category, the other explaining task behavior—without acknowledging the divergence. If that is the diagnosis, what would count as genuine progress? A formal model distinguishing competence predictions from performance predictions? A developmental or cross-linguistic study that one side could not absorb into its framework? A precise distinction that dissolves the shared heading? Or is the question itself ill-posed in a way that no empirical result could resolve?

Please answer in American English. Give the diagnosis, supported by what each side actually says about vignettes, blame, norms, and the interpretation of verbal reports, together with what—if anything—would count as progress given that diagnosis.
