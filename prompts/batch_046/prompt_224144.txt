I defend a domain-sensitive pluralism about metaphysical grounding: in natural domains, grounding is strictly well-founded, irreflexive, and asymmetric, but in conventional or institutional domains—such as the relation between being legal tender and being collectively treated as a store of value—grounding can be genuinely symmetric without generating vicious circularity. The idea is that institutional grounding depends on distributed collective acceptance, and acceptance can be structured so that each status is partly constitutive of the other, yet neither enjoys ontological priority. Consequently, well-foundedness applies selectively rather than universally. What would genuinely count against this position? I want more than the generic objection that symmetric grounding collapses into identity or that universal well-foundedness is non-negotiable. Specifically: which formal properties of grounding relations—such as transitivity, irreflexivity, uniqueness of grounds, or the requirement that grounding be explanatory—would be violated or strained by allowing symmetric conventional grounding, and can a pluralist framework absorb those costs? Which conceptual or institutional cases—perhaps involving the breakdown of a currency, the emergence of a competing medium of exchange, or analogies to natural-kind hierarchies—would reveal that one side of the relation is in fact more fundamental? Has the view insulated itself by restricting well-foundedness to natural domains, and if so, is that insulation a legitimate theoretical division or an ad hoc qualification? Please rank potential defeaters from decisive to merely awkward, distinguish objections that defenders of this view should concede from those they can plausibly dismiss, and assess whether the position risks unfalsifiability.
