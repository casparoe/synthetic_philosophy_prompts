Two members of a philosophy study group disagreed about a variant of the Sleeping Beauty problem designed to test the interaction between centered worlds and standard Bayesian conditioning. In the standard version, Beauty is put to sleep on Sunday, a fair coin is flipped, and if heads she is awakened once on Monday and asked for her credence in heads; if tails she is awakened on Monday and Tuesday with complete amnesia between them, and asked each time. The group was discussing whether the correct answer is one-half or one-third.

In the variant they considered, Beauty is told upon every awakening that she has been awakened either once (if heads) or twice (if tails). She is also told that a hidden clock is present in the room: if she checks it, she will see either "Monday 9:00" or "Tuesday 9:00," and she knows in advance what each outcome implies about the coin. She may choose whether to check.

Maya defended the thirder position. She said that even without checking the clock, Beauty should assign credence one-third to heads, because there are three subjectively indistinguishable centered possibilities—Monday-heads, Monday-tails, and Tuesday-tails—and rationality requires distributing credence equally over them. She added that if Beauty does check the clock and sees Tuesday, her credence in tails should become near-certain, not because she has learned an ordinary empirical fact, but because she has located herself in a centered world that can occur only under tails. For Maya, this shows that self-locating belief is genuinely evidential and that Jordan's refusal to update without the clock is inconsistent: the clock merely makes explicit what was already implicit in her first-person situation.

Jordan defended the halfer position. He said that the prior probability of heads is one-half, and the information that Beauty is currently awake provides no update, since she is guaranteed to be awake at some point regardless of the coin outcome. He granted that checking the clock and seeing Tuesday should change her credence, but he insisted this is ordinary conditioning on an empirical correlation—the clock reading is causally linked to the coin—not a mysterious update from self-location. He argued that Maya's view makes rational credence unstable: Beauty's belief should be one-half if she keeps her eyes closed, but near-certainty in tails if she glances at the clock, even though her epistemic situation is otherwise identical. Jordan concluded that self-locating knowledge only updates beliefs when it carries ordinary empirical information, and that without the clock Beauty should remain at one-half.

Maya replied that Jordan is treating self-location as always epistemically inert, which begs the question. She said that the instability Jordan points to is real but not a problem: rational agents should condition on all available information, and deciding not to check the clock is like deciding not to open an envelope containing relevant evidence. She added that if Jordan is right, then in the standard version—where there is no clock—Beauty should also stay at one-half, which means the thirder argument collapses entirely, and Jordan owes an explanation of why so many philosophers find the one-third answer intuitive if it is just a mistake about conditioning.

Please adjudicate their exchange move by move: evaluate Maya's claim about centered-world indifference, Jordan's guarantee argument, Maya's treatment of the clock as revealing implicit self-location, Jordan's charge of instability, and Maya's final defense of conditioning on available evidence. Say which specific points are well taken, where each side misses the distinction between centered-world updating and empirical conditioning, and who is more right overall about the role of self-locating belief in this variant.
