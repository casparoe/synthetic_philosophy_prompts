student answer:
The vertical/horizontal distinction captures something real. Vertical commemoration puts a figure literally on a pedestal, so removing a statue is a direct moral statement: the community is withdrawing its claim that this person is exemplary. Horizontal commemoration works differently—street names are mainly functional, part of the address system, so renaming one is closer to updating a record than to making an ethical judgment. The history isnt erased; its still in archives and textbooks.

The strongest argument for asymmetry is that vertical involves active public endorsement, while horizontal is passive embedding in everyday geography. But theres an important weakness: people form deep emotional attachments to street names, and these names exist in a narrative network with other local names that tell the story of the citys development. Because of this network effect, altering a horizontal name actually disrupts historical continuity more sharply than removing an isolated statue. So preservationists who worry about erasing history should prefer keeping old street names and removing statues, since statues isolate figures from context anyway. This means horizontal changes can be worse for historical continuity, so the asymmetry fails—renaming streets might actually carry more weight than we think.

Still, even if theres emotional and narrative cost to renaming, its not the same kind of moral statement as tearing down a monument. So the distinction holds in one direction, even if not as cleanly as the asymmetry thesis claims.
