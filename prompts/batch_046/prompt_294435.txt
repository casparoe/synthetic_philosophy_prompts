The exam question was: "Explain Kant's claim that we have only indirect duties regarding animals, and evaluate whether his distinction between direct and indirect moral obligations can accommodate the intuition that unnecessary suffering inflicted on a sentient being is wrong independently of any effect on human moral agents. In your answer, reference the specific reasoning Kant offers in the Metaphysics of Morals and explain how his account of rational nature as the ground of moral status bears on the possibility of extending direct obligations to non-rational beings."

The student wrote:

Kant denies direct duties to animals because only beings with rational nature—those capable of autonomy and self-legislation—possess moral personality and count as ends in themselves. Animals lack this capacity, so in the Metaphysics of Morals he treats them as things rather than persons, which is why our obligations regarding them are indirect. The familiar argument is that cruelty to animals is wrong not because it harms the animal, but because it hardens our heart and makes us more likely to violate our duties to other rational agents. This reasoning appears in the Groundwork, where Kant first establishes the contrast between rational beings and mere things.

That said, Kant's ethics also includes imperfect duties—such as the duty to cultivate sympathy and benevolence—which naturally extend toward animals. Because sympathy responds to suffering regardless of the sufferer's rationality, one might think these imperfect duties generate direct obligations to animals. But this confuses the target of the duty: the obligation is still directed at our own moral perfection, not at the animal as a bearer of rights. Nevertheless, if suffering is genuinely bad, and Kant accepts that unnecessary suffering should be avoided, then his framework is closer to modern welfare positions than the indirect-duty reading suggests. In particular, since the Formula of Humanity requires that we never treat humanity merely as a means, and since sentience is the feature that makes suffering matter, consistency demands that we extend a parallel protection to animals. Otherwise we arbitrarily restrict moral consideration to rational agents. So the student concludes that Kant either implicitly accepts direct duties or his view is undermined by his own commitment to the wrongness of unnecessary suffering.
