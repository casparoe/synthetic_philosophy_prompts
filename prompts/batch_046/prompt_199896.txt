Philosophers of mind sometimes distinguish four grades of cognitive architecture: Darwinian creatures, whose behavior is fixed by evolutionary hard-wiring; Skinnerian creatures, which learn by reinforcement across a lifetime; Popperian creatures, able to generate and evaluate candidate actions internally before behaving; and Gregorian creatures, which extend cognition through language and culturally accumulated tools. In recent years this taxonomy has been applied to artificial systems, producing a persistent disagreement over whether large language models should be classed as Popperian—genuinely testing hypotheses in an internal design space—or as sophisticated Skinnerian pattern-matchers that only simulate evaluation, with Gregorian capacities restricted to surface-level linguistic interaction.

The debate has stalled despite broad agreement on both the taxonomy and the underlying architecture: one camp cites chain-of-thought reasoning and self-correction as evidence of internal selection, while the other insists that every generated token is a probable continuation of training data and that the system lacks the causal autonomy required for true Popperian testing. Both sides appeal to the same benchmarks and model cards.

My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is not empirical but conceptual: the sides are using different implicit criteria for what counts as internal evaluation or genuine cultural-tool use, and may in fact be addressing different questions—one about mechanistic implementation, the other about functional equivalence—under a single four-part heading. Is that diagnosis right?
