I'm grading papers for an introductory metaphysics exam and want a second opinion on one answer before I finalize marks. The exam question was:

Epicurus argues that death is nothing to us because when we exist death is not present, and when death is present we do not exist. The standard deprivation-account reply holds that death harms us not by being a painful state but by depriving us of future goods we otherwise would have experienced. A related asymmetry argument—going back at least to Lucretius and revived in modern discussion—asks why we do not regard pre-natal non-existence as a misfortune if post-mortem non-existence is one.

Consider a modified subject, S, defined as follows: S exists only within a fixed temporal window; retains no memory of any prior state; knows with certainty that consciousness will not resume after termination; and possesses a psychology that values only present-tense experiences, not hypothetical future ones outside its present awareness. Does the deprivation objection apply to S with the same force as to an ordinary human? Or is there an independent reason—distinct from Epicurus's point that there is no subject present to suffer—why S is not harmed by death? In your answer, carefully distinguish the metaphysical claim that there is no subject of harm from the axiological claim that there is no stake in continued existence, and assess whether the pre-natal/post-mortem asymmetry still applies to S.

Epicurus's central point is that death cannot be an event in one's life, so there is no one present to suffer it. The deprivation account answers that harm need not be felt: death is bad because it cuts off a future in which good things would happen. For a normal person with long-term projects, this is convincing.

With S, the case changes. Because S only cares about present-tense states and knows it will not continue, there is no future good that S is losing—S has no stake in what comes after the present moment. So the deprivation objection does not apply. This is not just Epicurus's point restated; it is stronger, because it works even for someone who rejects the idea that harm requires a living subject.

As for the asymmetry argument—usually traced to Lucretius and later to Nagel—S's psychology makes it irrelevant. S has no memory of pre-existence and expects none after termination, so both sides are equally blank. There is therefore no puzzling asymmetry to explain.

So death is not bad for S, but for psychological reasons rather than metaphysical ones.
