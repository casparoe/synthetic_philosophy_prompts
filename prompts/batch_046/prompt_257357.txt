I'm drafting the final essay exam for a graduate seminar in philosophy of history. I need one 90-minute prompt, approximately 1000 words, that forces students to diagnose Whig history in a concrete, slightly invented case rather than reciting the familiar textbook warning about writing the past as progress toward the present. Use this framing: a scholar describes the decline of trial by ordeal in twelfth- and thirteenth-century Europe not as a transformation in religious cosmology or in the social authority of clergy, but as an emergent recognition of poor predictive validity, portraying participants as if they were implicitly running statistical experiments and gradually updating beliefs like Bayesian agents. The exam question should ask students to identify three distinct mechanisms by which this framing smuggles in teleology—without letting them rest on the simple objection that medieval people did not think like modern scientists—and to specify what a genuinely non-Whig reconstruction of the same transition would need to preserve, including at least one feature that a rational-reconstruction approach is likely to discard. Provide: (1) the exam question itself, worded clearly for the student; (2) a model answer of about 800 words demonstrating the required analytical moves; (3) a marking scheme out of 25 points that separates an A answer from a B answer by naming the specific moves each must contain—for instance, distinguishing rational reconstruction from causal explanation, identifying the implicit normative endpoint, recognizing the anachronistic attribution of evidential norms, and explaining the cost of preserving the discarded feature—with marks assigned to each category and a one-line note on the typical mistake that costs points there; and (4) a brief self-critical note stating exactly what argument or feature of the historian's account, if defended successfully, would cause you to retract the charge of Whiggishness, and where your own reasoning is most likely to fail. Do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the answer should stand on reasoning available without external sources. Keep the framing specific enough that it cannot be answered by generic pretraining summaries of Whig history alone.
