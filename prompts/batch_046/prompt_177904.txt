I sit on a national biosafety regulatory panel. A synthetic-biology consortium has engineered a self-sustaining microbial consortium—Project Helix—that integrates synthetic genes horizontally across previously isolated lineages and maintains functional coherence through metabolic interdependence rather than clonal or sexual reproduction. It behaves as a distributed evolutionary unit, blurring the boundary between individual organism, population, and local ecosystem. Our regulatory framework classifies entities by reproductive isolation and phylogenetic clustering, neither of which applies cleanly; the consortium is petitioning for “modified strain” status with lighter containment rather than “novel species” designation, which would trigger full ecological-risk review and long-term monitoring obligations. Draft a short written opinion—no more than three pages, suitable for circulation before Thursday’s meeting—recommending a concrete classification and containment decision with a clear bottom line. The opinion should treat seriously the consortium’s argument that applying traditional species concepts to synthetic distributed life is a category error, and should address whether the panel ought to revise its definitional criteria (for instance, toward an evolutionary-lineage or ecological-functional model) or apply them strictly. It must include an honest assessment of the strongest consideration on the losing side, should avoid bibliographies, citation lists, or appeals to specific literature, and should explain what practical and metaphysical stakes hang on calling Helix a species at all.
