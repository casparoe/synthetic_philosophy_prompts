I have just come away from a long and rather heated argument with a close friend about whether epistemic modesty — treating one's own beliefs as if they were formed by one more unreliable agent among many — can ever be coherently maintained without undermining the first-personal reasoning that actually produces them. We kept circling what I now think of as the 'indexical filter problem': that shifting to an 'outside view' assessment of one's cognitive reliability seems to require an 'inside view' identification of which beliefs and reasoning processes are properly one's own, yet that identification is not itself capturable by the outside view without circularity. I want to turn this into an exam for my final-year epistemology module, but I do not want candidates to get away with summarising the standard inside/outside distinction (as in forecasting or clinical judgement) and declaring that modesty is virtuous.

Please draft one 90-minute essay question worth 20 marks. The question should present a concrete proposed resolution — call it the 'double-entry ledger' method — in which an agent maintains two strictly separated registers: one for first-order beliefs governed by inside-view reasoning, and one for meta-level reliability adjustments derived from outside-view base rates, with an explicit rule that neither register may cite the other. Ask candidates to evaluate whether this method genuinely solves the indexical filter problem or merely relocates it, and to identify precisely what is conceptually and practically lost if cross-reference is forbidden.

Give a model answer and a marking scheme built around five must-hit points, the marks each is worth (total 20), and a one-line note on how candidates typically get each wrong. The difficulty should come from having to diagnose the interaction between indexical identification and statistical self-assessment, not from recalling labels. Do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the answer must be fully evaluable from conceptual analysis alone.
