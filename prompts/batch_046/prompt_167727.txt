In discussions of AI takeoff, the intelligence explosion is routinely analogized to a nuclear chain reaction reaching critical mass: once a system can improve its own algorithms, each generation produces smarter successors in an accelerating cascade, with the "fissile material" being available compute and talent, the "neutrons" being improved architectures or training methods, and the "moderator" being the feedback loop of evaluation and deployment. Proponents invoke this to license the conclusion that takeoff will be discontinuous, rapid, and effectively uncontrollable, like an unmoderated reactor. Give me the explicit correspondence table—what exactly plays the role of critical mass, the neutron multiplication factor, moderation, containment failure, and the energy-release curve—then separate the disanalogies that specifically undermine the speed and uncontrollability conclusion from those that are merely decorative. In particular, address whether algorithmic improvement has anything analogous to a physical mean free path or a conservation law, whether "more capable" plays the role of "more energetic" in a way that necessarily sustains exponential growth, and whether the analogy quietly imports deterministic physical dynamics that do not apply to strategic choices labs make about release schedules, safety testing, and resource allocation. Finally, tell me whether the conclusion that takeoff must be sudden survives once these breaks are on the table, or whether the nuclear imagery was carrying the argumentative work all along.
