Interpreters of Hume's essay on suicide have been divided for well over a century on whether he is genuinely advancing a defense of self-killing or merely exercising a skeptical dialectic, and whether his arguments cohere with his broader moral philosophy. The debate has survived every textual recovery and biographical clarification, yet no consensus has emerged on whether the disagreement is about Hume's intentions, about the consistency of his sentimentalist ethics, or about something deeper. My working theory is that the impasse is largely verbal, masked by an unacknowledged ambiguity in Hume's category of 'natural' obligations: one camp reads 'natural' as statistically regular in human sentiment, the other as rationally justified within his distinction between artificial and natural virtues, and the two sides therefore talk past each other when they ask whether suicide violates a natural duty. I want a diagnosis with reasons, not another survey of the interpretations: is the disagreement about facts of Hume's psychology or publication history, about which cases count as evidence for his considered view, about the standards required to attribute a normative thesis to an author, or about starting intuitions regarding the scope of moral sentimentalism that neither side can argue for? Is there a hidden formal question — for instance, about whether a virtue-based ethics can generate agent-relative prohibitions at all — that is actually driving the dispute? Or are the sides answering different questions under the name 'Hume's view on suicide' — one asking what Hume believed, another asking what his philosophy permits? If the diagnosis is that the question is ill-posed, explain why; if it is merely hard, say what kind of distinction, conceptual clarification, or formal result about artificial virtues and agent-relative reasons could actually move it forward.
