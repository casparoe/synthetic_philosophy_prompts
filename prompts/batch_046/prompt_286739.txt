I've been taking a course on normative ethics and find myself caught in what my professor called the paradox of deontology — the way strict agent-centered restrictions seem to generate worse outcomes in some configurations but not in others, almost as if the constraint is tracking something other than outcome severity. I'm trying to map my actual gut reactions to concrete cases and see if there's a coherent principle behind them, or whether I'm just rationalizing inconsistencies.

Here are my verdicts. In the classic footbridge scenario, pushing the large man onto the tracks to stop the trolley is not fine. In the switch scenario, diverting the trolley so it kills one instead of five feels permissible, though I hesitate to say it's morally required. In the transplant case, killing one healthy patient to harvest organs for five is clearly not fine. But here's where I start to wobble: if a hospital board changes the organ-allocation protocol so that the same outcome occurs through an automated system that no individual surgeon executes, I still think it's not fine, yet it feels slightly less wrong than the surgeon's direct act. Then there's a legislative analog: a policy that will certainly kill one identifiable worker (say, through exposure to a toxic byproduct) but save five other lives through cheaper medicine — I think passing that policy is wrong, but if the harm is statistical rather than identifiable, I think it might be permissible. On a different axis, lying to a murderer at the door is fine; writing a false check with the phrase "the check is in the mail" when it isn't is not fine; but setting up an automated reply system that sends false reassurances to creditors feels somewhere in between, though I'm not sure why. Finally, there's the dying patient case: telling a terminal patient who asks that their estranged son called, when he didn't, feels not fine to me, even though it comforts them — but I also think there's a difference between that and telling the patient their prognosis is better than it is, which also feels wrong but perhaps differently wrong.

Is there one principle that captures all of these, or close? If the answer involves a distinction between direct and indirect agency, how does it handle the automated-system cases? If it involves identifiability, why does the murderer-at-the-door lie remain fine despite being direct? Please state the principle precisely, say what it predicts for a case I haven't listed — perhaps a situation where a single agent must choose between two automated protocols, one that kills one identifiable person and one that kills one statistical person, both saving five — and be honest about which of my verdicts the principle forces me to drop. I need the answer in American English.
