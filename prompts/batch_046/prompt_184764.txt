I'm defending what I call the framing-decoupling thesis at an interdisciplinary philosophy-of-mind and clinical-neuroscience seminar: pain asymbolia reveals that the badness of pain is not an intrinsic sensory-affective quality but an evaluative frame—structurally analogous to aesthetic framing—that the brain's salience network applies to nociceptive signals, and this frame can be selectively disrupted while sensory registration and explicit identification remain intact. The audience is about twelve people: phenomenologists who treat badness as irreducible and embodied; neuroscientists committed to an affectivist model in which badness just is negative affect coupled to nociception; clinical neurologists familiar with asymbolia cases; and moral philosophers concerned that decoupling badness from sensation collapses arguments for animal suffering and analgesic obligations. Give me a speaker-prep sheet for the Q&A: the objections in roughly the order they'll come, phrased the way each camp would actually phrase them, with notes on what I should concede, what I should hold, a brief reply for each, a flag on where the reply is weakest, and the single objection I'm hoping nobody raises. Please write the response in American English.
