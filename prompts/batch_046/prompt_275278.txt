I hold a stronger reading of the hitchhiker case than the standard one: not merely that rational agents should pre-commit to acting against their later preferences, but that the scenario dissolves the boundary between instrumental and intrinsic rationality, since the agent who boards the ship and the agent stranded in the desert are not the same evaluative unit. What would genuinely count against this stronger claim? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward: formal results in decision theory showing that self-binding strategies are never optimal under standard rational-choice axioms; arguments about personal identity that either collapse the two-stage framing or establish that the stranded agent's preferences deserve equal normative weight rather than being overridden; empirical findings about actual human commitment behavior that bear directly on the normative claim; and conceptual objections that the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability. For each, indicate whether a defender should concede it, dismiss it, or absorb it by qualification — and whether such absorption strengthens the view or weakens it.
