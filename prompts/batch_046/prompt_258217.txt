In his economic writings, Berkeley treats money not as a commodity possessing intrinsic worth but as a conventional token that records and transfers a claim on labor and social cooperation, arguing that a commonwealth thrives when such tokens circulate actively rather than being hoarded. Taking this relational, immaterialist conception of money as given—where value derives from exchange relations and the capacity to direct labor rather than from the token's substance—what are the most important implications for evaluating modern proposals for fully automated digital currencies governed by algorithmic stabilization mechanisms that eliminate discretionary human exchange and suppress velocity fluctuations? Specifically, consider whether removing human discretion from circulation undermines or preserves the representational force Berkeley assigns to money, whether purely digital tokens are closer to or further from his notion of money as an insubstantial claim-marker, and what conditions must hold for a token to retain its efficacy as a mediator of social labor when its circulation is mechanized.
