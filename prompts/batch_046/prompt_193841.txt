Two members of my philosophy reading group have reached an impasse over a reconstructed transcendental argument about objective judgement. Alex maintains that for a subject genuinely to distinguish subjective states—say, a headache—from objective events—say, a thunderclap—there must actually exist a framework of spatially and temporally ordered objects that does not depend on that subject’s immediate experience. He insists this is not merely psychological: it concerns the conditions under which the distinction can be successfully applied, not merely the concepts we happen to deploy. Blake replies that Alex confuses conceptual necessity with metaphysical necessity. We may need the idea of mind-independent objects to structure thought, Blake says, yet that gives no guarantee such objects exist; Alex is sliding from “we must think X” to “X must be so,” committing a modal fallacy, and tacitly assuming our cognitive framework is transparent to reality—precisely what a sceptic denies.

Alex responds that he appeals not to transparency but to applicability conditions: if the distinction is genuinely drawn, then the circumstances that make it applicable must obtain, else we occupy the incoherent position of successfully applying a distinction where it cannot apply. Blake counters that this presupposes we do apply it successfully, whereas global scepticism permits that we systematically misapply the distinction while convinced of our success. The transcendental strategy, he argues, rules out widespread error only by assuming widespread error impossible, which begs the question. Alex then retorts that Blake implicitly relies on a Cartesian picture of private mental access that transcendental philosophy rejects: without a prior, non-objective vantage point from which to survey our judgements, the notion of “systematic misapplication” collapses, since there is no independent standpoint from which to declare the framework false.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move: for each step outlined above, indicate whether the point is clearly mistaken, largely correct, or somewhere between, with brief justification. Then say who is more right overall, and identify anything important both are neglecting—for instance, whether the dispute turns on an ambiguity in “condition of possibility,” or whether a distinction between strong transcendental arguments (establishing metaphysical conditions) and weak ones (establishing only conceptual conditions) might dissolve part of the disagreement.
