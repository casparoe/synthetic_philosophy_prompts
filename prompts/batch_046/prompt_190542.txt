Been trying to formalize the surprise exam for a chapter on self-reference and now I'm in a loop. The prof anounces a surprise quiz Mon-Fri, where "surprise" means you don't know it's that day when you wake up. (1) If it hasn't happened by Thu night, you know it's Friday, so Friday can't be surprising. (2) By backward induction the same kills Thu, Wed, Tue, Mon — so it can never happen. (3) But the prof can clearly hold it Wendesday and everyone is shocked, so the induction must break. (4) The standard fix is indexical: Wendesday morning you genuinely don't know, even if Tuesday night you could have predicted it. (5) But apply that same indexical move to Newcomb's problem: the predictor's knowledge is "before" your choice in the same epistemic sense, so causal reasoning should win out. (6) Yet one-boxing is still the rational move in Newcomb. (7) So either (4) doesn't actually save the exam paradox, or it applies inconsistently across cases. I really want to hang onto (4) because it captures something real about first-person knowledge, but then I seem stuck with rejecting causal decision theory in Newcomb, which also feels wrong. Which step is the sleight of hand?
