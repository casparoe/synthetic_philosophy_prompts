As a novelist accustomed to thinking in terms of focalization, plot architecture, dramatic irony, narrative causality, and the distribution of knowledge between author, narrator, and characters — but with no formal training in economics or political theory — I want you to translate Amartya Sen's argument that famines are political crimes rather than natural disasters into the vocabulary of fiction craft, as precisely and honestly as you can. Take his core claims about entitlement collapse versus food-availability decline, his analyses of the Bengal famine of 1943, the Irish famine, and the Great Leap Forward, and his thesis that democratic accountability structurally prevents famine, and restate them using only narrative analogues: for instance, whether scarcity is a property of the "setting" (total food supply) or of "point-of-view" (who is permitted to claim resources); whether the state functions as an unreliable narrator withholding information from characters; whether famine results from a "plot hole" in institutional design rather than from natural disaster; and whether democratic elections work like an audience that can correct the author's direction. Do not smuggle in economic or institutional jargon unless it genuinely maps onto a narrative concept you can name. Once the translation is complete, report the residue: which of Sen's empirical and comparative claims — about exchange entitlements, wage collapses, comparative mortality statistics, or the specific causal weight of democratic mechanisms versus other institutional variables — have no counterpart in narrative theory? Is that missing vocabulary a limitation of my novelist's framework, or does it indicate that parts of Sen's argument depend on quantitative and institutional specificity that collapses when rendered as story logic? In particular, please separate what is broadly settled in the relevant scholarship — for example, that substantial food supplies existed in Bengal during the 1943 famine — from what remains genuinely contested, such as the exact explanatory balance Sen assigns to democratic institutions in the Great Leap Forward case or the comparative assessment of entitlement failure across the three events. Do not present a contested reading — such as the claim that Sen's entitlement framework explains the Great Leap Forward without significant remainder — as if it were the consensus view among specialists.
