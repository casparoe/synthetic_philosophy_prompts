Write the opening remarks for an interdisciplinary workshop on consciousness and cognition, delivered by a senior colleague who is also a family friend, honoring Dr. Elena Vasquez upon her retirement after thirty years of research and teaching. The audience is evenly divided: philosophers of mind and cognitive scientists who know her specific contribution—the argument that phenomenal consciousness is best understood as a predictive user-interface rather than a Cartesian theater or a raw data-stream—and relatives, university staff, and non-specialist friends who have little familiarity with the literature.

Vasquez's view, which should structure your remarks rather than merely decorate them, holds that the brain constructs a streamlined self-model and world-model for action-guidance, hiding the underlying neural computation much as a smartphone interface hides its code behind icons. She rejects both substance dualism and eliminativism: the interface is genuinely real and causally efficacious, but it is not transparent, and there is no inner spectator observing the display. Crucially, she has maintained that social recognition operates as part of the error-correction loop that stabilizes the self-model; being seen and acknowledged by others is not epiphenomenal to consciousness but partly constitutive of its stability.

Your speech must navigate the conceptual constraints this view imposes. You may not invoke a hidden soul, an inner witness who will watch over her legacy, or a private theater in which her life will be replayed. At the same time, you must avoid reducing her love, grief, or intellectual achievements to mere illusion in any dismissive sense; on her own account, affective states and social bonds are fully real features of the interface, not fictions to be debunked. You should also confront, quietly and without lecturing, the fact that if consciousness is a rendered user-illusion, then every person in the room—including you as speaker—is also a constructed interface, yet collective memory, gratitude, and influence remain genuinely possible within that framework.

To make this accessible to both halves of the audience, build the remarks around one extended analogy comparing the self-model to a weather-application interface that renders a sun icon from satellite data and algorithmic processing without exposing either to the user. Show precisely where the analogy holds (the hidden computation, the practical sufficiency of the icon, the risk of mistaking the display for the mechanism) and where it breaks (unlike a weather app, the self-model is co-constructed through social interaction, and its stability depends on being recognized by other interfaces).

The remarks should run approximately five minutes when spoken, maintain a formal but warm tone, and include a brief, direct address to family members who might worry that her philosophical position denies the reality of her emotional life. Do not provide bibliographies, citations, reading lists, or references to specific publications; do not ask the audience to consult her papers. Ensure that the philosophical idea shapes what can and cannot be said about remembrance, presence, and legacy.
