I am writing a feature for a science-and-ideas magazine on how philosophy of biology seminars are redesigning final assessments so that they test conceptual grip rather than catalog recall, and I want to include a concrete example of the kind of exam item that forces students to think rather than recite. Please draft an upper-level essay question—answerable in about ninety minutes—on the species problem that cannot be solved by summarizing the Biological Species Concept, the phylogenetic concept, or the familiar list of reproductive-isolation objections. To keep the problem genuinely weedy and independent of web lookup, build the prompt around a fully described hypothetical scenario: a “reticulate archipelago” consisting of island populations in which horizontal gene transfer is frequent between geographically distant nodes but rare between immediate neighbors, producing a network of genetic exchange with no tree-like backbone and no clear boundary of reproductive isolation. Ask students to evaluate whether species pluralism—the view that different species concepts serve different legitimate biological purposes—actually resolves the tension raised by this network, or whether it merely relocates the problem by multiplying entities without explaining why any one concept should govern in a given case. Then provide a model answer of roughly five hundred words and a marking scheme built around five must-hit points (total marks 20), with each point carrying its own weight, a one-line description of the specific move an A answer must make there, and a one-line note on how candidates typically miss it—particularly whether they distinguish ontological pluralism from methodological pluralism, whether they confront the “rank problem” (why species should be treated as a special taxonomic rank if pluralism is true), and whether they see that the archipelago undermines not only monism but also the standard pluralist division of labor. Write in a formal, polished register appropriate to a journalistic profile of pedagogical innovation, and do not include any bibliographic requests, citation prompts, or reading lists in the exam design.
