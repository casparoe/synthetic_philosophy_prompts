I have been reading the literature on whether omnivorous diets cause more wild-animal suffering than plant-based ones, particularly the comparison between beef and chicken once you include the indirect mortality from crop cultivation—field rodents, birds, and insects killed during planting, harvesting, and storage of feed grains and human crops. I have no formal background in ethics or ecology, but I do have a strong working knowledge of managerial cost accounting: direct and indirect costs, joint-product allocation, overhead absorption rates, capital versus operating expenditure, externalities that sit off the balance sheet, amortization schedules, and break-even analysis. I want you to translate the central empirical and normative claims in this debate—such as the feed-conversion argument for beef, the lives-per-calorie argument for chicken, the claim that crop cultivation dominates total mortality regardless of dietary choice, and the attempt to weight suffering by neurological complexity—into that accounting vocabulary. Do this systematically: take each major position in turn, render it using accounting concepts without smuggling in ethical premises under the guise of "cost," and then report what survives intact, what must be distorted to fit (for instance, treating sentient harm as a depreciable intangible asset or applying a uniform overhead rate across species with different capacities for suffering), and what has no expression whatsoever in the framework—such as interspecies moral comparability, the distinction between intended and foreseen harm, or the idea that some costs should not be internalized at all. Finally, tell me whether the untranslatable residue is a defect of cost-accounting logic—showing it is the wrong tool for welfare comparison—or whether it exposes that the original ethical debate was relying on premises that only look quantitative but are actually normative commitments that accounting language makes visible by failing to accommodate them.
