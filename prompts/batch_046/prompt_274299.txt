I am a novelist preparing a single ninety-minute workshop for a small circle of fiction writers—none with formal training in mathematics or economics—on the prisoner's dilemma in its one-shot and iterated forms, and on how the logic of cooperation's evolution can serve as an invisible architecture for recurring character obligations. I do not want a lecture in game theory; I want a teaching plan. In what precise order must the conceptual pieces be introduced so that the payoff structure feels like a narrative inevitability rather than an abstract grid? Choose one concrete carrying scenario—drawn from a repeated character obligation rather than a single transaction—that must contain both the real possibility of defection and the shadow of the future, and explain exactly what properties it must possess and what properties it must lack in order to carry the idea without distortion. Then map the misconceptions these writers will predictably form: I expect they will conflate rational defection with moral villainy, treat the iterated version as guaranteeing eventual cooperation regardless of horizon, and assume that extending the number of rounds merely multiplies the one-shot logic rather than transforming the strategic landscape. For each misconception, specify the precise point in the sequence where it should be headed off and how. Design a brief forced-choice exercise—perhaps centered on a specific plot turn involving betrayal and renewal—that will reveal whether the participants have grasped the structural difference between the two forms rather than merely memorized the vocabulary. Finally, state what must be openly refused at this level: I believe the formal derivation of the Folk Theorem and the mechanics of evolutionary stable strategies should be named but not derived, but I want your judgment on what else must be left out, including any quantitative apparatus, and on how to acknowledge these omissions without undermining the conceptual force of the idea.
