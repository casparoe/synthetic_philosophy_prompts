I am a retired condensed-matter physicist who, after leaving the laboratory, have begun auditing graduate seminars in philosophy at a nearby university. At a recent session on philosophy of language, a visiting speaker offered a summary of what she called the knowledge norm of assertion, attributing it chiefly to Timothy Williamson and extending it into the social epistemology of scientific practice. Her account, as I recorded it, ran approximately thus: the norm governing assertion is the knowledge rule, according to which one ought to state a proposition only if one possesses direct observational or inferential access that establishes its truth with something approaching certainty. Because belief is private and internal while assertion is a public act, false assertions violate an explicit social warranty in a way that false beliefs do not, which explains the asymmetry in our reactive attitudes. The view, she continued, resolves the lottery paradox: since one does not know that one's specific ticket will lose, one is normatively prohibited from asserting it, and this demonstrates that the threshold for assertion is stricter than that for justified belief. She concluded that critics are therefore correct to reject the knowledge norm for science, since researchers routinely publish preliminary findings they do not yet know, and that a belief norm—permitting publication whenever the speaker sincerely holds the view—must take its place.

Having spent decades distinguishing, in my own research groups, between a formal result presented in a refereed article and a tentative conjecture offered at a group meeting, I suspect that this summary may conflate the speech-act of assertion with the institutional practices of scientific publication, and that it may misrepresent both the lottery case and the epistemic requirements of knowledge. Could you provide a careful audit of this characterization? I would like to know, point by point, which claims accurately capture Williamson's view and which are distortions—particularly the invocation of direct observational access, the presentation of the lottery scenario as a resolution rather than a challenge, and the inference that scientific practice falsifies the norm. Furthermore, does the speaker's conclusion—that a belief norm must replace the knowledge norm in scientific contexts—actually follow from the accurate parts of her account, or does it depend on the conflation I have noted? I am not seeking a bibliography or list of further readings, only a separation of the accurate from the misrepresented, together with an evaluation of whether her recommendation survives correction.
