I am writing a paper for my philosophy of belief course on the ethics of belief. I need an original thought experiment—explicitly not a religious conversion case or a variant on Pascal's wager—that is engineered to produce a precise three-way divergence. The scenario must be realistic and confined to ordinary life (a career choice, a friendship commitment, or a civic decision), and it must present a genuinely living, forced, and momentous option that is not decidable on purely intellectual grounds, so that James's will-to-believe doctrine permits belief-formation. At the same time, Clifford's evidentialist principle must clearly condemn belief in that same situation. Crucially, the case must contain a structural feature—specifically, an element of epistemic interdependence in which the agent's adopting the belief changes the evidential environment available to others—that causes a pragmatic-encroachment view (the view that practical stakes can affect whether a belief counts as knowledge, not just whether it is permissible) to deliver a verdict that differs from both James and Clifford. After constructing the scenario, please demonstrate: (1) James's reasoning, showing how the option is living, forced, and momentous and why belief is therefore permissible despite insufficient evidence; (2) Clifford's reasoning, identifying exactly which evidential standard is violated; (3) the pragmatic-encroachment reasoning, explaining why the stakes and the interdependence together yield a distinct verdict; and (4) an honest diagnosis of which structural feature does the work, together with an assessment of whether removing that feature collapses the case back into a standard two-way disagreement. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; analyze the case from first principles only.
