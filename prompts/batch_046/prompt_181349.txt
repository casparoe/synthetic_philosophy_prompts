I'm working through Lewis Carroll's dialogue between Achilles and the Tortoise and keep finding myself pushed toward a conclusion that seems to dissolve normative logic altogether. (1) Carroll's tortoise demonstrates that if we treat modus ponens as a premise that needs to be added to an argument in order to license the inference, then applying that very premise to reach the conclusion requires another instance of modus ponens, generating an infinite regress of ever-more-premises. (2) The standard escape is to distinguish rules of inference from premises: rules are constitutive of reasoning rather than parts of its content, so they govern our movement between premises without themselves needing to be premises within the system. (3) But if inference rules are genuinely constitutive—part of the form rather than the content of reasoning—then they are not available as objects of rational belief; we either instantiate them or we fail to, but there is no standpoint from which we can rationally evaluate whether we ought to instantiate them. (4) Yet we plainly do treat logical errors as rational failures rather than as mere structural variations: if someone sincerely rejects modus ponens in ordinary reasoning, we criticize them as reasoning badly, not simply describe them as possessing a different logical architecture. (5) So logical rules must be both constitutive (to block Carroll's regress) and rationally evaluable (to preserve the normativity of logic). (6) But to evaluate a rule rationally requires applying reasoning, which presupposes the very rule being evaluated; hence any justification of deduction is either circular—using the rule to justify itself—or launches an infinite meta-regress of rules-about-rules. (7) Therefore either logic is purely descriptive and logical criticism is a category mistake, or deduction has no rational foundation and every argument—including this one—is unjustified. I find step (6) especially suspicious, because it seems to slide between applying a rule in reasoning and believing that the rule is correct, but I cannot locate the exact equivocation. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost me—giving up on the normativity of logic, accepting some form of rule-circular justification, or paying some other price? If I must choose, I would prefer to keep both (2) and (4), but I want to understand what I am sacrificing.
