I am writing to ask for help sorting out a disagreement that arose during my volunteer work at a neurological rehabilitation center. There is a long-term resident, Mrs. Lin, who suffered damage that destroyed the associative networks linking her sensory processing regions to her prefrontal planning centers, while leaving her local sensory and motor modules intact. To restore her ability to navigate and respond, surgeons implanted a prosthetic routing system that sends sensory data directly to her motor-planning circuits, bypassing the usual integrative pathways altogether. The result is remarkable: she can walk through hallways, recognize family members, answer questions coherently, and even play chess, because the prosthetic allows globally appropriate behavior. Yet Mrs. Lin reports that nothing feels like anything—there is no inner picture, no sense of presence, only automatic outputs that occur without anyone experiencing them. Her doctors argue that she is fully conscious because the prosthetic achieves the same functional result as natural long-range brain integration: information is available everywhere it needs to be for reasoning, memory, and action. Her granddaughter, who reads philosophy on her own, insists that consciousness requires more than functional availability; she says that if there is genuinely nothing it is like to be Mrs. Lin—if all subjective experience is absent—then calling her “conscious” is merely labeling complex behavior, no different from attributing awareness to a very sophisticated machine. I find myself pulled in both directions. The doctors seem right that information is being shared globally across her brain, yet Mrs. Lin’s own testimony about the complete absence of any qualitative inner life feels authoritative. To test my intuition, I imagined a purely artificial system with the same prosthetic architecture—sensory inputs routed directly to decision outputs, with no intermediate associative layer that could generate experience. If such a machine behaved identically to Mrs. Lin, would we be obligated to treat it as conscious? I am not looking for general advice about empathy or medical ethics; I want to understand whether the kind of global information-sharing the doctors describe is sufficient for consciousness, or whether there is a separate dimension of inner experience that can be missing even when all the functional conditions are satisfied, and what that implies for how I should regard Mrs. Lin’s state. Could you help me map out the philosophical structure of this tension?
