hey, quick ontology-adjacent question for anyone who's thought about this. I'm in my third year of a phil of science phd and I keep running into this argumentative move in papers and seminars -- not sure if it has a name or if I'm just seeing a pattern that isn't there.

The move goes like this: someone defends a classification by saying it tracks something genuinely shared, not just our labeling. They point to clear cases -- metals all conduct electricity in a unified way, plastic doesn't, and that's not accidental. But then you bring up a weird edge case, like a semi-metal or a superconductor that only works near absolute zero, and they suddenly say "oh well categories are just predictive tools, there's no deeper fact here." The thing is, they don't drop the first claim. They keep both in play: the "real shared feature" talk to defend the core grouping, and the "just a tool" talk to dodge counter-examples at the margins. It's not quite equivocation, and it's not the standard realism-vs-nominalism debate either, because the same speaker shifts between seperate registers without noticing the tension. I've seen it in biology (species boundaries), aesthetics (genre definintions), even legal theory. Is there an established concept for this exact two-register defense? And if there isn't, can we name and define it precisely -- like, what's its boundry with honest context-sensitivity, and when does it collapse into plain pragmatism? I keep feeling like I need this distinction for my diss chapter and I don't want to reinvent the wheel if it's already named.
