last night i was sitting with two friends who got into it about whether archeologists can actually infer what people beleived just from digging up broken pottery and burial layouts. they were specifically arguing over those sites with deliberately shattered vessels in non-domestic pits.

(1) the first friend said its basically impossible - objects don't carry intentions, so calling a broken pot "ritual" is just storytelling. you can catalog fracture patterns but jumping to mental states is writing fiction into the dirt.

(2) the second friend pushed back that that's way too extreme. she pointed out that if standardized weights or measuring tools show up across five unrelated settlements, archeologists infer shared economic institutions or authority. that's cross-site pattern recognition, not fiction. banning that kind of inference would reduce the field to cataloging trash.

(3) the first friend said weights are different because they have an obvious practical payoff - measuring grain or goods. the inference stops at behavior and use. with ritual pottery there's no observable practical benefit, so people smuggle in "belief" or "symbolism" just to fill the explanatory gap. it's an argument from ignorance dressed up as interpretation.

(4) the second friend brought up those ceramic fragments with the specific rim-chipping pattern found in both coastal and highland burial contexts where there's no evidence of trade or contact. the chips actually make vessels worse for storage or transport, and diffusion is unlikely, so shared symbolic logic seems like the best explanation - even if we can't name the exact belief behind it.

please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the four moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify each rating. also, what are both of them missing in this exchange? and is there a principled line between inferring an institution and inferring a belief, or does the distinction collapse once you allow cross-site pattern matching?
