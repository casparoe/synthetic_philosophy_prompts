okay so i was arguing with my roommate about confucian role ethics — specifically the reconstructed version where you decouple it from patriarchal family structures and treat roles (parent/child, elder/younger) as performative positions maintained through ritual interaction (li) rather than ascribed by birth or gender. my view is that if you rebuild it as a kind of horizontal relationalism, it actually solves a problem feminist care ethics keeps running into: how to justify partial obligations to specific people without essentializing gender or biological family. because on this view, being "a daughter" or "an elder" is just occupying a node in a network of reciprocal expectations that gets negotiated through practice, not a natural kind. so the position predicts that moral failure in these relationships is always a failure of role-performance or ritual reciprocity, not innate character, and that this should hold even for transnational or digitally-mediated kinship — like diaspora families maintaining filial obligations through WeChat rituals — without requiring physical co-presence.

i want to know what would actually count against this — not just the usual "it still smuggles in hierarchy" objection, but serious defeaters. which intuitions, possible cases, or results in moral psychology or anthropology would undermine the claim that roles can be fully performative and horizontally negotiated? what would have to be true about embodied reciprocity, or about the limits of ritual mediation, for this reconstruction to collapse? and which of these problems would a defender of reconstructed role ethics definately have to concede are real, versus which ones can they absorb by tweaking the theory? rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and be honest about whether this position risks being qualified into unfalsifiability — if every failure of reciprocity just gets redescribed as "bad role-performance" — and whether that is actually a problem for a normative framework of this kind.
