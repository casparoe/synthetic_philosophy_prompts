I have come to defend a two-layer account of simultaneous invention—taking calculus and evolutionary theory as the central cases—which I call structural overdetermination coupled with representational path-dependence. The first layer holds that once a discipline reaches sufficient conceptual density, the formal result is essentially inevitable: some version of differential calculus or descent with modification was going to emerge within a generation regardless of individual agency. The second layer holds that the specific representational framing—Newton's fluxions versus Leibniz's notation, Darwin's gradualist narrative versus Wallace's biogeographic emphasis—is genuinely contingent, and it shapes how a scientific community normalizes, teaches, and extends the result for decades. So attribution is causally inert at the structural level but historically consequential at the representational level. What would count against this view? I want the arguments and historical patterns that should actually move me, sorted from decisive to merely awkward: episodes where framing made no lasting disciplinary difference, episodes where structural inevitability clearly failed and a result was genuinely lost or indefinitely delayed, and whether the two-layer distinction collapses into either flat inevitabilism or flat social constructivism. I am especially interested in whether the representational layer is doing any real explanatory work, or whether it has been qualified into unfalsifiability—and if so, whether that insulation is a problem for an account of this kind.
