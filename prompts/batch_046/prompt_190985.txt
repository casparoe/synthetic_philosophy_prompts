I am preparing material for a seminar on metaethics and find the standard particularism-versus-generalism exchange somewhat stale, so I reconstructed a sharper version of the debate I heard between two visiting philosophers in our common room. They were arguing over a hypothetical case they called the institutional-deception study: an IRB is asked to approve research that requires deliberately withholding a minor risk disclosure from participants in order to preserve behavioral validity, where the deception is calibrated to cause no lasting harm but does violate standard informed-consent norms. The exchange went as follows.

(1) The generalist argued that particularism is self-undermining because moral improvement requires transferable patterns. If the moral contribution of any feature—such as causing harm or preserving scientific validity—really depends on an open-ended holistic context in the way particularists claim, then experience of past cases gives us no stable grip on new ones, and moral education collapses into an endless series of unguided intuitions. She insisted that even defeasible general principles, such as deception in research requires especially strong justification, are necessary to explain how anyone learns.

(2) The particularist responded that moral learning is analogical and perceptual rather than subsumptive. He said we cultivate a capacity to recognize morally salient configurations, comparable to learning connoisseurship in wine or music, without ever formulating necessary-and-sufficient conditions. He granted that patterns exist, but denied they are captured by principles that specify invariant moral weights; rather, we learn to see what matters in a specific situation.

(3) The generalist then pressed what she termed the feature-inversion problem, using the IRB case. She noted that protecting study validity is normally a reason to approve a protocol, yet in this context—because the method of protection requires deception that risks eroding public trust—the very same feature seems to generate a reason to reject it. Without a principled account of when a feature inverts its valence, she said, particularism can only redescribe the outcome after the fact and offers no predictive or explanatory power.

(4) The particularist countered by distinguishing feature-override from feature-transformation. He argued that in particularism, the feature is not identical across contexts: validity achieved through calibrated deception in a vulnerable institutional setting is not the same moral feature as validity achieved through transparency, because the surrounding relational properties—participant vulnerability, institutional trust, the specific stakes of the study—alter what the feature amounts to morally. Thus there is no mysterious inversion requiring a meta-principle; the feature's contribution changes because the feature itself, relationally construed, is different.
