I'm delivering the opening remarks at a retirement workshop honoring Professor Elena Voss, who spent forty years on Frege's puzzle about identity statements. Her specific view — developed in papers most of the philosophers present have argued with but her family has never seen — is that an informative "a = b" does not merely register two mental representations of one object; it tracks what she calls different epistemic accessibility conditions on the same reference, so that learning Hesperus is Phosphorus is not acquiring a synonym but gaining a previously unavailable mode of access to Venus. Roughly half the room are philosophers of language and logic who will notice if I misstate her position; the other half are her grandchildren, her dean, and staff from the university press who need to understand why this matters without being lectured. Write a five-minute address that makes her philosophical distinction do real structural work: explain why saying "Elena is still Elena" misses the point of praising her, and why honoring her properly requires saying something that sounds redundant but is not — naming her in one register for the family and in another for her colleagues — with neither version false and neither condescending. Include a brief, concrete anecdote about her interrupting a speaker at a 1998 conference who treated "Hesperus is Hesperus" as a substantive identity claim, and use that moment to show exactly what she thinks an identity statement can and cannot do. The speech should perform, rather than merely describe, the kind of informative identity statement she spent her career analyzing.
