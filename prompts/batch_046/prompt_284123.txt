After retiring from experimental physics and turning to philosophy, I have been trying to formalize a view I call substrate-independent tranquilism: only the absence of craving and of suffering carries intrinsic positive value, while pleasure, achievement, conscious existence itself, and any other presence are axiologically neutral. A study partner pressed me with an argument that seems to turn this into a reductio, but I cannot locate the precise failure.

(1) If only absences possess intrinsic value, then assessing any world depends solely on the total quantity of craving and suffering present in it.

(2) Compare world W, in which a billion sentient beings live flourishing lives with moderate joy and occasional pain, with world W*, identical except that those beings never come to exist. W contains suffering that W* lacks, and while W contains joy, by (1) that adds no value. Therefore W* is strictly axiologically superior to W.

(3) The same reasoning applies within an existing life. A permanent, painless vegetative null-state eliminates all future craving and suffering for the subject, and removes no intrinsic goods, since consciousness and its contents are neutral. Hence that null-state is the best possible outcome for the subject.

(4) Therefore tranquilism entails that universal non-existence, or universal rational pursuit of vegetative nullity, is the optimal state of affairs, and that each agent should seek it.

(5) That conclusion is intolerable.

I suspect the slip involves a hidden substrate error—applying value-conditions meant for states of existing subjects to worlds in which no subject exists—but I am unsure whether blocking that slide saves the view or merely introduces an ad hoc exception that smuggles positive value back into bare existence. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I would prefer to preserve (1) if the price is not too high.
