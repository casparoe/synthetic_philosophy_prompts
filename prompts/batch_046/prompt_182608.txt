I am preparing the opening remarks for a workshop titled “Who Gets to Know? Credibility and Labor in Scientific Practice,” convened in the university’s interdisciplinary science and philosophy center. The audience is divided roughly evenly between practicing laboratory scientists—including senior technicians, postdoctoral researchers, and principal investigators, many of whom regard philosophical talk of “injustice” with suspicion, assuming it refers only to demographic discrimination—and philosophers of social epistemology who are familiar with standard accounts of epistemic injustice but may resist extending them beyond identity-based prejudice. I need you to write an eight-minute address that takes a specific, structurally embedded credibility deficit as its organizing case: experienced instrument technicians and animal-care staff routinely report anomalous calibration data or unexpected behavioral readings that conflict with a study’s theoretical predictions, yet these reports are systematically classified as “operational noise” rather than as potential falsifiers—not because the reporters belong to a marginalized social group, but because the institutional taxonomy assigns their labor to the category of “maintenance” rather than “discovery.” The speech should use this scenario to do genuine philosophical work, showing how this categorization functions analogously to testimonial injustice by systematically deflating a specific group’s epistemic authority, without reducing the argument to a generic complaint about workplace hierarchy. You must navigate the mixed audience with care: for the scientists, explain why this is not merely a management failure but an epistemic failure with direct methodological consequences; for the philosophers, show whether this case extends, complicates, or requires revision of familiar frameworks of testimonial and hermeneutical injustice, and defend a clear position on whether it demands a distinct conceptual category or should be subsumed under existing ones. Do not conclude with a non-committal summary; instead, justify a concrete normative proposal—perhaps restructuring co-authorship norms for technical reports, establishing anonymized channels for anomalous data, or revising peer-review protocols to weight maintenance-level observations differently—and ground that proposal in both epistemic and ethical value. The tone should be formal, respectful, and appropriate for an interdisciplinary opening ceremony. Avoid any request for bibliographies, reading lists, or specific citations; ground the argument entirely in the institutional dynamics described and in the conceptual distinction between maintenance-knowledge and discovery-knowledge. You may invoke the general idea of testimonial injustice as it figures in contemporary philosophy of science, but do not quote specific texts verbatim, and do not depend on precise publication dates, obscure secondary debates, or named theorists beyond what is necessary for clarity.
