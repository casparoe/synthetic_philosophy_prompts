Having spent four decades in experimental physics before turning, in retirement, to philosophy, I have become preoccupied with whether the disciplined habits of scientific reasoning can be exported to domains that lack controlled experiments. One framework that appeals to me is the forecasting approach that relies on reference classes, base rates, and the so-called outside view—particularly in its application to the life cycles of empires. I wish to examine its rigor against the dissolution of the Soviet Union in 1991, but with a deliberate twist: instead of treating the USSR purely as a territorial empire, I propose analyzing it through a hybrid reference class that combines (a) multi-ethnic federal states possessing nuclear arsenals and undergoing rapid ideological de-legitimization, and (b) large-scale scientific-bureaucratic institutions whose organizational structures persisted after the theoretical paradigms that originally justified them collapsed. Applying base-rate reasoning from both classes, what dissolution probabilities or lifespan expectations emerge? Does the 1991 collapse fall within the predicted range, or does it constitute an outlier that reveals a structural weakness—specifically, in how the outside view handles abrupt legitimacy crises as opposed to gradual institutional decay? Moreover, can the framework accommodate the particular mechanism at work—the swift withdrawal of collective belief in Marxist-Leninist governance—without reducing it to an undifferentiated external shock? I am interested not only in what the forecasting lens illuminates about 1991, but in what 1991 in turn exposes about the boundaries of reference-class reasoning when applied to political entities that were simultaneously ideological-scientific projects.
