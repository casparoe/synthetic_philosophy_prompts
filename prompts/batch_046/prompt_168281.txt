I'm trying to sort out my actual gut feelings about who belongs to the demos — like, who gets a say in democraic decisions — and I want to see if there's a principel behind them, or if I'm just being inconsistent.

Here are my verdicts on concrete cases:

1. A non-citizen who has lived in a city for thirty years, pays taxes, sends her kids to local schools — she should vote in local elections. She belongs.

2. A citizen born in that city but who left at age two, lives abroad now, holds only the passport for sentimental reasons — he should NOT vote in local elections. He doesn't belong.

3. A 16-year-old who works full-time, pays income tax, and is affectd by labor law — should have a say in those specific regulations, but I don't think he should vote in general national elections.

4. A prisoner serving a life sentence in the state — should still count for districting/apportionment, but I don't think he should actually cast a ballot. So he belongs for representation math but not for voting?

5. A corporation that operates locally, employs people, is regulated — it should not vote, even though it's heavily affected. Not part of the demos.

6. Future people who will live with the climate effects of today's carbon policy — I feel like they should have some kind of proxy or weighted consideration, but I can't tell if that means they "belong" or just that we should care.

7. A tourist visiting for two weeks — clearly not part of the demos, even if a local ordinance affects her stay.

The ones that really pull in different directions are case 1 vs case 2: affectedness and long-term presence seem to matter locally, but citizenship seems to matter nationally. And case 3 vs case 4: why does being affected grant some say for the teenager but being affected by prison conditions doesn't grant a vote? Or maybe it should?
