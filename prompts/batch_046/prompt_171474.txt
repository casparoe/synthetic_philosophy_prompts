Here are my judgments about when reactive attitudes—resentment, indignation, disappointment, and their kin—are morally warranted, together with a tentative principle I have been testing against them. The cases move from direct interpersonal harm through institutional harm to excused failure, and I want to know whether one account can hold them together or whether I must abandon one of my verdicts.

1. A stranger accidentally steps on my foot in a crowded subway car and never notices. I feel a flash of irritation that dissipates immediately. Verdict: no sustained reactive attitude is appropriate; momentary annoyance is not moral resentment.

2. The same stranger deliberately steps on my foot to claim space, looking at me as he does so. Verdict: sustained, personal resentment is fully appropriate.

3. A close friend misses my wedding because a severe depressive episode makes leaving bed genuinely impossible; they are devastated and apologize sincerely afterward. Verdict: deep disappointment and grief are appropriate, but resentment or moral anger are not, despite the seriousness of the harm.

4. A corporation deploys a hiring algorithm that systematically excludes qualified candidates like me. The designers never intended discrimination, but they ignored available fairness audits because meeting the launch deadline was commercially pressured. No single employee willed the harm. Verdict: indignation directed at the corporation, and perhaps at the design team collectively, is appropriate, even though there is no individual malicious will to respond to.

5. A surgeon, required by hospital policy to work thirty-six continuous hours, makes a fatigue-induced error that permanently injures me. The surgeon knew the policy was dangerous but faced termination if they refused the shift. Verdict: resentment toward the surgeon feels somewhat appropriate but should be heavily qualified; resentment toward the hospital administration feels fully appropriate. The tension: if systemic coercion largely excuses the surgeon in 5, why does commercial pressure in 4 not similarly excuse the designers?

6. My parent lies to me for years about my adoption, sincerely believing the truth would damage me, but the lie was unnecessary and caused lasting harm once revealed. Verdict: resentment is appropriate despite the absence of malice and the presence of genuinely protective intent.

My tentative principle is that a reactive attitude is appropriate if and only if (a) the agent's conduct manifests a deficiency in the quality of will that the relevant relational or institutional norm demands of them, and (b) within the constraints of that norm, the agent had a reasonable opportunity to avoid the deficiency. On this view, the surgeon in 5 is partly excused because the institutional norm of safe practice was itself violated by the hospital, constraining the surgeon's opportunity; the designers in 4 are not excused because the professional norm of care did not require them to ignore fairness checks.

Can a more coherent principle—perhaps one that distinguishes participant reactive attitudes from vicarious or institutional ones, or that indexes excuses differently—capture all six verdicts without the tension between 4 and 5? If the best available principle saves 1–3 and 6 but must overturn either 4 or 5, which should go, and why? Apply your best principle also to this unlisted case: a fully automated climate-control system (no conscious agent, no present institutional decision-maker) damages my property due to an error that was statistically predictable and left unpatched because patching was deemed too expensive by the manufacturer five years ago. Would any reactive attitude be appropriate, and if so, toward whom?

Please state your principle precisely, with the specific case that motivates each clause, its verdict on the automation case, and an honest statement of which of my six judgments it cannot accommodate. Also say what consideration—whether about institutional agency, the distinction between excuse and justification, or the limits of reactive attitudes—would lead you to revise the principle, and identify the point in your reasoning that is most vulnerable to failure.
