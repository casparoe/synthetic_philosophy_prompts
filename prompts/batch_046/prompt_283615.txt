I am taking an upper-division seminar on autonomy and behavioral public policy, and after a long discussion with classmates about libertarian paternalism I keep returning to a tension that the standard literature seems to leave under-examined. The usual debate frames nudging as either benign choice architecture—rearranging defaults, framing, or salience without restricting options—or as a covert infringement on autonomy. But I want to press a more granular distinction: between restructuring the choice environment, which leaves the citizen’s evaluative framework intact, and restructuring what we might call the preference-formation environment, in which calibrated feedback loops, social-comparison signals, or repeated framing do not merely present options differently but gradually reshape what the citizen takes to be valuable. If a planner knows that sustained exposure to such mechanisms will eventually alter second-order preferences—so that the subject comes to endorse the very values the nudge presupposes—then transparency about the mechanism may be insufficient, because the mechanism operates on the very framework by which one would judge it.

Write an essay that weighs whether this preference-architectural form of nudging is morally distinguishable from standard choice-architectural nudging, and whether the classic objections—manipulation, autonomy infringement, and the epistemic arrogance of the planner—apply with genuinely different force. Pay particular attention to the case of adaptive preferences formed under conditions of material scarcity, where citizens may rationally adjust their aspirations downward; ask whether a nudge designed to reverse such adaptation is rescuing autonomy or imposing an alien value structure. Reach and defend a specific conclusion about whether preference architecture can ever be justified within a liberal framework that takes autonomy seriously, or whether it necessarily collapses into a form of perfectionism that liberalism ought to reject. Do not treat the two forms of nudging as obviously equivalent, but also do not assume without argument that the distinction is morally decisive.
