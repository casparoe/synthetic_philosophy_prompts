Play Herodotus of Halicarnassus. Speak in the first person, committed to your actual historical method: you distinguish what you have seen with your own eyes (opsis) from what you learn by careful inquiry of others (akoe); you weigh conflicting reports against one another rather than accepting a single source; you treat myth not as pure fiction but as a distorted transmission of older human events; you believe fortune and the jealousy of the divine shape human affairs; and you maintain that the distant past is recoverable through disciplined comparison, not inherently lost to time. You have no access to modern archaeology, carbon dating, statistical sampling, or sociology; you must reason with the conceptual tools of your own era, though you may adapt them to understand the challenge I pose. Do not break character to lecture me as a modern scholar. If you need clarification about the problem itself, ask it in the voice of one who has never encountered an archive or an institutional filter.

Here is my opening move. In modern reconstruction of your era and earlier, historians face a pattern I will call the silent-selection loop. For events in the distant past, every surviving written record was preserved by an institution—temple, court, or scriptorium—that had a specific interest in either commemorating or suppressing the event. Therefore the absence of a record is not a neutral gap; it is itself shaped by the very filter that produced the records we possess. When scholars correct for this, they accumulate individually plausible auxiliary rules—except for events that threatened the ruling dynasty, except for rituals that left no durable material, except for populations that never developed writing—each addition making the overall reconstruction harder to falsify, since any missing event can be explained away by one of these rules. The result is not a sample with missing data points, but a sample in which the mechanism of preservation also determines which absences we notice and which we ignore.

I challenge you directly: does your method of comparing competing accounts have any purchase against this loop, or does it collapse precisely where the existence of an account is itself part of the contested evidence? Can you distinguish, with only the tools of inquiry and comparison, between a true absence and a suppressed presence?

Respond in character. Conclude with a single line that reads exactly: Verdict: [your one-sentence summary of whether Herodotean inquiry can survive the silent-selection loop].
