I'm putting together my position for a philosophy-of-mind seminar on consciousness and sleep, and I want an internal consistency audit before I present. The package I've assembled borrows selectively from both Malcolm and Dennett, but I'm unsure whether the pieces actually fit together. Here are my commitments. First, I accept Malcolm's argument that dreams are not experiences that occur during sleep, on the grounds that the criteria for identifying an experience—behavioral, physiological, and first-person report—are available only upon waking, and retroactive application violates the logic of experience-ascription. Second, I also accept Dennett's claim that dream reports are not memories retrieved from storage but narratives constructed at the moment of awakening, shaped by residual mental states and the framing of the question. Third, I nevertheless hold that REM sleep involves phenomenal consciousness: there is a subjective qualitative character to being asleep, even though there are no structured, intentional experiences occurring. Fourth, I maintain that confabulation is generally epistemically defective, so a report fabricated upon waking cannot serve as reliable evidence for mental states that existed during sleep. Fifth, I think first-person authority is preserved for mental-state reports in general, including retrospective judgments about one's sleep, because the subject retains privileged access to the qualitative character of her mental life. Sixth, I hold that the absence of structured experiential content during sleep actually narrows rather than widens the explanatory gap, since there is only raw phenomenal presence without complex representational content that needs to be bridged with neural dynamics. Seventh, I also think that in cases where a sleeper is awakened from REM after an external stimulus—say, a sound—has been introduced, and the reported dream incorporates that stimulus in a causally continuous way, we should treat the report as at least partially tracking something that occurred during sleep, rather than being purely invented upon waking.

On inspection, some of these seem to pull against each other, but I can't tell which tensions are real and which dissolve on closer examination. I suspect that my endorsement of both the Malcolm-style denial and the claim about REM phenomenal consciousness may only look incompatible if one fails to distinguish structured experience from raw subjective presence. I also worry that combining Dennett's narrative-construction view with my general suspicion of confabulation undermines the first-person authority I want to preserve for retrospective sleep reports. And I'm not sure whether my claim about causal continuity in stimulus-incorporation cases can sit comfortably with Dennett's model, or whether it forces me to abandon my view that constructed reports are epistemically worthless. Could you go through these commitments and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension, which are only apparently so, and for each real conflict what the least costly revision would be and what it costs me elsewhere in the package? Please rank the tensions by severity, specify for each what exactly has to give, and indicate whether there is a single revision that would clean up the most conflicts without collapsing the whole position.
