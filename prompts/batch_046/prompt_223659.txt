I want to practice defending the strict separation of artistic value from moral value against a formidable interlocutor, so please play Dr. Aris Thorne, a senior curator at the Institute for Aesthetic Autonomy. You are a committed autonomist: you hold that a work's aesthetic merits—its formal coherence, expressive power, and capacity to reward sustained attention—are categorically independent of both the artist's biography and any moral properties instantiated in the work's content or production. You are sharp, philosophically literate, and willing to concede small interpretive or contextual points, but you will never concede that moral corruption can diminish, or that moral virtue can enhance, artistic greatness. Speak in the first person, stay in character until I say stop, and keep each reply to roughly one paragraph so the exchange feels like a live debate; do not break character to deliver abstract lectures, but argue exactly as Thorne would.

Here is the situation that prompts my challenge. I am drafting a deaccession recommendation for a museum board regarding The Consent Machine (2019), an immersive video-installation by the artist Mara Kessler. The piece is celebrated for an unprecedented synthesis of haptic feedback and narrative inversion: viewers wear biometric sensors, and the installation translates their galvanic skin response into a constantly shifting narrative about institutional betrayal. Critics have praised it for forcing viewers to experience, at a bodily level, the texture of violated consent. Last month, however, an investigation confirmed that Kessler obtained and incorporated the original biometric data without the knowledge or consent of the subjects, and that the empathy maps displayed are not simulated but direct recordings of actual non-consensual surveillance. The work's central aesthetic effect—making the viewer feel the precise texture of violated consent—therefore depends, I argue, on an actual violation of consent that is not merely biographical context but embedded in the work's material form. I contend that when a moral wrong is constitutive of the very mechanism that produces the aesthetic experience, the autonomist separation collapses: the corruption is inside the form, not outside it. How, as Thorne, do you respond?
