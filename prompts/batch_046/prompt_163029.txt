As a novelist preparing a manuscript that reimagines the private deliberations of a largely forgotten nineteenth-century reformer whose papers survive only in fragments, I have been reflecting on what moral obligations—if any—bind an author to the factual record of a life now ended. A fellow writer recently summarized the prevailing philosophical view for me in these terms: "Once biological life ceases, the subject loses all capacity for experience, and with it every form of moral standing. Consequently, talk of harming the dead through inaccurate portrayal is purely metaphorical; the dead are not injured by fiction, only living relatives might be distressed. Therefore, artistic license is essentially unlimited, provided one avoids defaming survivors. Any insistence on fidelity to the dead is sentimentalism dressed in ethical language, not a coherent moral claim." This strikes me as a stark rendering of an Epicurean-style argument, yet I have also encountered discussions of posthumous interests and of the idea that personal identity is partly constituted by the narrative one leaves behind—considerations that seem to resist such a clean dismissal. Is the summary above a fair representation of the relevant philosophical debate? Please distinguish which parts capture genuinely defended positions, which are common simplifications, and which misrepresent the arguments of those who maintain that the dead retain some form of moral claim upon how they are represented. In your assessment, please also address a concept I am tentatively calling the dramaturgical covenant: the view that a novelist owes the dead not documentary fidelity to every episode, but fidelity to the moral arc implied by their documented choices, even when inventing dialogue and interiority. Is this a defensible intermediate position, or does it collapse into either pure biography or pure fiction?
