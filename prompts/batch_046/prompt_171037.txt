I am organizing a sixty-minute session for our high school philosophy club, which has about fifteen students aged sixteen to eighteen. I want a runnable in-class activity or structured role-play that makes the participants feel the force of the paradox of tolerance—specifically the argument, associated with Karl Popper, that a society which extends unlimited tolerance to intolerant groups risks destroying its own tolerance—without delivering a lecture first. To keep the exercise from being a generic debate, I have embedded a novel institutional twist: the students are divided into four factions and must collaboratively draft a moderation charter for an imaginary student-run digital forum. The charter must include an amendment clause allowing any two factions to vote away the posting rights of a third faction if that third faction advocates removing rights from others, and the group must also decide whether to adopt a recursive meta-tolerance rule that denies charter protection to any faction that rejects mutual protection. The rules, voting incentives, and faction roles should be arranged so that the students discover the paradox themselves—ideally through a procedural deadlock or a cascade of exclusions—before I name it in the debrief. Please design the full activity: specific roles and rules; required materials; a minute-by-minute timeline; a payoff or voting scheme that uses no real money; a debrief that maps each critical moment in the negotiation onto the corresponding logical step in the argument; an honest prediction of what will go wrong the first time I facilitate it and how to recover; and a frank statement of which feature of the original argument—such as the distinction between rational persuasion and coercive state power, or its reliance on long-run probabilistic reasoning—this simulation cannot reproduce and I must supply verbally. The deliverable should be precise enough for me to run it with minimal preparation.
