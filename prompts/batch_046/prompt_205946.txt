I have been working through Rosenthal’s higher-order thought (HOT) account of consciousness for a philosophy of mind seminar, and I believe I am importing a false presupposition about how misrepresentation is supposed to function within the framework, though I cannot pinpoint exactly where the error lies. The standard formulation holds that a mental state M becomes conscious only when accompanied by an actual, non-inferential higher-order thought representing that one is in M. From this, it seems to follow that if the first-order state is absent—say, in a hallucinated experience of pain—then the higher-order thought lacks an appropriate target, and therefore should be unable to generate any conscious experience at all. Yet phenomenologically there clearly is an illusory conscious state in such cases. To test the structure of the theory, I have been imagining a hypothetical neurocognitive architecture—call it a “dislocated monitor”—in which a second-order tracking module in prefrontal cortex remains active and generates representations of the form “I am seeing red” even after targeted suppression of the corresponding first-order visual representation in the ventral stream. In this scenario, is the subject conscious of seeing red, or is the higher-order state somehow “targetless” and therefore incapable of conferring consciousness?

I have seen the distinction drawn between “actualist” HOT, which requires an occurrent higher-order thought right now, and “dispositionalist” versions, which require only the appropriate disposition to form one. But it is unclear to me whether either version can accommodate targetless higher-order representations without either (a) conceding that consciousness can exist in the absence of any corresponding first-order content, which seems to collapse HOT into a form of self-generating phenomenal intentionality, or (b) insisting that the missing target renders the HOT itself unconscious, which appears to deny the reality of hallucination altogether. Could the confusion be that I am tacitly treating the higher-order representation as if it must be causally anchored to, or literally contain, the first-order state, when in fact the theory only requires a specific representational relation? Or does the “dislocated monitor” case reveal a deeper problem—namely, that HOT cannot distinguish veridical consciousness from hallucination without smuggling in externalist conditions that the theory was designed to avoid? I am trying to understand whether the mistake lies in my assumption about target-dependence, or whether the architecture I have sketched genuinely breaks the theory in a way that requires revising the conditions on conscious status.
