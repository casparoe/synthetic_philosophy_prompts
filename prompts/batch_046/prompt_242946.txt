I run a philosophy reading group for about 8-10 people, 90 minutes, mostly grad students with a few undergrads mixed in. I want an activity that lets them feel Montaigne's relativism about cannibals — specificaly how he compares eating prisoners to European religious torture — without naming him or using the word cannibal upfront. Design a card-based deliberaton: each player gets a card describing a practice (some drawn from 16th-century Europe, some from the Tupinambá context, some modern analogues), and they place them on a civilized vs barbaric spectrum using a criterian they all agree on. The catch is that after they've locked placements, they draw consequence cards showing their criterian also applies to familiar practices they don't want to call barbaric. Give me rules, roles — one judge who defends placements, one skeptic who challenges consistency, the rest as advocates — plus materials and a non-monetary payoff like reputation tokens. Include a debrief that maps the contradiction moment onto Montaigne's move, tells me what will predictably break (people spotting the trick too early, the skeptic dominating, or everyone getting defensive about cultural sensitivity), and is frank about which part of his point the exercise can't reproduce — probably the lived familiarity bias that makes Europeans blind to their own cruelty — and how I should supply that myself. Say clearly what they should come away seeing: not just everything is relative but the specific asymmetry in how we judge our own cruelty versus others'.
