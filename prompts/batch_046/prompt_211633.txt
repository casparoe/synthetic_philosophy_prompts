Before filing a long-form essay on how we distinguish science from its imitators, I want an external audit of my own methodological commitments, since I plan to accuse others of holding inconsistent standards. Here is my profile: (1) I accept Popper’s basic demarcation criterion—a claim counts as scientific only if some logically possible observation could count against it. (2) I grant the Duhem-Quine thesis fully: no hypothesis is ever tested alone, so a failed prediction never, by pure logic, forces abandonment of a core theory. (3) To save falsifiability from (2), I propose what I call an exposure contract: a scientific programme must specify in advance which auxiliary assumptions may be revised under failure and which parts of the core are genuinely at risk; revisions that respect the contract are legitimate, those that violate it are ad hoc. (4) I classify astrology and homeopathy as pseudoscientific not mainly because they are false, but because their histories are almost entirely violations of exposure contracts—vague predictions, retroactive reinterpretation, and un-pre-committed auxiliary excuses. (5) At the same time, I think Lakatos was essentially right that we should evaluate long-term research programmes rather than single hypotheses, using progressive versus degenerating problem shifts as the real metric. (6) I also believe institutional practices—pre-registration, open data, replication—do more actual boundary work than any logical criterion, including my exposure contract; logical criteria are useful mainly for post-hoc diagnosis, not for real-time policing. (7) Finally, I hold that protecting a well-confirmed core theory (say, general relativity) by revising an instrumental auxiliary is rational and scientific, provided the revision is independently testable.

Do these hang together? I sense friction between my Popperian commitment (1), my Lakatosian commitment (5), and my institutionalist claim (6). I also worry that my defense of core-protection in (7) licenses exactly the auxiliary-tinkering I condemn in (4). I want a precise audit, not reassurance: rank the tensions by severity, specify for each whether it is real or only apparent, identify the cheapest revision and what it costs me (for instance, abandoning Popper, weakening my critique of astrology, or conceding that logic does no demarcation work at all), and tell me whether one single change would clean up the most damage. If my framing rests on a confused premise—say, that an exposure contract can be formalized without smuggling in institutional norms, or that I can consistently hold both Popper and Lakatos—say so plainly rather than politely patching over the contradiction. I want direct pushback if the package is incoherent at the root.
