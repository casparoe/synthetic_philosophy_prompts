In the debate over whether dreams are experiences—famously contested between Malcolm, who denies that a sleeper undergoes anything it is like to dream, and Dennett, who treats dream narratives as largely constructed upon waking rather than experienced during sleep—my own verdicts on concrete cases feel mostly sensible but hard to reconcile into one account. Here they are.

1. A vivid REM dream of falling, with clear imagery and emotion, reported immediately upon waking: clearly an experience. The phenomenology is present and the first-person report is authoritative.

2. Dreamless sleep with no recall, no emotional residue, and no answer to "what was it like?": not an experience. There is nothing it was like to be me then.

3. Sleepwalking through a complex sequence—unlocking a door, making tea, returning to bed—with open eyes but complete amnesia and no subjective trace upon waking: not an experience. The behavior is purposeful, yet there is no conscious accompaniment.

4. A false awakening in which I dream I have woken up, walked to my desk, and begun writing; I realize it was a dream only when I actually wake moments later: this is an experience, specifically an experience of waking and writing. That its content is false does not cancel the phenomenology.

5. A lucid dream in which I maintain meta-awareness that I am dreaming and deliberately direct the narrative: not only an experience, but if anything a more authoritative one, since my first-person access includes knowledge of the state's conditions.

6. A deeply suggestible subject in a hypnotic trance who reports a vivid garden scene with specific colors; upon emergence she denies any memory, contradicts her earlier report, and shows no emotional or behavioral trace: I want to say this was not an experience at all, merely verbal production without phenomenological backing.

7. A five-second microsleep at the wheel producing a sudden fragmentary "flash" of being elsewhere, leaving only a vague sense that "something happened" with no recoverable content: I am inclined to call this an experience, but barely—an experiential flicker rather than a full episode.

The tension is sharpest between (4) and (6). Both occur outside normal waking conditions, involve unreliable or false content, and leave questionable memory. Yet I treat the false awakening as genuinely experiential and the hypnotic report as not. Meanwhile, (3) and (5) both involve sleep with motor or cognitive engagement, but I sharply distinguish them. Is there a coherent principle—perhaps involving first-person reportability, integration with memory and reasoning, phenomenological presence, or a specific causal-functional role—that captures all seven? Or must I abandon one? Please also consider what your principle says about further cases I have not mentioned: for instance, a ketamine-induced dissociative state with a structured narrative report upon emergence but no emotional integration or behavioral follow-through, and a "blank" dream reported only after leading questioning with no spontaneous recall. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question. State the principle precisely, assign each clause to the case that motivates it, indicate its verdict on the unconsidered cases, and be explicit about which of my verdicts—if any—it cannot accommodate and therefore forces me to drop.
