Design a 90-minute classroom activity for an upper-level philosophy seminar of about 12 students that stages Galen Strawson's Basic Argument against moral responsibility as an experienced deadlock rather than an abstract regress. Structure it as a role-play or structured deliberation with assigned roles and forced choices: participants should represent successive links in a causal chain that includes not only a decision and its psychological antecedents, but also the normative standards by which that decision is evaluated. Arrange the rules so that whenever a participant claims responsibility for one link, the activity forces them to account for responsibility for the standard or mental state that produced it, generating an infinite regress that the group feels as a practical coordination failure—no one can securely anchor responsibility—before the facilitator names it. Include a clear debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto the premises of the argument, explains what will predictably go wrong on a first run (for instance, students conflating causal explanation with moral excuse, or quietly redefining responsibility in compatibilist terms to escape the deadlock), and explicitly states which feature of Strawson's view the simulation cannot reproduce—namely, the metaphysical thesis that true moral responsibility requires being causa sui, which classroom roles can approximate but never genuinely instantiate. The activity should require no lecture up front; the problem should emerge from the rules and incentives themselves.
