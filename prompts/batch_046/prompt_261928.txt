A recent essay in a general-interest philosophy magazine summarizes Jain anekāntavāda as follows: The Jains hold that reality is irreducibly complex—anekānta—so that no single description can exhaust any object. Their doctrine of syādvāda requires that every assertion be qualified with syāt, conventionally rendered as perhaps: not as a rhetorical hedge, but because contradiction is literally impossible in Jain logic. If one party claims that the soul is eternal and another denies it, both are correct from different standpoints, and rejecting either view is the fundamental error. This makes Jainism the original epistemological pluralism: it rejects both the Hindu absolutist claim of a single universal Ātman and the Buddhist denial of all permanence, substituting an infinite mosaic of partial perspectives in which no single standpoint enjoys privilege. From this, the author concludes that Jainism eliminates objective moral judgment altogether—since every action reflects only one limited viewpoint among infinitely many, Jains can never condemn an action outright, only register it as one perspective among others, which is why anekāntavāda anticipates modern standpoint epistemology and undermines any robust claim to moral objectivity.

Something about this account feels both illuminating and suspiciously tidy. Is it a fair summary of the Jain position? Then evaluate whether the essay's conclusion—that anekāntavāda eliminates objective moral judgment and anticipates modern standpoint theory—follows from the corrected account, or whether it depends on the misrepresentations. Where the summary collapses technical distinctions that matter for the argument, explain exactly what is kept apart in classical Jain thought and why the collapse is not innocent.
