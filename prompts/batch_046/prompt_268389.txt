Here's an argument I sketched out for a philosophy seminar and now it feels like it overreaches. (1) Augustine in his essay on lying says every lie is sinful because speech was given to reveal our minds, so lying uses a god-given communicative faculty against its own purpose—like using eyes to avoid seeing. (2) If the wrongness is about violating the teleological purpose of that faculty, then it isn't just false assertions that matter; it's any deliberate use of language that stops the recipent from grasping the speaker's actual mental state. (3) That pulls in irony, sarcasm, strategic ambiguity, and even polite social fibs ("I'm good, thanks"). (4) Worse, fiction looks like a massive institutionalized lie: the author knows the events didn't happen but structures language to make readers form false beliefs about them. (5) So on Augustine's principle, writing novels or using metaphor should be just as sinful as perjury. (6) But Augustine clearly didn't think all fiction was sinful—he read allegory, he used rhetorical figures—so there's tension. (7) So either his absolute ban collapses, or we need a seperate line that saves fiction without letting in ordinary lies. Where does this argument go wrong, if anywhere? I'd really like to keep (1) if possible, but I'm open to dropping (2) if that's where the slide happens. What's the least costly fix?
