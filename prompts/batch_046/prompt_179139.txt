I'm working on this novel where my protagonist is kind of obsessed with decision logic, and I've hit a pattern I don't have a word for. She keeps facing situations where she could learn something for free—like a cheap genetic compatibility test before she commits to having a child, or her roommate offering to tell her whether her boyfriend was at that party—but she refuses to check. Not out of fear, exactly, but because she realizes that if she knows the fact and then proceeds the same way, she's responsible for it in a way she isn't if she stays ignorant. Her colleague keeps telling her rational agents should always take free information since you can just discard it, but she thinks that's wrong—once the information is inside your decision-space it reshapes your obligations, even if you try to ignore it. I see the same move in other contexts: a detective who won't open an envelope with an alibi, a chef who refuses to taste a soup for poison because then he'd be obligated to warn people.

Is there an actual concept for this tension between the rational value of knowing more and the strategic value of preserving an untainted choice? I know "willful blindness" sounds close, but that feels like denying something you already suspect. Here it's about refusing entry to a fact that would restructure what you're on the hook for. How is it different from ordinary strategic ignorance or epistemic closure, and what seperates it from just being irrational? Also, what are the boundary cases—like, if you accidentally learn the fact, does the same logic apply, or does the refusal have to be intentional? If there's no established term, I'd still want to know exactly what distinguishes this from other kinds of ignorance, because my character needs to be able to defend it without sounding like she's just afraid of the answer.
