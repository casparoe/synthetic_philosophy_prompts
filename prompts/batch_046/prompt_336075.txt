I serve as the senior philosophical advisor to a regional health authority's ethics review board, and in our quarterly sessions we are increasingly asked to adjudicate proposals that fall within the domain philosophers have long characterized as repugnant markets—mechanisms that introduce pricing, conditional exchange, or priority bidding into practices traditionally governed by need, civic equality, or non-instrumental reciprocity. The board must render a determination within forty-eight hours on whether a proposed mechanism is permissible, permissible only with structural safeguards, or impermissible because it corrupts the good being allocated. Rather than relying on case-by-case moral intuition, I want to equip the board with a reusable decision procedure that translates the conceptual distinction among distributive unfairness, relational displacement, and constitutive degradation into an ordered checklist that a non-specialist can run under time pressure and without philosophical training.

Please construct that procedure. It should take the form of a sequential audit with explicit questions asked in order, criteria for what counts as a sufficient answer at each stage, stopping rules where the determination is clear, and explicit flagging of the steps that are irreducibly judgment calls about values rather than empirical or institutional checks. The procedure must be able to distinguish goods that are vulnerable in only one layer—for instance, where market distribution is inefficient but does not alter the nature of the good—from goods vulnerable across all three layers. Then apply it to two proposals currently before the board. The first introduces what I will call a reciprocal priority voucher: donor families may make a tax-advantaged contribution that directs their donated organ toward an approved research consortium rather than the standard clinical waitlist; there is no direct payment to the family, but the contribution creates an explicit linkage between financial allocation and the pathway of the biological gift. The second permits private hospitals to implement dynamic surge pricing for intensive care unit beds during declared public-health emergencies, defended as a rationing device when state capacity is exceeded. For each application, show where the procedure yields a determinate answer, where it forces an explicit value judgment that must be signed off by the board chair, and where it produces a result that conflicts with widespread moral intuition—permitting what many would condemn or forbidding what many would accept. Finally, identify two classes of proposal for which this procedure is not designed to adjudicate, explaining precisely which layer or assumption fails to apply, and conclude by stating the procedure's most significant limitation: the kind of corruption it can reliably detect versus the kind it can only gesture at without resolving.
