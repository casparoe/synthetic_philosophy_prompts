Design a branching taxonomy—structured as a decision tree—for a graduate philosophy-of-mind seminar on whether pain is necessarily bad, using the clinical phenomenon of pain asymbolia (subjects who report intact nociceptive experience but deny unpleasantness or distress) as the central diagnostic case. Build the tree from logical space rather than from a list of named theorists: begin with the question of whether the asymbolic subject genuinely experiences pain, splitting on sensory-discriminative presence (S) versus mere reflexive nociception. From the S-present branch, split on two conceptually independent dimensions—affective valence (A) and motivational salience (M)—treating "affective nullity" and "motivational decoupling" as separate failures rather than a single deficit, then cross each with whether badness is essential to pain or merely contingent upon these dimensions. Required leaves must include: (1) Essentialist Affectivism, which holds that missing affect means missing pain and therefore denies that asymbolia involves real pain; (2) Representational Evaluativism, which treats pain as representing bodily damage and takes badness as an evaluative overlay that can be absent without dissolving the experience; (3) Motivational Dispositionalism, which grounds badness in the disposition to avoid and allows decoupling; (4) the unnamed combination in which S, A, and M are all present but the subject still denies badness—diagnose whether this cell is incoherent, unstable, or merely unexplored; and (5) a skeptical or eliminativist leaf that rejects the coherence of "pain that is not bad" altogether. For every leaf, provide the position name or "unnamed," and exactly one sentence describing its most awkward commitment (for example, having to maintain that a patient who withdraws and vocalizes in response to noxious heat feels no negative quality whatsoever). Explicitly flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot cleanly separate—particularly whether Essentialist Affectivism and certain Evaluativist positions make identical predictions about asymbolic behavior. Phrase every internal node as a concrete claim about the asymbolic case that a student could answer without already knowing the philosophical label it leads to. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and write for an audience with no web access.
