I'm currently studying Renaissance humanism in comparison with contemporary moral anthropology, and I recently came across an essay that summarizes Montaigne's "Of Cannibals" through the lens of what it calls "radical ethnographic incommensurability." According to the summary, Montaigne treats the Tupinambá primarily as a rhetorical mirror rather than as an actual people with historical agency; he claims their society operates without private property, judicial torture, or any structured hierarchy that Europeans could recognize; he holds that their ritual cannibalism is morally superior to European religious warfare because it is open, honest, and devoid of hypocrisy; and he concludes that European moral categories—barbarism, cruelty, virtue—simply have no purchase across cultural boundaries, yielding a thesis of total cultural relativism. The author draws from this the stronger conclusion that Montaigne's essay dissolves any belief in universal human nature or cross-cultural moral judgment into a skeptical void in which evaluation collapses entirely into local custom.

I find the reading persuasive in parts, yet I also have the impression that Montaigne appeals to nature and reason in ways that sit uneasily with full-blown relativism, and that his irony often creates distance rather than straightforward endorsement. Could you help me audit this characterization? In particular, I would like to know which elements a careful reader of the essay would accept as accurate, which involve subtle conflations—perhaps between describing a society's absence of certain vices and endorsing its specific practices, or between criticizing European arrogance and denying all universal standards—and whether the final skeptical conclusion actually follows from the corrected premises.
