I was discussing the problem of dirty hands in political ethics with a colleague who offered a synthetic reading of Walzer and Weber. Before I incorporate her account into a graduate seminar, I want to be sure I understand where it is faithful and where it slips. Here is her summary, reconstructed from our conversation:

'The dirty-hands problem, as Walzer frames it and Weber anticipates, turns on a justificatory asymmetry built into public office. The political actor can invoke collective ends—security, stability, the common good—that are structurally unavailable to private persons, and these reasons do more than excuse immoral conduct: they reclassify it as obligatory. Weber's distinction between an ethic of conviction and an ethic of responsibility captures this precisely. The leader is accountable solely for outcomes; the intrinsic character of the means drops out of moral evaluation. So when a leader orders an act that would be murder or deceit in private life, she does not violate a norm that is then overridden. The act is relocated outside ordinary morality altogether. Walzer's specific contribution is to diagnose remorse in such cases as a category mistake—an inappropriate application of private moral evaluation to a public decision. The proper stance is not guilt but a sober acceptance of outcome-responsibility, which means the politician's hands remain clean in any meaningful sense.'

Please audit this characterization carefully. Which sentences accurately capture Walzer's claim that political agents sometimes do wrong for the sake of right, and his insistence that such wrongs leave a genuine moral remainder? Where does the summary conflate Walzer with a straightforward Machiavellian or consequentialist exemption—particularly in its claim that the means are relocated outside morality rather than violated within it? Is it fair to attribute to Weber the view that intrinsic moral evaluation of the means 'drops out,' or does his ethic of responsibility preserve a tension between means and ends that this summary erases? Does Walzer really treat remorse as a category error, or does he in fact treat it as an appropriate, even necessary, response? Finally, assess whether the colleague's notion of 'justificatory asymmetry' illuminates the structure of role morality, or whether it obscures the distinction between having a justification and being free of moral cost.
