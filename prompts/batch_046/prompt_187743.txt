I'm working through Quine's account of radical translation with a small reading group that has a strong AI background, and we've been leaning heavily on an analogy I want to audit before I accept its conclusions. The comparison runs like this: the radical translator confronting "gavagai" — unable to decide among rabbit, undetached rabbit part, or temporal rabbit-stage without importing analytical hypotheses — is structurally identical to an inverse-reinforcement-learning system trying to recover a true reward function from observed state-action trajectories. In both cases, the behavioral evidence radically underdetermines the target; both require prior commitments (a grammar, a model class, a principle of charity) to break symmetry; and both invite a modern Bayesian response — pick the simplest prior consistent with the data and treat the residual ambiguity as an engineering problem rather than a philosophical crisis. Several people in the group are using this to argue that Quine's indeterminacy thesis is essentially a pre-computational version of ordinary statistical underdetermination, and that once you have well-specified priors there's no genuinely semantic residue left — only specification gaming in the space of possible manuals.

I'm suspicious the analogy is carrying the argument. Could you lay out the correspondence table explicitly — what plays the role of the linguist, the native speaker, the analytical hypotheses, the principle of charity, and the "true" meaning being recovered — then identify the fractures? In particular, does the ML framing quietly presuppose that there is a fact of the matter about the reward function that the algorithm approximates, an assumption Quine rejects for translation manuals? Does it conflate epistemic underdetermination (we don't yet know which manual is correct) with Quine's stronger claim that there is no fact of the matter about correctness even with all possible behavioral evidence? And if those disanalogies are serious, does the conclusion survive — that Quine's problem is dissolved once we have Bayesian machinery — or does the analogy collapse and leave the philosophical problem intact? If the analogy holds only in a restricted form, suggest what a better comparison would be, ideally one that doesn't smuggle in a hidden ground truth we're trying to approximate.
