I'm taking a course on political economy and recently argued with a friend about whether famines should be classified as natural disasters or as political crimes. Amartya Sen's entitlement framework—developed partly through the Bengal famine of 1943, with parallels drawn to the Irish famine and later analyses of the Great Leap Forward—seems to establish that starvation can occur without any aggregate food shortage, driven instead by entitlement collapses, market failures, and state policy. Yet economic historians and development economists continue to dispute this framing: some insist that food availability decline played a larger role in specific cases, others argue that labeling a famine a 'crime' requires intentional design that bureaucratic incompetence or systemic incentive structures do not provide, and still others treat Sen's thesis as methodologically unfalsifiable because any famine can be redescribed as a failure of entitlements. The literature has circled these points for decades without convergence.

My working hypothesis is that the stalemate is not primarily empirical—archival data on grain availability and mortality are largely settled—but conceptual and normative, turning on two hidden questions: first, whether a 'political' explanation requires a counterfactual institutional baseline in which the same ecological shock produces no mass starvation, and second, whether attributing moral culpability to a state requires a collective intentionality that bureaucratic systems structurally lack. If that is right, the sides may be answering different questions under the shared label "Was this famine political?"—one asking about causal structure, the other about moral ontology. But I am unsure whether there is also a formal disagreement hiding inside, perhaps about how to model distributional failure under conditions of radical uncertainty, or whether the impasse is simply that the question is ill-posed because 'natural' and 'political' are not mutually exclusive categories at all.

Can you diagnose the structure of this disagreement? I want to know whether it is verbal, substantive, or mixed; whether the sides differ about evidence, about which cases count as decisive data, about the standards of explanation appropriate to macro-historical events, or about starting intuitions—such as whether collective agency can bear criminal responsibility without individual intent—that neither side can fully defend from first principles. If there is a hidden empirical or formal question, name it. If the sides are answering different questions under one name, disentangle them. And tell me honestly what kind of progress—an archival discovery, a formal model of entitlement dynamics, a conceptual distinction, or a change of question—could actually resolve the impasse, or whether none should be expected and why.
