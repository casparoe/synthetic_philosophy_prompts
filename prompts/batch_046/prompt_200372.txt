As a journalist covering science, biography, and narrative ethics, I have been trying to articulate what dramatists owe to people who can no longer correct the record. My intuitions about particular cases feel firm in isolation, yet they resist easy systematization. Here are six judgments I keep returning to.

First, a biopic of a documented physicist invents a romantic affair with a colleague and presents it as the emotional engine behind her theoretical breakthrough; there is no archival evidence for it. I regard this as a clear violation. Second, the same film writes an original classroom monologue for her, articulating views she repeatedly expressed in published essays but never uttered in exactly those words or in that setting; this strikes me as permissible, even illuminating. Third, a historical drama depicts a dead political leader—whose private papers consistently show composure under pressure—in an unrecorded moment of visible cowardice during a crisis. The scene is plausible but unsupported, and I judge it a serious wrong, though somewhat less grave than inventing a private affair. Fourth, a novelist gives a dead artist, who left no account of her final hours, an invented deathbed conversation with her estranged sister intended to provide emotional closure; both figures are real, but the exchange is pure imagination. Here I am uneasy yet not clearly condemning. Fifth, a documentary employs synthetic voice reconstruction to have a dead civil-rights leader speak a sentence that aligns precisely with his documented philosophy but was never recorded; this seems broadly acceptable, though it leaves me faintly unsettled. Sixth, a stage play invents a private argument between two dead scientists about credit for a discovery, attributing to each positions that are speculative but not contradicted by the archive; I find this more defensible than the invented affair, yet more troubling than the reconstructed classroom speech.

What makes these hard to reconcile is that Cases Three and Four both reshape an unverifiable private moment, yet I react more strongly against the cowardice scene than against the deathbed reconciliation. Meanwhile, Cases Two, Five, and Six all involve invention in service of coherence or accessibility, yet my judgments split sharply among them. Is there a coherent principle—perhaps drawing a boundary between public intellectual legacy and private emotional existence, or between falsification and respectful elaboration—that captures all six verdicts? If no single principle can, which of my judgments should I overturn for the sake of consistency, and why? Finally, what would the best available principle predict about a case I have not listed: a museum exhibition using holography to stage an interaction between a dead inventor and his child apprentice, depicting a pedagogical method for which there is indirect but inconclusive archival evidence?
