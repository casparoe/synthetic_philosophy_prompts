I am a retired physicist who, since leaving the laboratory, has spent several years studying early modern philosophy. Next month I have been asked to deliver the opening remarks at a workshop on scientific collaboration and moral agency, attended by philosophers of science, practicing researchers, and graduate students. I want the address to take Leibniz's moral metaphysics seriously, not as historical ornament. Specifically, I want to build the speech around his claim that justice is charity guided by wisdom and that moral worth belongs to the internal perfection of a rational soul rather than to any external effect. The philosophical idea must structure what I say: it should determine whether I can praise institutional mechanisms like peer review and distributed authorship as substitutes for individual virtue, or whether such mechanisms violate Leibniz's core conviction that only simple, rational substances can be genuine moral agents. I want to explore a tension that is rarely discussed: if modern science replaces personal moral orientation with impersonal structures, does that constitute a collective form of Leibnizian charity, or does it eliminate the very locus of moral worth? The audience should follow without specialist knowledge of monadology, but philosophers present should recognize that I am testing Leibniz's system against the realities of contemporary research rather than illustrating it. Please write this address in about ten minutes of spoken text. Keep the tone formal, personally reflective, and respectful of both scientific and philosophical audiences. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations to specific editions. Do not put any words in quotation marks that come from actual texts unless you have verified them against a source; paraphrase freely instead. The speech should feel like it comes from someone who discovered philosophy late and is trying to think with it, not merely about it.
