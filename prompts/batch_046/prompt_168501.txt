I'm a PhD student in philosophy of science, working at the intersection of conceptual change and the evaluation of artificial systems. Kuhn's account of paradigm shifts and incommensurability is usually treated through historical cases—Copernican astronomy, quantum mechanics, chemistry's oxygen revolution—but I'm trying to apply it to a contemporary disciplinary transition: the move from behavioral benchmarking (accuracy, loss curves, leaderboards) to mechanistic interpretability (circuit tracing, feature visualization, causal intervention on internal representations) as the dominant framework for assessing what a model knows and whether it is safe to deploy.

Within this applied space, several open questions seem live, but I need to decide where to spend roughly two years of conceptual work given that my background is in formal epistemology and logic, not in sociology of science or lab ethnography. First, there is the question of whether the incommensurability between behavioral and mechanistic paradigms is merely methodological—different tools for the same object—or genuinely conceptual, such that there is no shared metric by which both frameworks can agree on what counts as "understanding" in a model. Second, there is the question of whether Kuhnian "normal science" phases actually occur in AI development, or whether commercial and open-source incentives suppress anomaly accumulation by continuously patching failures rather than allowing them to destabilize the governing framework. Third, there is a formal question about whether "anomaly" can be rigorously defined in engineering contexts where failure modes are graded and distributed rather than discrete and revolutionary. Fourth, there is the meta-question of whether the Kuhnian framework itself is obsolete for fields in which theory and instrumentation co-evolve on timescales of months rather than decades.

Given limited time and no direct access to industry labs or proprietary training logs, which two or three of these lines are actually worth pursuing? I want a dependency-aware prioritization: which questions are upstream of which, such that settling one would restructure the others; which are tractable with philosophical and formal tools rather than requiring empirical access I don't have; and which, despite sounding interesting, are likely dead ends for someone in my position. Defend the ordering with explicit criteria—tractability for a logic-trained philosopher, how much downstream work depends on the answer, and whether resolving it would actually change how scientists or policymakers think about model evaluation. I am not looking for a survey or a reading list; I want a defended research agenda.
