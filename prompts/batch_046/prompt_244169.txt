In meta-philosophical discussions about whether intuitions should constrain theory-building, I keep noticing a particular asymmetry. An author will invoke cross-cultural divergence—say, between Western and East Asian respondents on questions of free will, moral luck, or the weight of individual agency—to argue that the corresponding philosophical intuition is culturally parochial and therefore unreliable as evidence. Then, in the same work, they will build a positive argument on a different intuition—often resistance to plugging into the experience-machine scenario, or the asymmetry between pushing and switching in trolley-style cases—treating it as if it were stable across populations, without citing any cross-cultural data for that second intuition and without explaining why the debunking standard applies selectively. The asymmetry is not always explicit hypocrisy; sometimes the author offers a brief reason for treating the second case differently, but the reason usually collapses into the claim that this intuition is more fundamental or harder to explain away, which is itself contested and often tracks the author's theoretical commitments rather than independent empirical robustness. I have seen this most clearly in debates about well-being: critics of hedonism cite cultural variability in pleasure-attributions to weaken hedonist intuitions, then defend objective-list or desire-fulfillment theories by appealing to a supposedly universal intuition that real-world contact matters, despite the fact that empirical studies of experience-machine judgments across cultures are sparse, methodologically heterogeneous, and rarely invoked in those same arguments.

Is there an established concept—perhaps in the epistemology of disagreement, in philosophy of science, or in the methodology of experimental philosophy—that names this selective deployment of cross-cultural debunking? And if not, can you coin and delimit a term that captures exactly this pattern: using empirical intuition-variance as a defeater against opponent-friendly judgments while insulating one's own favored judgments from the same treatment, where the insulation is defended by appeals to fundamentality or theoretical fit rather than by comparative empirical evidence? I am not looking for a bare label; I want the carving—what counts as an instance, what distinguishes it from legitimate differential weighting of evidence, from plain special pleading, and from motivated reasoning generally, and where its edges lie (for instance, whether it requires explicit citation of cultural studies or can occur through implicit universalist framing).
