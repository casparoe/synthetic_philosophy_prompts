I've been in this weird ecology-ethics reading group for like three months and we're totally stuck on one question, and I want to figure out if we're even arguing about the same thing. The specific case is this: there's a proposal to drain a seasonal marsh to put up solar panels. One side — call them the reducers — say that yes, the marsh hosts a ton of sentient life, mostly insects and amphibians, and a lot of that life suffers from predation, parasitism, starvation, etc. So destroying the habitat kills current individuals but also prevents a much larger number of future individuals from coming into existence and suffering. They do this trophic-welfare accounting thing where they try to sum expected suffering across generations and apply some discount rate for non-identity problems, and they conclude the net welfare effect is probably positive. The other side — the preservationists — say that talking about net welfare of ecosystems is a category mistake, that ecological complexity has intrinsic or relational value that isn't reducible to individual sentient experience, and that destroying the marsh violates a duty of non-interference regardless of thier suffering calculus.

What's frustrating is that both sides agree on all the biological facts, they both agree suffering is bad, and they've both read the same papers on wild animal suffering. Yet nobody's moved an inch in months — it's like a disagreeement that just loops. So my question is: what kind of disagreement is this actually? Is there a hidden empirical question doing the work — maybe about whether small invertebrates have moral status, or about the correct discount rate for future wild populations? Or is it a difference about what counts as a moral reason, like aggregate welfare versus relational/ecological goods? Or are we just staring at bedrock intuitions about naturalness that neither side can argue for? And honestly, given whatever diagnosis you come up with, is there anything — a distinction, a formal result, a reframed case, or even just changing the question — that could actually break the impasse? Or is this just one of those debates where progress shouldn't be expected and we should stop expecting one side to convince the other?
