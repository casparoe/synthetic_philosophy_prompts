Here is an argument I constructed that seems to prove too much. (1) In her report on the Eichmann trial, Arendt claims that Eichmann was not ideologically motivated but was instead thoughtless: he failed to exercise the internal dialogue that she describes in The Human Condition as the foundation of moral judgment. (2) If that failure of internal dialogue genuinely explains how someone could organize mass deportations, then the absence of such dialogue is sufficient to make extreme evil possible. (3) Arendt also argues that modern bureaucratic and administrative life—structured around specialization, rule-following, and the suppression of public action in favor of labor and work—systematically discourages the very practice of sustained internal dialogue. (4) So her framework seems to entail that modern bureaucratic conditions are routinely sufficient for extreme evil. (5) Yet Arendt clearly distinguishes between the specific historical-structural failure she diagnoses in Eichmann and ordinary moral shortcomings; she does not treat every modern administrator as a potential perpetrator of genocide. (6) Therefore either her explanation of Eichmann overgeneralizes, or she must restrict the concept of thoughtlessness to a particular psychological-historical profile that her broader ontology of thinking does not clearly support. (7) This suggests that the banality of evil proves too much: it either collapses the distinction between ordinary bureaucratic life and extreme moral failure, or it requires an ad hoc limitation that undermines its explanatory power.

Where does this argument go wrong? I would prefer to keep premises (1) and (2) if possible, since they reflect what I understand to be her core claim about the relationship between thinking and evil, but I am open to giving up (3) or (4) if the cost is lower.
