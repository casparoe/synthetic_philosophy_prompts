Here is an argument I built for a seminar on Berkeley's master argument, and now I suspect it collapses everything into present solipsism. (1) Berkeley's core move is that I cannot conceive of a physical object existing unperceived, since in conceiving it I make it an object present to my own mind. (2) This relies on a general feature of conception: whatever is genuinely conceived is, in the act of conceiving, present to some mind. (3) Apply this to my own past mental states. Yesterday's headache, if it existed, is now over, so it exists (if at all) only as unperceived by my present consciousness. (4) To genuinely conceive of that headache as it was then requires conceiving it without its being present to my present mind. (5) But by (2), genuinely conceiving it makes it present to my present mind, turning it into a present representation rather than the past state itself. (6) So I can never genuinely conceive of my own past experiences as they actually occurred; the same reasoning applies to your present pain, which I can never conceive except as present to me. (7) Therefore, if the master argument is sound, coherent belief in other minds and in one's own mental history is impossible. (8) But such beliefs seem unavoidable. (9) So there must be a hidden slide—perhaps an ambiguity in "present to mind" between direct acquaintance and intentional directedness. (10) Yet if I allow that distinction, I risk reopening the door to unperceived matter. Which step do I reject, and what does it cost? Can I save belief in the past and other minds without losing Berkeley's case against material objects?
