Question: Bernard Williams distinguishes the integrity objection to utilitarianism from other familiar criticisms, and he illustrates it with the idea that some moral reasonings involve one thought too many. First, explain the integrity objection, being careful to distinguish it from the claim that morality makes excessively demanding requirements. Second, apply the structure of the one-thought-too-many problem to this modified case: Dr. Chen can save five dying patients only by killing one healthy donor, who happens to be her estranged brother. She reasons that saving five lives justifies the killing, that her family obligations weigh against it but are outweighed, and that she is not negatively responsible for the five patients' deaths if she refuses. Analyze whether Williams would regard her reasoning as exhibiting the relevant defect, and clarify how—if at all—the sibling relationship affects the force of the integrity objection here. Finally, explain how the integrity objection relates to Williams's separate discussion of negative responsibility in the Jim-and-the-Indians scenario, noting any important differences.

Student answer: Williams's integrity objection is that utilitarianism forces agents to treat their deepest commitments—ground projects—as just one more value in the calculus. This is different from saying morality asks too much; it is about alienation from one's own reasons. In the Jim-and-the-Indians case, Jim should refuse to kill the one Indian because he would then be negatively responsible for that death, whereas he is not responsible for the deaths the captain causes. This is where the problem of one thought too many appears: Jim might think that saving nineteen lives is worth one death, but that calculation is one thought too many—he should refuse solely on grounds of negative responsibility.

In Dr. Chen's case, her ground project of family loyalty is weakened by the estrangement, so she lacks the integrity-based reason not to kill her brother. She therefore correctly applies the utilitarian calculus: five lives outweigh one, and her family obligation is too weak to block it. She also correctly notes that she is not negatively responsible for the five patients dying of natural causes, only for the brother she kills. So the integrity objection does not apply here—there is no ground project being crushed—while negative responsibility still operates in her favor. The sibling relationship actually disarms the objection, because if she were close to him the ground project would conflict with morality, but since they are estranged there is no conflict. Thus Williams's two arguments remain separate: integrity protects ground projects when they exist, and negative responsibility governs direct causation.
