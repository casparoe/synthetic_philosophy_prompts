I am a doctoral student in philosophy of mind and cognitive science, and I have been asked to deliver the opening remarks at an interdisciplinary workshop convened jointly by a philosophy department and a machine-learning research group. The audience is deliberately mixed: senior philosophers of biology and mind, AI researchers who work on large-scale predictive architectures, and first-year graduate students from neuroscience and computer science who do not yet share a disciplinary vocabulary. I want to use Daniel Dennett's taxonomy of four kinds of minds—Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, and Gregorian creatures—not as a decorative framework but as the structural spine of the address. Specifically, I want the remarks to turn on a conceptual distinction that is often collapsed: Gregorian minds are not merely Popperian minds that have acquired language or culture as an add-on, but minds whose cognitive architecture is genuinely restructured by cultural tools—language, mathematical notation, institutional norms—in a way that opens up new spaces of possible thought rather than merely accelerating selection within an existing representational space. At the same time, the address must navigate two constraints that this distinction generates. First, I must avoid implying that current large-scale predictive models are Gregorian minds, since they lack the normative scaffolding and recursive self-modification characteristic of genuine cultural cognition; but I must also avoid reducing them to mere Skinnerian conditioning engines, because that misrepresents the internal representational dynamics that distinguish them from classic behaviorist systems. Second, I need to address the philosophers without jargon that excludes the engineers, and to address the engineers without making empirical claims about neural architecture or training dynamics that I am not qualified to defend. The speech should be approximately five minutes—around seven hundred words—and should be structured so that the four categories appear as a progressive problem rather than a list: what kinds of minds are the object of our study, what kinds are we in the process of building, and what kind of interdisciplinary humility is required by the gap between them? It should conclude not with a settled answer but with an open question that transforms the taxonomy into a shared workshop agenda. Please do not include citations, references to specific papers, or bibliographic recommendations; the framework is familiar to this audience, and my purpose is conceptual architecture, not scholarly display. Write in a formal but not ceremonial tone—appropriate for a junior scholar addressing senior colleagues across disciplines.
