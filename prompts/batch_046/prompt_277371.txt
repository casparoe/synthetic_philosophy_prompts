Read the film Groundhog Day through Benjamin Franklin's project of moral perfection as laid out in his Autobiography: specifically his list of thirteen virtues—including temperance, silence, order, and resolution—his method of tracking daily failures on a weekly grid in order to master them one at a time, and his candid admission that despite years of practice he never achieved perfect moral order but found the attempt made him a happier and more useful citizen. Does Phil Connors's repetition of February 2nd structurally mirror Franklin's cyclical self-examination, or does the film depend on a model of sudden moral conversion—triggered by romantic love and a single day of sustained altruism—that Franklin's incremental, laborious habit-formation explicitly rejects? I want a reading that takes seriously both the film's narrative mechanics (the loop as a moral laboratory with no external witness) and Franklin's actual practice (the never-completed chart, the virtue pursued for its social utility rather than for salvation), asking where the film dramatizes something Franklin's private accounting misses about the visibility and recognition of virtue, and where Franklin's ironic, lifelong failure exposes the film's redemption arc as a fantasy of moral finitude rather than a realistic psychology of improvement. I am preparing this for a philosophy of character discussion and need a criticism faithful in both directions: one that does not reduce the film to an allegory of self-help, and does not distort Franklin's secular, anti-heroic incrementalism to fit the plot's romantic resolution.
