I'm convening the final session of a philosophy of mind seminar next week, and the discussion has kept circling back to Dennett's attack on the Cartesian theater and his alternative picture of multiple drafts—parallel, competing narratives with no privileged version that gets presented to a central observer. The students are particularly stuck on whether eliminating the theater eliminates the subject, or whether it only distributes the subject across drafts that never converge on a single authoritative story. I want to close the session with a poem that holds this tension open rather than dissolving it into either mysticism or reductionism.

Please write a fourteen-line poem in blank verse with a rhyme scheme of ABAB CDCD EFEF GG, but with the volta deliberately broken: the turn that should arrive around line nine should be interrupted by a line that repeats the opening phrase of line five, as if the same draft has returned without being promoted to final authority. Treat consciousness as an editorial process in which the editor is not outside the drafts but is itself one more competing version—no hidden homunculus, no broadcast to an inner screen, no suggestion that the drafts are eventually unified for a spectator. The final couplet should rhyme stage with page and should leave undecided whether seeing is located in any single draft or precisely in the fact that none can claim the others false, avoiding both a celebration of fragmentation and a sneaky restoration of the theater. It needs to be accurate enough that someone familiar with Dennett's arguments recognizes the view rather than a caricature, yet still work as verse for listeners who have not studied the model, and it should contain no quotations, no references to specific texts, and no request for further reading.
