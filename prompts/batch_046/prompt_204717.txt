As a novelist constructing a multi-generational saga in which ethical understanding is transmitted not through explicit teaching but through contradictory family narratives—stories that embed opposing moral frameworks across generations without resolving them—I find myself drawn to the philosophical debate over moral testimony. Write an essay examining whether beliefs acquired through this mode of intergenerational narrative inheritance suffer the same epistemic deficiencies that critics assign to ordinary moral testimony: dependency on others, a deficit in first-person moral understanding, and vulnerability to uncritical error. In particular, weigh whether the presence of contradiction within the inherited tradition might paradoxically strengthen moral cognition by forcing a synthesizing engagement, or whether it merely compounds the fragility of testimony-based belief. Do not remain agnostic: defend a clear verdict on whether this narrative form of moral acquisition is more defensible, less defensible, or structurally different from direct testimony, and justify your conclusion with care.
