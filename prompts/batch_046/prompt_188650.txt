I have been thinking about the separateness of persons objection to utilitarianism—the claim that aggregating welfare across individuals as if they were parts of a single life erases the fact that people are genuinely distinct. Critics point to cases in which one innocent person is sacrificed for the greater good and say this treats that individual’s suffering as merely one negative entry in a collective ledger. Defenders respond that aggregation is only arithmetic applied to separate lives; adding up benefits does not fuse anyone into a super-person, and the objection confuses a decision rule with an ontological claim. Yet the exchange never seems to advance—critics keep appealing to an intuitive boundary between persons that arithmetic violates, while defenders keep insisting that arithmetic carries no ontology and therefore violates nothing. I suspect the stalemate hides a deeper disagreement about whether moral reasons must be tracked in separate, non-fungible ledgers for each agent—what I might call a moral bookkeeping requirement—versus whether welfare is genuinely interchangeable across persons. Is this dispute verbal, turning on different senses of “separate” or “aggregate”? Or is it substantive, resting on an unargued intuition that agent-relative constraints block interpersonal netting? Or are the two sides answering different questions—one about the ontology of groups, the other about permissible decision procedures—under a single heading? I would like a diagnosis supported by what each side actually says, together with an honest assessment of whether any conceptual distinction—such as separating distributive neutrality from an agent-relative prohibition on trading off lives—could actually move the debate forward, or whether progress is impossible because the disagreement ultimately traces back to starting intuitions that neither party can defend.
