Two members of my philosophy reading group got into a sustained disagreement about Richard Rorty’s antirepresentationalism, and they asked me to report it back for an outside judgment.

Maya argued that if we genuinely reject the idea that beliefs are representations of an independent reality, then scientific disagreement collapses into mere preference among vocabularies. She said that without a shared world that our theories can be more or less accurate about, there is no rational basis for saying that one climate model is better than another—only that different communities prefer different ways of talking.

Jon responded that Rorty never claimed all vocabularies are equal; he allows that some are more useful, more coherent, and better at letting us cope with problems. Jon said that scientific authority comes from practical success and peer solidarity, not from mirroring nature, so Maya was attacking a strawman.

Maya then pressed a specific case: suppose two research groups produce climate projections—one forecasting 2°C warming by 2100 and another forecasting 4°C—using different modeling vocabularies with incompatible parameterizations. If neither model is closer to an independent reality, Maya said, then the only reason to prefer one is sociological or practical, which makes climate science look like politics with equations. She added that even practical success seems to presuppose that our interventions track real causal structures, which sounds representational.

Jon countered that practical success is just causal feedback: the world pushes back against our actions regardless of whether we represent it. He said that when a model’s predictions allow successful engineering or policy outcomes, that is the vocabulary being tested by reality, not being mapped onto it. He insisted this distinction—causal interaction versus mental representation—is exactly what saves science from being arbitrary.

Maya replied that consistent causal feedback is just representational tracking under another name. If a vocabulary reliably allows us to manipulate outcomes, she said, that consistency is evidence the vocabulary is latching onto real patterns, not merely that it is socially useful. She concluded that Jon was smuggling representationalism back in through the side door of coping.

Jon’s final move was that Maya was begging the question: she defined any successful interaction with the world as representational, but Rorty’s whole point is that we can explain success without invoking mental mirroring. He said that if Maya will not allow non-representational explanations of predictive success, then of course antirepresentationalism looks self-defeating—but that is her presupposition, not Rorty’s failure.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move. For each of Maya’s and Jon’s turns, indicate whether the argument is clearly mistaken, mostly right, or somewhere in between, and briefly justify the rating. Then say who is more right overall, and identify what—if anything—both parties are missing in their framing of Rorty’s position.
