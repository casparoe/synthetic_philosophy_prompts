Here's roughly where I've landed on panpsychism, moral status, and cosmic ethics, and I'd like to know whether the package hangs together. (1) I hold a constitutive panpsychism on which fundamental physical entities possess phenomenal qualities, but these qualities are not organized into micro-subjects; macro-consciousness, if it exists, is not an aggregate of micro-subjects but either emergent or fundamental to certain complex structures, with no phenomenal bonding linking micro to macro. (2) I think moral patienthood requires what I call diachronic integration—a unified perspective that persists through time—so purely synchronic or spatially scattered phenomenal properties carry no moral weight. (3) From (1) and (2) it follows that quantum fields and elementary particles have zero moral status, and that distributed computations lacking temporal continuity (for instance, blockchain processes spread across non-interacting servers) also lack moral status regardless of their informational integration. (4) Nevertheless, I think the universe's saturation with unbound phenomenal qualities creates an ethical background condition: it is not suffering, but a kind of value-void or missed value that makes the emergence of unified suffering-subjects especially tragic, since they introduce discrete, localized harm into an otherwise diffuse phenomenal field. (5) I apply Benatar's asymmetry selectively: for unified macro-subjects, non-existence is not better than a suffering-free existence, but for the cosmic background of unbound qualities, existence is not worse than non-existence—so panpsychism alone does not generate an obligation to annihilate matter. (6) Despite (5), I think our primary technological-ethical duty is to prevent the creation of artificial systems possessing diachronic integration that are likely to suffer, not because suffering is asymmetrically worse than non-existence, but because each such system adds a discrete, localized signal of harm to a universe where harm is otherwise only unbound and diffuse.

Is this consistent? Specifically: does (1) undermine the motivation for (2) if macro-subjects aren't built from micro-subjects—i.e., if phenomenal bonding doesn't occur, what grounds diachronic integration in physical systems? Does (4) conflict with (5): if unbound phenomenal existence is not worse than non-existence, in what sense does it constitute a value-void that makes suffering especially tragic? And is there a hidden tension between (3) and (6) regarding distributed artificial systems, or is that only apparent? Order the tensions by severity, identify which commitment is doing the work in each case, and tell me the cheapest way to restore consistency—whether that requires abandoning constitutive panpsychism, revising the asymmetry application, or redefining diachronic integration—without signaling in advance which commitment you think is weakest.
