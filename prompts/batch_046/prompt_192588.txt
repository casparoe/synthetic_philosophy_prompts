For a 90-minute graduate seminar in historical theory with fifteen students and movable seating, design a simulation and reconstruction exercise built around Fernand Braudel’s distinction between geographical time, social time, and the time of events, but reframe it not as a taxonomy to memorize but as an epistemic trap that reconstructionists fall into. Inject the following conceptual twist: because material traces survive unevenly across temporal scales—slow geographical and climatic processes leaving diffuse, statistical signatures, while individual actions and institutional decisions leave dense, named, and narratively legible artifacts—historians face a systematic stratigraphic illusion that biases causal attribution toward event-level agency even when long-duration structures were determinative.

In the simulation phase (40 minutes), divide the class into three clusters of five. The Agents cluster manages a hypothetical Mediterranean port city facing crisis; each round they make discrete policy choices (alliances, allocations, edicts) with full visibility of immediate conditions but zero access to hidden slow variables held by the Geography cluster, such as progressive delta salinization or a shifting rainfall regime. The Structures cluster controls institutional rules (tax codes, trade regulations) that can be altered once per round but take two rounds to take effect. The Geography cluster applies hidden quantitative modifiers to outcomes and may emit ambiguous signals—water-level measurements, yield reports, sediment shifts—without causal framing, and they are forbidden from speaking in narrative terms. Run three rounds, then declare a transformation or collapse. The rules and hidden modifiers should be calibrated so that the outcome is overdetermined by the slow variables, yet the Agents experience it as a sudden, inexplicable failure of their choices.

In the reconstruction phase (25 minutes), dissolve the clusters and form three mixed groups of five. Give each group only an artifact deck encoding the simulation’s outcomes but not its processes: named coin issues, broken treaty fragments, harbor logs with captains’ names, architectural abandonment layers, and soil-core or pollen data cards that are statistical and require interpretation. Each group has twenty minutes to construct a three-minute causal narrative explaining the crisis, attributing it primarily to individual failure, institutional breakdown, or long-term geographical and climatic shift, using only the artifacts as evidence. After the presentations, the full seminar votes on the most convincing account, with the predictable result that the event-level and institutional narratives win because their supporting artifacts are specific and named, while the geographical evidence is treated as background context rather than cause.

The debrief (25 minutes) must map specific simulation moments onto Braudel’s three durations: show how the Agents lived in event-time, the Structures in social time, and the Geography in the longue durée; identify the predictable failure mode, namely that reconstruction groups will dispute which ruler or policy caused the collapse while dismissing soil data as inert setting; explain how to handle the inevitable frustration of the Geography cluster, whose deterministic influence was real but communicatively invisible; and be explicit about which feature of the idea the activity cannot reproduce—specifically, the actual phenomenological weight of centuries-long slowness, which is compressed into minutes, so the facilitator must verbally supply the sense of imperceptible drift that the mechanics can only symbolize. Include a minute-by-minute timeline, a description of the artifact deck with examples of each trace type and how they are weighted, the room layout for cluster separation, and an honest note on what flattens when temporal scale is gamified. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citation requests; ground the design in the mechanics of scale-biased trace survival and the asymmetry of causal visibility.
