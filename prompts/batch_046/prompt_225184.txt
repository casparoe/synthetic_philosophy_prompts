Having recently taken on the curatorial framing for an exhibition on bureaucratic violence, I find myself with unstable intuitions about which explanatory register—situational, cultural, or dispositional—properly applies to different perpetrator cases, and I want to know whether they cohere. Here are my verdicts. For Reserve Police Battalion 101, peer pressure and authority seem sufficient to explain why the majority participated, yet they feel inadequate for the minority who refused without facing punishment; situation explains the pattern but not the variance. For modern remote bureaucrats implementing harmful policies without direct physical presence, I find situational explanations implausible and think institutional culture or prior ideological commitment must do the work. The Milgram obedience studies seem mechanistically similar to Battalion 101, yet I resist treating them as morally or explanatorily equivalent because the temporal embedding and absence of sustained ideological framing change the normative structure. For volunteers who actively sought killing roles, dispositional or deep-cultural explanation feels mandatory, and situational accounts seem almost disrespectful to the specificity of their choice. The hard case is the comparison between Danish police, who largely resisted deportation orders, and other occupied forces who complied: I initially reach for national culture to explain the divergence, but then I worry that if culture explains Denmark, why does situation explain Battalion 101, and whether I am merely toggling explanations to preserve my moral preferences. I have been toying with a repair: perhaps the key is the agent's degree of "normative disembedding"—the extent to which an institutional ideology has replaced rather than merely pressured their prior moral framework. On this view, Battalion 101 members were situationally pressured within an intact framework, Danish police retained an embedded framework that resisted, and remote bureaucrats or ideologues had been fully disembedded. Does this principle capture all my verdicts, or does it require me to drop one? In particular, what does it predict for a case I have not settled: perpetrators who begin with full ideological commitment but later continue from habit and peer pressure once their original conviction has faded? And if the principle fails, which of my five verdicts is the outlier?
