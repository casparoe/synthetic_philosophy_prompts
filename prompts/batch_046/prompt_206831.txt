I work as a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I have been studying Bernard Williams’s integrity objection to utilitarianism—particularly the case in which an agent has one thought too many when weighing whether to save a spouse or a stranger. In the tech-ethics circles I follow, people invoke this same structure to resist purely consequentialist product optimization, yet the argument always stalls in the same way. One camp holds that agents possess ground projects that are non-fungible, and that treating them as entries in a cost-benefit calculus destroys the very integrity that makes commitment possible; the other camp treats integrity as a contingent psychological trait and claims the objection merely dresses up an unargued preference for the status quo in normative language. Both sides appeal to the same intuitions about personal projects, and neither has moved in years. My working hypothesis is that this is not a hidden empirical dispute about moral psychology, but largely a verbal disagreement about whether integrity denotes a normative constraint on reasons or a descriptive feature of human agency, with each side answering a different question under a single label. Is that diagnosis correct, or is there a substantive disagreement—perhaps about which kinds of reasons are authoritative, or about the conditions for practical identity—actually driving the impasse? I want a diagnosis of the disagreement’s structure rather than an adjudication of the sides, supported by what each position actually commits to, together with an honest assessment of whether anything—such as a conceptual distinction, an empirical result about practical reasoning, or a reframing that separates first-personal and third-personal evaluation—could genuinely resolve it or move it forward.
