As a journalist who writes about science and the conceptual frameworks that shape public policy, I have been tracking how philosophers discuss what they call consequentialist 'cluelessness'—not mere uncertainty about outcomes, but a structural inability to predict the long-range effects of institutional choices with any useful precision. Please restate the core thesis of consequentialist cluelessness, particularly as it applies to large-scale institutional design—say, selecting between regulatory regimes whose consequences unfold over centuries—using exclusively the vocabulary of ensemble climate forecasting: model divergence, structural uncertainty, initial-condition chaos, skill decay with lead time, and the distinction between parameter error and model-form error. Map moral value onto whatever forecasting analog makes sense, treat the institutional choice as an intervention in a coupled system, and treat the cluelessness claim as a specific limitation on predictive skill rather than a gap in moral knowledge.

Then provide the residue report: which philosophical claims translate cleanly into forecasting language, which must be distorted—perhaps by treating normative weights as physical state variables or ethical obligations as predictive targets—and which have no counterpart whatsoever in how meteorologists actually reason about deep uncertainty. I am particularly interested in whether the philosophical worry about moral 'unknown unknowns' is identical to structural model error, or whether it designates a separate category of ignorance that forecasting concepts cannot capture, and whether that residue reveals a weakness in the philosophical framing or in the forecasting vocabulary itself.
