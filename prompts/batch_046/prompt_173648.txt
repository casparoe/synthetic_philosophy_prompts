I am preparing a colloquium interview with an unnamed cognitive philosopher who advances what she calls “fragmented Cartesian theaters.” She accepts Dennett’s demolition of a single, centralized Cartesian theater but argues that the brain hosts numerous transient, modality-bound theaters—localized coalitions within visual, auditory, and affective streams—each possessing its own temporary neural “audience” and local “projection” dynamics. Cross-modal integration, on her view, is not achieved through a global workspace or a unified stream of drafts, but through competitive arbitration among these local stages; the felt unity of consciousness is then a post-hoc narrative authored by whichever theater temporarily dominates, not evidence of a central observer. I want a rigorous, progressive sequence of interview questions that presses her on three specific fronts. First, whether each local theater reintroduces the homuncular regress Dennett identified, and precisely what distinguishes her “local audience” from a miniature Cartesian observer that merely pushes the problem downward. Second, how her model accounts for the precise temporal dynamics of binocular rivalry and split-brain disunity—phenomena Dennett explains through editorial revision across drafts—without smuggling in either a global broadcast or an implicit central editor. Third, whether hierarchical predictive-processing architectures, which replace staged presentation with error-minimization, render even a fragmented theater redundant. For each front, the questions should anticipate her standard rejoinders—e.g., that local binding is purely mechanistic, that rivalry reflects inter-theater suppression rather than draft competition, or that predictive coding still requires a “presentation layer”—and include immediate follow-ups that block those deflections rather than allowing her to repeat them. The sequence should conclude with one sharply focused question asking what specific empirical finding—say, from high-resolution prefrontal-parietal dynamics in callosal-lesion patients or from predictive-coding failures during cross-modal binding—would compel her to abandon the fragmented-theater framework entirely in favor of Dennett’s multiple drafts model. Please avoid generic biographical or “how did you get into this” questions, do not request citations or a reading list, and ensure every question turns on the actual mechanics of her position rather than on broad methodological preferences.
