I'm a journalist covering philosophy and the social sciences, and I'm commissioning a satirical document for a feature on how institutional frameworks silently encode metaphysical commitments about the self and society. Write a parody of an academic peer-review evaluation form—specifically the template used by a fictional interdisciplinary journal, The Journal of Comparative Social Ontology—assessing manuscripts that address the relation between person and community across Western liberal and East Asian Confucian traditions. The form should be deadpan, bureaucratic, and approximately 650 words, formatted as numbered sections with rubric fields, checkboxes, and sidebar grading criteria.

The satire should target the familiar binary that pits an atomistic, rights-bearing Western individual against a harmoniously subsumed East Asian collective, and it should inject two genuinely niche conceptual moves: first, the feminist-philosophical notion of relational autonomy, which treats self-governance as socially constituted rather than ontologically prior to relationships; second, Confucian role ethics, in which the self is not merely embedded within but constituted by ritual roles (li) and reciprocal obligations (shu). The form's fields should exaggerate real methodological tensions—include a "Methodological Individualism Audit" that penalizes arguments for presupposing an unencumbered chooser, and a "Communal Consensus Index" that requires every normative claim to carry an implicit ancestral citation—while smuggling in the relational-autonomy position as a third, administratively inconvenient option that the rubric cannot accommodate.

Keep the piece readable without footnotes, bibliographies, or citations of specific works, and avoid naming any living scholar. The humor must come from the form's rigid attempt to quantify ontological commitments: include a "Role-Constitution Disclosure Statement," a checkbox asking whether the author has acknowledged that autonomy is interdependently generated, and a sidebar rubric grading submissions on whether they treat the community as a pre-existing container or as a dynamic field of reciprocal recognition. By the end, a reader should feel that both the individualist and collectivist frameworks have been reduced to compliance check-boxes, and that the relational synthesis—precisely because it refuses both ontologies—fails every institutional metric. Do not invent fake theories; ground every exaggeration in actual positions found in the literature on cross-cultural psychology (the interdependent versus independent self-construal) and comparative ethics, so that a specialist recognizes the satire as fair.
