I am preparing a feature on the afterlife of P.F. Strawson's essay Freedom and Resentment in contemporary institutional ethics, and I want to audit a summary I received during an interview with a philosopher of law. He characterized Strawson's position, and then offered an extension, in the following terms.

Reactive attitudes—resentment, gratitude, forgiveness, and indignation—are not cognitive judgments that an agent exercised a metaphysically free will; rather, they are natural, non-rational emotional dispositions that constitute the fundamental fabric of ordinary interpersonal relations. The traditional free-will debate therefore rests on a false presupposition: that our practices of holding people responsible depend on a prior belief that human agents are uncaused causes of their actions. Because reactive attitudes never contained that metaphysical commitment, showing determinism to be true could not, in principle, undermine them. Moral responsibility, on this account, is not a deep metaphysical property requiring libertarian freedom, but simply the status of being an appropriate target within the reciprocal network of reactive practices. The sole legitimate exception arises when one adopts the objective attitude toward an individual regarded as permanently incapable of reciprocal expectation—severe mental incapacity being the standard case.

The philosopher then proposed what he called reactive governance protocols: in large bureaucratic institutions, administrative sanctions should be redesigned not as instruments of deterrence or retribution, but as institutional analogues of personal reactive attitudes—expressive responses calibrated to the perceived quality of will behind a policy failure, intended to preserve reciprocal expectation between citizens and the state.

From this he concluded that Strawson's argument effectively demonstrates that determinism is ethically irrelevant, and that anyone who continues to treat free will as a live moral problem is clinging to a pre-Strawsonian confusion between causal explanation and the suspension of interpersonal engagement.

The summary sounds authoritative, yet I suspect it conflates several distinctions that careful readers of Strawson preserve. Which elements are faithful to the original essay, which belong to the philosopher's own extension, and which are subtle misrepresentations—particularly with respect to the cognitive content of reactive attitudes, the logical relation between determinism and these practices, and the scope of the objective attitude? Most importantly, does the conclusion that Strawson proves determinism is irrelevant actually survive once the misrepresentations are corrected?
