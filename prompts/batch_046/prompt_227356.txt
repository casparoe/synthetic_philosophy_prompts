I'm a journalist covering philosophy of mind and cognitive science, and I'm preparing a feature on whether the paradox of phenomenal judgment genuinely undermines epiphenomenalism. At a recent workshop I attended, two philosophers—call them Aris and Voss—got into a sustained disagreement. Aris argued that if phenomenal consciousness lacks causal efficacy, then our judgments about what it is like to experience red are produced not by the experience itself but only by underlying neural processes; that severs any tracking relation between judgment and phenomenal property, making our phenomenal reports systematically unreliable, which is implausible given the confidence with which we issue them. Voss replied that this presumes a causal theory of justification that phenomenal judgments do not need: they can be justified by coherence with our broader psychological economy and by the distinctive conceptual apparatus we deploy—phenomenal concepts that are cognitively isolated from physical descriptions—without requiring the phenomenal state to cause the judgment. Aris pushed back that coherence can stabilize false beliefs as easily as true ones, so without a causal link to the phenomenal property there is no reason to think coherence tracks phenomenal reality rather than a neural fiction. Voss then introduced a twist: artificial systems trained to produce phenomenal reports—statements about subjective experience—without any conscious experience can generate reports fully coherent with their other functional states. If coherence alone justified those reports, we should treat the artificial reports as equally justified, which seems wrong; but if we deny coherence justification for artificial reports, we need a reason applying only to conscious beings, and that reason usually appeals to causal interaction with phenomenal properties—precisely what the epiphenomenalist denies. Aris said this only shows coherence is insufficient, not that causal interaction is necessary; perhaps phenomenal judgments are justified by direct acquaintance that does not reduce to causation but still requires the property to exist, and artificial systems simply lack acquaintance altogether. Voss countered that direct acquaintance is either a causal relation in disguise or an unspecified placeholder, and that without explaining how acquaintance justifies judgments differently from coherence, Aris has not escaped the paradox. They asked me to referee. Please go through their exchange step by step: say who is right about which specific point, identify what both are missing, and assess whether Voss's artificial-system twist strengthens her case or backfires. Keep the reasoning accessible to a scientifically literate reader, and treat neither philosopher as a straw figure.
