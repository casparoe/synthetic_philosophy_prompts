I work as a software engineer on distributed build systems, where we model dependency graphs with strict partial orders to avoid circular references and determine compilation order. Lately, outside of work, I have been trying to understand the philosophical literature on metaphysical grounding — specifically, the claim that some facts or entities are fundamental while others depend on them in a non-causal, constitutive way. Among the people I have discussed this with, there is a persistent disagreement about whether grounding is best understood as a single, unified relation analogous to a universal dependency graph with unique root nodes, or as a heterogeneous family of relations that vary by domain — logical, normative, causal — with no guarantee of well-foundedness or unique foundations. My working hypothesis is that the debate has stalled not because of missing empirical data, but because the two sides are operating with incompatible formal intuitions: one camp treats grounding claims as requiring a kind of build-system determinism where every non-fundamental fact has an unambiguous compilation path back to fundamentals, while the other treats fundamentality as domain-relative and allows for multiple independent bases or even benign circularities in descriptive structure. I want a diagnosis rather than a survey of positions. Is this disagreement genuinely about the structure of reality, or is it largely verbal — a clash between standards for what counts as a legitimate explanation versus a correlation in our descriptive framework? Do the sides differ about which cases count as data, such as whether mathematical truths, moral properties, or mental states can serve as grounds, or about the evidential standards required to establish a grounding relation? And given that diagnosis, what would actually constitute progress: a formal result showing that grounding relations must be well-founded, a conceptual distinction that separates constitutive dependence from explanatory reduction, or should we conclude that the impasse indicates the original question is ill-posed because fundamentality lacks a stable extension across different ontological domains?
