You serve as an ethics consultant to a state professional licensing board that reviews complaints about practitioner conduct—specifically, allegations that a licensed professional acted with a vice relevant to their role, such as callousness, recklessness, or dishonesty. The board wants evaluation grounded in virtue ethics, judging whether the agent displayed a failure of practical wisdom, compassion, or integrity rather than mere rule-breaking. At the same time, the board accepts the situationist critique: global character traits are weak predictors of isolated behaviors, and situational pressures—time scarcity, hierarchical commands, ambiguous protocols, emotional fatigue—often explain conduct that looks vicious.

Design a reusable, thirty-minute evaluation procedure that a non-philosopher board investigator can apply consistently. The procedure should proceed in ordered stages: first identify the alleged virtue or vice tied to the practitioner’s role-specific obligations; second, screen for situational factors that could produce the behavior without reflecting character; third, apply a calibrated distinction to determine whether the behavior is more plausibly a trait-expression or a situation-response; fourth, specify the conditions that yield a conclusion of “no character failure—remedy the situation,” “possible failure—require structured remediation,” or “likely failure—proceed with sanction”; and fifth, explicitly flag every stage that remains a judgment call rather than a mechanical rule, with a brief explanation of why that judgment cannot be eliminated.

Then apply the procedure to three hypothetical complaints:
(a) A surgeon who dismissed a nurse’s warning during an emergency, where he had worked twenty-eight consecutive hours and the alarm signal was genuinely ambiguous.
(b) A social worker who repeatedly missed mandatory documentation deadlines despite adequate staffing and clear protocols, and whose private messages showed contempt for clients.
(c) A pilot who refused an unlawful dispatcher order to skip a safety check, but did so with extreme rudeness that humiliated the dispatcher in front of colleagues—where the board prefers to reward the refusal but is troubled by the manner.

The procedure must rely only on concepts generally available in philosophical and psychological training, must not request bibliographies or external citations, and must be usable by an investigator without web access.

In your response:
- Separate what is settled in virtue ethics and in situationist psychology from what is genuinely contested—such as whether virtue can be “localized” to professional domains, whether situationism undermines all character-based evaluation, or whether remediation can restore trait-level reliability—and do not present a contested view as the consensus.
- Write in American English.
- End with a single sentence summarizing the core tension the procedure navigates, formatted exactly as the final line: Summary: [your sentence here].
