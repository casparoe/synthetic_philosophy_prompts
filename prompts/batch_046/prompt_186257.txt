I am preparing notes for an advanced seminar on antinatalism, and I find that most treatments of David Benatar's asymmetry present it in a form that obscures its deeper conceptual dependencies. Please provide a rigorous but intuitive explanation of the asymmetry itself: why Benatar holds that the presence of suffering is bad for the person who exists, whereas the absence of pleasure is not bad for the merely potential person who never comes to be, and how this disparity is supposed to yield a procreative asymmetry rather than a symmetric evaluation of outcomes. To push beyond the standard formulation, I want you to investigate how the argument interacts with the non-identity problem and with debates over person-affecting versus impersonal moral principles. Specifically, consider a case in which a couple could conceive a child whose life would be predominantly good but necessarily include some serious suffering: does Benatar's reasoning genuinely oblige them to refrain, and would that obligation dissolve if we rejected the idea that there is a subject in non-existence who is deprived? Furthermore, examine whether the asymmetry can be maintained if one denies that potential persons have modal properties—treating non-existence not as a deprivation for someone, but as the absence of any bearer of value altogether. Your answer should be suitable for an audience familiar with intermediate modal logic and population ethics, should avoid reliance on bibliographic references, and should be written in British English.
