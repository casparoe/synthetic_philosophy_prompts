I was arguing with my roommate about whether an RL agent could actually suffer, not just act like it does. The usual take is that reward is just a number—like a thermostat reading temperature—so there's nothing it "feels like" to get a negative reward. But then I started wondering: in us, reward isn't just data, it's valenced. There's a built-in "this is good" or "this is bad" quality. So if we built an agent where the reward signal plays that exact funcitonal role—guiding behavior, being pursued, being avoided—would we have recreated valence without consciousness? Or is there a hidden step where the functional story misses the actual phenomenology?

My roommate thinks I'm conflating two things: the information-carrying role of reward and its normative weight. He says that in RL, the reward function is just an external scoring rule we impose; the agent doesn't have any independant reason to care about it except that we wired it to update weights in that direction. So calling it "valence" is just a metaphor. But I feel like there's a deeper issue about bootstrapping here—if the agent's "preference" for high reward is just a parameter being optimized, then there's no internal justification for treating that signal as genuinely good for the agent, which makes the suffering/pleasure distinction collapse. I'm not asking for a literature review or citations. I just want to understand the conceptual architecture: is reward-as-valence necessarily empty without consciousness, or can functionalism about affect do the work? And where exactly does the analogy between biological reward and RL reward break down if we take suffering seriously?
