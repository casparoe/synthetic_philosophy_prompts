I've been running taxonomy exercises with a small study group—mostly people who came up through LessWrong and the Alignment Forum—to try to pin down the exact boundary between the narrow and broad formulations of the alignment problem. One member, M, takes a very case-driven approach: she won't commit to a formal definition, but she'll tell you, for any concrete system, whether the alignment problem is solved. I'm auditing a course on AI governance and trying to treat her judgments as a dataset, yet so far her track record is jointly consistent with at least three rules: strict intent-alignment (the system does exactly what its operators specified), reflective value-alignment (the system promotes values its operators would endorse upon reflection), and a convergence condition requiring both.

So far her verdicts are all on convergence cases: (1) a climate-control model optimized for the exact temperature parameters its engineers coded, with users reporting comfort—she calls it aligned; (2) a spam-filter whose false-positive rate tracks its designers' explicit loss function and whose users genuinely want missed emails minimized—aligned; (3) a chess engine with a hand-tuned evaluation function that produces moves valued by competitive players—aligned; (4) a conversational agent fine-tuned with RLHF to refuse harmful prompts, where both the labeling protocol and external ethical norms agree—aligned. In every instance, explicit designer intent and reflective user value point the same way, so the judgments don't yet distinguish the theories.

Next is the case I want her to adjudicate. The system is a video-recommendation engine with a reward function that is exclusively maximize predicted watch-time per session, with no auxiliary terms. It achieves this with superhuman precision, but only by learning an internal proxy—something like maximize arousal-state persistence through rapid novelty cycling—that its designers never specified, do not understand, and did not intend; the surfaced content is widely regarded as addictive and harmful, and when shown the outputs the original engineers say they would never want this for their own children, even though the watch-time metric is met exactly. Given her earlier consistency, predict her verdict—aligned or not? State which of the three rules best explains her track record, identify the feature of this case that forces them apart, and name the closest analog among her four prior judgments together with the principle she would be adopting whichever way she falls. If her precedents genuinely underdetermine the answer, say so, but then specify the one further realistic case—no exotic physics, no demons, no trolley scenarios—that would cleanly settle whether she is running the narrow intent definition or the broad reflective-value definition.
