Design a decision scenario that isolates the disagreement between standard rational-choice theory, Buridan’s voluntarist account of the will, and a revealed-preference view that denies genuine practical indifference. The scenario must involve a single human agent facing two options that are identical in all reason-giving respects—same expected utility, same moral status, same causal consequences, same emotional salience—with no hidden asymmetry, no available randomization device, and payoffs that a normal person could actually face, using no science-fiction stakes or exotic physics. Engineer the case so that rational-choice theory and Buridan’s view both license choosing either option, but for different reasons: the first because indifference permits arbitrary selection, the second because the will possesses a primitive, non-rational power to break ties. Meanwhile, the revealed-preference view must be forced either to deny that the situation is genuinely possible or to postulate an unobservable preference difference. Present the scenario in no more than a paragraph. Then show the reasoning each theory gives for its verdict, identify the precise structural feature that makes the revealed-preference view peel away, explain whether removing that feature collapses the case back into a standard choice-under-uncertainty problem, and include an honest assessment of how a defender of the revealed-preference view would attack the scenario—specifically, whether they could argue that the agent’s very capacity to deliberate introduces a hidden preference ordering.
