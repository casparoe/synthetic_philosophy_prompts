You are delivering the opening remarks at a university workshop on institutional naming, convened because the board of trustees must reach a decision within a month on whether to retain, rename, or contextualize Columbus Hall. The audience is deliberately mixed: historians of Atlantic exploration, moral philosophers working on responsibility and collective judgment, undergraduate representatives, and the trustees themselves, half of whom have no philosophy background. Your remarks should run about ten minutes, and the philosophical framework must do real structural work: distinguish precisely between honorific naming, which expresses a present-tense endorsement of a figure's moral character, and indexical naming, which records only past causal influence, then show why the "man of his time" defense applies—if at all—only to the honorific case and therefore cannot by itself resolve the institutional question. Because the workshop's purpose is to establish conceptual terms of judgment rather than to preempt a vote, you must not recommend retention, removal, or plaque installation; instead, the argument itself should make clear why that restraint is intellectually required rather than merely diplomatic. Treat the "man of his time" defense with genuine philosophical precision—identify exactly where explanatory historical context succeeds and where it fails to provide exculpatory force—while keeping every distinction accessible to the non-specialists in the room. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, citations, or appeals to specific texts; keep all references general and build the speech entirely around the honorific-indexical distinction and its consequences for collective judgment.
