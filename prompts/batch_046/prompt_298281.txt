I am a doctoral student in moral philosophy working on obligations of remembrance after mass atrocity, and I have been trying to reconcile Margalit's claim that solidarity with victims requires active preservation of memory with Rieff's argument that certain forms of forgetting are necessary for both individual recovery and social cohesion. In a recent seminar I presented an argument that seemed to show the duty to remember collapses under its own weight, and I now find the conclusion—namely that public commemorative practice is either impossible or morally harmful—absurdly strong, yet I cannot see which step fails. The argument runs as follows. (1) If solidarity with victims requires remembering the wrong done to them, then any commemorative act must satisfy a fidelity condition: it must preserve the wrong in a form the victim could recognize as genuinely her own experience. (2) Every public representation—whether monument, curated archive, or mediated testimony—translates private trauma into shared symbolic form, and this translation necessarily introduces a hermeneutic gap between the phenomenological quality of the harm and its narrative or material representation. (3) Consequently, every available public commemorative act fails the fidelity condition to some non-trivial degree; it remembers something structurally adjacent to the wrong rather than the wrong itself. (4) If failing the fidelity condition amounts to failing the duty of solidarity, then every public act of remembrance is morally defective—either an unintentional betrayal or an empty ritual. (5) The only representations that avoid this defect are unmediated, first-person retentions of trauma; but as Rieff emphasizes, forcing survivors to maintain such retentions, or demanding that society access them directly, violates autonomy and impedes psychological healing. (6) Therefore, interpreting the duty to remember as a general obligation satisfied through public practice requires us either to do the impossible (achieve perfect fidelity) or to do harm (violate survivor autonomy), which means either the duty is not general, it is not satisfied by public remembrance, or premise (1) must be rejected. I would rather preserve (1) than accept that remembrance is either impossible or harmful, but I want to know what rejecting another premise costs me. Please respond as a numbered list with exactly five items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion: identify the weakest step, explain why it seemed plausible, and price the cost of abandoning it versus abandoning (1).
