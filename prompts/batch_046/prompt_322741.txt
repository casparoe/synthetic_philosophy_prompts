I've been turning over a line of reasoning about how we name mass atrocities—specifically Raphael Lemkin's original concept of genocide and the politics behind the 1948 Convention—and it keeps landing me in a conclusion I can't accept. The argument runs as follows. (1) Lemkin defined genocide as the coordinated destruction of a national, ethnic, or religious group not only through physical killing but through the systematic erasure of its culture, language, and social institutions—techniques like suppressing minority languages, destroying religious structures, and forcing the assimilation of children into another group. (2) The 1948 UN Convention narrowed genocide to physical and biological destruction carried out with specific intent, largely because powerful states wanted to avoid criminal liability for their own assimilationist policies toward minorities. (3) If the legal narrowing was driven by political convenience rather than conceptual refinement, then moral consistency demands that we judge atrocities by Lemkin's broader standard, not by the Convention's truncated one. (4) By that broader standard, any sustained state policy that systematically eliminates a minority's language, religious practice, and communal institutions—mandatory residential schooling in a dominant language, bans on minority-language public education, prohibitions on religious dress or ceremony—counts as destroying the group as such, not merely altering the preferences of its members. (5) Liberal democratic states routinely enact precisely these kinds of integrationist, secular, or civic-unity policies. (6) So if we apply the broad moral category consistently, liberal democracies routinely commit genocide; if we deny that, we must be introducing an arbitrary exemption that protects the powerful and renders the category politically hollow. (7) Therefore the politics of naming atrocities forces a choice: either genocide expands to cover ordinary governance and loses all moral force, or we admit that the naming of atrocities is governed by power rather than by principle.

I find steps (2) and (3) particularly hard to give up, since the drafting history really does seem to show that cultural destruction was excluded for diplomatic protection, not because the concept was incoherent. But step (6) feels like a reductio that must go wrong somewhere. I want to preserve the idea that legal categories are politically shaped and that this shaping matters morally, but I need to identify exactly which premise collapses and what abandoning it costs—whether the flaw lies in equating cultural suppression with group destruction, in overstating what liberal democracies actually do, or in the hidden slide between no physical killing and no specific intent. Which step should I reject, and what does giving it up do to the broader claim that naming an atrocity is never just a neutral description?
