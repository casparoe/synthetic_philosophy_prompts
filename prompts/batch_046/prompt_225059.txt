I serve on the clinical ethics review panel of a large psychiatric hospital. A senior psychiatrist has been referred to us after declining to complete the mandated standardized violence-risk assessment for a long-term outpatient she has treated for over a decade. Her written justification argues that the instrument’s general categories—derived from large-cohort statistical correlations—systematically misrepresent this patient’s specific profile: the patient’s history of reactive aggression is tightly coupled to a particular relational dynamic that has now been stable for years, and applying the generic score would trigger an unnecessary involuntary hold that the psychiatrist considers ethically wrong. The hospital’s policy requires completion of the instrument for all patients meeting broad eligibility criteria and treats refusal as a deviation requiring disciplinary review, though it permits “clinical override” documentation only in cases of documented instrument error, not conceptual disagreement.

The psychiatrist’s defense invokes moral particularism: she claims that the relevant moral reasons in this case—loyalty to a long therapeutic alliance, the specific narrative coherence of the patient’s life, and the contextual inapplicability of population-level correlations—are not captured by the general rule, and that treating the protocol as exceptionless commits a form of ethical generalism that ignores the particular shape of this patient’s situation. The hospital’s compliance office counters that allowing conceptual override would collapse the policy into arbitrariness, since every clinician could invoke “particular reasons” to bypass safeguards.

Please draft the review panel’s written opinion recommending a concrete decision—sustain the disciplinary referral, dismiss it, or impose a structured alternative—together with a clear time frame for implementation. The opinion should treat the psychiatrist’s particularist reasoning seriously rather than dismissing it as mere noncompliance; it should identify the strongest version of the particularist objection; and it should explain whether the panel’s recommended course can accommodate particularist judgment without undermining institutional reliability, or whether it must reject particularism to preserve the policy. The document should be written so that both the psychiatrist and the compliance office could see their positions addressed in good faith.
