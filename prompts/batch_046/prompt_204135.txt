As a novelist, I work daily in the gap between what a character literally utters and what the text actually communicates to a reader. I have been reading about Paul Grice's philosophy of language, particularly his distinction between natural and non-natural meaning, his cooperative principle with its four maxims, and the idea that conversational implicatures are calculable from the assumption that interlocutors are cooperating. I want you to translate this entire framework into the vocabulary I actually use: dialogue, subtext, point of view, dramatic irony, unreliable narration, authorial design, reader inference, narrative economy, and the distinction between a character's intention and an author's intention.

But I want the translation to handle a specific complication that fiction introduces. In ordinary conversation, Grice analyzes a single speaker addressing a single hearer who share the same context. In fiction, the speaker of a line is a character, yet the author constructs that line knowing the reader possesses information the character lacks. This produces what I will call a layered implicature: the character may implicate one thing to an interlocutor within the story world, while the author, through the same sentence and through dramatic irony or narrative framing, implicates something quite different to the reader. Please build this layered structure explicitly into your translation of Grice.

Then provide the residue report. Tell me which parts of Grice's account map cleanly onto narrative craft — for instance, does the maxim of quantity become a principle about how much exposition an author owes the reader? — and which parts require distortion. In particular, address whether Grice's claim that speaker meaning is conceptually prior to sentence meaning survives when the speaker is a fictional construct whose utterances are authored artifacts. Also tell me whether anything essential to fiction's generation of meaning — such as the reader's awareness that every word is doubly determined by character psychology and authorial design — has no counterpart in Grice's model, and whether that absence reveals a limitation in the philosophical framework or merely shows that fiction is an abnormal communicative context.

I am not asking for a bibliography or for citations of Grice's essays; I want an internal audit of the framework's fit with narrative practice, so that I can decide whether my intuition — that writing dialogue is essentially a disciplined form of Gricean implicature — holds up, or whether the comparison collapses at some precise point.
