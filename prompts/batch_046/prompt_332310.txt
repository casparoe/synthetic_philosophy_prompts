I am presenting a seminar next Thursday in our philosophy of cognitive science series, and the topic has become personally urgent because my own research group is now divided between colleagues who model cognition entirely in predictive-coding terms and others who think analog computation has been wrongly dismissed by contemporary neuroscience. The thesis I am defending is a specific, non-standard interpretation of von Neumann's late comparison of neural and electronic computation: I argue that he was not merely observing that the brain contains analog components, but advancing the stronger claim that neural tissue implements a form of error-correcting analog logic in which stochastic and thermal noise are structurally functional rather than epistemically problematic. I call this position "noise-structured analog logic," and I use it to explain rapid one-shot learning and robustness in biological systems, contending that predictive coding mischaracterizes noise as prediction error to be minimized, while standard connectionism mischaracterizes it as weight perturbation to be averaged away.
