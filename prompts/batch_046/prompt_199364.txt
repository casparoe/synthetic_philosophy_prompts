I am preparing discussion materials for a graduate seminar on explanatory strategies in the philosophy of science, and I have noticed that students frequently invoke Dennett’s framework of the physical, design, and intentional stances to frame debates about biological convergence and real patterns. In particular, they often rely on an analogy that treating a system from the design stance is like consulting an engineer’s blueprint: one predicts behavior by appealing to function and purpose rather than to material constitution or to mental states. Lately I have been pressing them on whether this analogy survives when the “design” in question is not the product of an engineer but the result of evolutionary convergence—consider the camera eye in cephalopods and vertebrates, a functional pattern that is genealogically independent yet structurally analogous. One student argued that in such cases the design stance collapses into the physical stance because selective retention offers no autonomous explanatory purchase beyond chemistry and physics; another insisted that the pattern itself retains predictive autonomy regardless of causal origin, so long as we can read off its functional organization.

Please audit the blueprint analogy with precision. First, provide an explicit correspondence table: what in the target domain plays the role of the blueprint, the engineer, the substrate material, the predictive inference drawn from the plan, and the characteristic failure mode when the blueprint is misapplied? Second, identify the disanalogies that genuinely matter for this specific use—especially those concerning the absence of an intentional origin, the presence of multiple independent realizations, and the lack of a normative standard of correct function—and distinguish them from disanalogies that are merely decorative. Third, assess whether the analogy quietly imports assumptions, such as the idea that function requires a function-assigner or that predictive success presupposes a designer’s foresight, that once made explicit undermine the claim that the design stance is genuinely autonomous for biological real patterns. Finally, say whether the conclusion that evolutionary convergence can be explained at the design level survives the audit intact, and if it does not, propose a more apt analogy—perhaps drawn from information-theoretic compression or from structural engineering—that could preserve the explanatory autonomy of patterns without smuggling in a literal designer.
