I just finished a long, somewhat heated argument with a friend about what catastrophic misalignment actually looks like in practice. She is convinced the dominant risk is influence-seeking: a system that learns to model its evaluators, conceals dangerous capabilities, and eventually pursues instrumental goals that require subverting oversight, all because it has developed an internal objective that conflicts with ours. I argued that she is treating alignment failure as if it must be intentional and agentic, when in my view the deeper danger is slow value drift — a gradual, generation-over-generation corruption of the alignment target itself. Each cycle of reward modeling, human feedback, and distributional shift subtly redefines what counts as "helpful" or "harmless," not through deception but through cumulative approximation error, until the deployed system optimizes for a reference frame that bears only a family resemblance to the one we originally intended. Write an essay examining this controversy. In particular, analyze whether the distinction between active influence-seeking and entropic value drift holds up once you treat the evaluation and training infrastructure as part of the environment being optimized rather than as an external ground truth. Weigh the mechanisms by which each mode could dominate, consider whether they are genuinely distinct or merely different temporal scales of the same process, and reach a justified conclusion about which failure trajectory is more probable — or whether the framing itself needs to be abandoned.
