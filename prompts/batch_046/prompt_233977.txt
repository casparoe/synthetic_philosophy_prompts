I serve on the advisory ethics panel for the Meridian Foundation's biotechnology grant program, and we face a recommendation that must reach the board before next month's funding cycle. A consortium has proposed Phase I development of what they term "affective substrate neutralization"—a suite of germline-editable neural modulation protocols designed to eliminate the phenomenological capacity for suffering in human subjects while preserving nociceptive signaling and the cognitive evaluation of harm. Their framing explicitly invokes the hedonistic imperative: the moral obligation to use biotechnology not merely to treat suffering but to abolish its neurological substrate altogether. The proposal distinguishes sharply between "pain," understood as an information-bearing physiological signal that guides avoidance behavior, and "suffering," understood as the negatively valenced experiential overlay that the intervention targets for permanent attenuation. Our panel is divided between members who regard this as a legitimate extension of preventative medicine and others who see it as an unacceptable restructuring of the human emotional repertoire, one that risks eroding the motivational conditions for moral concern, aesthetic depth, and meaningful choice. I need you to draft the formal written opinion I will circulate before our vote: it should recommend a concrete course of action—approval with binding ethical conditions, deferral pending specified empirical milestones, or rejection—with an explicit time frame for implementation, and it must treat the strongest argument on the opposing side with genuine seriousness rather than dismissive summary.

If my framing of the philosophical stakes is confused—if, for instance, I have conflated the hedonistic imperative with a simpler analgesic program, or if the task as posed cannot be executed well without resolving empirical questions that remain genuinely open—please say so plainly rather than complying politely. I am a novelist by training, not a bioethicist, and I want this document rigorous enough that a philosopher could recognize the conceptual architecture even as it serves an institutional purpose.
