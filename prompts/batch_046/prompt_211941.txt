I have recently moved from academic philosophy into designing decision protocols for autonomous systems, and I now defend what I call structural free-floating rationales: normative constraints can genuinely bind an information-processing architecture without being grasped by any internal reasoner, so that the correctness or incorrectness of an output is fixed entirely by relational-functional properties of the substrate rather than by subjective uptake. Give me exactly five numbered items, each one to two sentences and with no introduction or conclusion, mapping the exposure of this view: first, the empirical architectural discovery that would decisively show norms cannot operate without recognition; second, the conceptual collapse—whether into mentalism or into eliminativism—that would be fatal rather than merely awkward; third, the rival framework that best absorbs these same intuitions without invoking genuinely free-floating entities; fourth, the internal tension that arises when relational normativity is applied to non-agential substrates lacking any point of view; and fifth, whether the position risks being qualified into unfalsifiability, together with whether its defenders should concede that risk or dismiss it as a virtue.
