I'm delivering the opening remarks—about eight minutes—at a workshop called "Coordination at Scale," co-hosted by our university's philosophy department and its school of public policy. The room is split: on one side are decision theorists, AI governance researchers, and philosophers of action who will recognize immediately any mention of evidential reasoning across large worlds; on the other are public-health graduate students and community organizers who have never encountered the phrase. I want to welcome both groups without patronizing either, and I want the philosophical idea to do real structural work rather than serve as decoration.

Specifically, I want the speech built around evidential cooperation in large worlds—the idea that in a sufficiently large or structurally duplicated decision space, my reasoning about what to do is not causally isolated; the same arguments are being run elsewhere, so my local choice functions partly as evidence about what those other instances will choose. This should genuinely constrain what I can honestly say about collective responsibility: I should avoid falling back on standard collective-action language about "setting an example" or "pulling together," because evidential cooperation works through correlation of reasoning rather than through causal influence. At the same time, the remarks should leave the non-specialists feeling included, not excluded, and should sound like they come from a senior researcher who takes the idea seriously but is not lecturing. Please write the speech; do not include a bibliography, suggested readings, or citations, since the audience is sitting in front of me and the point is to speak with them, not direct them elsewhere.
