As a retired marine biologist who has spent the last several years studying philosophy—particularly the ethics of future generations and the conditions under which long-term projects retain their meaning—I now serve on my university’s Institutional Legacy and Intergenerational Obligations Committee. We must decide whether to approve the proposed Deep-Time Research Continuity Fund, an endowment that would legally bind the institution for the next two centuries to maintain ecological monitoring stations and an archival repository explicitly designed to transmit scientific knowledge and cultural records to generations not yet born. The proposal’s advocates invoke what philosophers call the afterlife conjecture—the claim that a great deal of what renders our present scientific and cultural work meaningful is its orientation toward a continuing human future—and they argue that endorsing this fund is not mere risk management but an affirmation of the normative conditions that make current research valuable at all.

Yet the university’s risk-assessment office has recently circulated updated modeling, integrating climate trajectories, biosecurity scenarios, and institutional fragility data, that assigns a substantial, though not determinative, probability to severe civilizational disruption or human extinction within that same window. A significant faction on the committee maintains that committing capital and legal obligations to a multi-generational project under such conditions amounts to institutional self-deception: it treats hypothetical future people as guaranteed beneficiaries, diverts resources from present needs with unambiguous claimants such as immediate climate adaptation and student support, and risks turning the university’s scientific mission into a performance of hope rather than a responsible exercise of judgment. Their opponents reply that sustaining long-horizon commitments precisely in the face of probable rupture is what gives present practice its coherence and dignity, and that to withdraw into present-focused allocation is to concede defeat in advance and thereby to undermine the very structure of meaning that makes our current work intelligible as science rather than as mere careerism.

Please draft the formal committee opinion I will circulate before Thursday’s meeting. Recommend one of three concrete courses—approve the fund with its continuity clause intact; approve it only if amended to include an extinction-contingency provision that redirects obligations should irreversible collapse be detected; or reject it in favor of a present-focused allocation—together with reasoning that researchers and administrators could actually respond to rather than treat as abstract speculation. The memo should address whether the value of the proposed commitment is primarily instrumental, depending on the actual existence of future recipients, or constitutive, functioning as a necessary feature of meaningful present practice regardless of outcome. Treat the strongest version of the opposing position with genuine intellectual honesty rather than dismissing it, include a defined time frame for review, and write it as a working decision document rather than an academic exercise—no bibliographies, reading lists, or citations are needed.
