As a retired palaeontologist who has lately turned to the philosophy of history, I wish to commission a formal branching taxonomy — presented as a decision tree rather than a flat list — on the ontological status of the conventional divisions 'the Middle Ages', 'the Renaissance', and 'the Scientific Revolution'. Each node must take the form of a concrete diagnostic question that a reader could answer without already knowing the historiographical labels, and each leaf must identify the corresponding position, whether established or unoccupied.

The root node should ask whether these divisions correspond to natural kinds (analogous to biological species defined by internal essences), to instrumental kinds (arranged for explanatory convenience, like chemical elements prior to their periodic ordering), or to purely nominal constructions. From each branch, split further according to whether the relevant boundaries are marked by internal structural discontinuities, by external causal ruptures, or by retrospective consensus among later scholars.

The tree must include the following named leaves where they exist: essentialist periodism; the Annales-school structuralist view that long-term mentalités transcend conventional boundaries; and radical constructivism, which holds that all three labels are retrospective inventions serving modern self-definition. It must also map two deliberately unoccupied branches, clearly flagged: one that accepts the Renaissance as a genuine epistemic regime but denies that status to the Scientific Revolution, and another corresponding to a view I have tentatively named 'chronometric pluralism' — the claim that period boundaries ought to be indexed to local astronomical alignments rather than universal calendars — which I suspect is incoherent but wish to see precisely located so that its failure can be diagnosed.

Every boundary case used as a diagnostic example should be specific: for instance, whether the fall of Constantinople in 1453 initiates a genuine discontinuity in European intellectual life, or whether the publication of Copernicus's heliocentric treatise in 1543 establishes a new epistemic regime independent of social context. The tone should be deadpan and academic, suitable for a seminar handout, and the response must contain no bibliography, no list of further reading, and no appeals to named authorities beyond those attached to the positions themselves — I am quite capable of misplacing references on my own.
