I work for a regional health-policy team and every other week we recieve briefs that chuck around heritability figures — h² of 0.6 for anxiety, 0.4 for BMI, whatever — to argue either that we should fund genetic screening or that social programmes are pointless because 'it's genetic'. I need a decision procedre I can run on these briefs in about 15 minutes without being a geneticist.

Translate what heritability actually means — population-level, enviroment-relative, not destiny, broad vs narrow sense, the equal-enviroments assumption and where it cracks — into a sequence of checks in order. For each check: the question to ask, what counts as pass or fail, and where the procedre stops with 'reject the brief's reasoning' or hands off to a person because it's a values call, not a fact.

Give me a small set of test claims I can apply, with one worked example for each kind: (1) a claim that uses heritability to dismiss social intervention, (2) a claim that uses it to justify genetic screening, and (3) a claim that gets it roughly right but overreaches on policy.

Then run the full thing on two proposal summaries: one proposing universal polygenic-risk screening for adolescent anxiety based on a twin-study h² of 0.55, and one proposing to cut winter-fuel subsidies for elderly people because 'low BMI heritability means diet is genetic, not economic'. The second should be the case where the procedre gives an answer I won't like — maybe it doesn't fully kill the argument because of a hidden values gap — and say exactly where that happens.

Flag the cases it can't settle, like when the brief correctly reports heritability but quietly assumes gene-enviroment independence in a population where universal schooling has compressed enviromental variance. And be honest about which steps are just judgement calls dressed up as tick-boxes.

Keep it casual, write it in British English, and make it something I can laminate and stick on the wall — no bibliographies, no 'further reading', just the checklist and the worked runs.
