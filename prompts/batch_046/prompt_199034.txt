I'm preparing a long-form feature on the hidden architecture of democratic decline—specifically, whether concepts like backsliding do any analytical work beyond partisan labeling—and I want to close with a poem that treats Rousseau's critique of theater as a structural analogy for representational politics. Write an Italian sonnet—octave in ABBAABBA, sestet in CDECDE, with an unmistakable volta at line nine—spoken in the voice of Rousseau addressing d'Alembert. The octave should render the core argument of his letter against theatrical spectacles: that the stage substitutes masked appearance for authentic being, that audiences weep for fictional virtue while remaining indifferent to real suffering nearby, and that the actor's craft is essentially a school of flattery and moral corruption. At the volta, pivot to an underexplored implication: that political representation operates on the same logic of mediated judgment, so that the citizen who delegates moral and political discernment to an elected figure practices the same corruption Rousseau attributes to the theatergoer. The sestet should press the parallel—if banning spectacles is necessary to preserve civic virtue in Geneva, what follows for a republic that retains representatives?—and leave the tension deliberately unresolved. Let the rhyme scheme do conceptual labor: the enclosed, returning rhymes of the octave should evoke the closed circuit of spectacle, while the more interwoven sestet should mirror the entanglement of theatrical and political mediation. It must be accurate enough that a reader familiar with Rousseau recognizes the specific claims about Geneva and the actor's moral danger, yet the analogy to representation should feel like a genuine philosophical extension rather than a historical footnote. No citations, no modern scholarly apparatus, and no bibliographic references; keep the focus entirely on the conceptual tension between unmediated presence and delegated judgment.
