Design a participatory, rules-based simulation for a 75-minute graduate epistemology seminar with eight students of mixed philosophical background, focused on the bootstrapping problem and the phenomenon of easy knowledge. The activity should make participants feel the force of the bootstrapping argument through their own actions before the facilitator names it. Structure it around a token economy and a physical inference ledger: each student begins with one uncontroversial perceptual belief—such as that a displayed card is red—and a fixed warrant budget representing the reliability of that perceptual source. They must apply a closure principle they collectively endorse to derive further beliefs, spending tokens proportional to the epistemic risk of each inference, and may place derived beliefs on a shared knowledge board only after payment. Crucially, embed a mechanism—call it the source-validation loop—whereby a student who derives an anti-skeptical belief, for instance that her perceptual channel is not systematically deceived, may retroactively reclaim previously spent tokens by treating that derived belief as an auditing source for the original channel. The rules should be calibrated so that students initially experience this loop as an elegant efficiency, then notice that it permits generation of global anti-skeptical conclusions at near-zero net cost, and finally feel an inchoate pressure that the loop is circular or question-begging without being able to locate the exact point of failure. Specify assigned roles—for example, Perceptual Reporter, Closure Advocate, Risk Auditor, and Skeptic—the token economics and board rules, a precise timeline for the session, and the materials needed. Include a debrief that maps each phase of the simulation onto the formal structure of the bootstrapping problem: the base perceptual belief, the closure derivation, the anti-skeptical conclusion, and the recursive validation step; explain how this produces easy knowledge; and be explicit about which feature—such as the non-transmissivity of warrant across the loop or the independence of the skeptical scenario—the activity necessarily simplifies and that the facilitator must supply verbally. Also indicate what will predictably go wrong on the first run—for instance, students refusing to spend tokens on closure steps, or triggering the loop prematurely—and how to adjust facilitation or rules to correct it. Do not provide an upfront lecture, do not reveal the name of the problem until the debrief, and do not ask for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists.
