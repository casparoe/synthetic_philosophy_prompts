In discussions of art forgery, a common analogy holds that preferring an original painting to a molecularly identical copy is like preferring a live musical performance to a perfect studio recording: in both cases the sensory deliverance is the same, but one retains a direct causal link to the originating agent—Vermeer’s hand, the musician’s breath—while the other is a mediated reproduction. The analogy is typically deployed to argue that the so-called aura of the original is merely nostalgia for proximity, and that genuine aesthetic value should be indifferent between original and forgery once perceptual identity is secured.

I want to audit this analogy carefully, but I also want to introduce a distinction that may complicate it: let us call indexical aesthetic value the kind of value an object has in virtue of being the specific historical product of a particular agent’s intentional act, analogous to the epistemic difference between hearing testimony directly from an eyewitness and hearing the same sentence spoken by someone who only read about it—even when the auditory experience is indistinguishable. Apply this framework: lay out the explicit correspondence table between the painting/forgery pair and the live/recorded pair (what plays the role of performer, audience, acoustic space, replay technology, and the causal chain?). Then identify the disanalogies that matter for the intended conclusion—especially the fact that a painting is a static object rather than a temporal event, and that the forger’s own manual labor is present in the copy in a way that differs from the engineer’s mediation of a recording. Which features of the source domain (performance) does the analogy quietly import into the target (painting) that the target lacks? Finally, assess whether the conclusion—that aesthetic evaluation should be blind to origin—survives once we separate purely perceptual value from indexical value, or whether the analogy was doing all the work by presupposing that all artistic value is experiential.
