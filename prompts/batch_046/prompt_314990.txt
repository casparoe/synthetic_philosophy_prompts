I am taking a seminar on moral philosophy and public memory, and I keep returning to a puzzle about how institutions evaluate figures such as Thomas Jefferson, Winston Churchill, and Christopher Columbus. In museums, school curricula, and monument committees, there is a widespread institutional practice—something like a split-register evaluative framework—where a figure’s achievements are narrated in a timeless, universal register (foundational contributions to liberty, democratic leadership, navigational discovery) while their serious moral failings are cordoned off in a historically indexed register, excused as the unavoidable “moral blind spots of the era” or the “limits of their time.” The framework is not merely descriptive; it carries normative weight. Students in standardized history assessments are often penalized for applying modern ethical categories without adequate “contextualization,” yet they are simultaneously expected to condemn the same actions by present-day standards. This produces what looks like an institutional contradiction: we praise the legacy unconditionally and excuse the agent conditionally, using two different moral calendars for the same life.

What philosophical commitments about moral responsibility and historical judgment does this bifocal practice strictly presuppose? Does it require a form of moral relativism in which the content of wrongness varies by epoch, or can it be reconstructed on universalist grounds—say, by treating “being a product of one’s time” as an excusing condition rooted in epistemic limitation or restricted moral alternatives rather than in shifting norms? Does the practice implicitly distinguish between evaluating an act (which might be timelessly wrong) and attributing blame (which tracks what the agent could have recognized and avoided given the conceptual resources available)? Furthermore, is this framework consistent with parallel practices in other domains—such as negligence law, where we generally refuse to excuse a defendant for failing to perceive a risk that was discoverable with reasonable care, regardless of how common the ignorance was in that profession or era—or does it quietly depend on a different theory of responsibility altogether? And if the underlying presupposition were replaced by a uniform moral universalism that denies epoch-relative excuses for actions whose wrongness was, in principle, accessible, what specific parts of the institutional framework—grading rubrics, monument design, curatorial narrative—would have to be rebuilt, and which parts might remain intact?
