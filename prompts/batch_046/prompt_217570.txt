During my metaphysics seminar this term, a fellow student presented a summary of Nāgārjuna's Madhyamaka view that he said he had drawn from a recent popular article on philosophy of religion. According to him, the tetralemma (catuṣkoṭi) operates as a four-valued logical scheme in which any entity is shown to be either existent, non-existent, both, or neither, and that Nāgārjuna uses this to establish śūnyatā—glossed in the article as the absolute non-existence of all phenomena, including emptiness itself. He went on to claim that the two truths, conventional (samvṛti-satya) and ultimate (paramārtha-satya), therefore collapse into one another, since there is no conventional reality left to preserve; conventional discourse is consequently nothing more than a therapeutic fiction employed by the Prāsaṅgika school to confuse interlocutors, lacking any genuine cognitive value. Finally, he concluded that dependent origination (pratītyasamutpāda) should not be read as a substantive philosophical premise but as a purely rhetorical device, so that Madhyamaka is best understood not as a metaphysical position at all but as an anti-rationalist strategy for silencing conceptual proliferation (prapañca), and that treating Nāgārjuna's reasoning as genuine argumentation is therefore a category mistake.

I am inclined to think this is unfair—particularly the collapse of the two truths and the dismissal of dependent origination—but I am not sure where exactly the distortions lie. Could you audit this summary? Specifically: which elements are accurate representations of Madhyamaka doctrine, which are subtle misrepresentations or conflations (for instance, of logical form with metaphysical conclusion, or of one school with another), what a careful defender of Nāgārjuna would object to, and whether the student's final conclusion—that Madhyamaka is not a philosophical view but a purely therapeutic anti-rationalism—actually follows from the corrected account?
