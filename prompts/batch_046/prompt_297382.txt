I'm writing an essay on the philosophical architecture of sex-work regulation, and I'm trying to understand what these institutional designs actually commit us to about autonomy, harm, and the ontology of labor. The three dominant frameworks are familiar: the Nordic model criminalizes purchase but not sale and frames sellers as victims entitled primarily to exit-oriented social services; legalization regimes in Germany and the Netherlands license venues, apply occupational health and zoning rules, and bring the activity under labor codes, yet workers typically remain outside full employment categorization and lack the collective-bargaining protections available in comparable service sectors; and decriminalization in New Zealand removes criminal penalties and subjects the work to standard workplace-safety statutes, though it often remains unrecognized as conventional employment within tax and welfare systems. What puzzles me is a cross-cutting pattern: each regime regulates the conditions of exchange—through criminal law, licensing boards, or health-and-safety protocols—yet none treats the withdrawal of consent inside an ongoing transaction as primarily a labor-rights issue analogous to a factory worker refusing an unsafe task. Instead, refusal is handled either through assault law (if a buyer ignores it), through venue-level health rules (if a specific act is declined), or through private negotiation without institutional backing. At the same time, all three frameworks impose state scrutiny—police interaction, registration or licensing, mandatory health oversight—that exceeds what applies to other intimate or emotional labor such as care work, therapy, or massage.

So my question is this: what implicit theory of consent, autonomy, and harm must each regime hold for this specific combination—intensive state regulation plus denial of full labor status plus exceptional treatment of withdrawn consent—to be coherent? Does legalization presuppose that consent is valid enough to justify occupational regulation but too structurally fragile to justify unionization and employment rights? Does the Nordic model presuppose that consent is impossible in this domain regardless of individual circumstances, and if so, why does it stop at criminalizing demand rather than prohibiting supply altogether? Does decriminalization presuppose that criminal stigma, not economic coercion, is the primary harm to autonomy? Reconstruct the implicit philosophical theory for each framework, distinguish what each strictly requires from what merely happens to fit it, and say whether the three regimes are genuinely incompatible or whether they share a deeper, unstated commitment—perhaps that sexual labor poses a unique threat to autonomy that other forms of bodily or emotional labor do not. I want a committed answer that takes a definite stand on whether any of these regimes actually rests on a coherent view of autonomy and harm, and that explains what would have to change in institutional practice if that underlying view were replaced with a straightforward labor-rights or harm-reduction framework. Don't just map the options for me; argue for the reconstruction you find most defensible and point out where each regime collapses into philosophical incoherence.
