In a seminar on philosophy of science, a colleague proposed the following analogy for Rorty's antirepresentationalism: adopting a scientific theory is like using a navigation app that makes no claim to mirror the actual landscape but simply routes you efficiently; if you switch from one app to another, the terrain itself has not been misrepresented—only your path has changed. She applied this to the historical shift from phlogiston chemistry to the oxygen theory, arguing that on Rorty's view this was not a case of drawing closer to reality but merely of installing more efficient software, and she concluded that Rorty must therefore treat scientific progress as indistinguishable from arbitrary fashion. Please audit this analogy with care. What in Rorty's account corresponds to the app, the landscape, the user, and the switch? Where does the mapping fail—especially regarding Rorty's insistence that justification is a social, purpose-relative practice rather than an individual consumer choice, and regarding his distinction between coping with the environment and copying its intrinsic nature? Does the analogy quietly import representationalist assumptions by presupposing a pre-given territory that could in principle be correctly mapped? Once these disanalogies are made explicit, does her conclusion—that Rorty collapses progress into fashion—still stand? And if the navigation-app image is inadequate, what alternative comparison—perhaps from craft tradition, linguistic translation, or tool refinement—would more faithfully convey what it means for a vocabulary to be genuinely more useful without being representational?
