The student wrote: "The Grand Inquisitor contends that human beings lack the spiritual fortitude to sustain the freedom Christ offered, and will therefore voluntarily exchange liberty for material security, miraculous certainty, and authoritarian guidance. His critique applies most directly to negative liberty—the absence of external interference—because he treats the burden of autonomous choice as a source of existential anxiety rather than an enabling condition. Although he does not invoke Berlin's terminology, his proposal implicitly substitutes a collective, state-administered form of positive liberty—directing citizens toward a unified rational end of universal happiness—for the disorder of individual self-determination. This reading is supported by the Inquisitor's claim that people crave authority because they cannot bear the responsibility of moral self-legislation. Psychologically, the argument presupposes a model of adaptive preference formation: agents revise their desires downward to match what authority makes available, coming to love the security they initially feared losing. Finally, Dostoevsky uses Ivan's poem to confirm this diagnosis as his own authoritative judgment, showing that modernity's loss of faith has left humanity incapable of the love that alone makes freedom bearable."
