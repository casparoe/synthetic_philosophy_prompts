I serve as a senior program officer at a philanthropic foundation that funds high-risk scientific pilots, and our board has adopted a geometric-rationality mandate: we must allocate our fixed annual budget to maximize the long-run growth rate of the portfolio's aggregate social value, using reasoning analogous to Kelly betting, rather than maximizing expected value. I need a reusable, one-page decision procedure I can execute in roughly thirty minutes per proposal, because I evaluate four to six pilots per quarter and must defend every allocation to a non-technical board.

The procedure must accept four raw inputs that I actually have: the applicant's stated success probability; my field-specific base-rate adjustment; a rough, ordinal-to-cardinal payoff rating (low, moderate, high, transformative) mapped to a scalar for computation; and a correlation coefficient (0 to 1) measuring how much this pilot's failure mode overlaps with failures already present in our portfolio. From these, it must derive a Kelly-style optimal fraction, but with three modifications that standard treatments omit: first, an adversarial-shrinkage factor applied to the applicant's reported probability, calibrated by default to the applicant's institutional incentive to inflate results, which I may override only if I possess direct contradictory evidence; second, a discrete-allocation rule, since capital is released in $50,000 blocks, specifying whether to round the continuous Kelly fraction up or down based on the geometric harm of over- versus under-investment; and third, a correlation penalty that reduces the effective fraction once systemic exposure exceeds a threshold you should define, including the exact condition under which the penalty forces a rejection regardless of standalone attractiveness.

Structure the procedure as an ordered sequence of questions: what to ask first, what counts as a sufficient answer, the arithmetic step, the judgment point where values intrude, and the explicit stop conditions that yield "fund at reduced level," "reject," or "escalate to board." Mark every step that is genuinely procedural versus every step that is a judgment call disguised as arithmetic—especially the mapping from ordinal payoff ratings to cardinal values and the selection of the correlation coefficient.

Then apply the procedure to three proposals: (A) a $180,000 request for a forty-subject pilot study testing whether a standardized mindfulness protocol improves sustained attention in shift workers, with an applicant-claimed 55 percent success rate, a behavioral-intervention base rate of roughly 25 percent, a payoff you should rate as moderate, and near-zero correlation with our existing portfolio; (B) a $500,000 climate-adaptation pilot with a 15 percent success rate, a transformative payoff if successful, and a correlation coefficient of 0.7 with two existing funded projects; and (C) a proposal that the arithmetic recommends funding at a substantial fraction, but whose failure mode involves a specific, severe ethical harm—design the case so that the geometric logic conflicts with precautionary intuition, and show exactly where in the procedure the officer must either override the math or accept an uncomfortable recommendation.

Conclude by stating clearly what the procedure cannot do: where probabilities are structurally ambiguous rather than merely unknown, where correlation structures are too complex to reduce to a single coefficient, and where the board's risk preferences or ethical constraints must supersede geometric optimization.

Write the response in American English, in a formal, practical tone suitable for an internal foundation memo, and do not include bibliographies, citations, or requests for further reading.
