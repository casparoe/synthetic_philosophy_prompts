A standard analogy in comparative federalism contrasts the US constitutional structure with Germany's: the US is often described as a "layer cake" (separate tiers with distinct competences), while Germany is described as a "marble cake" (intermingled powers, shared administration, and cooperative legislation). An interlocutor has recently extended this analogy to argue that the EU's principle of subsidiarity—particularly after the Lisbon Treaty's provisions allowing national parliaments to challenge EU legislation on subsidiarity grounds—naturally produces marble-cake dynamics rather than layered ones. They further claim that Switzerland, with its direct-democratic cantons and inter-cantonal fiscal equalization, is therefore the true outlier: closer to a stack of separate plates, while the US, Germany, and the EU converge on interwoven authority once subsidiarity is taken seriously.

Please audit how far the layer-cake / marble-cake analogy holds across these four polities, using the following sections with approximate lengths:

1. The explicit mapping (about 150 words): lay out what in the baking metaphor corresponds to constitutional competences, fiscal flows, legislative procedures, and democratic accountability in the US, Germany, Switzerland, and the EU. Specify which institutional features of each polity are being mapped onto discrete layers versus intermingled swirls.

2. The disanalogies that matter (about 200 words): identify features the metaphor quietly imports—immobility of layers, uniform texture, absence of democratic veto points, or irrelevance of scale—that these four architectures either share or lack. Pay particular attention to whether the EU's lack of a unified European demos, Switzerland's constitutional double-majority requirement (popular vote plus cantonal majorities), and Germany's Bundesrat representation of state governments fit the marble-cake image or fracture it.

3. Switzerland as alleged outlier (about 150 words): examine whether Swiss cantonal autonomy is genuinely layer-cake-like given inter-cantonal concordats, fiscal equalization, and federal framework legislation, or whether these mechanisms already produce intermingled authority that the culinary metaphor obscures.

4. Survival of the convergence claim (about 150 words): assess whether the argument that subsidiarity pushes all four systems toward marble-cake convergence survives once these disanalogies are explicit. If the analogy collapses or misleads for one or more cases, specify which; if it survives only in qualified form, state the qualification.

5. A sharper structural analogy (about 100 words): propose an alternative comparison—perhaps nested strategic games, ecological niches, or constitutional pluralism—that better captures the interaction between territorial division of powers and democratic legitimation in these four cases, without relying on culinary imagery.

Keep the analysis conceptual and comparative rather than historical. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
