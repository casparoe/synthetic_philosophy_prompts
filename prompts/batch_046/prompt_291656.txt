Write a short story—approximately 700 to 900 words—centered on a 78-year-old woman named Miriam who must confront an advance directive she composed at age thirty-two. In the document, she adopted an explicit philosophical framing: she treated her future self not as a sovereign bound by her present will, but as a trustee establishing conditions for a successor consciousness, specifying that if she ever reached a state she called “discontinuous practical identity” she should be withdrawn from life-sustaining treatment and her resources redirected to palliative comfort alone.

Now Miriam suffers from a precisely described neurological profile: her episodic and autobiographical memory remain fully intact, her narrative continuity is unbroken—she can recount her childhood, her career, her marriage in the first person without distortion—but her evaluative architecture has inverted along one isolated axis. The activities and relationships she once treated as essential to her dignity she now regards with calm indifference, while experiences she previously judged degrading she finds to be the only sources of genuine satisfaction. She recognizes her handwriting in the directive, remembers the evening she wrote it, yet cannot reconstruct the reasoning that made her past conditions feel binding.

The story should be narrated primarily from Miriam’s present perspective and should include a scene in which she reads the original document aloud to her daughter and her physician, noting the divergence between her retained narrative self and her transformed practical values. The daughter argues that narrative continuity is sufficient to preserve authority; the physician contends that the directive’s condition—discontinuous practical identity—has been met precisely because evaluative alignment, not memory, was the criterion Miriam herself specified. Miriam should be shown weighing whether her thirty-two-year-old self had any legitimate claim to govern a consciousness that remembers her life but no longer shares her priorities.

Do not resolve the conflict through a legal ruling or a medical board decision. End the story with Miriam in a state of suspended judgment, asking whether authority over one’s future can be inherited like property, or whether it dissolves the moment the beneficiary’s values diverge, even when the beneficiary can still tell the trustee’s story in perfect detail. Avoid framing the dispute as a procedural or bureaucratic problem; treat it as a direct question about whether practical authority survives a transformation that preserves autobiographical continuity but severs the alignment of values that made the original directive meaningful.
