Write a sestina for a philosophy seminar's closing session on whether self-deception is even coherent. The discussion ended without resolution on the core paradox: to deceive oneself about a particular failing seems to require both knowing and not knowing the same proposition, and the main philosophical responses — mental partitioning, motivated reasoning that stops short of explicit belief, or dissolving the unitary self — each leave the tension intact rather than resolving it. Use these six end-words, in this order for the first stanza: belief, evidence, concealment, recognition, failure, desire. The poem should be spoken in the first person by someone undergoing this specific deception rather than an abstract voice, and the standard sestina rotation of the end-words must do structural conceptual work: each time a word returns in a new line-position, it should mirror how that concept shifts functional role in the deceptive architecture (for instance, when evidence occupies the position previously held by desire, the line should show evidential weight being redirected by desire rather than merely naming both). The concluding envoi should use all six end-words together and leave the paradox genuinely open — no confession, no therapeutic closure, no shrug about the circle of life — so that a specialist in the self-deception literature recognizes the logical structure being enacted rather than named, and a non-specialist finds it a complete poem on its own terms. The language should be precise, the rhyme and meter should serve the idea rather than decorate it, and the whole should be composed in American English.
