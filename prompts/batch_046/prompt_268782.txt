I have been revisiting the debate between Daniel Dennett and Gregg Caruso on whether retributive desert survives a naturalistic understanding of agency, and I am trying to map my own reflective judgments onto concrete scenarios rather than theoretical commitments. Dennett suggests that desert is embedded in our practices of holding agents responsible when they possess the requisite competences, while Caruso contends that such practices are best replaced by a public-health model that abandons backward-looking blame altogether. I find both positions too neat for my intuitions, and I am attempting to see whether there is a principle that organizes my verdicts across the following cases.

Here are my judgments. First, a financially secure professional who meticulously plans an embezzlement scheme purely for the pleasure of deception: I regard punishment as fully deserved, and the absence of need or coercion seems to strengthen rather than weaken the desert claim. Second, an individual raised in severe material deprivation with no exposure to moral education who takes food to survive; they understand that the law prohibits it, but I do not regard punishment as deserved, though I accept that protective containment may be permissible for independent reasons. Third, consider two people with identical neurological and developmental profiles: one receives an early institutional intervention that cultivates impulse control, the other does not, and the latter assaults a stranger in adulthood. I am inclined to say the untreated individual deserves punishment, yet I simultaneously feel that the arbitrary assignment of the intervention undermines the fairness of holding them fully responsible — a tension I cannot resolve. Fourth, an individual who acquires a violent impulse disorder through a brain lesion at age forty, after a lifetime of normal moral agency, and then commits assault: I deny that punishment is deserved, favoring only medical management, but I feel less resistance to this denial than I do in the case of someone born with the same neurological condition, for whom I also deny desert but with a different emotional quality. Fifth, a driver who runs a red light while rushing an estranged child to emergency care, causing injury: I judge punishment undeserved, though compensation for harm remains appropriate. Sixth, a society that institutes preemptive detention based solely on predictive psychological profiling of citizens who have never violated a norm: I regard this as never deserved. Seventh, the same wealthy professional from the first case, but now the act is tax fraud rather than embezzlement, committed with identical motive: I maintain full desert, yet I notice my intuition is slightly less severe, which I find puzzling given that the harm is more diffuse.

Is there a single principle — or a small family of principles — that captures these verdicts without excessive ad hoc adjustment? I have considered framing desert in terms of normative competence combined with the absence of excusing conditions that sever the agent from the moral community, but this struggles with the third case, where competence is partly constituted by an arbitrarily assigned intervention. Another possibility is to distinguish between thin reactive-desert, appropriate to any agent who exercises deliberative capacities in the act, and thick proportional-desert, which requires that those capacities were developed under conditions of fair opportunity; on this view, the untreated twin might have thin but not thick desert. Could such a distinction accommodate all seven cases, and if not, which verdict must be revised? Furthermore, what does the principle imply for a case I have not settled — an artificial system designed with human-equivalent deliberative architecture that commits a harmful act: does desert apply, or does it require biological embodiment? Please provide the principle or candidates precisely stated, with the clause that addresses each case, the verdict on the artificial-system case, and an honest acknowledgment of which of my original judgments it must sacrifice for coherence.
