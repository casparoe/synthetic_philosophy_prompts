As a retired experimental physicist who has recently begun studying formal philosophy and social choice theory, I have become preoccupied with a concept that seems under-discussed in the literature on strategic voting: representational resilience, by which I mean the property of an aggregation rule that preserves the ordinal ranking of outcomes when individual preference reports are perturbed within a locally bounded neighborhood, unless that perturbation crosses a defined magnitude threshold. I want you to draft the joint statement that two opposing camps might produce after a good-faith adversarial collaboration on the normative significance of strategy-proofness, evaluated specifically in relation to this notion.

Name the two camps precisely. Camp One—the Inviolability Camp—holds that the Gibbard-Satterthwaite theorem establishes that, for any deterministic social choice function with at least three alternatives, an unrestricted domain permitting every strict preference ordering, surjectivity onto the set of alternatives, and non-dictatorship, strategy-proofness is impossible; consequently, they treat strategy-proofness as a non-negotiable condition of democratic legitimacy, and they reject any mechanism that permits strategic misrepresentation, regardless of whether it exhibits representational resilience. Camp Two—the Expressive Strategy Camp—holds that the theorem shows only that perfect strategy-proofness is incompatible with unrestricted domains and non-dictatorship, not that strategic behavior is always harmful; they maintain that well-designed mechanisms should tolerate strategic adjustments that function as costly signals of preference intensity, provided the mechanism maintains representational resilience, which they take to be a more realistic and ethically adequate criterion than absolute strategy-proofness.

Your deliverable must contain four parts.

First, a numbered section of agreed claims. These should include precise agreement on: the formal conditions and conclusion of the Gibbard-Satterthwaite theorem (three or more alternatives, unrestricted domain of strict rankings, surjectivity, non-dictatorship, and the resulting impossibility of strategy-proofness); the logical distinction between misrepresenting one's preferences and engaging in strategic sequencing of actions; the empirical observation that strategic considerations arise in actual committees even under rules that nominally require truthful reporting; and the definitional claim that representational resilience, as specified above, is logically independent of strategy-proofness—so that a rule may possess one property without the other.

Second, a disagreements table. Each entry should state the disagreement in neutral wording, followed by Camp One's formulation in language Camp One would accept, and Camp Two's formulation in language Camp Two would accept. Cover at least these three disagreements: (i) whether representational resilience can substitute for strategy-proofness as a condition of democratic legitimacy; (ii) whether strategic expression in voting reveals information about preference intensity that improves aggregate outcomes; (iii) whether the impossibility result retains its full normative force for mechanisms with restricted domains or with disclosure rules that require voters to justify deviations from their stated rankings.

Third, identify the cruxes—sub-questions such that resolving them would move at least one camp. For each crux, present a concrete institutional case that both camps accept as relevant: a three-alternative scientific funding-allocation rule in which voters rank projects, may optionally submit a brief justification for any adjacent-rank swap, and in which aggregation uses a modified Borda count combined with a stability-check clause that freezes the outcome if local perturbations fall below a specified magnitude. For each crux, record Camp One's predicted assessment and Camp Two's predicted assessment, with explicit reference to whether the case satisfies strategy-proofness, representational resilience, or both, and to whether either camp regards the case as democratically legitimate.

Fourth, a brief concluding analysis identifying any disagreements that are merely verbal—particularly around whether "manipulation" denotes any strategic adjustment of one's reported ranking or only welfare-reducing misrepresentation that violates a prior commitment to truthful reporting—and explaining why resolving that verbal issue does or does not affect the substantive dispute between the camps.

Constraints: Do not request bibliographies, citation lists, or reading recommendations. Do not invoke named theorems other than Gibbard-Satterthwaite. Do not quote any passage verbatim from published sources; paraphrase without quotation marks if you refer to standard formulations. Define all specialized terms within the prompt itself so that the argument can be evaluated without external lookup.
