I'm trying to work out what distinguishes biography that genuinely cultivates moral judgment from biography that merely celebrates, explains away, or documents. My intuitions about particular cases are partly consistent but not fully, and I want to see whether a single principle can accommodate them. When I read Plutarch's Life of Cato, I feel genuinely addressed by Cato's republican integrity, and I think this works partly because his failures—his financial irregularities, the political miscalculation of his suicide—are rendered with enough specificity that I can imagine myself facing analogous pressures and repeating his errors. By contrast, a modern hagiographic portrait that scrubs away those same failures feels instructive in name only; it offers a mascot rather than a model, and I learn nothing from it because there is no point at which my own possible agency intersects with the subject's. Yet Augustine's Confessions complicates this: it is spiritually framed, highly selective about sins and conversions, and in many ways self-justifying, but I still feel morally engaged by it—perhaps because the omissions are themselves structurally visible and invite me into the labor of self-examination rather than asking me to admire a finished product. Meanwhile, a rigorously documented, warts-and-all biography of an essentially mediocre bureaucrat seems to fail as moral education not through dishonesty but through absence: there is no aspirational gap, no tension between the subject's choices and my own potential choices, and the narrative collapses into pure information. I am also inclined to think that a biography of a genuinely vicious figure—say, a war criminal—can be morally instructive if it preserves what I might call counterfactual proximity, the reader's access to the decision-points where the subject chose badly and might have chosen otherwise; but if that proximity collapses into sympathetic identification with the subject's rationalizations, the work becomes harmful rather than educational.

Given these verdicts, is there a coherent principle that determines when biography functions as moral education, when it functions as ideology or recruitment, and when it is simply inert? I am particularly interested in whether the decisive factor is the presence of moral failure in the subject (Cato succeeds, hagiography fails, Augustine is ambiguous), the transparency of the biographer's framing (Augustine succeeds despite selectivity, hagiography fails because its framing is concealed), or the structural preservation of the reader's imaginative access to the subject's agency under recognizable conditions—this counterfactual proximity I mentioned. Does an adequate account require that the biographer maintain an aspirational gap that the reader can cross only through her own judgment, and does it require that systemic or contextual explanations never fully absorb the moments of individual choice? And how should this principle treat the case I keep returning to: a meticulously researched biography of a contemporary activist in which every error is explained away by structural forces, leaving the reader with no moment at which she can say, "I, too, might have acted differently," yet in which no falsehood is told? Does this biography fail on the same grounds as the hagiography, or on different ones, and what does that imply for the distinction between explanatory charity and moral insulation?
