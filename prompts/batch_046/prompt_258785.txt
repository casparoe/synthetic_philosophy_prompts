I'm working out my own position on whether beliefs and desires exist as real states, testing it against concrete attribution judgments rather than against named theories. Here are my verdicts. A chess engine evaluating a threat to its queen: no belief, only computation. A dog burying a bone: yes, it genuinely desires to preserve it. A corporation pursuing profit: I hesitate—there is coordinated goal-directed behavior, but I don't think there is any inner want. A sleepwalker brewing and drinking tea: no real desire for tea, only dissociated motor execution. A thermostat maintaining seventy degrees: no want at all, just causal feedback. Yet I feel pulled in opposite directions: the dog acts without conscious reflection, just like the sleepwalker, so if unconsciousness eliminates desire in one case, why not the other? And I deny mental states to the thermostat, but I'm unsure whether to say the same about a reinforcement-learning agent that optimizes solely for reward—its behavior looks structurally closer to the dog's. Is there a unifying principle—perhaps about explanatory indispensability, or about the kind of functional architecture that supports counterfactual reasoning—that captures all five judgments without ad hoc exceptions? I want the principle stated precisely, with the case that motivates each clause, its verdict on the reinforcement-learning agent, and an honest statement of whether I must overturn my intuition about the dog or about the sleepwalker to keep it simple. Then apply it to a case I haven't decided: a large language model that consistently applies a coherent deontological framework across varied moral prompts. Should we say it holds those norms as beliefs, or is that attribution always a useful fiction?
