I am drafting an essay on Whig history in the historiography of science, and I would appreciate comments on its structure and on a conceptual framing I am trying to develop. My central claim is that the standard narrative of the Scientific Revolution treats medieval Aristotelian natural philosophy not as a different research program with its own legitimate questions—about substantial forms, teleology, and the coherence of qualitative change—but as an obstacle that mechanistic science eventually overcame. I want to argue that this framing casts the past as deficient rather than differently oriented, and that it creates what I am calling an "epistemic debt": a failure to acknowledge that present science inherited not just corrected errors but dissolved problems, leaving certain philosophical concerns unaddressed rather than resolved. I plan to use the shift from final causes to efficient causation as a case study, not to defend Aristotelianism, but to show that labeling it "pre-scientific" assumes a teleology of progress that the historical record does not mandate. Specifically, I want to ask whether recognizing this debt requires us to abandon the idea of scientific progress altogether, or only to complicate it with a distinction between solving and dissolving a problem. I also worry that my framing might romanticize the past by treating lost questions as automatically valuable rather than genuinely obsolete. Could you comment on whether the concept of "epistemic debt" holds up philosophically, whether my distinction between dissolving and solving problems is sharp enough to carry the argument, and whether there is a coherent middle position between Whig triumphalism and full-blown relativism that my essay should consider?
