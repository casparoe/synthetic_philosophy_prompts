I am a doctoral student in aesthetics, and in a recent graduate seminar on artistic value we repeatedly encountered the maxim 'separate the art from the artist' whenever discussion turned to creators with well-documented histories of serious moral wrongdoing. The phrase was invoked to defend the continued exhibition and praise of such works, yet I noticed that different interlocutors seemed to be advancing very different claims: one treated it as a psychological reminder not to let biographical knowledge distract from formal analysis; another seemed to propose an ontological independence of the artwork from its maker; and a third appeared to be asserting that moral and aesthetic evaluation belong to entirely non-interacting normative domains.

Could you lay out the distinct precise theses that 'separate the art from the artist' might stand for — specifying, for each reading, what kind of separation is at issue (causal, epistemic, evaluative, or ontological), whose judgement is being separated from what, and whether the claim is trivial, substantive but defensible, or false? In particular, please address whether any of these readings can accommodate what I take to be a hard case: an artwork — for instance, a narrative whose authoritative voice depends on a manipulative relation to the audience that structurally mirrors the author's documented moral behaviour — in which a moral vice is not merely biographically attached but integrally woven into the very feature that generates aesthetic value. For each reading you identify, indicate whether it permits such structural integration of moral flaw into artistic achievement or rules it out, and say which interpretation people invoking the slogan in these debates are most likely to intend. Please present your answer as a menu of disambiguated options with verdicts rather than selecting a single one, and write it in British English.
