As a retired experimental physicist who has recently begun auditing graduate seminars in moral philosophy, I find myself returning to an argument I sketched after reflecting on cross-cultural variation in moral cognition. The claim is not merely that WEIRD populations differ from others in their judgments—this is familiar from work on moral foundations—but that the formal structure of that difference, once articulated, undermines moral objectivity in a way that proves far more than its proponents intend. I want to locate the precise misstep, and I am particularly interested in whether the error is a hidden equivocation, a modal slide, or a genuinely plausible premise that is simply too costly to keep.

Here is the argument, numbered for clarity. (1) Moral objectivity requires that for any genuine moral disagreement, exactly one party is in error, regardless of anyone's epistemic access to that fact. (2) Cross-cultural studies indicate that WEIRD subjects treat harm and fairness as lexically dominant, while many non-WEIRD subjects distribute moral weight across loyalty, authority, and purity in ways not reducible to harm-avoidance; call this difference in moral calibration profiles. (3) These profiles are not strategic performances but stable cognitive defaults, shaped by local social ecology in a manner analogous to phenotypic plasticity—what I have taken to calling moral calibrationism. (4) If objectivity holds, then in a conflict between a WEIRD subject who condemns nepotism and a non-WEIRD subject who defends it as loyalty, exactly one calibration profile is correct and the other represents systematic moral error. (5) Any argument for the superiority of one profile must invoke normative premises—e.g., that harm-avoidance should override loyalty—that are precisely the premises in dispute, so there is no culture-neutral arbitration procedure. (6) Therefore, if objectivity holds, then in every such conflict one culture is objectively wrong, but there exists no possible epistemic pathway, even in principle, to identify which one. (7) A metaphysical property—objective wrongness—that can be instantiated but never, even in principle, assigned to the correct bearer in the very cases that motivate it plays no explanatory or normative role distinguishable from its absence. (8) Hence moral objectivity collapses into a position that asserts widespread, systematically unidentifiable error; but the same reasoning applies to epistemic norms (e.g., whether testimony is a basic source of justification), which also vary cross-culturally, yet we do not treat epistemology as purely relative. (9) So either the argument proves too much—undermining objectivity everywhere—or one of its steps must be rejected.

My instinct is that the slide occurs between (5) and (6), perhaps by conflating the absence of a neutral arbitration procedure with the impossibility of rational adjudication altogether, or between (6) and (7), by treating epistemic inaccessibility as metaphysical emptiness. But I am unsure whether rejecting (5) requires embracing a form of moral intuitionism that is itself culturally parochial, or whether rejecting (7) forces one to accept silent objective properties that do no philosophical work. Which step should I give up, why did it initially appear compelling, and what do the standard positions—moral realism with a response-dependent epistemology, anti-realist expressivism, and a pluralist calibrationist view—each have to pay to reject it?
