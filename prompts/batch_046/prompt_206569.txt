As a novelist drafting a scene in which a seminar on historical objectivity collapses into argument, I need a rigorously structured classroom activity that feels dramatically credible rather than pedagogically tidy. Please design a 90-minute exercise for fourteen doctoral students in a philosophy-of-history workshop, staged as a role-play with rules and assigned roles, that makes them physically experience the failure of Ranke’s aspiration to record events exactly as they occurred—before anyone names Carr’s scepticism about present-minded selection or Hayden White’s claim that narrative form prefigures the historical field. To avoid mere repetition of familiar debates, inject a conceptual twist I am calling narrative gravity: the idea that historical events do not possess stable ontological properties—causal boundaries, significance, even temporal edges—prior to being retrieved by a narrative frame, but rather acquire those features structurally through the demands of the emplotment that selects them. The students should construct a single minor diplomatic signing in 1843 across three successive rounds of documentation—first as an archivist preserving a treaty, then as a diplomat recalling it for state interest, then as a present-day historian ordering it within a larger arc—discovering in each round that the event they are describing has materially changed its properties, not merely its interpretation. Include specific roles with conflicting incentives, rules that forbid any participant from appealing to an untouched original record, and physical materials—index cards, redacted copies, rewritten chronologies—that force the group to negotiate rather than consult. The debrief must map each round precisely onto Ranke’s empirical faith, Carr’s selection by present concern, and White’s tropological prefiguration; it must also be frank that the exercise can only gesture at, not reproduce, the metaphysical claim that events are constituted rather than discovered by narrative, and that this gap must be supplied by the facilitator. Specify what will predictably go wrong on first running—particularly with students who enter believing documents preserve rather than generate reality—and tell me how to handle the moment when the group realises they have destroyed the very object they set out to study. The idea must emerge from what happens in the room, not from anything said in introduction; no lecture should precede the activity, and the names should only appear in the final mapping. Write in formal British English, avoid bibliographies or reading lists, and do not depend on obscure theories outside standard philosophy-of-history curricula.
