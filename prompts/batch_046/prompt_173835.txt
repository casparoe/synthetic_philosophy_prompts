Below is a passage drawn from an English translation of the Zhuangzi published more than a century ago. Once Chuang Tzu dreamed he was a butterfly, flitting about and enjoying itself, with no awareness that it was Chuang Tzu. Suddenly he awoke and was unmistakably Chuang Tzu again, solid and definite. He then reflected that he could not tell whether it was Chuang Tzu who had dreamed he was a butterfly, or whether it was a butterfly now dreaming that it was Chuang Tzu. Between Chuang Tzu and the butterfly there must be some distinction, and this is called the transformation of things.

I am trying to understand the logical structure of the skeptical move here. Is the passage best interpreted as denying that there is any enduring substrate—any minimal, persisting self—across the two states, so that the very idea of asking which state is the real one presupposes a stable point of comparison that Zhuangzi's metaphysics of constant transformation undermines? Or does the argument instead presuppose just such a minimal ego, since without an enduring subject there would be no one to undergo the transformation and therefore no coherent skeptical challenge? In particular, how should we relate this to modern discussions of de se knowledge and whether dream skepticism requires a continuous first-personal perspective to be intelligible?
