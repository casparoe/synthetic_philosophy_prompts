I'm coordinating a small graduate seminar on the sociology of moral norms, and for the past month we have been unable to move past a single disagreement about how moral revolutions actually happen. We have been working with Kwame Anthony Appiah's analysis of dueling and footbinding—where the decisive factor seems to be that these practices gradually became incompatible with a group's code of honor, so that continuing them brought social shame rather than elevated status—and with what our syllabus labels the Bernard Williams thesis on slavery, which treats the end of a norm as occurring primarily when the practice loses its functional place within a broader system of social cooperation, with explicit moral argument serving mainly as retrospective rationalization rather than causal driver.

Our group is split along predictable lines. Some members argue that slavery's abolition fits Appiah's honor model because slaveholding was deeply bound up with planter-class identity and gentlemanly prestige, so that abolition threatened a kind of social death within that status group regardless of economic calculus; others insist it fits Williams's structural model because slave labor became legally untenable and economically redundant, and that honor-talk was merely the vocabulary through which that structural shift was experienced and justified. We have mapped both explanations onto the same historical chronology—the decline of dueling tracking the spread of legal arbitration and middle-class sensibility, abolition tracking both economic transformation and evangelical rhetoric—but no participant has revised their view. My own suspicion is that we are not actually disagreeing about historical causation, but about what the term 'moral revolution' is meant to pick out: Appiah may be describing a micro-sociological shift in status-group semantics, while Williams may be describing a macro-structural shift in institutional viability, and we are treating them as competing explanations of the same event only because they share a common vocabulary of 'norm collapse.' Is this diagnosis correct—are we facing a verbal disagreement disguised as a substantive dispute about honor versus interest—or is there a genuine causal conflict hidden beneath the terminology? Furthermore, if the disagreement is substantive, what form of evidence—comparative archival studies of planter self-conception, cross-national data from societies where slavery ended with or without moral rhetoric, or conceptual analysis of whether honor can operate independently of material interest—would actually settle it, or should we conclude that the two frameworks are observationally equivalent and therefore that the underlying question is ill-posed?
