A philosophy forum post offers the following summary of the debate over epistemic akrasia and higher-order evidence. Epistemic akrasia occurs when an agent believes p on the basis of first-order evidence E, yet also possesses higher-order evidence H indicating that her evaluation of E is likely flawed—say, a documented history of overconfidence in analogous judgments. The post distinguishes an “integrationist” view, which holds that rationality requires a single all-things-considered doxastic attitude formed by aggregating E and H (through weighted averaging or Bayesian self-monitoring), from a “partitionist” view, which permits “level-splitting”: maintaining the first-order belief that p while simultaneously holding the second-order belief that one ought not believe p. According to the post, partitionism avoids incoherence because the second-order attitude targets one’s belief-forming dispositions rather than p itself, and because the two levels serve functionally distinct roles—first-order truth-tracking versus second-order calibration—so they do not rationally interact. The post further claims that integrationism, not partitionism, is vulnerable to an infinite regress: any aggregation rule applied to E and H is itself a belief-forming process open to further higher-order scrutiny, whereas partitionism blocks the regress by insulating the first-order level from second-order revision. How fair is this summary? Assess whether the characterization of integrationism captures the actual aggregation requirement, whether the functional-role defense genuinely answers the standard charge that level-splitting violates rational transparency or enkratic norms, and whether the regress objection is correctly assigned—specifically, does it misunderstand how integrationists treat the meta-level application of their own rules?
