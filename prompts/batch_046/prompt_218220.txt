As a PhD student in philosophy of mind working on embodied predictive processing, I am trying to determine whether the classical heterogeneity problem for hedonism survives—rather than collapses—when translated into the vocabulary of active inference and interoceptive state-estimation. The problem, in brief, is that pleasures appear phenomenologically disparate (the sweetness of a ripe fig, the relief of a resolved proof, the warmth of sunlight on chilled skin), yet hedonists maintain that they all instantiate a single intrinsic quality—positive valence—that makes them good in exactly the same way. Rather than asking for a survey of the literature, I want you to perform a systematic translation followed by a residue report.

First, restate the hedonist thesis that there exists a unified phenomenal property shared by all pleasures, using only the conceptual machinery of the free-energy framework: prediction-error minimization, precision weighting, interoceptive inference, and valence understood as the modulation of expected precision over bodily states. Be precise about what plays the role of (a) the heterogeneous sensory or cognitive inputs, (b) the putative common phenomenal quality, and (c) the normative claim that this quality alone bears intrinsic value. Keep the translation honest—do not smuggle hedonist metaphysics into the computation by treating precision-modulation as intrinsically normative, and do not reduce the phenomenological claim to a mere functional description without noting the cost.

Second, provide the residue report. Identify which parts of the original debate map cleanly onto this framework—for example, whether diverse pleasures can be treated as convergent reductions in interoceptive surprise without loss of the intuition that they feel alike in some respect. Note the distortions required: if treating pleasure as precision-weighting forces us to abandon the idea that pleasure is non-representational, or if the normative judgment that pleasure is the sole good has no functional counterpart in inference machinery, say so explicitly. Flag anything that has no counterpart at all in the target vocabulary—such as a qualitative common currency that is not reducible to statistical parameters, or the phenomenological distinction between liking and wanting if it cannot be mapped onto separate predictive streams without ad hoc adjustments. In particular, consider whether the hedonist claim survives if we treat pleasure not as representational content but as a non-representational mode of presentation in the interoceptive hierarchy, a possibility raised in recent work on embodied prediction but rarely applied to axiology.

Third, conclude with an assessment: does the residue indicate that predictive processing is missing a genuine dimension of affective experience—perhaps a phenomenal homogeneity that is not captured by computational convergence—or does it reveal that the hedonist's common quality was always an artifact of conflating functional similarity with phenomenal identity? I am not asking for citations, a bibliography, or references to specific papers; assume I can locate the relevant literature myself. I want the argument to engage directly with the conceptual tension between phenomenological homogeneity and computational heterogeneity, reaching a defended verdict rather than ending on a non-committal note.
