I'm writing for a philosophy-of-mind seminar on personal identity, specifically working through Douglas Hofstadter's pattern-based account of the self—the idea that an "I" is not tied to a particular brain but to a high-level strange loop of self-reference, and that in principle a sustained, high-fidelity isomorphism between two brains' self-referential structures could allow one pattern of "I"-hood to be partially instantiated across both substrates rather than duplicated in each. My study group dismisses this entire line of thought by repeating the proverb, "You can't be in two places at once," treating it as an obvious, decisive refutation of any distributed-self hypothesis. I suspect the slogan is concealing several different arguments, and I want to see them separated.

Can you lay out the distinct precise claims this proverb might be expressing when applied to consciousness and identity—distinguishing, for example, between a spatiotemporal claim about the location of physical substrates, a phenomenological claim about the indivisible unity of a single stream of consciousness, a causal-functional claim about the closure of one decision loop, a metaphysical claim about the identity conditions of a particular pattern-instance, and a normative claim about the conditions for attributing agency or responsibility? For each reading, say whether it is trivial, whether it is substantive and defensible, whether it genuinely addresses Hofstadter's view that the self is a substrate-independent pattern rather than merely restating the intuition that physical separation feels wrong, and whether, precisely formulated, it actually rules out the possibility that a strange-loop pattern of self-reference could span two brains without being duplicated in each. Please present these as a menu rather than selecting one, indicate which interpretation my interlocutors most likely intend when they use the line to shut down discussion, and say whether that intended reading, once made exact, contradicts the distributed-self scenario or only asserts that it sounds implausible.
