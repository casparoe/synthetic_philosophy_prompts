I am preparing a feature on the moral logic of bringing beings into existence, centered on a cluster of verdicts that orbit the familiar asymmetry between suffering and pleasure with respect to non-existence, but extended into less examined territory. Here are my judgments. First, creating a person who will live a genuinely good life is permissible, but never obligatory. Second, creating a person who will suffer severely is wrong. Third, failing to create a happy person—through childlessness or contraception—is not a harm and requires no justification. Fourth, a genetic screening program that prevents a suffering birth, even if it unavoidably prevents an unrelated happy birth, is still justified. Fifth, however, an irreversible, civilization-wide cessation of reproduction that eliminates both future suffering and future joy strikes me as tragically wasteful, not morally neutral. Sixth, the pattern extends to synthetic minds: engineering an artificial system that genuinely suffers is wrong, but refraining from engineering one that would genuinely flourish is not wrong. I remain unsettled on a seventh case: suppose we could causally substitute, in the very same ontological slot, one potential being destined to suffer with a different potential being destined to flourish, leaving total numbers unchanged. Is there a positive duty to perform the substitution? Affirming one seems to treat the non-existence of the flourishing substitute as a remediable harm, which conflicts with judgment three. Is there a single principle—perhaps a modified asymmetry, or a non-identity-weighted rule—that accommodates judgments one through six and resolves the substitution case? If coherence demands abandoning one verdict, which should go? And what does the best principle predict for voluntary human extinction pursued solely to prevent all future suffering?
