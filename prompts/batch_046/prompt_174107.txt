People regularly compress Maimonides' account of sacrifice into the slogan that God commanded animal offerings only as a concession to Israel's deep-rooted habit of idolatrous worship, lacking any intrinsic worth. I recently heard this used to argue that ritual practices whose origin is transitional should be discarded once society has moved past the need — in this case, to justify abandoning certain modern ritual obligations. Before I evaluate that inference, I need the slogan disambiguated. Can you lay out the distinct precise theses it could be expressing — for example: (1) a narrow historical claim about Israelite psychology at one moment; (2) a pedagogical claim about gradual divine education away from pagan forms; (3) a metaphysical claim that human beings always require embodied, symbolic mediation to approach the divine; (4) a normative claim that transitional institutions automatically lose authority once their transitional purpose is fulfilled; and (5) a comparative claim that all religious ritual originates in concession rather than in positive expression — and for each indicate whether it is trivial, defensible, genuinely Maimonidean, false, or simply not doing the work the reform argument needs? Then specify which of these readings the argument about discarding modern ritual actually depends on, and whether that reading holds without importing an unstated premise about the nature of legal permanence.
