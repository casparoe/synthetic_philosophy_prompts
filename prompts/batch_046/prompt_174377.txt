In philosophical discussions of biology and computation, a recurring analogy treats Darwinian natural selection and Turing computation as parallel cases of competence without comprehension: both are said to produce sophisticated, apparently reason-guided outcomes—adaptive biological form in one case, correct symbolic derivation in the other—without any internal understanding, intentionality, or grasp of meaning. The comparison is often invoked to support the claim that comprehension is not a necessary condition for high-level performance, whether in organisms or in machines. On this mapping, evolutionary variation corresponds to initial tape inscription and state transitions, selection pressure to correctness conditions or halting criteria, heritability to deterministic rule-following, and population-level dynamics over deep time to iterative algorithmic execution. Present this correspondence explicitly, then audit it. Where does the biological process lack the explicit syntactic structure that defines computation? Does evolutionary success constitute a normative evaluation comparable to logical validity, or does the analogy smuggle a teleological vocabulary into biology? Conversely, does mechanized computation genuinely lack all forms of comprehension, or does the architecture of a universal machine implicitly encode a kind of procedural understanding absent from blind selection? Separate the disanalogies that merely differ in scale or substrate from those that undermine the shared conclusion that reasoning can be inverted—achieved from the bottom up without a reasoning subject. Finally, judge whether the philosophical point about the separability of competence and comprehension stands on each flank independently, or whether the parallel between Darwin and Turing was doing essential argumentative work that collapses once the mapping is scrutinized.
