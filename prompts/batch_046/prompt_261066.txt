As a retired natural scientist who has recently begun studying philosophy of law and moral psychology, I find myself puzzled by a particular cluster of institutional practices surrounding capital punishment. In American capital trials, prospective jurors are routinely excluded—death-qualified—if they indicate they could never vote for execution, so that the seated jury is drawn only from those who accept the penalty in principle. Yet in many of the same jurisdictions, a single juror's refusal to recommend death blocks the sentence entirely and yields life imprisonment, even though that same juror might have been excluded during voir dire had their opposition been expressed in more absolute terms. Meanwhile, the legal system invests enormous resources in post-conviction review, including DNA testing, specifically to prevent the execution of an innocent person; but once an execution is carried out and innocence is later demonstrated, the state's response is limited to a symbolic posthumous pardon rather than any structural acknowledgment that an irreversible wrong comparable to wrongful imprisonment has occurred. The public rationales for retaining the practice oscillate between retributive proportionality—execution as the fitting response to the worst crimes—and instrumental deterrence.

What implicit philosophical commitments about moral desert, democratic authority, epistemic fallibility, and the nature of state punishment does this specific cluster presuppose? Reconstruct the theory that makes sense of filtering jurors for willingness to impose death, allowing a lone holdout to veto the sentence, treating wrongful execution as institutionally unrecoverable, and justifying the practice through both desert-based and consequentialist reasoning. Is the underlying view coherent, or does it combine incompatible theories—perhaps a Kantian commitment to treating persons as ends that conflicts with a utilitarian treatment of execution as a social instrument—while also presupposing a peculiar epistemology in which pre-execution doubt must be minimized but post-execution error is treated as beyond institutional repair? Furthermore, if we replaced these commitments with a consistently probabilistic framework—treating capital sentencing like an irreversible scientific experiment in which the evidentiary burden must match the irreversibility of the outcome—what specific institutional features would have to be redesigned?
