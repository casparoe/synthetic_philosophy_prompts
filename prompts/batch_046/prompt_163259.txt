I'm trying to reach reflective equilibrium on what actually counts as corruption, going by my gut on concrete cases rather than on abstract definitions. Here are my verdicts.

Case one: A senior procurement officer awards a construction contract to her brother-in-law's firm. The firm is genuinely well-qualified and submits the lowest compliant bid, but she suppresses the tender documentation to prevent competitors from challenging the award and hides the family relation. I say this is corrupt.

Case two: In a rural health clinic, patients traditionally present the physician with livestock or produce after successful treatment. Refusing the gift is read as a rejection of kinship obligations that underpin community trust in the clinic, and acceptance creates no explicit debt. The doctor accepts. I say this is not corrupt.

Case three: A corporate regional director regularly hosts expensive dinners for regulators and funds a policy institute that employs former officials. No explicit quid pro quo exists, and all published research conclusions are sincerely held by the authors. Nevertheless, ambiguous regulations are consistently interpreted in the firm's favour. I say this is corrupt, but in a structural rather than transactional sense.

Case four: In a bureaucracy where formal merit rules were introduced by a colonial administration and are widely ignored by every other department, a department head hires exclusively from his own ethnic network. The appointees are competent and work diligently; the formal examination system lacks local legitimacy. I say this is not corrupt — violating an illegitimate rule is not a betrayal of a legitimate institutional role.

Case five: A Western NGO refuses to pay so-called facilitation fees that are standard and expected for processing permits in a particular country. The refusal stalls a health project and leaves local staff unpaid. I say refusing is not corrupt; the harm arises from institutional rigidity, not from moral failure on the NGO's part.

The tension I keep returning to is between case one and case four. Both involve favouring kin or network over formal procedure, yet one feels like a clear breach and the other like reasonable adaptation to a broken institutional frame. At the same time, case three feels corrupt despite the absence of personal gain or explicit exchange, whereas case two involves a clear material transfer that feels entirely acceptable.

Is there a single principle — perhaps something about the legitimacy of the institutional frame, the presence of deceptive concealment, or the distinction between reciprocal gift and strategic capture — that delivers all five verdicts? If not, which verdict should I overturn, and why?

Please state your principle precisely, identify which case motivates each clause, apply it to a further case I have not listed — specifically, an AI hiring system trained on historical employment data that reproduces the network-bias pattern of case four without any human agent directing it — and be honest about which of my five verdicts it cannot accommodate.
