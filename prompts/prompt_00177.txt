I've spent a fair amount of time on LessWrong and Overcoming Bias threads about theory choice, and I keep running into the underdetermination of theory by evidence as a kind of background worry that never quite gets resolved. The strong Quine-Duhem version says that any theory can be saved from refuting evidence by adjusting auxiliary hypotheses, while the more specific "contrastive" cases -- Bohmian mechanics vs. standard collapse QM, or rival interpretations of the same formalism more generally -- present us with empirically equivalent rivals that no experiment could ever distinguish. Given that Bayesian updating is supposed to be the rationalist's standard tool for resolving disagreements about which theory is true, I'd like to understand what work is actually left for priors, simplicity considerations (Solomonoff-style or otherwise), and things like Occam's razor once we take underdetermination seriously.

Could you lay out the most important implications of underdetermination of theory by evidence for how someone trying to reason like a good Bayesian should actually assign and update credences among empirically equivalent or near-equivalent theories? I'm especially interested in whether underdetermination poses a problem that goes beyond ordinary uncertainty -- i.e., whether it means our credences in such cases are in some sense arbitrary or merely conventional rather than tracking truth -- and what practical difference this should make to how confidently I hold views on things like scientific realism, or how much stock I should put in elegance and parsimony as truth-tracking heuristics rather than just aesthetic preferences.
