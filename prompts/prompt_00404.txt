I'm a backend engineer, not a mathematician, but I got pulled into a long Hacker News thread last week about constructive type theory and Coq, and the discussion kept circling back to the Axiom of Choice. As I understand it, invoking AC in a proof lets you assert that a choice function exists over an arbitrary (possibly infinite, possibly uncountable) collection of sets without ever giving an algorithm or rule for actually producing that function. That sounds to me functionally identical to the situation with the Halting Problem: we know that for any given program, it's a determinate fact whether it halts or not, yet Turing proved there's no general algorithm that can compute the answer. In both cases you seem to have "the thing exists/is true" decoupled from "we have a procedure to construct/verify it."

Given that parallel, I'm having trouble understanding why set theorists treat the Axiom of Choice as this uniquely controversial, independent axiom that some mathematicians choose to reject outright, rather than just treating it as the halting problem's natural extension into infinite set theory. Shouldn't AC basically fall out of the same underlying computability limits Turing already identified, just dressed up in set-theoretic vocabulary? If that's right, it seems strange that anyone would frame accepting or rejecting AC as a foundational choice about the nature of mathematics, as opposed to simply acknowledging a computability fact we already accepted decades ago in another guise.
