A friend and I spent a long evening arguing over the least-discussed part of Nozick's Anarchy, State, and Utopia: not the experience machine, but the section called "Constraints and Animals." There Nozick floats what's since become a well-known formula, "utilitarianism for animals, Kantianism for people" — maximize total happiness across all sentient beings, but place strict, inviolable side constraints on how human beings specifically may be treated, so a human may never be sacrificed for the aggregate good the way an animal may. He then complicates his own proposal with a thought experiment: a race of superior "superbeings," related to us as we are usually thought to be related to animals, and he asks whether we'd accept their treating us the way his view lets us treat animals. My friend took this as fatal to the whole scheme: whatever trait grounds the human side constraints — rationality, free will, the capacity to guide one's life by some overall plan — either exposes us to the superbeings whenever they outstrip us on that trait, or, restated as an all-or-nothing threshold, excludes infants and the severely cognitively impaired right along with the animals (the classic "argument from marginal cases"). I suspected there's a way to thread this needle without just stipulating that humans are special. Please argue for that suspicion: that Nozick's line between people and animals can be given a principled, non-question-begging rationale that survives both the superbeings thought experiment and the problem of marginal cases.
