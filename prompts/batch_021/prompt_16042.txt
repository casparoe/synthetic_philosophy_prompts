Last week a friend and I ended up in a two-hour argument, well after the rest of our reading group had gone home, about whether van Fraassen's Reflection Principle is really just a diachronic cousin of ordinary conditionalization or whether it smuggles in something considerably stronger. My friend's position was that the principle -- roughly, that your current credence in a proposition, conditional on the supposition that your credence in it at some later time will equal r, should itself equal r -- falls straight out of the same Dutch-book reasoning used to justify conditionalization: refuse to align your present opinion with your anticipated future one, and a sufficiently clever bookie can arrange, in advance, a combination of bets that guarantees you a loss no matter which opinion you end up holding. I pushed back with what I take to be the standard worry: suppose I know with certainty that tomorrow, after taking some credence-scrambling drug (or, less dramatically, after a few drinks), my confidence in some perfectly mundane claim will shift to a specific new value for reasons that have nothing to do with evidence. It seems obviously wrong that I should adopt that value today merely because I am sure I will hold it later -- yet the Dutch-book argument, taken at face value, does not appear to care whether the future credence arises from good reasoning or from pharmacology.

My friend's reply was that van Fraassen's original 1984 paper on the subject already restricts Reflection to cases where you regard your future opinion as something like that of a reliable expert, so the drugged case simply falls outside the principle's intended scope. Neither of us, though, could work out whether that restriction is principled -- tracking some real epistemic property that genuinely distinguishes the two cases -- or is instead an ad hoc patch introduced after the fact to keep the conclusion palatable. Could you help me settle this? I would especially like to understand whether the coherence-based argument for Reflection can be repaired so that it binds only anticipated rational belief change, without either collapsing into circularity (defining "rational change" as simply "change I ought to defer to") or becoming so hedged that it no longer rules out anything at all.
