I have no formal training in law or philosophy, but for the past year I have been working through the literature on legal positivism on my own, starting with the "grudge informer" case that Hart and Fuller famously argued over in 1958: the woman in Nazi Germany who denounced her husband to the authorities over a remark he had made about Hitler, and whether the postwar German court was right to treat the wartime statute she relied on as never having been valid law at all. That case pulled me into their wider exchange, and eventually into the second round of the argument some years later, once Fuller had set out, through his parable of the hapless King Rex who fails to make law in eight different ways, the principles that make up what he called the internal morality of law in The Morality of Law -- things like generality, publicity, prospectivity, clarity, non-contradiction, achievability, constancy through time, and congruence between the rules as declared and as actually applied. What I cannot get past is Hart's response, in his review of that book, that satisfying all eight principles makes a legal system merely competent rather than moral in any real sense, in the way that even a torturer or a poisoner needs workable, consistent, intelligible procedures to do what they do efficiently. I mentioned this to a friend who went to law school, and she was adamant that the comparison does not really hold, but she could not explain to me why, and I have not managed to work it out on my own.

So rather than an explanation, I would like to ask you for a dialogue: two characters, a committed Hartian positivist and an equally committed Fullerian, arguing specifically about whether Fuller's eight principles add up to a genuine, if minimal, internal morality of law, or whether they are simply the neutral efficiency conditions of any sufficiently organized purposive activity, dressed up in moral vocabulary. I would like the Fullerian to do more than just insist that legality tends to be good for people -- ideally they should bring in Fuller's own idea that law involves a kind of reciprocity between lawgiver and citizen that a poisoner's private rules of thumb do not -- and I would like the Hartian to push back hard on whether that reciprocity really blocks the comparison, for instance by asking whether a sufficiently rule-bound and consistently administered regime of oppression could have exactly the same reciprocal structure while still being monstrous. Please do not let either side concede too easily or simply talk past the other. What I really want to know is whether this particular disagreement can be settled on its own terms, or whether it just bottoms out in two incompatible ideas of what "morality" is supposed to mean in the first place.
