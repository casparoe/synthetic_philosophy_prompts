I'm working on a feature about panpsychism's return to respectability in philosophy of mind, and I've become interested in a fault line within the camp that rarely surfaces in popular coverage. Write a dialogue between two philosophers who both hold that experience is fundamental to reality but disagree sharply about where to locate it: a constitutive micropsychist, who maintains that the rich, unified experience of a subject like a human being is wholly constituted by the combined experiences of countless simple micro-level subjects, and a priority cosmopsychist, who maintains that the only truly fundamental subject is the cosmos as a whole, with individual minds such as ours somehow dependent on, or carved out of, that single cosmic subject. Have each press the other on their signature difficulty: the micropsychist must explain how many simple subjects could combine into one without covertly presupposing the very unity the combination is meant to produce, while the cosmopsychist must explain how one undivided cosmic subject could give rise to apparently separate, bounded subjects like you and me without that boundary-drawing looking just as arbitrary as the combination it was meant to avoid. Rather than each simply insisting the other's problem is worse, they should be equally sharp, respond directly to what the other has just said, and argue the substantive question of whether "combination" and its mirror image are really symmetrical difficulties, or whether one direction is principled in a way the other isn't.
