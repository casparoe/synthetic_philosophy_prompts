I'm a PhD student working on the economics of information, and I've noticed that discussions of the value of information often conflate two results that actually pull in opposite directions. Good's theorem holds that a Bayesian decision-maker who is free to act optimally on new evidence should never expect, in advance, to be worse off for having observed it. Hirshleifer's classic rejoinder shows, however, that once such evidence becomes available to an entire community rather than a lone agent, it can unravel the very risk-sharing arrangements that depended on the relevant uncertainty persisting a while longer, leaving the group worse off precisely because the information arrived. Please write a short story of roughly 700-900 words in which a community's access to some new and sharper form of foresight is individually rational for each member to seek out, yet corrosive once it actually spreads, with the harm traceable specifically to the collapse of an insurance-like arrangement rather than to the news merely being unwelcome. The ending should make clear -- through the situation itself, not through a character reciting the theorem -- why this outcome is fully compatible with Good's theorem rather than a refutation of it.
