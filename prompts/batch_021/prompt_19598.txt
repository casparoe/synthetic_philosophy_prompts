In a seminar on classical Indian epistemology that I have been sitting in on, we recently spent a couple of sessions on the Nyāya theory of the pramāṇas, and one point in particular has stuck with me since. The Naiyāyikas count śabda -- the testimony of a trustworthy speaker (āpta) -- as a fourth, irreducible pramāṇa alongside perception, inference, and comparison. The Buddhist epistemologists in the lineage of Dignāga and Dharmakīrti reject this: on their view there are only two pramāṇas, and what looks like reliance on testimony is really a disguised case of inference, since we accept an utterance as true only insofar as we tacitly infer that the speaker is not being deceptive or mistaken about the matter -- much as we infer fire from smoke. My instructor mentioned that Gaṅgeśa devotes an extended rebuttal to this reduction in the śabda section of the Tattvacintāmaṇi, but we did not have time to work through it in class, and the secondary reading I tracked down on my own gave the conclusion without really showing the dialectical steps that get you there.

Could you write a dialogue between a Naiyāyika epistemologist and a Buddhist epistemologist that works through exactly this dispute? The Naiyāyika should do more than simply assert that verbal cognition is sui generis: have the Naiyāyika actually argue for it, for instance by appealing to the roles that syntactic expectancy (ākāṅkṣā), semantic compatibility (yogyatā), and proximity (sannidhi) play in producing understanding of a sentence, and by pointing to cases -- a child taking instruction, say, or a stranger's directions accepted with no assessment of character -- where testimonial knowledge seems to arise without anyone grasping an invariable concomitance (vyāpti) between reliable assertion and truth. The Buddhist should push back hard on all of this, including with the suggestion that the Naiyāyika's own conditions for successful testimony are just the premises of a suppressed inference wearing different clothes. I would like both sides to actually engage with each other's strongest point rather than talk past one another, and I would like the dialogue to end not with a resolution but with whatever turns out to be the sharpest, most stubborn point of disagreement between them.
