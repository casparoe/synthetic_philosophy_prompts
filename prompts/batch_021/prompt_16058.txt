As someone who has spent years reading Less Wrong and the old Overcoming Bias archives, I keep noticing that the community's default treatment of theism is thoroughly Bayesian: "God exists" is treated as an ordinary empirical hypothesis to be assigned a prior and revised on evidence, and Yudkowsky's "Belief in Belief" essay treats believers who resist this framing as self-deceived rather than as making a different kind of claim altogether. This is essentially the picture of religious language that the New Atheists — Dawkins especially, with his insistence that God's existence is a scientific question like any other — popularized from the mid-2000s onward, decades after Wittgenstein's death in 1951 and decades after his 1938 Lectures on Religious Belief argued that religious "belief" isn't held with a degree of confidence at all, but functions more like a picture that organizes an entire way of living, insulated from the sort of evidential doubt appropriate to ordinary empirical claims.

Had Wittgenstein lived to encounter both the New Atheist argument and its Bayesian gloss on Less Wrong, how do you think he would have responded? Would he have judged both sides confused about what kind of thing religious belief is, or would he have had to concede ground to believers and apologists who explicitly do treat faith as an empirical wager (Pascal, or contemporary fine-tuning arguments)? Please answer with reference to his actual views on belief, doubt, and certainty, rather than just his reputation as a "fideist."
