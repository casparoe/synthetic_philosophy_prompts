"Evidential cooperation in large worlds" (ECL) is the proposal that agents who cannot causally influence one another may nonetheless have reason to cooperate, provided their decision-making is suitably correlated; the term superseded an earlier label, "multiverse-wide superrationality," under which the idea is still sometimes discussed. The argument runs roughly as follows: if the universe (or multiverse) is large enough, it contains enormous numbers of causally isolated agents, some of whom reason in ways relevantly similar to how we reason. Since one's own choice is then evidence about what these correlated agents choose, an evidential decision theorist can treat "cooperating" with them — for instance, by giving some weight to values other than one's own, or by declining purely parochial or zero-sum courses of action — as raising the expected value of the outcome across the whole population of correlated agents, even though no causal channel links us to any of them. The idea is often presented as a generalization of Hofstadter-style superrationality in the one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma, extended from a single interaction between two agents to an entire "large world" of mutually unreachable others.

ECL has attracted serious attention in parts of the decision-theory and effective-altruism-adjacent literature, but it remains genuinely disputed, and not merely because it inherits the standard quarrel between evidential and causal decision theory. Critics have pressed objections specific to ECL itself: that we have no principled way to estimate how many relevantly correlated agents exist or what values they hold, so the supposed gains from "trade" with them are unmeasurable and could as easily be negative as positive; that the argument seems to prove too much, licensing an open-ended willingness to sacrifice one's own values for the sake of causally inert, unfalsifiable partners in a way that starts to resemble a Pascal's mugging; and that the notion of "correlation" between decision procedures separated by an unbridgeable causal gap is left doing mysterious work the theory never fully explains. Defenders respond that these are difficulties of estimation and implementation rather than refutations of the underlying decision-theoretic logic, and that some modest but genuine behavioral upshot survives them — for instance, giving somewhat more weight to widely shared, compromise-friendly values relative to narrowly parochial ones.
