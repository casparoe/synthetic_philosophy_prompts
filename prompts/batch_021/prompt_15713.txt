Retired field biologist here (birds mostly, some fish behaviour work too), and since I hung up my binoculars I've fallen into reading philosophy of science for fun — currently working through Lakatos, who I somehow avoided my entire career. I wrote up some notes trying to think through the Gould/Lewontin spandrels paper in Lakatosian terms, and I'd like your comments before I inflict it on my old department's reading group. Not after polish, just: does the central move actually work, am I being unfair to anyone, and have I got any of the history wrong.

Here's the gist:

Gould and Lewontin's complaint, stripped of the San Marco flourish, is basically Popperian — adaptationists dream up a plausible fitness story for a trait, don't test it against a serious alternative, and treat the story as confirmed anyway. Fine, granted. But they never actually offer a rival methodology that escapes the identical charge. Their preferred alternatives — developmental constraint, allometry, historical accident, Baupläne — are exactly as hard to pin down with a clean falsifying observation as an adaptive story is. So I don't think what's on display is "bad science vs. good science." I think it's two Lakatosian research programmes, adaptationism and structuralism, each running on an unfalsifiable hard core (roughly: "selection mainly shapes form" vs. "internal constraint mainly shapes form") protected by a belt of auxiliary assumptions that gets adjusted case by case. Lakatos's whole point was that you can't knock out a programme with one anomaly, you compare programmes by whether they're progressive (novel confirmed predictions) or degenerating (just absorbing data after the fact). Judged that way I don't think the spandrels paper actually settles anything on its own — optimality modelling and the comparative method have thrown up a decent number of novel, later-confirmed predictions, which isn't nothing.

The bit that's really nagging at me though: three years after the spandrels paper, Gould needed a whole new word, "exaptation," just to seperate byproduct-features that stay useless (true spandrels) from byproduct-features that get co-opted for some new job later (exaptations). But deciding after the fact which bin a given trait belongs in seems to require exactly the retrospective, hard-to-falsify storytelling they were accusing the adaptationists of. If so, the critique might be reflexively self-undermining — the bookkeeping needed to keep the taxonomy honest reproduces the disease it's diagnosing. (I also have it in my head that the actual architectural feature at San Marco is a pendentive, not a spandrel proper, which would be a nice irony if true, but I'm honestly not sure I'm remembering that right.)

So: is the Lakatos mapping defensible, or am I forcing a square peg into it? Is the reflexivity point actually novel, or is it something Sober or Godfrey-Smith already dealt with ages ago? And flag anything in the history that's just wrong — dates, the pendentive claim, what exaptation is actually supposed to distinguish.
