Okay so I'm deep in research mode for a novel about a small-town judge who slowly starts to suspect that the law he's spent his career enforcing was never really "just" so much as just... enforced. To get into the headspace I've been rereading Pascal's Pensées (yes, a bit on-the-nose, my writing group already teased me about it), and there's one fragment on justice and force that I keep circling back to and can't quite pin down. In the old Trotter translation it goes: "It is right that what is just should be obeyed; it is necessary that what is strongest should be obeyed. Justice without might is helpless; might without justice is tyrannical. Justice without might is gainsaid, because there are always offenders; might without justice is condemned. We must then combine justice and might, and for this end make what is just strong, or what is strong just." (It's numbered differently in every edition I've seen, I think 298 in Brunschvicg, don't quote me on that.) Right after that he basically argues that because justice is endlessly disputable while force is plain for everyone to see and nobody argues with it, that's exactly the swap that actually happened historically — not making the just strong, but making the strong count as just.

My first instinct is "okay, cynical, might makes right, got it," except Pascal isn't some straightforward Machiavelli guy — he's writing as a believer trying to make sense of a fallen world where he thinks our reason can't reliably track real justice on its own anymore anyway (which feels connected to that other one-liner of his about truth on one side of the Pyrenees and error on the other, i.e. justice depending on where you happen to be standing). So is he saying that dressing force up as justice is basically a necessary, even good, illusion society needs so it doesn't collapse into open war — something he thinks we should want people to keep believing even though its false? Or is he doing something more diagnostic/destabilizing, actually exposing how authority gets its legitimacy in a way that's supposed to unsettle the reader rather than reassure them? I'd love a genuinely close reading of the logic of the passage (not the "yeah, might makes right" one-liner you get in most summaries), and some sense of how it fits with the more theological, post-Adam stuff he's doing elsewhere in the Pensées about concupiscence and the limits of reason.
