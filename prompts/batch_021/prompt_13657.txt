About two years ago I fell down an internet rabbit hole reading about Newcomb's Problem, and it eventually pulled me into decision theory papers from the nineteen-seventies that I now work through slowly on my own time, without any formal training in philosophy or logic to fall back on. A few people I met through that same rabbit hole have started trading short problem sets with one another, on the understanding that whoever sets the question also grades the answer, and has to justify every mark honestly rather than just being encouraging, since none of us has a real instructor to appeal to. The question I set this week, based on the Gibbard and Harper paper that's usually credited as one of the founding statements of causal decision theory, was this: "Explain the 'Death in Damascus' puzzle. What does evidential decision theory recommend the man do, and why? What does causal decision theory recommend, and why? Then explain the further difficulty the case raises about 'ratifiability' — why does the causal theorist's recommendation run into trouble too, and not only the evidential theorist's? Finally, say what you think the case shows about the prospects for a fully general theory of rational choice."

My study partner, who generally has a firmer grip on this material than I do, sent back the answer below, and it is my turn to mark it under our rule. Before I commit to a grade I would like an outside opinion, mainly because I know my own inexperience could make me either too easily won over by confident, fluent writing or too harsh out of insecurity about my own grasp of it, and I would rather calibrate against something steadier than my own gut. Here is the answer in full:

"Gibbard and Harper's story updates the old 'Appointment in Samarra' tale: a man runs into Death on the street in Damascus, gets a look that convinces him Death has business with him, and has to decide overnight whether to stay in Damascus or flee to Aleppo. The catch is that Death is a nearly infallible predictor of what people will do, and has already gone to whichever city he predicted the man would be in. So the man cannot outrun Death by choosing to flee, because Death's foresight covers flight just as well as it covers staying put; whichever city he ends up in is, with very high probability, the city Death is waiting in.

An evidential decision theorist works out expected utility by conditionalizing on each act. Conditional on staying, the probability that Death is in Damascus is high, so staying looks bad; conditional on fleeing, the probability that Death is in Aleppo is equally high, so fleeing looks just as bad. The two acts tie, and they tie at a terrible expected value, which is an odd result to hang your deliberation on, since the man's choice does not cause Death to be anywhere; Death's whereabouts were already settled by a prediction made in advance. A causal decision theorist tries to respect that by scoring each act using the probabilities of Death's location that would hold no matter which act is chosen, rather than the probabilities conditional on the act, so the correlation gets set aside as merely evidential rather than causal. This, too, ties the two acts, but now at a much better expected value (fifty-fifty, say, rather than near-certain death), which looks like the right diagnosis. In that sense, Death in Damascus is really just Newcomb's Problem wearing a different coat: an excellent predictor has already fixed things in advance, and the whole question is whether that fixed, merely correlational fact should be allowed to drive a choice it cannot causally affect.

The wrinkle is that the causal theorist's tie does not survive deliberation either. Suppose the man leans toward staying; since Death predicts actual choices rather than idle inclinations, that lean should raise his credence that Death is in Damascus, which immediately makes fleeing look better, which would in turn raise his credence that Death is in Aleppo, and so on. Richard Jeffrey called a choice 'ratifiable' if it still maximizes expected utility once you are certain you are going to make it, precisely so that an agent can settle on the ratifiable option and stop second-guessing. In Death in Damascus, neither staying nor fleeing is ratifiable, so the causal theorist can never land on a choice that survives its own selection; causal decision theory ends up with nothing determinate to recommend here, and the man is left with no principled way to pick a city at all.

I think the honest conclusion is that Gibbard and Harper's case shows the limits of expecting any general theory of rational choice to always deliver a stable answer, rather than a simple victory for one theory over the other."
