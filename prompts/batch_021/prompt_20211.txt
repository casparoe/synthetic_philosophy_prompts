I'm taking a seminar this term built largely around the literature on peer disagreement, and something that happened in section last week has stuck with me more than the readings themselves. As an ungraded warm-up, our TA had everyone privately write down a probability that the department's outdoor end-of-year reception, three weeks off, would get rained on, using the same regional forecast data she handed out. Two students, Renata and Owen, have posted essentially identical scores on every calibration exercise we've done this term, so it caught the room's attention when Renata came in at 55% and Owen at 20%. The argument that followed went roughly like this:

1. Renata said that since they'd worked from the same data and had matched each other all term, neither of them had any standing claim to being the more reliable forecaster here, so the rational response for both was to move toward something like the midpoint between their two numbers.

2. Owen answered that on going back through his own reasoning, he still found it more convincing than what she'd described to him: he thought her 55% leaned on treating this month's cloud cover as more predictive of rain than the historical data really supports, and that this kind of difference in reasoning quality should count for something, not just their matched scores on past exercises.

3. Renata replied that this was exactly the move she'd expect from someone still in the grip of a mistake: he was using the very reasoning under dispute to certify that his own reasoning was better, which is circular. Unless he had some independent reason for thinking he was the more reliable one here, one that didn't itself presuppose he'd reasoned correctly about the rain, symmetry should win.

4. Owen said that if that standard were applied consistently, no one could ever rationally stand their ground on anything, since virtually all of a person's evidence that they've reasoned well is exactly the "first-order" kind Renata wanted him to set aside. He added that she was not holding herself to the same standard: several epistemologists on the syllabus reject the equal-weight principle she was invoking, so by her own logic she ought to be less than fully confident in it too, yet she was not treating that disagreement with the same symmetry she was demanding of him.

Neither of them budged, and the TA moved on before it got resolved. Could you go through the four moves one at a time and say who has the better of it at each step, rather than just declaring an overall winner? I'd also like to know what you think both of them are overlooking, and I'd like a one-line verdict at the end on whether either of them comes out ahead overall.
