I am roughly a third of the way into a novel built around a single point of divergence: the Fourth Crusade never manages to sack Constantinople in 1204 — no Latin Empire, no Palaiologan restoration fifty-seven years later, none of the fatal weakening that most historians treat as a hinge in Byzantium's long slide toward 1453. My writing group has split badly over how far downstream the consequences should travel. Half of them want a Byzantium that limps along, absorbs the Ottoman shock on different terms, and perhaps holds Anatolia deep into the fifteenth century; the other half keep insisting that if I let the empire survive 1204 intact, the same deep forces — the loss of Anatolia since Manzikert, the rise of Italian maritime capital, the demographic tide that produced the Ottomans regardless of any single Latin misadventure — will simply reassert themselves, so that Constantinople still falls, only on a slightly delayed schedule. Partway through this argument I realized we were re-litigating a much older dispute in Byzantine costume, and that I did not actually understand that older dispute well enough to referee it, let alone write a novel that takes a side in it.

What I would like from you is an essay that stakes out an actual position on whether "counterfactual history" — reasoning seriously about what would have followed from a different outcome at some historical juncture — can be a rigorous, defensible mode of historical explanation, or whether it is closer to what E. H. Carr had in mind when he waved it away as little more than an unhistorical parlour game. Please take seriously the more disciplined defenses of the practice, such as the Weberian notion of "objective possibility," which Niall Ferguson and his contributors to Virtual History later invoked to restrict legitimate counterfactuals to the possibilities that contemporaries themselves regarded as live options, and weigh that against the historical-materialist instinct that runs from Marx and Engels, through Plekhanov's essay on the role of the individual in history, to Tolstoy's second epilogue to War and Peace — on which any particular juncture matters far less than it feels like it should, because deep structural forces would produce broadly similar outcomes whichever way this or that battle, doge, or emperor happened to go. Bring in something like Robert Fogel's railroad counterfactual, if it is useful to your case, as an example of this style of reasoning being put to real, falsifiable historiographical work rather than mere narrative speculation.

I do not want a survey that concludes both sides have a point; I get enough of that from my writing group already. Please reach an actual verdict on two connected questions: first, under what conditions, if any, counterfactual reasoning earns the right to be treated as serious historical explanation rather than parlour-game indulgence; second, whether a near-miss like Byzantium's in 1204 is the kind of juncture at which history was genuinely up for grabs, or the kind at which the details are contingent but the broad trajectory was already locked in. If you can, close with the practical upshot for a novelist: roughly how long a shadow should one battle be allowed to cast before I am cheating the deep structure my more skeptical friends believe in, or before I am being too timid, and betraying the very possibility that things could have gone otherwise?
