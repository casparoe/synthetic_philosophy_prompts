I spent my career as a structural biologist, and for the last decade or so before retiring I also ran my university's shared cryo-electron microscopy facility. Since stepping back from the lab, I have been working through an online course on epistemology, largely to fill the time. A recent unit on evidence and justified belief brought back an old dispute from my facility-directing days that I have never quite managed to resolve in my own mind, and I would value your perspective on it.

One Monday morning we discovered that one of the microscope's detectors, a piece of equipment worth the better part of a million dollars, had been badly damaged over the weekend in a way that could only have resulted from serious mishandling. The booking system showed that, purely as an accident of scheduling, 38 of the 40 people with card access to the room that weekend belonged to a single visiting research group, while the remaining two belonged to unrelated labs with no plausible connection to the incident. The colleague who ran our day-to-day operations wrote an incident report stating, as settled fact, that the damage had been caused by a member of the visiting group, and moved to bill their host institution for the repair, on the grounds that this was true with roughly 95 percent probability. When I objected that she had no business simply asserting this as fact, since nobody had a shred of evidence pointing to that group specifically beyond the raw head count, she and most of the oversight committee thought I was splitting hairs: ninety-five percent is ninety-five percent, they said, adding that I would have raised no objection at all had she instead relied on, say, a card-swipe log independently known to be only 95 percent reliable, or a single credible witness we had good reason to trust 95 percent of the time. I had to admit that those other sources would not have troubled me nearly as much, but I could not explain why bare head-count statistics should be treated any differently, and the argument was never really settled.

I raised the episode again recently over dinner with two of those former colleagues, and opinion was just as divided as it had been at the time. Having since read a little more philosophy, I suspect I was groping toward something real, though I still cannot articulate it convincingly, and I am no longer sure I was even entitled to my original discomfort. Was there a principled basis for treating that statistic as insufficient grounds for declaring who was responsible, one that would not apply equally to the card-swipe log or the trustworthy witness — or were my colleagues right that a probability is a probability, however it arises, and I was merely indulging a groundless scruple? I would be grateful for your judgment on who had the better of that argument, and why.
