The question was: "State Arrow's Impossibility Theorem, giving a precise statement of the four conditions imposed on a social welfare function. In one paragraph, sketch the core idea behind the standard 'pivotal voter' proof of the theorem. Finally, in a short paragraph, assess whether it is accurate to say that Arrow's theorem shows that 'there is no fair way to vote,' and explain your reasoning."

Here is the student's answer, verbatim:

"Arrow's Impossibility Theorem states that whenever there are at least three alternatives to rank, no social welfare function — a rule that takes each voter's complete, transitive ranking of the alternatives and aggregates them into a single social ranking — can simultaneously satisfy four conditions: (1) Unrestricted Domain, which requires the rule to accept any logically possible combination of individual rankings as input; (2) the Pareto Principle, which requires that if every individual ranks x above y, society must also rank x above y; (3) Independence of Irrelevant Alternatives (IIA), which requires that the social ranking of any two options x and y depend only on how individuals rank x against y, not on how they rank other alternatives; and (4) Non-Dictatorship, which rules out the existence of a single voter whose strict preference between any pair of alternatives is always adopted as the social preference, regardless of everyone else's preferences.

The standard proof identifies a 'pivotal voter' for some pair of alternatives. Suppose every voter ranks some option b last; by the Pareto Principle, society must also rank b last. Now move b, one voter at a time, from the bottom to the top of that voter's individual ranking, leaving the relative order of every other alternative fixed. Since the social ranking of b can only change a finite number of times as we go through the voters, there must be some voter, call her voter k, whose shift causes b to jump all at once from the very bottom of the social ranking to the very top. Using IIA, one can then show that voter k's decisiveness is not really about b at all: the same voter turns out to be decisive over every other pair of alternatives too, which makes voter k a dictator. Since this contradicts Non-Dictatorship, no social welfare function can satisfy all four conditions together.

I think it is basically fair to say that Arrow's theorem shows there is no fair way to vote. The theorem doesn't just apply to majority rule, which we already knew could cycle from Condorcet's paradox — it covers any conceivable method for turning individual rankings into a group decision, including rank-based systems like the Borda count as well as rating-based systems like approval voting and range voting, since all of them are ultimately just procedures for converting individual input into a social choice. This is also why Arrow's theorem is the reason we know that every non-dictatorial voting system is vulnerable to strategic voting: since no rule can be simultaneously Paretian, independent, and non-dictatorial, there will always be some configuration of preferences in which a voter can secure a better outcome by misreporting their true ranking. So although the details of the proof are about rankings rather than winners, the upshot is the one people usually take from it: there is no rule for aggregating preferences, however cleverly designed, that is simultaneously fair, consistent, and immune to manipulation."
