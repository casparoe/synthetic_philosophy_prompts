Earlier this year I served on a jury for a vehicular manslaughter trial, and a conversation with a fellow juror once the trial had concluded has been bothering me ever since. The defendant had driven home from a work function well over the legal limit and killed a pedestrian who stepped into the road mid-block. Over coffee afterward, this juror — a second-year law student — mentioned that a close friend of hers had done essentially the same thing the same month: same blood-alcohol level, same failure to notice someone stepping out until it was too late, except that the street happened to be empty a second earlier, so her friend walked away with a license suspension rather than a criminal conviction. Since the trial I've been reading around the philosophical literature this obviously maps onto — Williams's and Nagel's writing on moral luck, and specifically what they call resultant luck, the way an action's moral standing seems to shift with how it happens to turn out. What keeps catching my attention is a move that's clearly meant to dissolve the discomfort rather than sit with it: the idea, which I gather traces back to Judith Andre's early reply to Nagel and Williams and was developed further by Norvin Richards and others, that the two drivers aren't actually unequal in blameworthiness at all. On this view, the harsher judgment we pass on the one who kills only tracks an epistemic asymmetry — her conduct simply gives us better evidence of how reckless and inattentive she really was — so that once the two drivers are stipulated to be genuinely on a par beforehand, the moral difference disappears, and the broadly Kantian thought that only what's within an agent's control can affect her culpability survives intact.

Please argue against this epistemic dissolution of resultant moral luck. Take the strongest version of the case: two drivers, matched in every antecedent respect — identical intoxication, identical inattention, identical foreseeable risk, and, by stipulation, identical evidence available to any outside observer about their prior character and conduct — who differ only in whether a pedestrian happens to be crossing at the relevant moment. Defend the claim that the one whose recklessness issues in a death is genuinely more blameworthy than her counterpart, not merely unluckier, and that holding the epistemic situation fixed does not make this residue disappear. In making the case, engage directly with at least three fallback moves rather than talking past them: the Zimmerman-style attempt to let luck affect only the wrongness of the act while insisting blameworthiness tracks the agent's will alone; the suggestion that Williams's notion of "agent-regret" is best read as a fitting but non-moral sentiment rather than evidence of a real moral difference; and the objection that stipulating full epistemic parity between the two drivers is itself an idealization that quietly reintroduces the very asymmetry the epistemic account is supposed to explain away. I'd rather you land on a clear, defended verdict than survey the debate evenhandedly.
