Here's the student's answer, verbatim:

"On the buck-passing view associated with Scanlon, an object's being good just consists in its having other properties that give us reasons to have a pro-attitude toward it -- desiring it, admiring it, and so on. Goodness itself isn't a further reason-giving property; it 'passes the buck' to whatever lower-level features do the actual reason-giving work. The trouble is that all sorts of things give us reasons to admire or desire something without those reasons having anything to do with the object's actual value. Parfit's demon case makes this vivid: a demon threatens to cause you terrible suffering unless you admire him. That certainly gives you a reason to admire him, but not because he's admirable, so if we just say 'x is good iff there is reason to have a pro-attitude toward x,' we get the absurd result that the demon comes out admirable. This is the wrong kind of reasons (WKR) problem: not every reason to have a pro-attitude is the right kind of reason, one that tracks whether the object actually has the value-relevant features.

The standard proposed fix, following Parfit, distinguishes object-given reasons -- grounded in features of the object itself, like the demon's cruelty -- from state-given reasons -- grounded in the value of being in the attitudinal state itself, like the suffering you avoid by admiring him. Wrong-kind reasons are state-given; right-kind reasons are object-given. Build that restriction into the analysis and the demon case is defused, since the demon only ever supplies a state-given reason.

The trouble is that this pushes the question back a level rather than answering it: we now need an independent story about what makes a reason 'object-given' that doesn't just presuppose the very notion of fittingness the analysis was supposed to explain, and it's not obvious one can be given without circularity."
