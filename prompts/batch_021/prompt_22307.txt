I'm a novelist working on a book set in Paris in the early 1670s, a few years after the posthumous publication of Pascal's Pensées, in which a disillusioned young magistrate at the Parlement drifts into the orbit of Port-Royal and becomes preoccupied with one of Pascal's fragments on justice and force. I want a later scene—an argument between the magistrate and an older, more worldly colleague—to actually engage with the substance of Pascal's reasoning rather than wave vaguely at "might makes right," so before I put words in either character's mouth I've been sitting with the fragment myself, in the old Trotter translation, and I keep tripping over its exact logical movement.

The passage runs: "Justice, might.—It is right that what is just should be obeyed; it is necessary that what is strongest should be obeyed. Justice without might is helpless; might without justice is tyrannical. Justice without might is gainsaid, because there are always offenders; might without justice is condemned. We must then combine justice and might, and for this end make what is just strong, or what is strong just. Justice is subject to dispute; might is easily recognised and is not disputed. So we cannot give might to justice, because might has gainsaid justice, and has declared that it is she herself who is just. And thus being unable to make what is just strong, we have made what is strong just."

What I can't settle for myself is whether this amounts to a piece of cynical political realism—justice simply is whatever force manages to entrench, full stop—or something more layered: a diagnosis of a historical failure (we could not do the hard thing, make justice strong, so we did the easy, corrupting thing instead, and called strength just) that still leaves room, at least logically, for a "true" justice that force only ever failed to serve. The passage seems to slide between a normative register ("we must combine justice and might") and a resigned, descriptive one (this is how might came to wear justice's name), and I don't understand why "justice without might is gainsaid, because there are always offenders" and "might without justice is condemned" are presented as a matched pair of failures rather than two quite different kinds of trouble—in the French I have to hand it's "contredite" against "accusée," which don't feel like natural opposites either. Could you walk through the argument sentence by sentence, help me see how these pieces actually connect, and say a little about how the whole thing sits with what Pascal is doing elsewhere in the Pensées with the fallenness of human reason—so that I can decide, honestly, what my magistrate is entitled to conclude from it?
