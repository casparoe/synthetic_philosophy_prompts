I'm a PhD student in the history and philosophy of biology, currently drafting a dissertation chapter on how the principal architects of the Modern Synthesis differed in their assessments of the relative power of selection versus drift. Fisher's *The Genetical Theory of Natural Selection* has taken up most of my last month, and last week our reading group got sidetracked arguing about whether Fisher is simply too easy a target for later critiques of adaptationism. The more I sit with his fundamental theorem of natural selection, his insistence that mass selection acting on abundant standing variation would swamp drift in any population of realistic size, his theory of the evolution of dominance via modifier genes, and his runaway model of sexual selection, the more it seems to me that Fisher's entire scientific temperament amounts to about as pure an instance as one could find of what Gould and Lewontin, writing seventeen years after Fisher's death, would come to christen "the adaptationist programme" and lampoon as Panglossian.

What I'd like from you is a well-grounded reconstruction of how Fisher himself might have responded had he lived to read Gould and Lewontin's "The Spandrels of San Marco and the Panglossian Paradigm: A Critique of the Adaptationist Programme." Rather than a generic verdict that "he would have disagreed," I'd like you to actually engage with a few specific threads: first, whether Fisher -- whose own scientific style was rigorously quantitative rather than narrative -- might have conceded something to the "just-so story" charge against loose, untested adaptive storytelling even while denying that it applied to his own work; second, whether he would have tried to reabsorb architectural byproducts and pleiotropic side effects back into his selection-centered mathematics (say, as correlated responses to selection on other traits) rather than granting them the status of a genuinely independent explanatory category, as Gould and Lewontin wanted; and third, given how much of his polemical energy during his lifetime went into resisting Sewall Wright's pluralism about drift and multiple selective peaks, whether he might have read the spandrels critique largely through the lens of that older quarrel -- as a rhetorically updated version of Wright-style anti-selectionism -- rather than as a challenge to be met on the San Marco architectural analogy's own terms. Where the historical record genuinely underdetermines the answer, feel free to flag the speculation as such, but I would rather have a historically textured guess than a noncommittal summary.
