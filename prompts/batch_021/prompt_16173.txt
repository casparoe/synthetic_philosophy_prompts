I'm working on a magazine feature about paradoxes of the infinite, and this week I sat in on a philosophy-of-mathematics seminar that closed with Thomson's lamp: a lamp switched on at the half-minute mark, off at three-quarters, on again at seven-eighths, and so on, each interval half the length of the last, all converging on the one-minute mark. The class was left with the standard question of whether the lamp is on or off once the full minute has elapsed, which the professor presented as a live, unresolved puzzle about whether "supertasks" are even coherent. That framing struck me as odd, so I raised it afterward with a mathematician I'm interviewing for the piece; she waved it off as "basically a solved problem dressed up as a mystery" but had to run before she could explain why, and I haven't managed to reach her since.

Here is my worry, and I would like your help sorting it out before I write this up. As I understood it from an undergraduate course years ago, Zeno's paradoxes of motion were essentially retired once mathematicians showed that an infinite series of shrinking intervals can sum to a finite total, so that completing infinitely many sub-steps in finite time is not actually contradictory. Thomson's lamp is built from exactly that kind of convergent series. So it seems to me that the very same convergence that lets Achilles overtake the tortoise ought to settle the lamp case too: if the switch-presses crowd ever closer to the one-minute mark the way Achilles' strides crowd ever closer to the tortoise, then the convergence of the series should fix what state the lamp is in at the limit, just as it fixes the moment the race is decided. So why do philosophers still write papers treating the lamp's final state as an open question, decades after Thomson first posed it, instead of simply pointing to the convergent sum and moving on? Is there a genuine disanalogy between the two cases that I'm missing, or does this puzzle persist mainly because philosophers enjoy it?
