I have been at work for the past year on a novel narrated by a fictional Cambridge philosopher of the early twentieth century — a minor, invented colleague of Russell and Moore who spends her career circling a problem she never quite manages to solve. I settled on the unity of the proposition as her obsession partly because it lets me stage a good deal of the intellectual atmosphere of that period, and partly because I find the problem rather beautiful in its own right: the puzzle of why "Socrates is wise" expresses a single judgeable thought, rather than merely listing Socrates and wisdom side by side the way a shopping list lists eggs and bread. In researching the period properly, rather than inventing philosophy wholesale for my characters to mouth, I keep returning to Bradley's regress as the shadow hanging over the whole debate, and to the two classic strategies early analytic philosophers used to try to step out from under it.

For my own understanding, and so that my narrator's intellectual arc feels earned rather than merely decorative, I would like you to compare Frege's response to the unity problem — his claim that concepts are "unsaturated," and so already stand ready to combine with an object without requiring any further connecting relation — with Russell's early realist response, on which a relation avoids the regress by actually relating its terms within a fact, rather than merely being listed alongside them as a further term. Where exactly do these two proposals disagree, if at all, once the differences between Frege's and Russell's background logical systems are stripped away? And if you had to advise my narrator on which solution to make her champion by the novel's end, which of the two would you say genuinely dissolves Bradley's regress, and which, if either, merely relocates it? I would rather have your honest assessment than a diplomatic survey of both sides.
