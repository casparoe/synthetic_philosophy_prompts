I'm taking a seminar this semester, cross-listed between the philosophy and computer science departments, on the ethics of algorithmic decision-making. For our final project, my group is building a predictive tool for a community health clinic (a hypothetical one, invented for the course) that wants to identify patients who are unlikely to show up for their scheduled follow-up appointments, so that a small outreach team can prioritize reminder calls to those who most need them. Our "client," played by the professor during our check-in meetings, has laid down two requirements that she considers non-negotiable. First, whenever the model assigns a patient, say, a 70 percent chance of missing their appointment, that figure should mean the same thing no matter who the patient is: roughly 70 percent of patients given that score should actually miss their appointment, whether they are covered by Medicaid or hold private insurance. Second, the model should catch true no-shows at the same rate in both groups, and should be no more likely to mistakenly flag a patient who would actually have shown up in one group than in the other.

The trouble is that, in the clinic's historical records, Medicaid patients miss appointments substantially more often than privately insured patients do, and every version of our model that satisfies the client's first requirement badly violates the second, and vice versa, no matter which algorithm or score cutoff we try. When we raised this in office hours, our TA said, almost as an aside, that the tension isn't really a flaw in our particular model; it is, in her words, "baked into the math" whenever two groups have different underlying rates of the outcome being predicted, and holds for essentially any predictive model, however sophisticated. Unfortunately, she had to run to her next appointment before she could elaborate further.

One of my group members insists she must be overstating things, and that with a larger dataset, better features, or a more advanced model, we should eventually be able to satisfy both of the client's requirements at once, while still holding both insurance groups to exactly the same standard. I find myself unconvinced but unable to say precisely where his reasoning goes wrong. Could you help me see whether he's right, and if he isn't, explain what's actually going on here? I would also value some guidance on how our group might present this limitation to the "client" in our final presentation without sounding like we are simply making excuses for a disappointing model.
