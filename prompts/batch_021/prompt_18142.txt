I'm a novelist currently at work on a campus novel whose central figure is a cantankerous, formidably well-read Hume scholar -- the sort of person who bristles whenever colleagues reduce her author to "the is-ought guy" and forget that he also wrote a whole, still-underrated cluster of essays on money and commerce. In the scene I'm drafting, she is invited onto a public radio programme and asked, half as a parlour trick, what Hume himself would make of two live economic debates: the recent wave of tariffs that governments justify by pointing to bilateral trade deficits, and the steady, seemingly unstoppable growth of sovereign debt across the rich world since 2008.

I want her answer, and my own research behind it, to be recognisably grounded in the actual content of essays like "Of the Balance of Trade," "Of the Jealousy of Trade," and "Of Public Credit," rather than in generic free-market talking points anachronistically dressed up in his name. Could you reconstruct, in reasonably precise terms, how Hume's own arguments would bear on each debate: the self-correcting flow of specie between trading nations that historians of economic thought now call the price-specie-flow mechanism, his explicit rejection of the mercantilist assumption that a neighbour's prosperity comes at our expense, and his anxieties about the long-run sustainability of public credit? I would also like you to flag where his eighteenth-century premises -- money as gold and silver coin, no central bank creating money electronically, no floating exchange rates -- start to strain against a modern financial system, since that friction between his framework and ours is exactly what I want the radio scene to dramatise. If you can close with two or three sentences in something like Hume's own idiom, rendering his verdict on one of the two debates, that would be a welcome bonus for the draft.
