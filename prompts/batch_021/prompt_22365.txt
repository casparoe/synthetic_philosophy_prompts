Over the past year I have repeatedly found myself in the same argument, at work and among friends, about whether cryptocurrencies deserve to be called "real money," and the discussion invariably turns on someone invoking Aristotle's supposed checklist of the essential properties of money (durability, portability, divisibility, and so on) as though this settled the matter once and for all. Tired of only knowing this argument at second hand, I joined a small reading group that meets monthly at my local library and is currently working through the relevant sections of the Nicomachean Ethics. This month's text was Book V, chapter 5, on reciprocal justice in exchange, and I found myself genuinely stuck on the central argument concerning money and demand, for which the material on usury and chrematistics in the Politics that people usually cite had not really prepared me. I would like help working through it properly before our next meeting.

Here is the passage, in W. D. Ross's early-twentieth-century translation, which is the version most of us in the group ended up using since it is the one freely available online (I have trimmed a couple of digressions, marked with ellipses and brackets):

"Now proportionate return is secured by cross-conjunction. Let A be a builder, B a shoemaker, C a house, D a shoe. The builder, then, must get from the shoemaker the latter's work, and must himself give him in return his own. If, then, first there is proportionate equality of goods, and then reciprocal action takes place, the result we mention will be effected... This is why all things that are exchanged must be somehow comparable. It is for this end that money has been introduced, and it becomes in a sense an intermediate; for it measures all things, and therefore the excess and the defect—how many shoes are equal to a house or to a given amount of food. The number of shoes exchanged for a house (or for a given amount of food) must therefore correspond to the ratio of builder to shoemaker... All goods must therefore be measured by some one thing, as we said before. Now this unit is in truth demand, which holds all things together (for if men did not need one another's goods at all, or did not need them equally, there would be either no exchange or not the same exchange); but money has become by convention a sort of representative of demand; and this is why it has the name 'money' (nomisma)—because it exists not by nature but by law (nomos) and it is in our power to change it and make it useless.

[The chapter continues with a parallel example involving a farmer and a shoemaker, before concluding:]

Money, then, acting as a measure, makes goods commensurate and equates them; for neither would there have been association if there were not exchange, nor exchange if there were not equality, nor equality if there were not commensurability. Now in truth it is impossible that things differing so much should become commensurate, but with reference to demand they may become so sufficiently. There must, then, be a unit, and that fixed by agreement (for which reason it is called money); for it is this that makes all things commensurate, since all things are measured by money. Let A be a house, B ten minae, C a bed. A is half of B, if the house is worth five minae or equal to them; the bed, C, is a tenth of B; it is plain, then, how many beds are equal to a house, viz. five."

Our discussion leader mentioned, without having time to explain, that this is something of a locus classicus for disputes over whether Aristotle is groping toward a demand-based or even subjective theory of value, and that Marx made a great deal of this very passage in a footnote in Capital. What I cannot work out on my own is how the argument is actually supposed to hang together. Aristotle first sets up a proportion between the producers themselves (builder is to shoemaker as shoe is to house), with money apparently entering just to let us read off how many shoes equal a house within that ratio; but then he says the true common "unit" of exchange is not money at all but demand (chreia—my edition's footnote adds that the word can equally be rendered "need"), with money merely serving as demand's conventional proxy. Most puzzlingly, he then states outright that it is "impossible" for things as different as a house and a bed to become truly commensurable, in almost the same breath as claiming that money does make them commensurate. Is this a plain contradiction, or is there a consistent view on which money supplies only a workable, convention-fixed commensuration that is parasitic on demand, without either money or demand constituting a real common measure the way length or weight would? And is the opening builder-shoemaker-shoe-house proportion meant to be the same kind of relation as the closing house-bed-minae arithmetic, or does Aristotle quietly shift from a geometric proportion (an equality between two ratios) to a simple arithmetic equivalence—and would that shift matter for whether the argument actually succeeds? I would rather not be steered back to the familiar material on chrematistics that I already know reasonably well; it is specifically this argument about demand, convention, and commensurability that I have not managed to reconstruct without it collapsing into circularity.
