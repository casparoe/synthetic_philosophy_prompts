A friend from my logic reading group and I had a long argument last night about Skolem's paradox that neither of us won. Her position was that the paradox simply dissolves once you accept that 'countable' and 'uncountable' are relative to a model rather than absolute features of a set, and that there is nothing more to say about it. I objected that this cannot be the whole story, since it does not explain why the relativity singles out cardinality the way it does: a countable transitive model of ZFC (assuming one exists) still correctly identifies which of its own ordinals are finite, and its version of omega just is the true omega — even though, from the standpoint of the universe in which the model is constructed, the entire model is countable, and so is every set inside it, including whichever one the model insists is an uncountable powerset. Finiteness survives the trip between model and universe unscathed; uncountability does not. Could you explain, in real mechanistic detail, why that asymmetry holds? Specifically, what exactly becomes of the bijection that, from outside, shows the model's powerset really is countable — and does this genuinely vindicate her claim that 'uncountable' fails to be an absolute feature of a set, or is that reading too much into a technical artifact?
