My friend Priya went through a breakup a few weeks back, and at one point she said, completely straight-faced, "it's over, but I don't believe it." I told her the sentence doesn't even make sense: if part of you doesn't believe its over, you can't just flatly assert that it's over in the same breath, so she's contradicting herself. She got annoyed and said no, she really does believe its over, fully, and she also doesn't believe it, and that isn't a contradiction, that's just what heartbreak feels like, and if it sounds contradictory to me that's a limit of my ear for language, not of her feelings. We argued about this for a good 40 minutes and neither of us gave an inch - I kept saying you can't hold P and not-P in one head at once, she kept saying beliefs aren't that tidy and people carry stuff like this around all the time without imploding. Afterward it hit me that this is basically G.E. Moore's old puzzle about "it's raining, but I don't believe it," so presumably real philosophers have actually fought over whether sentences like that are outright contradictions, or just absurd to say out loud, or something else again entirely. Could you write me a dialogue between two philosophers hashing this out properly? One should argue the weirdness lives entirely at the level of assertion - saying something true while giving a false impression of your own mind is a strange, self-undermining move, but it doesn't show the belief itself is impossible to hold. The other should argue it isn't just about talking - that the way self-knowledge actually works makes stably holding both halves at once, inside one head, genuinely unstable, not merely embarrassing to say aloud. I want them actually engaging with each other's best points instead of talking past each other, and have them end by figuring out whether they're even disagreeing about the same thing, or whether each one has just been right about a different half of the puzzle.
