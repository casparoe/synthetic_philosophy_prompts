I have been asked to give the toast at the wedding of a former doctoral student, and I would like your help getting it right. Elena wrote her dissertation with me on the structure of bad faith in Sartre, and she and her fiancé, Marcus, have made an unusual request: they do not want a toast that gestures politely at "freedom" and "authenticity" in the vague way wedding speeches borrow from philosophy for atmosphere, only to abandon the substance once things turn sentimental. They think that move would itself be a piece of bad faith. What they want instead is a toast that takes seriously what Sartre actually says about love and commitment: the account, in the chapter on concrete relations with others, of the lover who wants to be the Other's whole world and to be freely chosen as such forever, which Sartre treats as an inherently unstable demand, since any attempt to secure or guarantee that choice collapses the very freedom that made it worth having in the first place; and the discussion, in the pages on anguish, of the man who sincerely resolves never to gamble again and then discovers, on approaching the table a second time, that the resolution has no power at all over his present freedom. They want the toast to sit with the fact that, on Sartre's own terms, a wedding vow looks uncomfortably like the gambler's resolution, and then to find something true and worth celebrating on the other side of that discomfort, rather than changing the subject.

The difficulty is the room. One half is Elena's dissertation committee and her old Sartre reading group, who will notice immediately if I flatten facticity and transcendence into a single idea or misuse the Look; the other half is Marcus's family, almost none of whom have read a word of Sartre and who are there to see two people they love get married. I have drafted three versions of this toast myself: one turns into a short lecture on Being and Nothingness that the family will politely endure, one is warm and funny but quietly drops the philosophy by the second paragraph, and neither is what Elena and Marcus actually asked for.

Could you write the toast for me? It should run to about four or five minutes read aloud, use bad faith and radical freedom as genuine constraints on what can honestly be said about a lifelong promise rather than as decorative references, and still land, for everyone in the room, as a toast -- an act of affection for two specific people getting married -- rather than as a seminar, or worse, as an argument against marriage.
