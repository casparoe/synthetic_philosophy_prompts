So I went down a rabbit hole this week that started with an old Overcoming Bias post name-dropping Jim Joyce's "nonpragmatic vindication of probabilism" as basically the real reason to be Bayesian, as opposed to the Dutch Book argument (which always felt a bit too mercenary to me for something that's supposed to be a purely epistemic requirement, not a betting requirement). The rough idea I picked up is: your credences count as inaccurate to the extent they're far from the truth-values (0 or 1) they're tracking, and Joyce's move is to show that any incoherent credence function is "accuracy-dominated" by some coherent one -- i.e. there's a coherent alternative thats guaranteed to be at least as accurate, and sometimes strictly more accurate, no matter how the world turns out. So incoherence isn't just imprudent, its a straightforwardly epistemic mistake.

What I can't get my head around, and what my reading group couldn't really resolve either, is how much this whole argument apparently hinges on which specific measure of "inaccuracy" you plug in. I vaguely remember that if you use the naive/obvious measure -- just the absolute distance between your credence and the truth-value -- the domination result breaks down and coherence stops looking special at all. And I think I recall that Joyce's original 1998 axioms for what counts as a "legitimate" inaccuracy measure turned out to be too permissive -- someone (Maher? possibly misremembering) found a scoring rule that satisfied every axiom he listed but still didn't give the result he wanted, which forced a revision in a later paper. Could you actually walk me through why the argument is so sensitive to the choice of measure, what specifically goes wrong with the absolute-value version, and what that counterexample showed? I'd also love to understand how "strict propriety" of scoring rules ended up being the thing people converged on afterwards (I think via a cleaner, more geometric argument -- Predd et al maybe?) as the fix, and whether that's now the settled consensus in epistemic utility theory or if there's still live disagreement about which scoring rules should really count as "legitimate" measures of accuracy.
