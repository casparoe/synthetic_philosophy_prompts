The whole-brain criterion for death, still embedded in most legal definitions (including the Uniform Determination of Death Act), rests on the rationale that irreversible loss of all brain function suffices for death because the brain is the organism's indispensable integrator: the structure that binds the body into a single functioning whole. Alan Shewmon's well-documented cases of patients who meet whole-brain-death criteria yet continue for months or years on a ventilator to grow, heal wounds, fight infections, and, in a few pregnant patients, sustain a pregnancy to term are often invoked as decisive evidence against this rationale, since the organism plainly goes on functioning in an integrated way without any functioning brain. Yet the apparent alternatives are each costly: a purely circulatory-respiratory criterion looks arbitrary once brainless integration is conceded, a "higher-brain" or personhood-based criterion would count many breathing, unconscious patients as already dead, and a merely stipulative definition abandons the idea that there is a fact of the matter to be determined. Write an essay assessing whether Shewmon's evidence genuinely refutes the integrative-unity rationale for whole-brain death, and defend a considered verdict on which criterion for death, if any, survives the challenge, rather than simply cataloguing the options.
