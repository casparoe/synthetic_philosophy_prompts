I'm in a metaphysics reading group and we just finished Peter van Inwagen's Material Beings, and there's a move in it that's been bugging me for days. His whole case against restricted answers to the Special Composition Question (contact, fastening, cohesion, being glued, whatever) is that you can always build a Sorites-style series — like gradually welding two rods together — where composition would have to switch on or off at some perfectly precise point, and since he thinks existence can't be a vague matter, that's supposed to sink the view. That's basically what pushes him to organicism: the xs compose something iff their activity constitutes a life (plus the trivial case of just one thing). And he gets the problem of the many for stuff like clouds or mountains almost for free, since on his view there are no clouds or mountains to begin with — just simples arranged cloudwise, a manner of speaking, so there's nothing to be "many of."

But that trick isn't available for organisms, since organisms are exactly the composites he thinks are real. And "constituting a life" looks exactly as vague as "fastened together" to me — viruses, prions, slime molds mid-aggregation, a Portugese man o' war (a colony of zooids, not a single organism) are all borderline cases. And even a completely uncontroversial case, like an ordinary shedding cat, should generate the classic problem of the many all over again: there are tons of only-slightly-different aggregates of particles (with vs without some loose hair or flaking skin cell) that all seem equally good candidates for "caught up in a life." I vaguely remember his actual response involves just biting the bullet — something like, fine, there really are some enormous-but-finite number of "cats" occupying that spot on the mat, and our habit of saying "there's one cat" is just a loose, practical way of talking that doesn't track the exact mereological facts. That feels like exactly the kind of move he'd never let a defender of "fastening" get away with, but I'm having trouble turning that hunch into an actual argument. One guy in my group thinks I'm being unfair to van Inwagen and that this is all foreseen and handled fine in the book.

Could you help me build a real, careful argument that organicism doesn't earn its keep — i.e. that it's just as vulnerable to (or has to swallow something just as costly as) the vagueness/arbitrariness objection van Inwagen uses to eliminate rival restricted answers to the SCQ? I don't want a generic "vagueness is everywhere, gotcha" complaint — I want something that actually engages with his specific argument that a sharp cutoff in composition would require an impossible kind of "ontological" vagueness, and that explains why "life," and the problem of the many for organisms, doesn't get to dodge that argument the way he seems to think it does.
