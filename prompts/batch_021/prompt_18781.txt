I am a novelist most of the way through a novel structured as several branching timelines that share one cast of characters: my protagonist, Mara, survives a car accident in the branch I intend to keep and dies in the one I intend to cut, and two side characters become just aware enough of their counterparts in the other branches to grieve or envy them. My editor described the manuscript, approvingly, as "basically David Lewis's modal realism," and before I use that phrase in interviews I would like to have actually earned it. Please describe the most important implications of Lewis's real modal realism, as distinct from the popular gloss of "many worlds," for a project like mine: whether his commitment to counterpart theory means that the Mara who survives and the Mara who doesn't are strictly two people who merely resemble each other, not one woman appearing twice; whether his requirement that genuine possible worlds be causally and spatiotemporally isolated from one another rules out the cross-branch awareness I've written as any kind of depiction of real possible worlds at all; and whether, on his view, the branch I cut is any less real, or its ending any less final for the people in it, simply for never reaching the page.
