I spent my career as a field biologist studying alarm-call systems in birds, and since retiring I have been working through the philosophy of mind literature on the side. Lately I have become preoccupied with teleosemantics, particularly Ruth Millikan's proposal that a signal's content is fixed not by everything that happens to trigger it, but by whatever the mechanisms producing and consuming it were selected by evolution to track. What keeps nagging at me is the stock objection built on cases like the frog that will snap at a BB pellet flicked past its eye just as readily as it snaps at a real fly, even though the snapping response was shaped by evolution specifically for catching flies: teleosemantics wants to call the BB-pellet snap a misrepresentation, rather than concede that the response's real content was always the disjunctive "fly-or-small-dark-moving-thing." Could you write me a short story, set wherever you think best serves the idea, that dramatizes this exact tension between selected function and a recalcitrant triggering case -- built toward a moment where a character has to decide whether what they are witnessing is an error or an overlooked truth about what a signal has meant all along? Something in the neighborhood of 700 to 900 words would be ideal.
