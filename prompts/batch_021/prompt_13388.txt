**Question:** (a) Explain David Rosenthal's Higher-Order Thought (HOT) theory of state consciousness, distinguishing it from Higher-Order Perception (HOP) theories such as those defended by Armstrong and Lycan. (10 points) (b) One influential objection to HOT theory concerns the possibility of a "targetless" higher-order thought. Explain the objection and Rosenthal's response to it. (10 points)

**Student's answer:** (a) According to Rosenthal, a mental state is a conscious state just in case the subject has a roughly simultaneous, non-inferential, assertoric higher-order thought (HOT) to the effect that she is in that very state. This is what happens, for instance, when I consciously feel a pain: I am not merely in pain, but I have a HOT whose content is that I am in pain. Because it is the presence of a suitable HOT, rather than any intrinsic property of the first-order state, that confers consciousness, Rosenthal's theory is often described as relational or extrinsic. HOT theory differs from Higher-Order Perception theories, such as those defended by Armstrong and Lycan, in the nature of the higher-order state doing the representing: HOP theories model that state on the analogy of perception, positing a quasi-sensory, non-conceptual "inner sense" that scans first-order states, whereas Rosenthal's higher-order states are thoughts, deploying ordinary mentalistic concepts rather than anything sensory. Rosenthal is also what we might call an actualist about the HOT requirement: an actual HOT, not merely a disposition to form one, is needed to render a first-order state conscious, which is precisely the point on which Carruthers' dispositionalist HOT theory departs from Rosenthal's. So, on Rosenthal's view, a first-order state becomes a conscious one exactly when the subject introspects it, forming a HOT directed at that state; this is why, on Rosenthal's account, unconscious perception and masked-priming cases involve no accompanying introspective HOT and hence no conscious experience.

(b) A well-known problem for HOT theory is that, since the HOT is a distinct existence from its target and merely caused by, rather than identical to or constituted by, the first-order state, the relevant causal chain could in principle misfire, delivering a HOT to the effect that one is in state M when no token of M — or nothing at all — is occurring at the first order: a "targetless" HOT. Rosenthal's official response is to rule this possibility out by definition: he stipulates that a HOT only counts as the right kind of HOT, and hence only confers consciousness, when it is caused by, and thus accurately targets, a first-order state that is actually occurring. Since accuracy is built into the definition of a state-appropriate HOT, targetless higher-order thoughts of the sort the objection envisages are conceptually impossible on Rosenthal's view, so the objection fails to get any grip on the theory.
