I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, but a debate at work over whether large language models could ever genuinely "understand" something, rather than just manipulate symbols very convincingly, sent me down a rabbit hole. A colleague invoked Gödel's incompleteness theorems as proof that no computer program could ever fully match human reasoning, citing "the Gödel argument against AI" and mentioning John Lucas and Roger Penrose in the same breath, as if they'd made the same case. When I actually looked into it, though, most explanations I found online treated the two arguments as interchangeable, whereas the primary sources suggest Penrose didn't just restate Lucas's 1961 case in more modern dress — he substantially reworked it, apparently because the original ran into real trouble.

I'd like help understanding exactly what changed between Lucas's original argument in "Minds, Machines and Gödel" and the version Penrose develops across The Emperor's New Mind and Shadows of the Mind. I keep running into the claim that Penrose shifts the argument from talking about a system's consistency to talking about its soundness, and about whether that soundness could ever be "knowable," but without a logic background I can't tell whether that's a substantive fix to a real flaw in Lucas's reasoning or just a rephrasing that quietly dodges the same issue. I'd also like to know which specific objections to Lucas — Putnam's, for instance — Penrose was trying to get around, and whether his revised version actually succeeds where Lucas's didn't. Separately, since Penrose ties his conclusion to a speculative physical story involving quantum effects in neuronal microtubules, I'm curious whether that physical hypothesis does any real work for the anti-mechanist argument itself, or whether it's a wholly separate, more speculative claim bolted on afterward. On balance, whose version of the argument do you find more defensible, and why?
