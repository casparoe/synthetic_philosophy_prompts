I'm teaching a graduate seminar on the ethics of war this term, and we've just spent two sessions on Jeff McMahan's revisionist attack on Walzer's moral equality of combatants (MEC) thesis, followed by Seth Lazar's 2010 Philosophy & Public Affairs review essay, in which he presses what he calls the "responsibility dilemma" against McMahan's liability-based account of who may permissibly be killed in war. The dilemma, briefly, is this: set the degree of responsibility required for liability to lethal defense low enough to capture ordinary unjust combatants — who typically fight under some mix of propaganda, coercion, and limited information — and the same standard seems to implicate large numbers of civilians as well, since the causal and voluntary contribution of willing taxpayers, munitions workers, and propagandists to the unjust war effort is not obviously smaller than that of a coerced conscript. This is what Lazar calls the "near-total war" horn. Set the threshold high enough to shield those civilians, however, and most actual unjust combatants turn out to clear it too, so that even the just side may not permissibly kill them: this is the "contingent pacifism" horn, which threatens to collapse McMahan's revisionist project into pacifism about most real-world wars.

McMahan's preferred way out is to distinguish the deep morality of war from the law of war and the norms of battlefield convention: he can concede that few actual wars meet the standards set by the deep morality of killing, while still mounting a separate, practically oriented argument — appealing to combatants' epistemic limitations, the risks of self-serving abuse, and the value of a bright-line convention that keeps incentives for restraint intact on both sides — for retaining something like the traditional, symmetric rules of engagement in law and in a soldier's own practical reasoning. In seminar, two of my students dismissed this move as a face-saving dodge: once the underlying theory of individual responsibility yields verdicts nobody wants to live with, they argued, quietly switching to a different, convention-based argument for practical purposes just abandons the revisionist project rather than vindicating it. I'd like to see the strongest version of the opposing case. Please argue for the position that McMahan's distinction between the deep morality and the law of war is a principled, independently motivated way of absorbing the dilemma's "contingent pacifism" horn, rather than an ad hoc retreat — and be explicit about which horn (or horns) of the dilemma the distinction actually defuses, and which, if any, it merely relocates rather than resolves.
