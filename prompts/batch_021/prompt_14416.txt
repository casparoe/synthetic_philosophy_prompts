Outside of my day job as a backend software engineer, I've been spending my evenings reading up on AI alignment research, mostly out of personal curiosity rather than any formal training in philosophy or ML theory. One proposal that keeps resurfacing, and that came up again in a long Slack thread with a couple of colleagues last week, is "AI safety via debate" as a scalable oversight technique. The core idea, going back to a 2018 OpenAI paper, is that a comparatively weak judge (a human, or a weaker model standing in for one) can still supervise a much more capable AI system by having two copies of that system argue opposing sides of a question in front of the judge: since honest argument is supposed to be easier to verify than to fake convincingly, truth should win out in the end even though the judge could never have evaluated the underlying question unaided.

The more I've read around this, the more objections I've run into as to why this might quietly stop working once the debaters get much smarter than the judge, and I'm having a hard time telling how serious each objection really is relative to the others. The list I've collected so far is: (1) the "obfuscated arguments problem," where a dishonest debater buries a flaw inside a large tree of sub-claims so that the honest debater can never cheaply point to exactly where the flaw lives; (2) judge exploitability, where a debater wins by exploiting the judge's biases or rhetorical blind spots rather than by being correct; (3) collusion between debaters, since in practice both sides of the debate are typically instances of the very same trained model; (4) a length/complexity asymmetry, where the true position simply needs a much longer or more technical justification than the false one it's up against; (5) non-convergence of self-play training, i.e., the worry that gradient descent plus self-play has no particular reason to land on the "honesty is the optimal strategy" equilibrium the whole scheme depends on; (6) unfaithful question decomposition, where breaking a hard question into sub-questions, as debate and related amplification schemes both require, quietly loses or distorts information along the way; (7) train/deploy distributional shift, where a protocol validated on problems humans can already sanity-check gives little evidence about how it behaves on problems where no human has any traction at all; and (8) a hard ceiling on judge capacity, where some claims may simply be beyond what any feasible judge could ever adjudicate, no matter how the debate is structured or how much time is allowed.

Could you sort these eight failure modes into a tier list, from S (most fundamentally damaging to debate's core theoretical promise, in a way that no amount of clever protocol engineering seems likely to patch) down to D (a real but comparatively minor nuisance that better protocol design, more rounds, cross-examination, or similar tweaks could plausibly fix)?
