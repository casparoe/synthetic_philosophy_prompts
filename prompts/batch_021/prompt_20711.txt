I'm a backend engineer at a mid-sized SaaS company, and one of my recurring duties is writing postmortems after production incidents. Last month, following a nasty cascading failure in our caching layer that took checkout down for almost two hours, my team lead and I ended up in a surprisingly heated disagreement about how the postmortem itself should be written. She wanted the root-cause section organized around a general failure pattern we could state as a rule ("unbounded retry storms occur whenever a downstream dependency's latency exceeds the client-side timeout under sustained high concurrency"), so that we could scan the rest of our systems for the same pattern and fix it everywhere at once. I preferred writing it as a chronological narrative of what actually happened that night: which specific service degraded first, which specific on-call engineer made which specific decision at which specific time, and how one contingent choice fed into the next. Neither of us could really articulate why we felt so strongly about our preferred format, and we ended up just splitting the difference in a way that satisfied nobody.

Afterward I went looking for whether this disagreement had a name, and stumbled into an old debate in the philosophy of history between defenders of "covering-law" explanation, in the spirit of Hempel's deductive-nomological model, and defenders of narrative explanation, who argue that historical (and by extension organizational) understanding can't be fully reduced to general laws. I don't have any formal training in philosophy, so I'd like your help translating this into terms I can actually use at work: could you lay out the most important implications of the covering-law-versus-narrative debate for how a team like mine should structure root-cause analyses and postmortems? I'm especially interested in whether one approach is more vulnerable to hindsight bias or to quietly assigning blame, whether the two are ultimately reconcilable within a single document, and whether there's a principled way to tell when reaching for a general "law" is genuinely warranted rather than just imposing false generality on what was really a one-off fluke.
