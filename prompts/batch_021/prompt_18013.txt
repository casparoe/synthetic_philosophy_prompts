Here is the question as it was set: "Derek Parfit's Hitchhiker is dying of thirst in the desert. A driver who is a reliable predictor of behavior will only take her to town if he predicts that she will pay an agreed sum once she is safe, at which point paying is no longer necessary to secure the ride. (a) Explain why a causal decision theorist, once safely in town, will not pay, and why this makes causal decision theory indirectly self-defeating in Parfit's sense. (b) Does an evidential decision theorist do any better in this case? Explain why or why not. (c) Briefly explain how updateless or functional decision theory purports to handle the case differently, and say what this implies about whether instrumental rationality should be assessed act by act or at the level of dispositions and policies."

And here is the student's submission in full: "A causal decision theorist reasons that, once she is already in town, handing over the money cannot causally affect whether she was picked up, since that lies in the past; paying is now pure cost with no causal benefit, so she keeps her money. But the driver is a good predictor, so a driver facing a passenger who reasons this way would have predicted the refusal and simply left her in the desert — in effect, refusing to pay later retroactively causes the driver not to have picked her up in the first place. Since agents who correctly follow CDT end up dying of thirst while agents disposed to pay survive, CDT is indirectly self-defeating in Parfit's sense: acting on the theory makes things go worse by the theory's own standard than some alternative disposition would have. Evidential decision theory does better here, because for the evidentialist, paying is strong evidence that one is the type of person who gets picked up by predictors, and an action's evidential bearing on one's own type is exactly what EDT cares about; so an EDT agent pays, and CDT alone is left looking irrational. Updateless or functional decision theory generalizes this same idea by evaluating the policy or logical output that governs both the predictor's forecast and the later choice, rather than the isolated act in town, and it is this shift that recommends paying. This suggests that in environments with reliable predictors, rationality may need to be assessed at the level of dispositions or decision procedures rather than isolated acts, which is close to what Parfit seems to want when he argues that self-interest theory can be indirectly self-defeating."
