My father died two weeks ago after forty years running a small, well-regarded pasture-raised livestock farm. He had studied philosophy before he ever kept an animal, and he was unusually explicit about the reasoning behind how he ran it. He built that reasoning on Nozick's brief, exploratory remarks about animals in Anarchy, State, and Utopia -- passages that raise the problem without quite resolving it -- and then resolved it for himself: animal suffering counts and must be minimized on broadly utilitarian grounds, which is why he was fussy about pasture space, slaughter method, and veterinary care, but animals lack the further, inviolable side-constraints that he thought protect persons, and only persons, from ever being used merely as a means. He was consequently untroubled, in principle, by painlessly killing an animal that had had a good life -- a conclusion he refused to extend to a person under any circumstances, no matter how good that person's life was or how freely that person had consented. I am giving the eulogy to a room that is half his lifelong farming community and half my sister's friends from the animal sanctuary she runs; she and my father argued lovingly and inconclusively about exactly this for twenty years. Please write a eulogy that states his actual position precisely enough that neither side can mistake it for something cruder -- not "animals are just property," not "he secretly agreed with the sanctuary" -- while still working as a eulogy that unites the room rather than restaging the argument.
