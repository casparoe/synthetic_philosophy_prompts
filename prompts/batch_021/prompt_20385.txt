ok I need an essay out of you on the backward-induction paradox in the centipede game -- partly research for my own qualifying paper on epistemic game theory, but mostly because I can't decide what I actually think here.

Quick setup in case it's useful: Aumann's result says common knowledge of rationality (CKR) in a perfect-information game forces the backward-induction outcome, so full CKR in the centipede game should get you defection right at node 1. But Pettit & Sugden's classic complaint (Reny makes a similar point) is that this is kind of self-undermining -- the backward induction argument needs claims about what a rational player would do at nodes that, by the theory's own lights, a rational player should never let you reach. So the moment somone actually deviates, has CKR just been falsified? And if so, what licenses still treating the other player as rational going forward? Binmore and Aumann sparred over whether that's a genuine contradiction or just conflating "what-would-happen" counterfactuals with real belief updating, and Stalnaker and Samet each tried rescuing something backward-induction-like with belief-revision or "hypothetical knowledge" machinery instead of plain CKR.

Write me an essay that actually engages that debate (not the "but people cooperate in the lab" point, that's a separate issue). Walk through why the paradox is a genuine problem for treating backward induction as the uniquely rational solution, give the rescue attempts a fair hearing, then commit to a real verdict: does backward induction survive as normatively binding once off-path beliefs are sorted out properly, or does something else -- CKR itself, the whole common-knowledge-of-rationality setup -- have to go? No "both sides have a point" cop-outs, actually pick a lane and defend it.
