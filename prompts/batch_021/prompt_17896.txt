The question, worth 10 points, read: "Describe Thomson's Loop case, a variant of the trolley problem. Explain specifically why this case poses a problem for the version of the doctrine of double effect that distinguishes the permissible switch case from the impermissible footbridge case by asking whether the one person's death is a means to saving the five or merely a foreseen side effect of saving them. Then state briefly what you take the case to show about the doctrine of double effect more generally." Here is the answer one of my students submitted:

"In the Loop case, a runaway trolley is heading toward five people on the main track, but the agent can divert it onto a side track. Unlike the ordinary switch case, this side track loops back and rejoins the main track before it reaches the five, so if nothing else were in the way the trolley would simply loop around and kill them anyway. However, there is one very large man standing on the loop, and if the trolley hits him, the impact will stop it before it can complete the loop, so diverting the trolley saves the five at the cost of his life. This creates a problem for the version of the doctrine of double effect that separates the switch case from the footbridge case by asking whether the one person's death is merely a foreseen side effect (switch) or an intended means (footbridge): in Loop, exactly as in Footbridge, the one man's body is what physically stops the trolley, so his death is doing causal work as a means to saving the five, not merely occurring alongside the rescue. Since most people nonetheless judge Loop to be permissible, much like the ordinary switch case, and not on a par with Footbridge, this shows that whether an agent acts directly, with her own hands, versus indirectly, via a mechanism like a switch, cannot be what is really tracking our intuitions, because Loop has the mechanical, hands-off structure of Switch while sharing the means relation of Footbridge. This is essentially Thomson's closeness problem: because the harm in Loop can be redescribed at a fine enough grain that the man's death looks like a side effect of stopping the trolley rather than the point of the action, and because this kind of redescription is available in more or less any case, the intended/foreseen distinction the doctrine relies on threatens to collapse across the board. I take this to show that our judgments about these cases are ultimately being driven by the resulting balance of lives saved and lost rather than by anything to do with intentions, so the doctrine of double effect should really be given up in favor of a straightforwardly consequentialist treatment of these dilemmas."
