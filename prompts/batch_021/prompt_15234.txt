I write about science and ideas for a general-interest publication, and I'm working on a piece about the paradoxes of rational choice that surface once we drop the assumption that an agent can always remember what she has already decided. I'd like to dramatize the cleanest instance of this, the absent-minded driver problem, as a dialogue rather than as exposition. The setup: a driver must get home via a highway with two consecutive, visually identical exits, and she needs the second one. Taking the first exit yields a payoff of 0, taking the second yields 4, and overshooting both yields 1. Because the two intersections look identical, and because she has no memory, once she reaches one, of whether she has already passed the other, she can act only on a single disposition — some probability p of exiting — applied identically at whichever intersection she happens to be facing.

Write a dialogue between two decision theorists watching from the passenger seat as she nears the first intersection. One holds that the only defensible standard of rationality here is the planning-optimal one: derive, from the outside, the p that maximizes expected payoff before the drive begins, and have the driver simply execute it, whatever her in-the-moment intuitions suggest. The other holds that rational choice must instead be action-optimal — vindicated at the moment of choice itself, from the driver's own epistemic position at the intersection — and suspects that reasoning honestly from her actual credence, right then, about which intersection she is facing might not vindicate that same p, or that the credence cannot even be fixed without already presupposing an answer to the very question at issue. Have the two of them work through the arithmetic out loud, hold each other to it, and press on the exact point at which the other's notion of rationality gives out, rather than letting either side retreat into agreeing to disagree.
