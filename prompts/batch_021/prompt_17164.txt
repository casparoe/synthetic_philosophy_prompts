In a reading group on formal epistemology, we recently spent an entire session on Bas van Fraassen's "Judy Benjamin problem," and I still don't feel I have a fully satisfying intuitive grip on it. The setup: a soldier's prior credence is spread uniformly over four possibilities formed by crossing "Red territory" versus "Blue territory" with "First Company area" versus "Second Company area." She then receives a radio message telling her, in effect, that if she is in Red territory, the odds are 3-to-1 that she is in the Second Company area rather than the First — a piece of information that, on its face, says nothing about whether she is in Red or Blue territory at all. What makes the case notorious is that if she updates by choosing the new credence function that maximizes entropy subject to that single conditional constraint (equivalent here, since her prior is uniform, to minimizing the Kullback–Leibler divergence from that prior), her credence that she is in Blue territory shifts away from one-half — even though the message seems entirely irrelevant to the Red/Blue question. The case is usually presented as a problem for treating entropy-maximization as the "natural," minimal-change way of updating on conditional evidence.

What I would find genuinely useful is a second example with exactly this structure, but dressed up differently enough that it can't just be pattern-matched onto the original: a different cover story, a different partition (it needn't be a two-by-two grid the way the original is), and different numbers for both the prior and the conditional odds. Please state your uniform prior, state the piece of conditional evidence received, and then actually work through what happens under maximum-entropy updating — showing why the probability of some proposition the evidence doesn't seem to touch nonetheless moves, and by how much. Finally, add a few sentences on what feature of the underlying mathematics is doing the work here, and on whether you take the result to be a mark against entropy-maximization as an updating rule, or rather a sign that our everyday sense of when evidence is "irrelevant" to a proposition was never a reliable guide to genuine probabilistic independence.
