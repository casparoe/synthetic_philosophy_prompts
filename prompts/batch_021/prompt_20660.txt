I'm taking a graduate seminar on formal epistemology this semester, and we've just moved from the Dutch Book argument for probabilism to Jim Joyce's accuracy-dominance argument, which he explicitly bills (in the title of his 1998 paper) as a "nonpragmatic vindication of probabilism" meant to improve on the older betting-based argument. In section, our TA suggested that Joyce's argument basically supersedes the Dutch Book argument, since it grounds coherence in purely epistemic considerations rather than in facts about hypothetical bets, and I'd like to push on that claim rather than just accept it.

First, I'd like you to spell out carefully what "nonpragmatic" is actually supposed to mean here, and then assess whether Joyce earns the label. A strictly proper scoring rule rewards or penalizes a credence depending on how the world turns out, which structurally looks a lot like a wager against nature, and Joyce has to build in substantive "legitimacy" constraints on which inaccuracy measures are even allowed into the argument; I want to know whether that move genuinely removes the pragmatic element or just relocates it into the choice and defense of the scoring rule. Second, I've separately been bothered by something like Selim Berker's "separateness of propositions" worry about accuracy-first, consequentialist approaches to epistemic rationality: because an accuracy-dominance theorem scores your whole credence function additively, it seems to permit trade-offs in which the rational credence you should have toward one proposition is partly determined by accuracy considerations that really concern some entirely different proposition, which sits oddly with the thought that your degree of belief in P should answer only to your evidence bearing on P. I want to know whether the Dutch Book argument is actually any better off on this front, given that a Dutch book is also built by bundling bets on several propositions into a single package on which the agent is assessed as a whole, or whether this "aggregation across propositions" problem is really distinctive to the accuracy-first approach.

Finally, stepping back from these two specific worries, I'd be curious to hear your own overall assessment: of the Dutch Book argument and the accuracy-dominance argument, which do you think currently gives probabilism the more secure foundation, and why? Feel free to disagree with the way I've set up the comparison if you think I'm running together considerations that should be kept apart.
