**Question (15 points, 5 per part):** In "Counterpart Theory and Quantified Modal Logic," Lewis holds that de re modal claims about an individual are made true by facts about that individual's counterparts in other worlds — things resembling it closely in the relevant respects — rather than by the individual itself possessing modal properties in, or existing in, those worlds. In a well-known footnote to *Naming and Necessity*, Kripke objects that this delivers the wrong truth conditions for ordinary de re modal claims, using the example of Hubert Humphrey and the 1968 election. (a) State Kripke's objection precisely. (b) Explain how a counterpart theorist might reply to it. (c) Kripke also holds that "transworld identity" is not a genuine problem requiring a theory in the first place. Explain why, with reference to his distinction between stipulating a possible situation and discovering one.

**Student's answer:**

According to Lewis, a claim like "Humphrey could have won the election" is not made true by Humphrey himself possessing the property of winning in some other world; it is made true by one of Humphrey's counterparts — someone in another world resembling him closely enough in the relevant respects — winning there instead. Kripke's objection is that this misdescribes what we mean: when Humphrey contemplates the counterfactual situation in which he wins, he is not thinking about, or caring about, the fortunes of some other, merely similar person; he wants to know about himself. On this objection, counterpart theory changes the subject, substituting a claim about a look-alike for a claim about Humphrey.

A counterpart theorist can reply that the complaint has no special force against counterpart theory specifically, since an analogous question can be pressed against any theory that grounds de re modal truth in some cross-world relation rather than in the brute possession of properties. Even on a transworld-identity view, what makes "Humphrey could have won" true is a fact about a merely possible individual existing in a different, causally isolated world; that this individual is strictly identical to Humphrey does not obviously give present-day Humphrey any more reason to care about his fortunes than a resemblance relation would. So the objection either proves too much, or it fails to discriminate between the two theories.

Kripke's own view is that the demand for a theory of transworld identity rests on a confusion, since it treats possible worlds as distant places we discover and must then identify individuals within by their qualitative profile, as though observing them through a telescope. On his view, we do not locate Humphrey in another world by matching descriptions; we simply stipulate, in describing a possible situation, that it is a situation involving Humphrey, in just the way we can stipulate that a story concerns this very coin without surveying every coin in the story to see which one qualifies. Kripke's account of transworld identity thus supplies the missing qualitative criterion directly: an individual in another world counts as Humphrey just in case he is Humphrey's closest continuer there, i.e., the most similar candidate along the dimensions — biological origin, especially — that Kripke takes to be essential to him. This is how Kripke can maintain that an individual's essential properties are fixed across every world in which he exists, without having to help himself to Lewis's counterpart relation to do the same job.
