While reading up on the Vienna Circle, I noticed that two of its associates ended up attaching the same word, "picture," to strikingly different theoretical projects, and I'd like help working out how substantive the resemblance really is. Wittgenstein's Tractatus holds that every meaningful proposition is a picture of a possible state of affairs, sharing a logical form with what it depicts; crucially, the picturing relation itself cannot be stated but only shown, which is part of why Wittgenstein treats his own theoretical remarks as a ladder to be climbed and then discarded. Otto Neurath, working in the same milieu, spent much of his career instead developing Isotype, a literal visual "international picture language" of standardized pictograms meant to convey statistical and social facts across barriers of literacy and national language — and he was notably hostile to talk of the mystical or the unsayable, preferring to picture the growth of knowledge through his own well-known image of sailors who must rebuild their ship at sea, plank by plank, with no dry dock to retreat to and no ladder they might climb up and then kick away.

Could you compare these two notions of "picture" in some depth? I'm especially interested in whether the terminological overlap is largely coincidental, given how differently "depicting" functions in each account, or whether there's a more substantive point of contact worth drawing out — for instance, does Neurath's picture language ever end up relying on something like Wittgenstein's showing-but-not-saying idea (is there anything an Isotype chart communicates that couldn't, in principle, be fully paraphrased into ordinary physicalist sentences), or is Isotype precisely designed to avoid any such ineffable remainder, in keeping with Neurath's general impatience with the closing sections of the Tractatus?
