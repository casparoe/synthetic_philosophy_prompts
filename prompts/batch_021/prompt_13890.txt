I'm working on a feature about the private metaphysical commitments of great twentieth-century scientists and mathematicians, and Kurt Gödel is turning out to be the strangest case among them. Everyone remembers him for the incompleteness theorems, but going through his correspondence I found that he also mounted a serious, carefully reasoned argument for personal survival after death — not as an article of religious faith, but as a conclusion he thought followed from premises he took to be nearly self-evident. The occasion, sometime in the early 1960s, was a letter from his mother back in Austria, who had asked him outright whether he believed anything of a person continues once the body dies.

As best I can reconstruct his answer: Gödel held that the world is "rational" in a strong sense — that nothing in it happens without a reason, and nothing is ultimately in vain — and he thought this had to be true quite apart from the truth of any particular religion. He then observed that an ordinary human life lets a person develop only a small fraction of their intellectual and moral capacities before it ends, rather the way someone might spend decades mastering a skill only to die just as they were finally in a position to use it well. Given a genuinely rational world, he argued, that kind of wholesale waste cannot be the end of the story, so whatever it is that develops over the course of a life — Gödel's preferred Leibnizian term was the "monad" — must go on developing somewhere else after death. He reinforced this with a further Leibnizian move: a mind, being simple rather than composite, cannot be destroyed the way composite things are destroyed, namely by their parts coming apart, so the mere biological fact of bodily death is not, on its face, evidence that the monad itself has been annihilated.

I'd like to set aside Gödel's much more famous, purely formal proof of God's existence entirely — that's a distinct piece of logic with its own history, and I don't want it folded into this. What I need instead is a genuine essay on the afterlife argument by itself: reconstruct it as precisely and charitably as you can, work through its strongest and weakest steps — including whether "the world is rational" is an independently defensible premise or simply a restatement of the conclusion, and whether the monad argument trades on a slide between a mind's ceasing to exist and its merely losing its connection to a body — and then reach and defend an actual verdict on whether this amounts to a serious piece of philosophy or a rationalization dressed up in technical vocabulary. Please commit to a reading rather than leaving the question open.
