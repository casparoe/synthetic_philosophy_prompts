I spent almost forty years as a fish taxonomist before I hung up my hat, and I never took a philosophy class in my life until I retired and picked up a stack of old library books out of boredom. Last week I ran across this old Chinese text, Gongsun Long's White Horse Dialogue, where he argues dead serious that "a white horse is not a horse" -- and the whole School of Names crowd around him spent their careers on puzzles just like that, about wether names actually carve up the world the way we assume they do. It hit close to home, because half my career was spent having arguments of exactly that shape: cladists will tell you theres no such thing as "fish" as a real category, since tetrapods evolved from within that group and leaving legged critters out doesn't give you a real clade, and I've watched colleagues nearly come to blows over whether some isolated population counts as a real species or is just a subspecies with delusions of grandeur. So, what are the most important implications of the White Horse Dialogue and the whole School of Names names-vs-actualities project for how working scientists ought to think about disputes like that?
