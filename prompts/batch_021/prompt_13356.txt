I am a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I have been trying to move past the pop-science version of the Doomsday Argument to understand the actual technical dispute underneath it. As I understand it, the classic argument rests on the Self-Sampling Assumption (SSA): treat your birth rank as a random draw from all humans who will ever live, so that finding yourself relatively early shifts your credence toward a smaller total population, that is, sooner extinction. But I have also read that the Self-Indication Assumption (SIA) — which weights hypotheses by how many observers they predict — is supposed to exactly cancel that shift, since a hypothesis with far more people simply gives you far more chances to be the one asking the question. At the same time, SIA has its own famous problem: the "presumptuous philosopher" case, where it seems to let you out-argue physicists from the armchair just by favoring whatever cosmological theory posits more observers. Could you compare SSA and SIA as competing anthropic principles, explain precisely why SIA cancels the doomsday update rather than merely counterbalancing it, and then tell me which of the two — if either — you find more defensible once each one's characteristic failure mode is factored in? Please justify your verdict.
