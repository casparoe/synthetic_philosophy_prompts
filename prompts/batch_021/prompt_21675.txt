For the past few weeks I have been reporting a piece on the boom in political and financial prediction markets — Polymarket, Kalshi, and the rest — and one detour keeps pulling me off course: the economist Robin Hanson's decades-old proposal for what he calls "futarchy."

The idea, in brief, is that a society would first vote democratically on a single measurable definition of national welfare, and then hand every substantive policy decision to conditional prediction markets rather than to legislators, on the theory that speculators wagering real money will forecast more accurately than politicians which option actually raises that welfare measure. Hanson's own shorthand for the scheme is "vote on values, bet on beliefs," and it is the cleanness of that division that I cannot stop poking at: it sounds airtight in a working paper, but I suspect the line between what a community wants and what will get it there blurs considerably the moment you imagine the mechanism applied to one actual, contested decision rather than to policy in the abstract.

That is why I would rather see it worked through in fiction than in another explainer piece. Could you write me a short story, on the order of 1,000 to 1,500 words, set in a near-future town, workplace, or small polity that has adopted a futarchy-style mechanism to settle one genuinely contested, fairly local decision, rather than restaging some sweeping future-history? I would like at least one point-of-view character who has to live with the market's recommendation without being a trader herself, so the story can register the gap between an aggregated, incentive-compatible forecast and what the decision actually costs a particular person. I would also ask you to steer clear of the well-worn objections to prediction markets as such — whales moving the odds, thin markets, insider trading — since I am after something that dramatizes the quieter problem underneath the whole "vote on values, bet on beliefs" framing: that collapsing the good of a community into one number, fixed once and voted on, is already a substantive and contestable value judgment, not the neutral piece of plumbing the slogan implies. An ambiguous or unresolved ending is entirely welcome; I would rather have something that sits with the difficulty than a tidy fable with a moral tacked on at the close.
