So I've been kind of obsessed with Ben Franklin's autobiography this month, i got into it after seeing one of those "13 virtues" printables going around on productivity instagram, and unlike everyone just reposting the list, I actually read the whole book. This somehow turned into a whole argument between my friend Marcus and his girlfriend Ellie while we were all repainting his new apartment, and I couldn't referee it so now I'm bringing it here.

Ellie started it, saying Franklin's virtue-chart project is basically the original self-improvement hack and kind of ahead of its time: the guy tracked his own faults on a little grid every day for years, openly admits he never got anywhere close to perfect, but still says the attempt left him a "better and happier man" than he'd otherwise have been, which she thought was more mature than most productivity gurus today who pretend their system actually works. Marcus pushed back and said not so fast, because earlier in that same book Franklin describes deliberately wheeling his own paper through the streets of Philadelphia in a wheelbarrow so people would see him "at it" and think he was industrious, and later flat-out says that with Humility specifically he never got the real thing, only the appearance of it — so isn't the whole project just reputation management with extra steps? Ellie said that doesn't actually undercut it, because even if you start out faking a virtue for appearances, doing the fake version over and over is literally how habits get formed, so eventually it stops being fake — the starting motive shouldn't matter if the end state is the same. Marcus said okay then explain the part about Order, where Franklin basically gives up and tells this story about a guy who wants his axe totally polished, gets sick of turning the grindstone for the smith, and decides he "likes a speckled axe best" — his read was that this is Franklin admitting the habit loop just doesn't always finish, and that he's rationalizing a quit rather than proving the method works. Ellie's comeback was that Franklin immediately follows that up by saying a person with literally zero faults would probably just be envied and hated by everyone around him, so maybe staying a little "speckled" on purpose is its own kind of wisdom, not a cop-out at all.

Nobody backed down, we just went back to painting trim. Could you go through this move by move and say who actually had the stronger point at each step, not just an overall winner, and call out anything you think both of them are missing? And underneath all that, I guess the real question bugging me is whether Franklin's own book actually backs up this "fake it till it's real" theory of virtue, or whether his own honesty about failing kind of quietly undermines it.
