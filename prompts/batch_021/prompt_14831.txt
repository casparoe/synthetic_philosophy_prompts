Over the past year I've been teaching myself about betting and investment strategy in my spare time. I have no formal background in math or economics — just a lot of late nights on forums, a secondhand book on the Kelly criterion, and a cheap spreadsheet. The idea that has stuck with me most is the gap between what happens to the average member of an imagined crowd of bettors and what actually happens to any single one of them over time. The example that finally made it click for me — I later learned it's sometimes called "the Peters coin toss" — goes like this: imagine a fair coin flip that increases your current wealth by 50 percent if it lands heads, and cuts it by 40 percent if it lands tails. The "expected" multiplier per flip is 1.05, a 5 percent gain, so a bettor who wagers everything every round should, on paper, get richer forever on average. But if you actually track one person doing this over and over, their wealth collapses toward zero, because what governs a single trajectory over time is the geometric mean of the two multipliers (about 0.949), not the arithmetic one. I gather this is the whole reason the Kelly criterion says to bet only a fraction of your bankroll rather than going all-in on a favorable bet, and it's apparently also behind a broader idea I've seen floating around forums, "geometric rationality" — the notion that this ensemble-versus-time-average gap should be basic to rational choice rather than a footnote to expected-value theory.

I'd like a joke built around exactly this gap, something I could tell to a couple of friends from my regular poker game who already know what the Kelly criterion is and would groan appropriately. I don't want a generic "gambling is risky" or "math nerd" joke; the punchline should only really land for someone who understands why maximizing expected value and maximizing expected growth rate are different projects, and ideally it should play on the idea that the "average bettor" who comes out ahead is really a kind of statistical fiction that no actual person ever gets to be. A short one-liner or a brief exchange between two characters would both work, whichever serves the joke best.
