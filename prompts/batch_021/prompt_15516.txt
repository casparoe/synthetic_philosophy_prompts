I'm a PhD student in political philosophy, currently drafting a dissertation chapter on whether Mill's harm principle can survive contact with contemporary media technology. One passage I keep returning to is Mill's discussion in On Liberty of the opinion that corn-dealers are starvers of the poor: he holds that such an opinion ought to go unmolested when merely circulated through the press, but may justly be restrained when delivered to an already-excited crowd gathered outside a corn-dealer's house, or handed round that same crowd as a placard. My advisor keeps pushing back on my instinct to treat this as equivalent to Holmes's famous "shouting fire in a crowded theatre" analogy from Schenck v. United States, and I've come around to thinking she's right that the two arguments turn on different things: Holmes cares about imminent, foreseeable danger, whereas Mill's corn-dealer passage seems to turn on whether the audience's rational agency is still doing any real work, or whether the speech is instead operating directly on an already-inflamed crowd, more like a match to dry tinder than a contribution to deliberation.

What I'd like from you is a genuinely new test case for that second distinction - not campus hate-speech codes, and not the fire-in-a-theatre scenario, since I already treat both elsewhere in the chapter. Please construct a concrete, detailed scenario involving some feature of a real or realistic modern information environment - an engagement-maximizing recommender system, a coordinated bot-driven amplification campaign, or a generative-AI system producing personalized persuasive content, for instance - in which it becomes genuinely unclear which side of Mill's line the speech act falls on. Make the example specific enough to stand on its own: who says or produces what, how it spreads, and what harm results. Then argue the case using Mill's own reasoning, rather than importing later free-speech doctrine: does it come out as protected opinion, as punishable incitement, or does it instead reveal that Mill's distinction quietly presupposes a kind of audience, and a kind of public, that no longer straightforwardly exists?
