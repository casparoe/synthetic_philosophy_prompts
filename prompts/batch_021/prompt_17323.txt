Last night a friend and I spent almost two hours arguing about whether it's ever rational to prefer not knowing something, and neither of us really won. The argument started because her cousin is eligible for predictive genetic testing for Huntington's disease and has chosen not to take it, even though nothing more than a blood draw and a short wait stands between her and a definitive answer. My friend thinks her cousin is making a perfectly reasonable choice. I disagreed, and brought up something I'd once come across called Good's theorem: I. J. Good proved, in a 1967 paper often described as a formal vindication of Carnap's older "requirement of total evidence," that for an idealized Bayesian decision-maker, the expected utility of gathering more evidence before acting is never lower than the expected utility of deciding without it — provided the evidence is free, and the agent updates on it correctly and then acts optimally afterward. On paper, a perfectly rational agent should therefore always want to look, never look away. My friend granted that the math probably works out that way in Good's own terms, but argued that this only shows the theorem is too narrow to say anything about her cousin's actual situation, since it quietly assumes a single decision-maker facing a single fixed decision problem with a fixed utility function — and once you allow that finding out can change the shape of that problem itself, say by destroying the peace of mind that uncertainty currently supplies, or by altering how relatives and insurers treat her afterward, the theorem's guarantee no longer obviously applies. I answered that "peace of mind" is just another preference that a sufficiently rich utility function should already price in, so it isn't a real counterexample at all, just an argument for modeling her cousin's preferences more carefully. Neither of us budged.

I'd like you to write a short story that takes this dispute somewhere neither of us managed to reach on our own: a compact philosophical fable, something like 1000 to 1500 words. Center it on a character who has, or can readily obtain, some concrete, cost-free way of learning a consequential fact about their own future — the setup needn't be medical, or even realistic, so feel free to use a speculative or slightly uncanny device or setting if that serves the dilemma better than a literal doctor's office would. Please don't stack the deck for "always look" or "sometimes look away," and don't have any character deliver the theory out loud; let the plot itself — through what the character does and what happens as a result — make it legible why a result like Good's theorem feels so compelling in the abstract, and why thoughtful people still sometimes have good reason to prefer not knowing. The ending should leave genuine doubt, until fairly late, about which choice was actually the wiser one.
