A friend and I spent much of last night arguing about her longtime bookkeeper, and I still cannot locate the flaw in her position, even though it strikes me as clearly mistaken. She insists that she simply knows the bookkeeper is honest, based on fifteen years of working together. So when I pointed out several irregularities in the accounts that looked troubling, she replied that they must have some innocent explanation, because if she genuinely knows he is honest, then anything that looks like evidence against this has to be misleading — and she is therefore entitled to set it aside without investigating further. What unsettles me is that her reasoning seems hard to fault on its own terms: if knowledge requires truth, then evidence against something you truly know must indeed be misleading evidence, so why shouldn't she discount it in advance? I would like to understand exactly where, if anywhere, this reasoning breaks down, and what it should tell me about how much weight I owe to new evidence that tells against beliefs I currently feel very confident in.
