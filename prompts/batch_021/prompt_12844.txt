So, through a weird chain of events (a friend who's a postdoc needed a "community member" with zero ties to the university for their IACUC — that's the committee that signs off on animal research protocols — and apparently I qualified because I just write backend code for a logistics company and have literally never set foot in their bio building), I've been sitting on my university's animal care and use committee for about a year now. Most of our agenda is routine, but next week we've got a renewal request that's split the room: a neuroscience lab wants to continue a study that induces a chronic neuropathic pain model in macaques to test a new spinal-cord stimulation implant, the eventual goal being a device for humans with treatment-resistant chronic pain (phantom-limb pain, failed back surgery cases, that kind of thing). Our committee's standard move is a "harm-benefit analysis" — basically weighing the animal suffering against the expected human payoff — and by that math this protocol clears the bar easily given how big the potential benefit is. But one member, who actually has a philosophy PhD (unlike me), won't sign off on those terms. Her argument is that just running the aggregate math treats the monkeys as nothing but line items, and that at some point you're not allowed to do that to a being that cognitively sophisticated no matter what the spreadsheet says.

Trying to actually prep for this instead of winging it, I went down a rabbit hole and found that Robert Nozick — I'd only ever heard of him because of the "experience machine" thing — apparently ran into this exact problem in Anarchy, State, and Utopia. From what I can tell he floated something like "utilitarianism for animals, Kantianism for people," where people's rights work as absolute side-constraints on what can be done to them but animals just get weighed in the aggregate calculation, and then more or less admitted himself that the split looks arbitrary and doesnt obviously survive contact with edge cases — why does the math-vs-rights line fall exactly on the species boundary, what about humans below whatever cognitive threshold is doing the work, what about animals (like, arguably, macaques) above it. Which is basically our committee's exact fight, just with better vocabulary than we're using.

Could you write the actual memo I'll circulate before Thursday's meeting? Whatever it ends up recommending — approve as-is, reject, or approve with specific modifications (fewer animals, an earlier euthanasia endpoint, whatever) — I need it to land on one concrete recommendation, not a "here are both sides" shrug. And whichever way it lands, it needs to seriously engage with the side it's ruling against instead of dodging the hard part by hiding behind "we ran the harm-benefit process" or "the regulations were satisfied." I want it to actually take a position on whether the species line (and the marginal-cases problem with it) is defensible here, since that's exactly what my colleague is going to raise regardless of which way we land.
