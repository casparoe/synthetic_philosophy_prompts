Hey, I'm putting together a graduate seminar for next term on logic and language in classical Chinese philosophy -- pairing Gongsun Long and the Later Mohists with a bit of Frege and Quine at the end, basically asking what "equivocation" even means once you leave Indo-European grammar behind. There's one move in the 白馬論 (the "White Horse Dialogue," one of the few surviving chapters of the 公孫龍子, traditionally credited to Gongsun Long of the School of Names, c. 4th-3rd century BCE) that I keep failing to pin down properly. Everyone knows the punchline, "a white horse is not a horse," but the actual argument behind it is more interesting then the punchline, and I don't think I've ever layed it out carefully for myself before. Below is my own rough translation -- I've smoothed the syntax and added speaker labels ("Q" and "Gongsun Long") that aren't in the original, but tried to keep the inference itself intact:

Q: Is it acceptable to say, "a white horse is not a horse"?
Gongsun Long: It is.
Q: How so?
Gongsun Long: "Horse" is what we use to name a shape; "white" is what we use to name a color. To name a color is not to name a shape. Hence: a white horse is not a horse.
Q: But surely there being a white horse can't count as there being no horse. And if it doesn't count as there being no horse, then isn't there a horse there? So a white horse just is a case of there being a horse -- why call the white one "not a horse"?
Gongsun Long: When someone asks to be brought a horse, a yellow horse or a black one will each do. When someone asks to be brought a white horse, a yellow one or a black one will not do. If a white horse simply were a horse, then what's being asked for in the two cases would be one and the same thing, and "white" would make no difference to it. But it plainly does make a difference: a yellow horse can satisfy "bring me a horse" but not "bring me a white horse." Since one and the same yellow horse satisfies one request and not the other, the two things being requested are not the same. Hence: a white horse is not a horse.

What I can't settle is the logical anatomy of that second argument, the "seeking a horse" bit. On one reading it's a perfectly respectable non-identity argument -- horse and white-horse differ in a property (only one is satisfied by a yellow horse), so they're not identical, full stop, and the whiff of paradox is just an artifact of translating the conclusion as "is not a" rather than "is not identical to." On another reading something more specific to the grammar is doing the real work: I recall Chad Hansen argued that classical Chinese nominals like 馬 function more like mass terms than count nouns -- denoting a mereological whole ("horse-stuff") rather than a bounded kind -- in which case "white-horse-stuff is not horse-stuff" comes out true in a flat, part-whole sense with no equivocation needed at all. (Graham and some others were pretty unconvinced by that, if I remember right.) A colleague of mine in linguistics just says the whole thing is a scope ambiguity in disguise and nothing more, which feels too quick to me but I can't say exactly why. Could you help me work through what's actually doing the logical work in the "seeking a horse" passage, and how the identity/class-membership story and the mass-noun story relate -- are they rivals, compatible, does one subsume the other -- before I have to stand in front of a room of students pretending I've had this figured out for years.
