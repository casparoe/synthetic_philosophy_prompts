I spent my career as an observational astrophysicist, and it is only in retirement that I have had time to read philosophy in any depth. Frank Ramsey's name was already familiar to me from his foundational work on subjective probability and expected utility, which quietly underlies much of the Bayesian statistics I relied on throughout my working life. What I only recently discovered is his short essay "Epilogue," in which he argues that the sheer physical scale of the universe gives us no genuine reason to feel insignificant, since importance, on his view, is an inherently human-scale, perspectival matter rather than a further fact that could be read off from astronomy. I find it a nice irony that the expected-utility framework he helped pioneer is precisely the engine behind Nick Bostrom-style "astronomical waste" arguments in contemporary longtermism, which invoke the vast number of potential future people to argue that reducing existential risk should dominate our present moral priorities. Given his own stance in "Epilogue," would Ramsey's deflationary response to cosmic pessimism about the vastness of space commit him to being equally deflationary about the moral urgency longtermists derive from the vastness of future time and population, or is there a principled way of extending his view that lets him reject scale-driven despair while still endorsing scale-driven priority-setting?
