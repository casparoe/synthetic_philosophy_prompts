I'm a PhD student in the history of economic thought and the graduate representative on my economics department's Prizes and Awards Committee, which is reconsidering our annual "Adam Smith Prize" for the best undergraduate essay on economic liberalism. A student petition wants the prize retired, arguing that Smith's economics supplied intellectual cover for British imperial expansion; several faculty want to reject the petition outright, citing his description of the East India Company and similar chartered monopolies, in Book IV, chapter 7 of the Wealth of Nations, as among the "worst of all governments," and his conclusion that the colonial trade monopoly left Britain as a whole with "mere loss instead of profit." I think the same chapter actually cuts against both sides. The petition skips over Smith's preferred alternative to simply losing the colonies: incorporating them into Britain with parliamentary representation proportional to their tax contribution, hardly an anti-imperial position. The faculty response skips over the fact that Smith's case against empire is almost entirely about what the monopoly cost British consumers and taxpayers, not about any wrong done to the colonized — even his lament over "the savage injustice of the Europeans" toward native peoples sits inside a chapter mostly concerned with Britain's own balance sheet. I need a short recommendation memo before next week's meeting, choosing among retaining the prize as is, retiring it, or keeping it under a revised name with a rewritten citation. Please draft that memo: give a clear recommendation, argue for it from what Smith actually wrote rather than slogans about whether he was "for" or "against" empire, and represent the losing side's strongest version rather than a caricature of it.
