I spent about 35 years running a pharmacology lab before I retired, and now that I finally have time to read instead of just skim methods sections, I've been reading a lot of philosophy of science stuff lately, especially around the replication crisis and the whole frequentist/Bayesian fight. It's made me second-guess stuff we used to do without thinking twice, like checking the data every few animals and stopping once things looked significant, to keep the animal numbers down.

Two of my former postdocs were over for dinner recently and ended up arguing about exactly this, and I still cant decide who had the better of it.

Meg, who's stayed pretty old-school statistically, said that whole "peek and stop once p<.05" habit was p-hacking by another name: every time you look and could stop, you inflate the real false-positive rate well above 5%, and no amount of careful writeup afterward undoes that.

Alex, who went full Bayesian back in grad school, said this is exactly the kind of problem Bayes factors were built to sidestep, since they only depend on the data you actually collected and not on when or why you stopped -- so switching would've made the whole peeking issue disappear.

Meg answered that this doesn't remove the fudging, it just relocates it: instead of quietly choosing when to stop, you're quietly choosing what prior to put on the effect, and in a small lab where everyone badly wants the drug to work, that prior isn't going to get picked any more innocently then the stopping point was.

Alex said the two aren't really equivalent -- the stopping-rule bias never shows up anywhere in the final paper, nobody reading it could ever catch it, while a prior sits right there in the methods section for a reviewer to question or rerun with a different number.

Then Meg brought up something she'd read recently: that with enough data you can apparently get a p-value like 0.001 while a Bayesian analysis of those exact same numbers increasingly favors the null -- and asked what's the point of switching frameworks if the two can stare at identical data and flatly disagree.

Nobody at the table had a good comeback to that one, and it's been bugging me ever since. Could you go through the whole exchange move by move, say who's actually got the better argument at each point (not just who "wins" overall), and flag anything you think both of them are missing?
