I'm a backend/ML engineer at a startup that builds an "agentic" assistant capable of taking real actions on a user's behalf — booking travel, rescheduling meetings, and, in our upcoming release, making small purchases or editing files in a connected cloud drive without asking for approval on every single action. I sit on our internal Autonomy Review Board, a small cross-functional group (two engineers, one PM, one person from legal) that has to sign off before any new autonomy feature ships. The feature currently up for review is what the team internally calls the "commitment preamble": before a user grants the agent a broader permission scope (say, autonomous spending up to $200/month, or write access to files outside a sandboxed folder), the agent is prompted to generate a short, specific, user-facing statement of self-imposed limits it says it will respect (e.g., "I will not authorize any single charge above $50 without confirming with you first"). The statement is logged and shown to the user as a trust signal, but nothing about it is technically enforced at runtime — there's no code checking the agent's later actions against what it promised, since the whole point, according to the feature's designers, is to offer some assurance in exactly the cases where hard-coding every constraint isn't feasible.

At our last meeting, one of the other engineers said the design reminded her of a philosophy thought experiment she'd read about in college, something like Kavka's toxin puzzle: a wealthy eccentric offers you a huge reward if, by a certain deadline, you genuinely intend to drink a vial of toxin the next day that will make you painfully ill but do no lasting harm, and he has some reliable way of telling whether you truly intend it — crucially, you collect the reward just for intending, whether or not you actually go through with drinking it later. She argued our feature has the same structure: we grant the valuable thing (permission) in exchange for a stated intention, with nothing tying the agent's subsequent behavior back to that intention once the permission is already banked, so we shouldn't be reassured either way by what the agent "said" it would do. Half the board thought this was a genuinely useful analogy showing the commitment preamble is close to worthless as a safety measure and should be paired with, or replaced by, hard technical limits; the other half thought the analogy mostly breaks down — a trained model's stated behavior isn't the output of a self-interested agent quietly deciding whether to renege once it's gotten what it wants, it's closer to a disposition, and plenty of ordinary trust relationships (employee handbooks, professional codes) work reasonably well on self-reported commitments without perfect enforcement, so the comparison proves less than she thinks.

Could you draft the memo I'll bring to the board on Monday? I need an actual recommendation — approve as-is, reject, or approve with specific conditions — with reasoning laid out in a way the whole board can follow, not just the half that already agrees with me. I'd like it to take the toxin-puzzle comparison seriously enough to say exactly where it does and doesn't hold, rather than either waving it away as a cute analogy or treating it as decisive, and to close with concrete next steps and a rough timeframe for revisiting the decision.
