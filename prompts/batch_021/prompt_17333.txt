I've been reading about trolley problems on my own for a while now: the standard case where you can divert a runaway trolley onto a side track where it kills one person instead of five, versus the version where the only way to save the five is to push one person off a footbridge into the trolley's path. Almost every explanation I've come across says the moral difference between these two cases rests on what's called the doctrine of double effect, and that the idea traces back to a specific passage in Aquinas. So I tracked down the actual text: Summa Theologica, Part II-II, Question 64, Article 7, which asks whether it is lawful to kill a man in self-defense (the old Dominican Fathers translation). Here is the core of it:

"Nothing hinders one act from having two effects, only one of which is intended, while the other is beside the intention. Now moral acts take their species according to what is intended, and not according to what is beside the intention, since this is accidental... Accordingly, the act of self-defense may have two effects: one is the saving of one's own life, the other is the slaying of the aggressor. Therefore this act, since one's intention is to save one's own life, is not unlawful, seeing that it is natural to everything to keep itself in being, as far as it can. And yet, though proceeding from a good intention, an act may be rendered unlawful if it be out of proportion to the end. Wherefore if a man, in self-defense, uses more violence than is necessary, it will be unlawful; whereas if he repel force with moderation, his defense will be lawful... Nor is it necessary for salvation that a man omit the act of moderate self-defense in order to avoid killing the other man, since one is bound to take more care of one's own life than of another's."

I can follow this sentence by sentence, but I'm stuck on something bigger. Aquinas is describing someone defending himself against a person who is actively attacking him. The one person on the side track, or on the footbridge, hasn't attacked anyone; the trolley is the threat, and the real question in that case is what you're permitted to do to an uninvolved bystander for the sake of other uninvolved bystanders. Does Aquinas's distinction between what's intended and what's merely beside the intention, together with his proportionality condition, actually give you the resources to handle that kind of case, or does stretching his reasoning from "defending yourself against your own attacker" to "sacrificing one bystander to help others" smuggle in some extra assumption that isn't in this passage at all? I would like to understand what Aquinas himself is actually claiming before anything else, and then get your sense of how much weight this passage can bear when it is invoked to explain the trolley cases.
