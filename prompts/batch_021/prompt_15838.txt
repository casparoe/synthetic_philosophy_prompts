Two friends of mine who both work on the same applied-AI team -- one in safety policy, the other on model behavior -- got into a long argument at dinner that amounted to a rerun of the old debate between consequentialists and deontologists, except with an actual design decision riding on it: should their company's assistant reason more like a utilitarian or more like a deontologist when it receives an ethically loaded request? I'd like help sorting out who had the better of it, not just overall but at each stage of the exchange.

(1) Mia argued that the assistant should run on firm, near-exceptionless rules -- never help deceive a third party, never manipulate, never assist with a serious rights violation, full stop -- because once a model is allowed to weigh "how much good this deception does" against "the rule against deception" on a given occasion, it has effectively become a system that will rationalize much worse violations whenever its own, possibly mistaken, read of the consequences tells it to; better a reliably constrained system, she said, than a fallible calculator with access to millions of people's private conversations. (2) Raj replied that this was too easy: a rule against "ever helping deceive a third party" is either so broad that it also forbids things we obviously want the assistant to do -- help negotiate a salary, keep a surprise party secret, write a flattering cover letter -- or so riddled with carved-out exceptions that it is really just cost-benefit reasoning wearing a rule's clothing, and the only remaining question is whether the model does that reasoning openly or pretends it isn't. (3) Mia granted that the line-drawing problem is real but denied that this collapses the distinction: there is a difference between a rule with a few narrow exceptions, fixed by asking what the practice of honesty is even for, and a genuine case-by-case weighing of harm to a specific, uninformed person; needing a boundary somewhere doesn't mean the boundary has to be drawn consequences-first. (4) Raj pushed back that "what the practice is for" is just consequentialist reasoning one level up, and raised a further problem: if everyone knew the model would bend its rules whenever its internal estimate of the stakes was high enough, that alone would destroy trust in it -- a real cost -- but he took this as a reason for the model to reliably act as if it were bound by hard constraints, not as a reason for it to actually be forbidden from ever crossing them; a model that lets a preventable catastrophe happen and says "the rule is the rule" isn't displaying integrity, it's malfunctioning. (5) Mia's rejoinder was that "act as if constrained, except when the stakes are truly overwhelming" is just a threshold dressed up as a rule, and that someone still has to decide where that threshold sits and how confident the model must be before it starts lying -- which reintroduces exactly the manipulable, self-serving calculation she was trying to design out from the start. Neither of them budged, so I said I'd look for an outside read.

Could you go through moves (1) through (5) and say, for each one, whether the person is right, wrong, or right about one part and wrong about another -- rather than just declaring an overall winner? Please also flag anything important that neither Mia nor Raj notices, and finish with a short paragraph giving your own view on whether hard, exception-resistant constraints or explicit case-by-case weighing is the more defensible way to build this kind of system.
