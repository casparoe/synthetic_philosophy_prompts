On the Alignment Forum and in various LessWrong posts on multi-agent safety, I keep running into the "commitment races" problem: because sufficiently sophisticated agents — via updateless decision theory, self-modification, or just old-fashioned Schelling-style credibility — can lock in a bargaining stance before a counterpart does, and because whoever commits first effectively forces the other side to best-respond to a fait accompli, there is pressure to commit to favorable terms as early as possible, even "earlier" than calendar time would suggest once you allow for reasoning about logical time. The lesson I keep drawing from this is that if you were designing an advanced AI system that might end up bargaining or trading threats with another AI, the dominant strategy seems obvious: make the system commit to the most favorable stance it can credibly hold, as fast and as hard as possible, since any hesitation just hands the first-mover advantage to whichever agent commits first.

A friend from my local rationalist meetup insists this gets the point of the literature backwards — that Kokotajlo and others present the commitment race as a problem to be defused rather than a strategy to be embraced, and that this is supposedly why people have proposed things like surrogate goals and safe Pareto improvements instead of simply telling AI developers to "commit early and often." I don't see why that would be the wrong takeaway, though: if committing first is genuinely what determines who wins the bargain, doesn't the internal logic of the argument itself recommend racing to commit, rather than warning us away from it? I would appreciate help seeing where my reasoning breaks down.
