Question (worth 20 points on our take-home exam): In his 1615 Letter to the Grand Duchess Christina, how does Galileo argue that apparent conflicts between scripture and claims about the natural world should be resolved? Discuss his use of Augustine, the doctrine of accommodation, his treatment of the Joshua passage (Joshua 10:12–13), and how his position compares to Cardinal Bellarmine's roughly contemporaneous letter to Foscarini.

Sample answer: Galileo's guiding assumption is that scripture and nature are both expressions of God's word, so a passage correctly interpreted and a natural fact correctly demonstrated cannot really contradict one another; where they seem to, the fault lies with the interpretation, not the demonstration. He backs this with Augustine's rule that when a proven truth about nature conflicts with the literal sense of an ambiguous passage, we should prefer a figurative reading rather than reject the proof, since a literal reading later shown false would bring scripture itself into disrepute. Galileo extends this with the doctrine of accommodation: scripture was written for ordinary, unlettered people, and so it frequently speaks in the idiom of everyday appearances -- describing God as angry or repentant, or the sun as rising -- not because these are literal claims, but because that language served scripture's real purpose, which is moral and spiritual instruction rather than natural philosophy. On this basis, Galileo treats the Joshua passage as a purely poetic description of a miraculously lengthened day, with no genuine astronomical content, so that it poses no obstacle to Copernicanism either way. This puts him at odds with Bellarmine, who, in his letter to Foscarini, agreed that Copernican astronomy had already been demonstrated but insisted that the Church should wait for official doctrinal clarification before revising its reading of scripture.
