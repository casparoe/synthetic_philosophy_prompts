I'm deep in the research for a novel set in Bentham's household at Queen's Square Place in the last years of his life, narrated in part by an invented young clerk who is helping sort the manuscripts and comes across the boxes Bentham's actual editors were never allowed near -- the essay sometimes titled "Offences Against One's Self," written in the 1780s, in which Bentham applies the felicific calculus with total consistency to what he called paederasty and finds nothing in the arithmetic that justifies the gallows. He never published it in his lifetime; Louis Crompton didn't get the material into print until 1978, and men were still being hanged in England for the very offence Bentham had dismantled on paper as late as 1835, three years after he died. My reading group spent a whole evening arguing over whether this makes him a gay-rights hero born a century and a half too early, and I came away wanting to write my clerk's discovery scene from inside the argument rather than settle the question for him on the page.

To get that scene right, I've arranged a proper interview with a Bentham scholar who has spent years with this material, and in the one public talk of hers I've been able to track down, she waves off the obvious question -- why a man willing to publish plenty of other unpopular conclusions left this one in a drawer for forty years -- with some version of "he was a careful lawyer, not a martyr." I don't want that line to survive our actual conversation intact. Could you draft eight to ten interview questions, each paired with a short follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to reach for? I want at least one question that presses on the suppression puzzle itself and on whether the "cautious lawyer" answer secretly concedes something Bentham's own framework says shouldn't count; one on how he can show that a law rests on nothing but antipathy without that same test quietly undoing the disgust-based judgments he wants to keep, toward cruelty say; and one on how far his consent-based reasoning is committed to travelling once it's followed out of the case he's remembered for and into the other "irregularities" the same manuscripts take up, where consent gets much harder to define. End with a question asking her to name the piece of evidence that would most damage the flattering, proto-gay-rights reading of him, so the interview doesn't just end up confirming the story I already like.
