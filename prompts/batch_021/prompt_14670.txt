Here's the question as she wrote it:

(a) State Jeffrey's rule for updating credences across a finite partition {E_1,...,E_n}, and explain what the "rigidity" assumption is doing in the derivation.
(b) Show explicitly that strict/ordinary conditionalization is the special case of Jeffrey's rule where experience makes you certain of one cell of the partition.
(c) You're trying to figure out whether a bottle of wine is corked (H). Your prior is P(H) = 0.4. You've calibrated your own nose before, so you know P(H | smells off) = 0.7 and P(H | doesn't smell off) = 0.1, where E = {smells off, doesn't smell off} is the obvious partition. Tonight you've got a stuffy nose, so a sniff doesn't make you certain either way - it just shifts your credence that the wine smells off from an even 50/50 up to 0.8. Use Jeffrey's rule to find your new credence that the wine is corked, and show your work.
(d) Suppose right after this your dinner companion also pulls a face and says the wine "might taste a little off too," shifting your credence over a second partition F = {tastes off, doesn't taste off} via the same kind of update. If you apply Jeffrey's rule for the smell-evidence and then the taste-evidence, does it matter which one you do first? Explain.

And here's Owen's answer, in full:

(a) Ordinary conditionalization tells you what to do when you learn some E for certain: P_new(X) = P_old(X|E). But most of our evidence isn't like that - glancing at something in bad light might make you more confident it's green rather than blue without making you certain. Jeffrey's rule handles this: if experience directly resets your credences over a partition {E_1,...,E_n} to new numbers P_new(E_i) summing to 1, then for any H, P_new(H) = Σ_i P_old(H|E_i)·P_new(E_i). This relies on "rigidity": your conditional credence P(H|E_i) stays fixed across the update, so what changes is only the weight on each E_i, not how relevant each E_i is to H. (This move is also called "imaging" the old distribution onto the new partition-weights - the term's from David Lewis, who wanted a way to shift credences around without any cell becoming certain.) Without rigidity the rule isn't well-defined, since we'd have no fixed point to anchor the weighted average to.

(b) If experience makes you certain of one cell, say P_new(E_k)=1 and P_new(E_i)=0 for i≠k, the sum collapses to one term: P_new(H) = P_old(H|E_k). That's just strict conditionalization on E_k. So strict conditionalization is the special case of Jeffrey's rule where the new distribution happens to sit entirely on one cell.

(c) P_new(E) = 0.8, P_new(¬E) = 0.2. By rigidity the conditionals don't move, so: P_new(H) = (0.7)(0.8) + (0.1)(0.2) = 0.56 + 0.02 = 0.58. [Sanity check: the old P(E) must've been 0.5, since 0.7x + 0.1(1-x) = 0.4 gives x = 0.5, so this all hangs together - you got more confident in something that's evidence for corkedness, and your credence in corkedness rose accordingly, from 0.4 to 0.58.]

(d) No, order doesn't matter here, for the same reason it doesn't for ordinary conditionalization. We just showed in (b) that Jeffrey's rule is nothing more than conditionalization generalized into a weighted average, and conditionalizing on E1 then E2 gets you to the same place as E2 then E1 - both just give P(·|E1∩E2). Since Jeffrey updating is just a continuous, weighted version of that same underlying operation, running the smell-update and the taste-update in either order just mixes the same conditional probabilities in a different sequence, and mixing/averaging doesn't care what order you add the terms in. So you end up in the same place either way.
