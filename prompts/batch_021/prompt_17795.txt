I'm about two-thirds into a novel set in a made-up provincial court -- vaguely 17th century, but I've fudged the geography and titles so I'm not on the hook for every historical detail -- and I've hit a scene I can't crack. My narrator is clerk to an old magistrate who, one night after too much wine, lays out that Pascal bit from the Pensées about justice and force: that since we could never actually make what's just strong, we quietly made what's strong "just" instead, and the whole thing only holds up as long as most people never figure that out.

What I want isn't the novel itself, just a stand-alone short story, 2000 words or under, that I can raid for tone. Something where a minor official stumbles onto this "secret" about the law he enforces and has to decide, over the course of one day, whether to tell anyone. The bit I keep botching is the ending -- Pascal himself seems to think exposing this to ordinary people is actually dangerous, not just embarassing, and I want the story to sit with that instead of resolving into an easy "the truth will set you free" moral. Extra credit if you can dramatize how, on his view, the newly-disillusioned and the genuinely wise end up in totally different places even though they've both seen through the same illusion -- that distinction is the part I actually can't figure out how to put into a plot.
