And here's the student's answer, transcribed as-is (typos and all):

"Anscombe wrote 'Mr Truman's Degree' in 1956 to protest Oxford's decision to give Truman an honorary degree, and her central charge is that the atomic bombings were murder because they involved the intentional killing of the innocent. The doctrine of double effect, which she lays out for the first time in this pamphlet, is what lets her make the charge precise: an act that kills civilians as a foreseen but unintended side effect of striking a legitimate military target can be permissible, but an act that kills civilians because their deaths (or the terror those deaths cause) are the very mechanism by which the goal is acheived cannot be, no matter how good the goal is. Hiroshima and Nagasaki were the second kind of case: the cities were targeted precisely because killing large numbers of civilians was expected to break Japan's will to keep fighting, so the killing was intended, not merely foreseen.

Given this, her objection is not a consequentialist one. She isn't arguing that a proportionality calculation comes out negative, that the deaths outweighed whatever was gained by ending the war sooner. She's arguing that intentionally killing the innocent is absolutely forbidden regardless of the balance of consequences, which is exactly the kind of moral absolute she thinks consequentialism (a term she coins two years later, in 'Modern Moral Philosophy') can't accommodate: in that essay she says that anyone who thinks it's even an open question whether judicially executing an innocent person could be justified by good enough consequences shows, in her words, 'a corrupt mind.' Since Anscombe was a committed pacifist who held that no killing, even in self-defense, could ever be justified, it follows that she would have condemned the bombings on these same grounds even if they had targeted only military installations and killed civilians purely as a foreseen side effect."
