I've been on a bit of a Kolmogorov complexity kick lately after falling down a Less Wrong rabbit hole (some of the Sequences, the old Occam's razor wiki page, a couple of Solomonoff induction explainers). I think I finally got the basic pitch: Kolmogorov complexity formalizes "simplicity" as the length of the shortest program that outputs your hypothesis on some fixed universal machine, and Solomonoff induction basically says the prior probability of a hypothesis is proportional to 2^-(that length), so shorter programs get more prior weight. This made me feel like Occam's razor isn't just a vibe about liking tidy theories, its an actual mathematical result about induction, which felt like a satisfying upgrade.

But then a friend and I got into it last night and I couldn't resolve the thing that was bugging me. If simplicity just means "shortest description," why doesn't "an intelligent being made the universe exactly like this, for its own reasons" beat basically any physical theory on offer? It's like a dozen words, versus pages of field equations and boundary conditions to even state something like the standard model. I have this nagging half-memory of some lightning-bolt-and-Thor example from the Sequences that was supposed to defuse exactly this move, but I can't reconstruct the actual argument, and neither me nor my friend could figure out where the trick is supposed to be, since the "shortest description wins" framing really does seem to just be about length. So which is it — is my intuition that something's wrong here correct, and if so where exactly does it break down? Is it about what's allowed to count as a "description," or a prior-vs-posterior thing, or something about how the choice of universal machine matters, or am I just missing the point of the formalism entirely? I'd like the actual mechanics of the fix, not just a one-line "that's not really simple, trust me."
