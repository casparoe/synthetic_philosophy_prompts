I'm a second-year Classicist at Oxford, and this term's set text for my ancient philosophy paper is Plato's Laws. I also sit on my college's Wine Committee, which advises the Governing Body on what is served at formal hall. Our alcohol policy says wine there should be served "in a manner appropriate to the welfare of all present," without saying what that means, and after a fresher was taken to A&E from last term's Freshers' Formal having drunk far more than she was used to, the Dean wants that clause read as requiring a ban on wine at any formal first-years attend until after Christmas. Our Laws tutor, who also sits on the Committee, objects that this reverses the Athenian Stranger's actual argument: just as facing danger under supervision trains soldiers to master fear, he holds, facing wine under a sober president trains the young to master pleasure, and he treats Sparta's and Crete's outright prohibition as the position he is arguing against, not for. Other members think invoking a fourth-century-BC case for supervised drinking to justify serving eighteen-year-olds strains the analogy past its breaking point, and that our actual welfare obligations should settle this regardless of what a well-run symposium could once achieve. Draft the memo the Committee will send to the Governing Body recommending a concrete policy for this year's Freshers' Formals, with a stated review date, and give whichever side loses -- the Platonic case or the pastoral one -- a serious hearing rather than a token one.
