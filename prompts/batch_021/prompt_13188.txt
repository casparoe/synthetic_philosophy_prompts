I spent nearly forty years as an experimental physicist working with ultracold atomic gases, so the question of what makes two particles "the same" or "different" was, in a very concrete sense, part of my daily work long before I ever framed it as philosophy. Since retiring I have joined a small philosophy discussion group, and a recent session on Leibniz's Principle of the Identity of Indiscernibles left me rather unsettled. We spent most of the evening on Max Black's 1952 thought experiment about a universe containing nothing but two perfectly similar spheres, some fixed distance apart, sharing every intrinsic quality, and yet supposedly numerically distinct. It struck me at once how closely this resembles, and yet perhaps does not resemble, the identical particles I dealt with for decades: electrons subject to the Pauli exclusion principle, bosons condensing into a single quantum state, and so forth. I have not been able to settle in my own mind whether Black's puzzle and the quantum case present the same challenge to Leibniz's principle in different dress, or whether they are importantly different.

I would like you to compare the two directly. Some philosophers of physics defend a weakened version of the Identity of Indiscernibles against the quantum case by arguing that particles such as a pair of electrons in a spin-singlet state are only "weakly discernible": neither has any intrinsic property the other lacks, but the two are individuated by an irreflexive relation, such as "has opposite spin to," that holds between them without ever holding of anything to itself. It seems to me that Black's two spheres could be weakly discerned in exactly the same formal sense, simply by the relation of being some fixed distance apart, since nothing is ever some nonzero distance from itself. Yet the appeal to distance in Black's own dialogue is usually dismissed as failing to individuate the spheres at all, on the grounds that it is either circular or equally true of each sphere considered on its own. So: is there a principled reason the distance relation should fail where the spin relation is thought to succeed, or does consistency require treating both cases alike, either as vindicating a relational reading of the Identity of Indiscernibles or as refuting it? I would appreciate a considered verdict, rather than a mere survey of the positions on either side.
