I spent most of my career as an experimental physicist doing Bayesian analysis of particle-detector data, and since retiring I have been working my way through formal epistemology as a kind of second education. One thing that startled me was learning how much of orthodox Bayesian rationality rests on an assumption of logical omniscience: because credences are required to obey the axioms of probability, an ideally rational agent must assign probability 1 to every logical and mathematical truth, and identical credence to any two logically equivalent statements, whether or not the agent could ever discover the relevant proof. No real reasoner, biological or artificial, can live up to this outside of trivial cases, and I hadn't appreciated how open a problem this remains until a former colleague, now doing AI-safety work, pointed me to MIRI's 2016 "logical induction" paper by Scott Garrabrant and several coauthors, which tries to give a fully constructive, computable account of how a bounded reasoner should assign credences to mathematical statements it has not yet proved or refuted.

The construction, as I understand it, treats the sentences of a fixed formal language as assets in a kind of stock market: a countably infinite population of computable trading strategies buy and sell "shares" in sentences at prices set by a market mechanism, and the sole governing constraint -- the logical induction criterion -- requires that no trading strategy running within bounded computational resources can drive its own wealth to infinity by exploiting mispriced sentences. From that single no-arbitrage-style condition, the authors derive a long list of properties one would want from a reasonable scheme of logical credences: convergence to 1 or 0 on anything eventually proved or refuted, calibration, non-dogmatism, and an uncanny ability to track statistical regularities among sentences well before any individual instance is settled. What I cannot work out to my own satisfaction is whether this actually dissolves the philosophical problem I started with or merely relocates it into more tractable mathematics. In its favor, the whole apparatus falls out of one intuitively motivated constraint rather than a list of stipulations chosen for convenience, and it delivers determinate, computable answers exactly where classical probability theory and deductive logic fall silent. Against it: every guarantee in the theorem is asymptotic, so at any actual finite stage of reasoning -- the only place a real agent is ever situated -- the credence assigned to something like the twin prime conjecture is substantially underdetermined and would differ across equally legitimate inductors; and the individual trading strategies must themselves already be capable of a good deal of clever logical reasoning to spot an exploitable mispricing in the first place, which leaves me wondering whether the framework explains our capacity for logical reasoning or simply presupposes it and hides the presupposition inside the traders.

I would like you to write me an essay that treats this as a live question rather than a settled one: does the logical-induction framework, or approaches in its spirit, amount to a genuine solution to the problem of logical uncertainty and logical omniscience for computationally bounded reasoners, or does it at best furnish an elegant mathematical proxy that leaves the underlying philosophical question unanswered? Please weigh the strongest form of each side -- feel free to bring in considerations I have not raised here, including, if it seems apt, comparisons to other asymptotic or "in the limit" vindications of a method, such as Reichenbach's pragmatic defense of induction -- but do not conclude with an even-handed shrug. I would like a definite verdict, argued for rather than merely asserted.
