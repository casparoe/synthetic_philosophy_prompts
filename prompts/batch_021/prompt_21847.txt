My opa died last week at 96 and I got roped into giving the eulogy this Saturday, which is terrifying because I write backend code for a living and have never given a speech in my life, and also because this isn't a simple "he was a wonderful man" situation. He was drafted into the German army in early 1943 at 18, spent the rest of the war as a radio operator (kinda funny, since I do networking stuff now, feels like an inherited trait), and got captured by the Americans a few months before it ended. He never once pretended the regime he fought for wasn't evil, never went to any veteran reunions, and as far as anyone in the family knows he never actually fired at another person and once got chewed out for sneaking food to a couple of downed Allied airmen he was supposed to be guarding. Basically the most sympathetic possible version of "grandpa fought for the wrong side," but still, the wrong side.

Here's my actual problem. I fell down a rabbit hole this week reading about just war theory, specifically this idea called the moral equality of combatants — the traditional Walzer-ish view that whether a soldier's doing something wrong depends only on how he fights (does he target civilians, does he treat prisoners decently, etc) and not on whether his side's cause was just, so a decent conscript and a decent GI are morally in the same boat as soldiers even though their governments obviously weren't. Then apparently theres a whole newer wave of philosophers, McMahan is the name that keeps coming up, arguing that's too easy, that fighting cleanly for an unjust war doesn't get you off the hook the same way it would if the cause were just, even though being a scared drafted 18-year-old can still excuse you personally for a killing that wasn't actually permissible. My opa seemed to hold some version of both of these his whole life without ever hearing of either theory — he'd tell us flat out he did nothing in those two years he was ashamed of, and then in the same breath say nobody should call him a good man for 1943 to 1945.

I need to actually write this eulogy for Saturday and I have no idea how. It has to be honest about all of it, no glossing over which uniform he wore, it can't turn into a philosophy 101 lecture since most of the room is his old card-club friends and a bunch of my teenage cousins, but my uncle is a WWII history buff and will absolutely notice if I flatten the moral complexity into something too easy. Can you write me a eulogy that threads that needle — something honest enough to survive my uncle's raised eyebrow but that still lands as comfort for a room of non-philosophers who just lost their friend and grandfather?
