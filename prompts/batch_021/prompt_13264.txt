I live on a small private road maintained by a road association, and two of its board members, Priya and Dan, got into an extended disagreement at last week's meeting that the rest of us couldn't settle.

Six households pay annual dues to the association and vote on its decisions: grading, snow removal, and a proposed gate at the entrance. Three further households down the same road rely on it every day to reach their driveways, but were never enrolled as members, because an old survey put their lots on a separate parcel map decades ago; they hold a legal easement to use the road but have no vote in the association. A motion is now before the board to admit those three households as full voting members, and the debate went roughly as follows.

1. Dan argued that the three households should get a vote: they use the road daily, their vehicles cause as much wear as anyone else's, and they will be just as affected by a gate or a fee increase as any paying member. Denying a say to people who are bound by the rules every day isn't fair, whatever an old survey happens to say.

2. Priya replied that "affected" is too generous a standard: if the association enfranchises everyone meaningfully affected by the road, it would also have to include the mail carrier and the neighbors who use it as a shortcut to the trailhead. Membership has to be drawn somewhere, and "who signed the original maintenance agreement" is at least a stable, principled line, not an arbitrary one.

3. Dan answered that stability isn't the same as fairness: the original agreement was drawn up fifty years ago by people who simply left the other three households off it, for reasons that had nothing to do with anyone's actual stake in the road. Treating that old line as principled just launders an old exclusion as though it were a considered decision.

4. Priya countered that every association has to start with some membership list drawn up by somebody at some point, so noting that the current line has a historical origin doesn't show it's arbitrary in any damning sense; by that logic, no membership rule anywhere could ever be legitimate. She added that the three households neither fund the association nor bear its liabilities, so it's reasonable that they have no say over how it spends money they don't contribute.

5. Dan's closing point was that the upcoming vote on whether to admit the three households will itself be held only among the six current members, meaning the people whose exclusion is in question have no say in whether it continues. That, he said, is what makes the current arrangement indefensible, regardless of how it originated.

Nobody at the meeting could agree on who had the better of this. Could you go through the disagreement point by point, assess who is right at each stage, note anything important that both Priya and Dan are overlooking, and then give an overall verdict on how the association should decide (a) who gets to vote on the membership motion, and (b) what the outcome of that vote should be?
