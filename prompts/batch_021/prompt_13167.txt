After forty years as a bench chemist, I have spent my retirement finally reading the philosophy I never had time for, and Benjamin Franklin's Autobiography has occupied our small reading group of fellow retirees for several sessions now -- partly, I confess, because I feel a certain kinship with a fellow experimentalist. What strikes me most is how laboratory-like his "project of arriving at moral perfection" really is: thirteen virtues, a little ruled notebook with a column for each day of the week, a black mark entered for every lapse, and a deliberate order of practice -- one virtue at a time -- chosen so that mastering the earlier ones would make the later ones easier to acquire. He kept at this discipline for decades and, by his own account, never fully succeeded: he confesses he remained hopeless at Order, and late in the project a Quaker friend informed him that he was widely thought proud, prompting him to add Humility to the list -- accompanied by the rueful admission that even fully conquering the vice would probably leave him proud of his humility.

This has left me stuck on a question I can't settle on my own. Aristotle draws a firm line between the merely continent person, who does the right thing only by continuously fighting down contrary impulses, and the genuinely virtuous person, for whom right action has become second nature, performed readily and without inner struggle. Judged by that distinction, does the very need for a lifelong tracking chart -- one still catching Franklin out on pride decades into the project -- show that he never advanced past continence to real virtue, however faithfully he kept score? Or would it be unfair to hold him to that standard at all, given that his own stated reason for the whole scheme was frankly practical (a route to personal happiness and worldly success) rather than anything resembling Aristotelian flourishing pursued for its own sake? Write an essay that weighs both sides of this question carefully and then defends a definite verdict on whether Franklin's method deserves to count as a genuine, philosophically respectable path to virtue, or as something importantly short of it. A qualified or conditional verdict is fine, so long as you commit to it rather than leaving things at "it's complicated."
