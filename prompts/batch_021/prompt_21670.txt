I spent thirty-odd years as a marine biologist running the benthic ecology side of a field station that my old university has long shared with a research institute up the coast, and even though I retired a few years back I still sit on the small joint steering committee that oversees the arrangement. Since giving up the bench I've fallen into reading a lot of philosophy of rationality and epistemic game theory for fun - started with Aumann's agreement theorem and ended up down the rabbit hole of "common knowledge of rationality" and backward induction, which I find facinating and a little maddening in equal measure.

Here's the actual situation prompting this memo. The two institutions are renegotiating the field-station agreement, which currently runs as a five-year rolling commitment renewed annually by mutual consent, each side covering roughly half the running costs. One committee member, an economist by training, has been making a very sharp argument thats basically the old Centipede-game paradox dressed up in institutional clothes: since both sides are rational, and everyone at the table knows the other is rational, and knows that they know it, and so on, then in the final year neither institution has any reason to pay for a facility it won't need again, so it should walk; but then the exact same logic unravels year four, then year three, all the way back to year one, so the whole rolling arrangement is really just a mirage held up by people not being quite as rational or as transparent with each other as we'd like to think. He wants to scrap the rolling structure entirely and replace it with one lump-sum contract up front, precisely so nobody's ever tempted to exploit the endgame.

Could you draft the short memo I'd actually bring to committee? I need it to land on a real recommendation about how to structure the next agreement - rolling renewal, lump sum, or some third option, with reasons - but I especially want it to take the economist's argument seriously on its own terms rather than wave it off as overly cynical. That means engaging with why people like Aumann thought common knowledge of rationality really does force the backward-induction conclusion in games like ours, and why others pushed back that this whole line of reasoning quietly undercuts itself the moment you imagine actually sitting down to play it out. I also vaguely remember reading that when people actually play the Centipede game in lab experiments they keep cooperating well past the point where the theory says they should stop, which feels relevant but I'm not sure how to use it. Keep the whole thing tight enough that I could read it aloud in a twenty-minute agenda slot.
