I host a science-and-ideas podcast, and next month's long-form interview is with a decision theorist who has staked out a distinctive position in the small technical literature on the AI "shutdown problem." Where MIRI's original 2015 corrigibility paper tries to patch a shutdown-safe disposition onto an otherwise ordinary expected-utility maximizer through a utility-indifference trick, and where Hadfield-Menell and Russell's "off-switch game" derives a willingness to be switched off from the agent's uncertainty about human preferences, my guest thinks both are treating a symptom. Her claim is that any agent with complete preferences over outcomes will, generically, have some incentive to manipulate whether and when it gets shut down, so corrigibility has to be built in from the start by making the agent's preferences incomplete -- governed by a choice rule she calls Timestep Dominance rather than an ordinary utility function. I've spent enough time with the primary material to notice that most coverage of "AI off-switches" never gets past the intuitive worry that a capable agent won't let you turn it off, and never touches the actual argument, which turns on whether completeness itself is the culprit.

Draft eight to ten interview questions that hold her to that technical substance rather than letting her re-narrate the shutdown problem in the abstract. I want at least one question on why she locates the source of the shutdown-manipulation incentive in completeness specifically, and on why utility indifference doesn't remove that incentive so much as mask it; at least one on how Timestep Dominance is supposed to survive the standard money-pump argument against incomplete preferences without quietly smuggling back in something that behaves like completeness; at least one on whether the incompleteness can stay surgically confined to shutdown-relevant comparisons, or threatens to spread into ordinary incoherence once the agent can build subagents or revise its own policy; and at least one that sets her proposal directly against the off-switch game's uncertainty-based route to corrigibility, asking whether the two are compatible fixes or rivals. For each of these four, add a follow-up question keyed to the answer she's most likely to give, so that a well-rehearsed response doesn't just end the exchange. Close with a question asking her to respond to Yudkowsky's charge that corrigibility is "anti-natural" for a consequentialist agent -- whether deliberately hobbling an agent's preference structure concedes that point rather than answering it.
