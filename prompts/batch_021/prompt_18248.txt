I'm taking a graduate seminar on the metaphysics of properties this term, and this week's session was devoted to the simple conditional analysis of dispositional predicates: the proposal that to say an object is disposed to give response r to stimulus s is just to say that it would give r if it underwent s. I understand, in rough outline, that C. B. Martin's "finkish" dispositions and Mark Johnston's "masked" dispositions (a closely related phenomenon that Alexander Bird later reworked through poison-and-antidote cases) are both meant to be counterexamples to this analysis, and that David Lewis's "Reformed Conditional Analysis" — which replaces the naive conditional with one built around an object's intrinsic causal basis — is usually credited with handling the first but not the second. What I haven't managed to articulate for myself is exactly why finks and masks count as two genuinely distinct problems rather than merely two names for the same trick.

Could you lay out, precisely, the structural difference between a finkish disposition and a masked (or "antidoted") one — that is, what each phenomenon does to the object's disposition or its causal basis, as opposed to what it does to the causal process running from stimulus to manifestation — and explain why exactly this difference is what lets Lewis's causal-basis analysis defuse finks while leaving it exposed to masking? So that the distinction isn't just being read off memorized textbook cases (the live wire, the bubble-wrapped glass, the poisoned drink with its antidote), please also devise one new example of your own for each phenomenon, drawn from some ordinary object or situation not standardly used in this literature, and justify why each one is a fink rather than a mask, or vice versa. Finally, say which of the two phenomena you think poses the deeper problem for anyone still hoping to give a reductive, counterfactual-based analysis of dispositions, and defend that judgment.
