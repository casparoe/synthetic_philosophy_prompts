I'm a software engineer with no background in philosophy, but a dinner-table argument with friends last weekend has been nagging at me since, and I'd like a more rigorous outside take than anyone at the table could give. For the past year, my partner and I have had a private arrangement. I've been vegetarian since college because I think factory farming is a serious moral wrong, but I've never managed to care much about labor conditions in the garment industry, even though I know intellectually that fast fashion is tied to sweatshop labor. My partner is the mirror image: factory farming barely registers for her, but she feels strongly that buying from certain clothing brands makes her complicit in exploiting workers. So we made a deal: she eats vegetarian at home, and I only buy secondhand or from a short list of brands she's approved as ethical. Neither of us has actually come around to caring about the other's issue -- we're each just holding up our end because it's what the other person cares about, and we don't really check up on each other when we're apart, so the whole thing runs on trust more than enforcement.

At dinner, one friend said this was basically "paying each other to be good" and shouldn't count as real ethical behavior, since we're only doing it as part of a bargain rather than because we believe it's right. Another friend said outcomes are all that matter -- less factory-farmed meat gets eaten and fewer sweatshop clothes get bought either way -- so our reasons for doing it are beside the point. A third suggested we're overcomplicating things and should each just offset unilaterally instead: I could donate to a garment-workers' charity in proportion to whatever fast fashion I still buy, and she could donate to an animal charity instead of going vegetarian, so that neither of us depends on the other keeping up their side. My instinct, as someone who thinks in terms of incentives and trade-offs all day at work, is that we've simply found an efficient exchange that leaves us both better off by our own lights, and that the offsetting alternative amounts to the same thing wearing a different hat.

But I suspect there's something philosophically real that I'm missing. Is there actually something ethically suspect about swapping moral behavior this way, comparable to what's supposedly wrong with buying indulgences, or is this a perfectly legitimate arrangement between two people with different values? And is offsetting unilaterally really equivalent to what we're doing, or does something important change once the arrangement no longer depends on the other person coming through? I'd like your actual verdict here, not just a list of considerations on both sides.
