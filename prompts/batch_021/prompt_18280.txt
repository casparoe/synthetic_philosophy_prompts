I am a novelist nearing the climax of a novel set during the July Crisis of 1914, in which catastrophe arrives, much as it did in Sarajevo, through some small and almost trivial contingent event -- a wrong turn taken by a driver -- that ignites a war every character in the book has spent years assuming was coming anyway, for large structural reasons: the alliance system, rigid mobilization timetables, a continent's militarist temper. A careful early reader has objected that I cannot have it both ways. If the deeper causes were sufficient to produce the war regardless, the contingent trigger did no real causal work and the scene is a kind of dramatic sleight of hand; if the trigger really mattered, the earlier chapters spent establishing the war's inevitability were overstating their case. I would rather resolve this properly than paper over it in revision. Write me a dialogue between a historian who maintains that the war's outbreak was inevitable in every sense worth defending, and a historian who maintains that it remained genuinely contingent down to its particulars, in which each must answer the other's strongest argument -- including this exact objection about whether an overdetermined event can still be said to have been caused by the detail that triggered it. Neither should be a straw man for the other; each should press on real weaknesses in the other's position rather than talk past them, and neither should be allowed to dissolve the disagreement with the bare assertion that "both structure and contingency matter" without specifying what that would mean for the causal status of the wrong turn itself.
