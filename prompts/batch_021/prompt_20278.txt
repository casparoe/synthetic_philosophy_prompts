I'm working on a novel with a secondary character, Elena Voss, a Cambridge-trained logician who has spent a decade writing a biography of Frege and has only now let herself read his 1924 political diary in full. Below is a page from her draft manuscript, which she reads aloud to a colleague in a later scene. Before I write that colleague's reply, I need to know whether the page would actually survive scrutiny from someone who knows the material.

"In March and April of 1924, a little over a year before his death, Gottlob Frege kept a diary. It went unpublished for seventy years, surfacing only in 1994 in the Deutsche Zeitschrift für Philosophie, with an English translation appearing a couple of years later. Its contents startled a discipline that had spent the twentieth century restoring Frege to his rightful place as the father of modern logic: entries expressing contempt for the Weimar constitution, longing for a strong national leader, and a program for stripping Jews of political rights and, ideally, expelling them from Germany. Michael Dummett, who had done more than anyone to make Frege legible to English-speaking philosophy, wrote afterward, in evident pain, that reading it had shaken him badly.

The easy response is that none of this touches the philosophy — that the Begriffsschrift and the Grundgesetze stand or fall on their own logical merits, indifferent to the character of the man who wrote them, exactly as a valid proof does not become invalid because its author was cruel to his family. I think that's true, and I think it lets us stop thinking a beat too soon.

Frege's great polemical energy, across forty years, was directed at psychologism: the view, which he pinned on Mill and much of German logic of his generation, that the laws of logic are only generalizations about how human beings happen to reason, so that if enough minds reasoned differently, the laws would change with them. Against this he insisted that a thought's truth has nothing to do with anyone's holding it — that logic answers to what he called, in 1918, a 'third realm,' immune to majority opinion. It's hard, having noticed that the German word he chose there was Reich — the same word historians would later capitalize and prefix with 'Third' for the regime his own diary all but calls for — to entirely un-notice it. I know Reich just means realm, that Germans use it for the animal kingdom and the postal service, and that Frege wrote the phrase a decade and a half before it became the name of a regime, so I don't think it means anything; I just can't make myself leave it out. What I do think is not a coincidence is that six years later, in the diary itself, he dismissed parliamentary democracy in strikingly similar terms, as a system that mistakes counting heads for finding truth. I don't think Frege's antisemitism follows from his logicism — too many committed anti-psychologists have been perfectly good democrats for that. But I suspect one and the same impatience with letting truth be settled by aggregation runs under both, and that it deserves more than a footnote."

I'd like your honest reaction, as if you were the colleague in that scene. Is the history accurate as far as you know? Does the central move — tying third-realm objectivism to the contempt for democracy — actually hold up, or is it a conflation dressed as an insight? And the aside about Reich: a legitimate observation, or a device for raising an insinuation while formally disclaiming it? Don't soften this for my sake; I'd rather rewrite the page than let a character get away with bad reasoning.
