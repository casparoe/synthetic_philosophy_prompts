Question 3: Aumann's agreement theorem is sometimes summarized as showing that "rational agents cannot agree to disagree." The Milgrom-Stokey no-trade theorem extends this idea into a setting with an asset market. (a) State the no-trade theorem's core claim and the assumptions it depends on [6 pts]. (b) Explain in what sense the trading volume we actually observe in real markets counts as a "puzzle" given the theorem [6 pts]. (c) Give two distinct ways the literature has tried to reconcile the theorem with real-world trading volume, and briefly evaluate whether each is a genuine escape from the theorem's assumptions or more of a relabeling of the puzzle [8 pts].

Here's the student's answer, verbatim:

"Aumann showed that if two people share a common prior and each person's posterior probability for some event is common knowledge between them, then those posteriors must be equal -- you can't have common knowledge that the two of you disagree. This holds even if the two of you have seen completely different evidence; it's the common knowledge of each other's opinions, not shared evidence, that forces convergence.

Milgrom and Stokey bring this into a market setting: if a group of risk-averse expected-utility maximizers share a common prior, start from an allocation that's already Pareto-optimal relative to that prior, and it is common knowledge that everyone updates rationally, then no new private information can generate a trade that all parties want to make. The mechanism is the same as in Aumann's proof: an offer to trade is itself a signal, so if I offer to sell you my shares you should immediately ask why, and updating on that fact erodes whatever edge made the trade look profitable to me in the first place. Purely speculative trade -- trade based on nothing but asymmetric information -- unravels before it starts.

This makes actual markets look puzzling, since enormous volumes of shares change hands every trading day, seemingly because people believe they know something others don't. If the theorem's assumptions were satisfied, essentially none of that volume should be information-driven.

Two standard ways of closing the gap: first, traders may simply not share a common prior -- Bayesians are entitled to start out with different priors, and once that assumption goes the conclusion no longer follows, so heterogeneous beliefs restore trading volume without anyone in the model behaving irrationally. Second, plenty of real trades are driven by liquidity needs, portfolio rebalancing, or hedging rather than by private information at all -- someone selling shares to make a mortgage payment isn't signalling anything about the asset, so the no-trade logic simply doesn't bite on that trade. Both routes matter, but ultimately they're just restating the Grossman-Stiglitz point in different words: since fully efficient prices would leave no reward for costly information-gathering, no one would gather information, so the no-trade theorem and the impossibility of informationally efficient markets are really just two names for the same underlying tension between information and equilibrium."
