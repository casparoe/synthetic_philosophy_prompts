A friend and I spent much of the evening after our metaethics seminar arguing about the "wrong kind of reasons" problem, and neither of us managed to convince the other. The seminar had covered fitting-attitude analyses of value, on which something's being good consists in there being reason to adopt a favorable attitude toward it, and our professor illustrated the standard objection with a demon who will unleash some catastrophe unless you come to admire something utterly unremarkable — a puddle of mud, say. You now have a reason to admire the mud, yet the reason plainly has nothing to do with any admirable quality of the mud itself, so "there is reason to favor X" cannot simply be equated with "X is good." Everyone in the room agreed that reasons of this coerced, self-interested kind are somehow disqualified; the hard part, apparently, is saying in a principled way what disqualifies them without covertly reintroducing the very notion of value the analysis was supposed to explain.

Afterward, my friend insisted that this is a narrow difficulty peculiar to theories of value, generated only because such theories try to define goodness in terms of reasons for attitudes in the first place. I argued that the same structure appears in the ethics of belief: a demon (or, less dramatically, Pascal's God) who rewards you for believing something regardless of the evidence gives you a reason to believe that has nothing to do with the belief's being true or well-supported — evidently the same phenomenon, just transposed from evaluative attitudes onto doxastic ones. My friend suspects the resemblance is only superficial, since belief might answer to quite different norms than admiration, love, or contempt do, and thinks the difficulty in the ethics of belief was never in need of any special "solution" borrowed from value theory in the first place.

Could you compare the wrong-kind-of-reasons problem as it arises for fitting-attitude accounts of value with its apparent counterpart in debates over pragmatic versus evidential reasons for belief? In particular, I would like to know whether the moves philosophers standardly use to separate the "right" from the "wrong" kind of reason in the value case — for instance, distinguishing reasons grounded in an object's own reason-relevant properties from reasons grounded merely in the benefits of occupying the attitude — genuinely carry over to the case of belief, or whether there is some principled disanalogy that my friend was gesturing at but couldn't quite articulate.
