I maintain a moderately popular open-source data library in my spare time -- nothing famous, but a few thousand downloads a week -- and for the past year I've served on the three-person conduct committee that handles behavior disputes for the project. None of us has any HR or legal training; we're just maintainers who happened to volunteer. A case is coming to a head before our steering call next Tuesday, and right now the three of us are mostly talking past each other, so I'd like a decision memo I can actually put in front of them.

One of our longest-tenured contributors has, over the past several months, repeatedly berated other contributors on pull requests and issues -- calling patches "garbage," mocking non-native English, and in one thread telling a newcomer to "go back to whatever bootcamp you came from." At least three people, including two we had specifically recruited through a mentorship push for first-time contributors, have since stopped participating, and one named him directly in a farewell comment. When we raised it with him, he told us he had been in the middle of a severe, clinically diagnosed depressive episode, is now in treatment, and that some of what he wrote "doesn't feel like something I'd write." Our code of conduct lets the committee suspend or remove maintainer status for "conduct harmful to the community," but it says nothing about how a disclosed mental-health crisis should bear on that.

The other two committee members have landed on opposite readings of the same essay -- Strawson's "Freedom and Resentment," which a friend doing a philosophy PhD sent me after I complained to her about the whole situation. One argues that Strawson's real point is that once someone is genuinely in the grip of a psychological disturbance, the fitting response is the "objective" attitude rather than resentment or indignation, so a formal warning plus a required check-in is the honest response, and anything harsher just punishes an illness. The other insists that leaning on the diagnosis this way is precisely the move Strawson would resist, and that the indignation owed to the contributors we lost doesn't evaporate just because the person who drove them off has since acquired a diagnosis. I can't referee this myself. Could you draft the memo the committee needs? It should recommend concrete action -- suspension or removal, and for how long -- and address what, if anything, we say publicly, given that he never consented to our sharing his diagnosis. I'd also want reasoning solid enough that both he and the contributors who left could recognize it as fair, and that treats whichever side you end up rejecting as a real argument rather than a strawman.
