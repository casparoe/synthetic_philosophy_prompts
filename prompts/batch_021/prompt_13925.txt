I've been revisiting Hayek's political economy for a graduate seminar on the epistemic foundations of markets, and one question keeps nagging at me. Friedrich Hayek died in 1992, several years before Robin Hanson formulated "futarchy": a proposal under which legislatures would vote to fix a single, formally defined measure of national welfare, after which speculative markets would be used to bet on which policies are likely to raise that measure, with the policy favored by the markets then adopted automatically. Hayek's account of the price system as a mechanism for aggregating the dispersed, largely tacit knowledge of a society reads, on its face, like an argument for exactly this sort of market-based policy selection. Yet his warnings against "the pretense of knowledge" and against constructivist attempts to specify and optimize a society's aggregate welfare seem to sit very uneasily with the "vote on values" half of the same proposal. Which of these two strands of his thought do you think would have won out, and how do you think he would have argued for that verdict?
