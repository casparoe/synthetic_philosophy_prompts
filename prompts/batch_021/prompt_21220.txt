I was recently invited to serve as the public member on a small panel that helps a research-funding charity choose among finalists for the one grant left in that round's budget. Two proposals ended up in an exact dead heat -- the reviewers scored them identically, and everyone agreed the expected benefit to science of funding either team was the same. One panelist insisted we settle it with a coin flip, arguing that an actual random draw was the only defensible way to choose between two equally meritorious teams. Another panelist thought that was an empty formality: since the numbers came out identical either way, they wanted to just fund whichever applicant's surname came first alphabetically and move on. Nobody disputed the merits of either proposal -- the disagreement was purely about whether the method used to break the tie matters in itself, given that it changes nothing about the expected payoff. I still cannot decide who had the better of that argument: was the coin-flip panelist defending something real, or just dressing up a preference as a principle? And if it is something real, what exactly is it, given that it shows up nowhere in anyone's expected payoff?
