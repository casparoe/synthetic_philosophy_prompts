Fell down a bit of a rabbit hole this week after someone in a LW comment thread mentioned, almost in passing, that Newton spent way more of his working life on Bible chronology and prophecy than on physics. That sent me back to the old fun-fact that he privately calculated the world wouldn't end before 2060 (the classic year-day trick applied to Daniel's "time, times, and half a time" -- 1260 years tacked onto roughly 800 AD, if I'm remembering the manuscript right, from the Observations upon the Prophecies of Daniel). But this time I noticed a tension in his own method that I don't think I've seen anyone actually chase down. Newton was also a secret anti-trinitarian -- dangerous to admit openly at the time, which is part of why he dodged taking holy orders at Cambridge -- and he wrote an essay for Locke, "Two Notable Corruptions of Scripture," arguing on close textual grounds that the Johannine Comma (1 John 5:7, the one verse that spells out the Trinity outright) was a later forgery, not original to the text. He chickened out of publishing it, but on the substance he's aged extremely well: pretty much no modern textual critic thinks that verse is authentic anymore.

Here's the thing though: the exact same style of argument -- internal evidence, doesn't fit the linguistic and historical profile of when it claims to be from, smells like it was written after the fact to serve an agenda -- is what modern scholars use to date the book of Daniel itself to the 160s BC, composed during the Antiochus Epiphanes persecution rather than the Babylonian exile Daniel claims to be narrating from. And that dating, if Newton had to swallow it, wrecks the load-bearing assumption behind his whole prophetic chronology, since the 2060 math only works if Daniel is genuinely a sixth-century prophet who foresaw Persia, Greece and Rome centuries out, rather than someone narrating recent history in prophetic costume. So: hypothetically sit 1690s Newton down and walk him through the Maccabean-dating case for Daniel, using basically the same kind of philological reasoning he used against the Comma. What does he actually do with it? I don't want "he'd be upset" -- I want you to reconstruct the actual argument he'd make to try and hold the line (or the argument for why he'd have to concede), given both how sharp he was and how much of his religious identity was staked on the prophecy project.
