I'm taking an advanced seminar this term on Wittgenstein's *On Certainty* and its contemporary reception, and this week's session was devoted to comparing two rival ways of cashing out what it means for a proposition to function as a "hinge." Crispin Wright's entitlement theory, laid out in "Warrant for Nothing (and Foundations for Free)?", treats hinges such as "the Earth has existed for a long time" as propositions we hold without evidence but are nonetheless rationally entitled to accept, on the grounds that no inquiry could get off the ground without trusting them and that the cost of doing so, should they turn out false, would be no worse than the cost of remaining agnostic. Duncan Pritchard, in *Epistemic Angst*, argues that this concedes too much ground to the skeptic: on his view, hinge commitments aren't warranted beliefs at all but a groundless, "animal" certainty that lies outside the space of reasons altogether, closer to a precondition of believing anything than to a belief we could be entitled to hold. When we discussed the readings in class, opinions kept splitting over which view actually honors Wittgenstein's remarks about the river-bed and the language-game, and which one can better explain why a hinge has to be the kind of proposition that cannot simply be swapped out for any other.

Rather than have the two positions summarized again, I'd like to see them argued out directly. Could you write a dialogue of roughly 600-900 words between a committed Wrightian and a committed Pritchardian, staged as a conversation that continues in the hallway after the seminar has ended? Each speaker should engage directly with the other's preceding point rather than restating their own position in parallel: the Pritchardian should press the Wrightian on whether "entitlement" is doing any genuine epistemic work or is just an honorific label for something groundless, while the Wrightian should press the Pritchardian on how a merely arational commitment can play the premise-like role hinges obviously play in inference, and on how the non-doxastic view is supposed to block the objection that it leaves us unable to say a flat-earther's "hinge" is any less legitimate than our own. Please keep the two figures evenly matched in argumentative skill, and don't feel obliged to hand the dispute a tidy resolution at the end, since it doesn't have one in the literature either.
