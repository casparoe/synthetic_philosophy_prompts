After forty years as a physicist working on nuclear decay statistics, I retired three years ago and have spent much of my time since reading philosophy, a subject I neglected almost entirely during my working life. One thing that drew me in was noticing how comfortable my own field had made me: every atom of a given isotope is exactly like every other, so a claim such as "this nucleus has a 50% chance of decaying within one half-life" picks out its reference class -- atoms of this isotope -- without any real rival, and I never had occasion to ask where that number actually came from. I have since discovered, through a volunteer role I took up after retiring, that almost nothing outside physics is so obliging. I now sit on my state's parole board, which meets monthly to rule on release petitions from inmates who have served their minimum terms, and there the comfortable uniqueness of the reference class disappears entirely.

For years the board has supplemented each caseworker's narrative report with a score from a standard actuarial recidivism instrument: a checklist of prior convictions, age at first offense, employment history and similar items, normed against past release cohorts to yield a figure such as "34% likelihood of reconviction within three years." Standing policy treats any score above 40% as a strong presumption against release. What I only recognized as a substantive problem, rather than a rounding quirk, after a year of reading, is that the instrument's manual permits examiners to norm a given inmate against any of several comparison groups -- our state's own release cohort, a national sample matched on offense type, or a narrower group matched on both age and offense history -- and the resulting figures are not close. At my request, our analyst ran one upcoming petitioner's file against all three permitted groups and returned 22%, 34%, and 51%. Nothing in the manual, or in statistical practice generally as far as I can determine, picks out which of these populations is the one this particular man "really" belongs to for purposes of assigning him a single probability of reoffending; unlike my nuclei, he belongs to all three groups at once, and to countless others besides. Roughly half the board wants to keep using whichever figure the analyst happens to produce, since a number feels more defensible after the fact than a caseworker's impression; the other half wants to drop numerical scoring altogether and decide each case on narrative grounds alone.

Our chair, aware of my background, has asked me to draft the short policy memo the full board will vote on at next month's meeting, and I would welcome your help writing it. It should reach one specific, workable recommendation -- retaining the score exactly as used now, demoting it to one input among several under an explicit weighting rule, or dropping it in favor of narrative review -- rather than simply cataloguing the difficulty, and it should propose a timeframe for putting whatever policy it recommends into effect and revisiting it later. Above all, I would like the memo to take the strongest version of the losing side's argument seriously, rather than letting one faction prevail simply because its position happens to sound more quantitatively rigorous.
