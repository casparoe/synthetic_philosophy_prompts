I'm a philosophy PhD student finishing a dissertation chapter on causal decision theory, and for the past few weeks I've been stuck on the literature around ratifiability: Richard Jeffrey's use of it to patch up evidential decision theory, Gibbard and Harper's discussion of it in their 1978 paper on counterfactuals and expected utility, and Andy Egan's later use of structurally similar cases to argue that ratifiability doesn't actually do the work causal decision theorists want from it. The example I can't stop thinking about is "Death in Damascus": a man runs into Death in a crowded market, and Death, looking startled, tells him he has an appointment with him tomorrow, in one of two cities. Death is a near-perfect predictor of where the man will go, and the man knows this. Whatever city the man settles on, the very fact that he's settled on it seems to be exactly the sort of thing Death would have predicted, which gives him reason to switch to the other city — but switching puts him in precisely the same bind all over again, since now that's the choice Death would predict. There doesn't seem to be any pure choice that survives its own consequences being taken into account, and even resorting to a coin flip doesn't obviously help, since Death might reasonably be credited with predicting that too.

Every version of this story I can find is just Maugham's old "Appointment in Samarra" fable with the city names swapped, and it flattens the puzzle into a moral about the futility of fleeing one's fate — which isn't the interesting part at all. Could you write me a short story, something like 1000-1500 words, that actually dramatizes the decision-theoretic problem rather than the fatalism? I'd like a protagonist who reasons explicitly through the loop above — settling on a choice, realizing that settling on it is exactly what undermines it, reconsidering, and so on — clearly enough that a reader who's never heard the term "ratifiable act" can still feel the specific shape of the trap closing, and a reader who has can recognize it precisely. You're welcome to keep the ancient setting or move it somewhere else entirely, so long as the predictor's reliability is established clearly enough, and early enough, for the instability to actually bite rather than reading as ordinary bad luck.

I'd rather you didn't resolve this too neatly. If the protagonist ends up randomizing, I'd like the story to at least register the question of whether that genuinely escapes the loop or merely relocates it one level up, rather than treating a coin flip as a clean way out. Beyond that, the ending is yours to decide — bleak, wry, ambiguous, whatever suits — as long as it stays honest about the possibility that there is no stable, rationally defensible choice to be made here at all, rather than smuggling in a lesson about hubris or acceptance that the underlying puzzle doesn't actually support.
