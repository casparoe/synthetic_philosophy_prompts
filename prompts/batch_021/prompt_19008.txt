I've been part of a small in-person reading group that grew out of our city's old ACX meetup for a few years now; every couple of weeks we work through a paper or long-form post on AI, decision theory, or epistemics and then argue about it over drinks. A few sessions ago we finally got around to Janus's "Simulators" post, and it reopened a running disagreement between two of our most stubborn regulars -- I'll call them Felix and Naomi -- about whether it even makes sense to describe something like GPT-4 as "an agent." Neither of them budged over about forty minutes of back-and-forth, and since I was mostly just refilling glasses and listening, I'd like an outside adjudication of who had the better of each exchange, since I honestly can't tell.

The argument went roughly like this. (1) Felix opened with the simulator framing: the pretraining objective is just predicting the next token over an enormous mixture of human-generated text, so the trained network is best understood as something that models a whole distribution over possible speakers, or "simulacra," rather than being a speaker (or agent) itself -- on this view, calling the network "an agent" is a bit like calling a physics engine "a person" because it can render one walking around. (2) Naomi granted the pretraining story but said it doesn't describe the thing anyone actually talks to: production models are further trained with RLHF against a learned reward model, and optimizing a policy against a reward signal across many contexts is precisely the setup the mesa-optimization literature worries about -- you can easily end up selecting for an internal process that robustly pursues something, whatever the outer training objective was, and that's a different, more agent-like animal than a raw simulacra-sampler. (3) Felix countered that if RLHF had really installed one robust goal-pursuing process, the assistant persona shouldn't be so easy to knock out of character -- he brought up the "waluigi effect" write-up that made the rounds on LessWrong, arguing that the ease with which adversarial prompts flip a helpful persona into its opposite is exactly the signature you'd expect from a distribution over characters, not from a unified agent with a stable goal. (4) Naomi replied that the ease of flipping the persona doesn't prove there's no agent underneath -- humans, too, can be talked out of their normal character by social engineering without ceasing to be agents -- and, more importantly, that Gwern's old point about tool AIs facing pressure to become agent AIs means the "it's just a simulator" comfort is at best temporary: whatever is true of the bare network, the scaffolded, memory-and-tool-using systems people actually deploy are agents in every sense that matters, so arguing about the substrate is arguing about the wrong layer.

Nobody at the table could agree on who had actually won, and I suspect part of the problem is that "agent" was doing different work for each of them at different points. Could you go through the exchange move by move and say who has the better of the argument at each step, rather than just declaring an overall winner, and flag anywhere you think Felix and Naomi are talking past each other or equivocating on what "agent" means? I would also be curious whether you think the simulator framing and the mesa-optimizer framing are genuinely in tension, as both of them assumed, or whether there is a way of holding both at once that neither considered.
