So I've gone down a bit of a rabbit hole the last few months reading old LessWrong posts about decision theory at like 1am, and there's one thought experiment I genuinely cannot stop chewing on: the "counterfactual mugging" thing. Setup is basically this - Omega (a perfect predictor who never lies or bluffs) flips a fair coin. If it lands tails, Omega will hand you $10,000, but only if it predicted that you WOULD have paid $100 in the case it landed heads instead. If it lands heads, Omega just shows up and asks you for the $100, and that's that - the tails-world money was already decided one way or the other based on the prediction, it's not like paying now unlocks it. Say the coin actually comes up heads and Omega's standing there with its hand out. A causal decision theorist seems to just obviously refuse - the flip already happened, handing over cash can't reach back and change it, so why throw $100 in the bin for nothing. But apparently "updateless" decision theorists say you should pay anyway, and every explanation I've read for why kind of hand-waves past the part where you already *know* your in the heads-branch and the tails-branch isn't even real anymore. I keep flip-flopping between "obviously don't pay, that money's just gone either way" and "no wait, if you're the kind of agent who doesn't pay, you'd never have gotten the 10k in the first place, so maybe paying IS what a truly rational agent does" like every ten minutes.

Could you write me a dialogue between a causal decision theorist and an updateless decision theorist, set at the exact moment Omega has flipped the coin, gotten heads, and is asking for the $100? I want both of them to be genuinely sharp about it, not one of them just a mouthpiece getting steamrolled - the CDT person should push hard on why anyone would pay a cost with zero causal upside once you already know which branch of reality you're in, and the UDT person actually has to engage with that instead of just repeating "policies, not acts" like a slogan - like, what's the real argument for why the correct unit of rational evaluation is a policy fixed before you knew the coin flip, rather than the decision that's actually in front of you right now, at this moment, with the coin already landed? I'd also love it if somewhere in there they get into whether this is really a disagreement about what rationality requires, or more a disagreement about whether there's even a stable "you" whose interests are being maximized across branches you're now certain you're not in. It doesn't need to end in agreement - honestly I'd prefer if it didn't, since that's basically the state I'm stuck in myself.
