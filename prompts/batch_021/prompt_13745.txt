Here is the question as it was posed to students:

Clark Glymour's "problem of old evidence" is often introduced via the case of the anomalous advance of Mercury's perihelion and Einstein's general theory of relativity (GTR). By the time Einstein completed GTR in November 1915, it had been common knowledge among astronomers for decades that the observed precession of Mercury's orbit exceeded the Newtonian prediction by about 43 arcseconds per century — a discrepancy first isolated by Le Verrier in 1859 — yet GTR's exact recovery of this figure from its field equations is standardly cited as some of the strongest evidence in the theory's favor.

(a) [6 points] Explain precisely why this case seems to be a counterexample to the claim that confirmation is well measured by a function such as the difference measure c(H,E) = P(H|E) - P(H).
(b) [8 points] Present the response to this problem associated with Daniel Garber, Richard Jeffrey, and Ilkka Niiniluoto, which diagnoses the trouble as an artifact of the logical-omniscience idealization built into orthodox Bayesian confirmation theory. Be careful to say exactly what new proposition is supposed to get confirmed on this view, and why an agent's credence in it can be less than 1 even though their credence in E itself is already 1.
(c) [6 points] Raise one substantive difficulty for the response you presented in (b).

And here is the student's answer, in full:

(a) The problem is that orthodox Bayesian confirmation theory measures the degree to which E confirms H by something like the amount by which learning E raises the probability of H, e.g. c(H,E) = P(H|E) - P(H). But the anomalous precession of Mercury's perihelion was already known — and so already assigned probability 1 — decades before Einstein had even begun working on general relativity. Since P(E) = 1, we have P(H|E) = P(H∩E)/P(E) = P(H), so for absolutely any hypothesis H whatsoever, c(H,E) = 0. Taken at face value, this measure implies that the perihelion data could not possibly have confirmed general relativity at all — which contradicts the near-universal judgment among historians and physicists that it did, and quite strongly.

(b) This worry is resolved by what is sometimes called the Howson–Garber response. The core idea is that real scientists, unlike the idealized agents of orthodox Bayesian theory, are not logically omniscient: they do not instantaneously know every logical and mathematical consequence of every hypothesis they entertain. So although the precession data E was old news in 1915, what Einstein learned that November was not E itself but the further fact that the field equations of GTR entail E — call this proposition E*. Since Einstein (and everyone else) had not yet worked out this derivation, E* was not already assigned probability 1; discovering that the very difficult calculation goes through is what moved his credence in E* up toward 1. Because E* is positively relevant to H in a way that the now-certain E no longer is, conditionalizing on E* rather than on E can raise P(H) after all. This move essentially relocates the confirming evidence from an old empirical fact to a new logical fact about the theory, and thereby dissolves the problem of old evidence: since there is always, in principle, some such derivational fact that a non-omniscient agent has yet to learn, Bayesians can explain the confirmatory role of old evidence quite generally along these lines.

(c) One difficulty is that this move requires treating claims about logical and mathematical entailment, such as E*, as ordinary contingent propositions that a rational agent can assign a probability strictly between 0 and 1 — but the probability calculus, on its standard (Kolmogorov) axiomatization, already assigns probability 1 to every logical truth as a theorem of the axioms themselves. So the solution seems to need a distinct, non-standard logic of partial belief, on which an agent can be uncertain of logical truths, just to make the relevant proposition E* available as an object of less-than-certain credence in the first place — and it's not obvious that such a framework is a mere addition to Bayesian confirmation theory, rather than a fairly substantial revision of it.
