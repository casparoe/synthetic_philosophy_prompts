So I've been down a predictive processing rabbit hole for the last few weeks (finally read Scott Alexander's old review of Andy Clark's "Surfing Uncertainty," then fell into a bunch of Anil Seth talks about perception being a "controlled hallucination"), and its made me think a debate I ran into in a philosophy of perception course last year is basically over, even though nobody seems to have told the philosophers. The setup is the argument from illusion: illusions feel exactly the same from the inside as veridical perception, so it looks like there must be some common factor -- some inner representation or sense-datum -- present in both cases, which is what pushes you toward indirect realism. Disjunctivists are the people who deny this: they say a veridical perception and a matching hallucination aren't two instances of the same kind of mental state at all, they're completely different things that just happen to feel alike, precisely so they can keep naive realism intact. But if predictive processing is basically right, the brain is running the literal same hierarchical prediction-and-error-correction routine regardless of whether the input is "real" or not -- a hallucination and an actual perception come out of the identical algorithm, just fed different data. That sounds a lot like neuroscience directly confirming the "common factor" that disjunctivists have spent decades insisting isn't there.

I tried this on my housemate, who's ABD in phil of mind, and she got weirdly annoyed and said something like "that's just not the kind of claim disjunctivism is even making," but it was midnight and she didn't want to properly get into it, and honestly im not convinced she fully talked herself out of my version either. So: is she right that I'm mashing together two different levels of description that shouldn't be compared like this, or does predictive processing actually put a thumb on the scale against disjunctivism the way I think it does?
