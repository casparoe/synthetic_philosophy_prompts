I trained as a reporter before I ever published a novel, and my old journalism school has asked me back to deliver this spring's commencement address, mostly, I suspect, because my last book followed a regional newspaper through the decade leading up to its closure. In preparing the speech I went back to a text that has been nagging at me for years: Kierkegaard's 'The Present Age,' the closing section of his 1846 review 'Two Ages.' His argument is that 'the public' is not some pre-existing body that journalism merely happens to serve, but a phantom the press itself calls into being and keeps in motion -- an abstraction with no qualities of its own, sustained by chatter rather than by the passionate, committed action he associates with a genuine age of revolution, and held together, in the absence of anything positive, by envy: a reflexive resentment of anyone who stands out from the level. I can't pretend this argument stays politely in 1846. It is aimed, with real precision, at the trade some thirty graduates are about to enter, and, if I'm honest, at my own trade too, since a novelist depends on much the same machinery of coverage and buzz in order to be read at all.

What I don't want is a commencement address that either scolds these graduates for choosing a profession Kierkegaard considered structurally corrosive, or one that quotes him for a moment of gravity and then pivots, unearned, into the usual 'seek truth, speak up' send-off. I'd like the speech to take his diagnosis seriously enough that it actually constrains the ending: if there's any honest way to send these graduates off encouraged, it should have to survive contact with his distinction between the anonymous, leveling public and whatever a single, accountable individual can still do or say, rather than simply talking around it. The audience will include the graduates, families who mainly want something warm enough to quote afterward, and a few of my old professors who will notice immediately if the philosophy has been flattened just to make the ending come out nicely. Could you write that address for me -- around eight minutes, one continuous line of thought running from the diagnosis to whatever real counsel it can actually support?
