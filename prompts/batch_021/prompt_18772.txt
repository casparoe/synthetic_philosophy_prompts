A friend of a friend has bipolar I. Years ago, while stable, she signed what her psychiatrist called a "Ulysses contract": her husband and doctor are pre-authorized to hospitalize and medicate her against her wishes if she ever goes manic and refuses care again. Last month she went manic, refused, and they invoked it anyway, over her objections in the moment. Two people at a dinner I was at ended up in a whole argument about whether that was actually justified.

1. Nadia said the family obviously did the right thing -- this is exactly what a Ulysses contract is for, same as Ulysses ordering his crew to bind him to the mast and ignore whatever he yells once the Sirens start.

2. Owen said the analogy doesn't hold: Ulysses' crew were ignoring a guy whos still Ulysses, just overpowered by desire. On most accounts of mania her whole belief-and-desire system temporarily reorganizes, so overriding her isn't restraining a tempted person, its closer to letting a past directive bind someone else who happens to share her body.

3. Nadia said that's true of basically every precommitment device -- a constitution's supermajority-to-amend rule exists precisely to bind a future majority that will disagree, and nobody calls that "binding a different group of people" just because that majority is worked up about some crisis. If sincere in-the-moment disagreement were enough to void a precommitment, the whole category would be pointless.

4. Owen said the constitutional case differs in a way that matters: the future majority bound by an amendment rule is still, the whole time, the kind of agent whose say is supposed to count. The entire premise of the psychiatric contract is that manic-her currently isn't, which is a much bigger claim to make about someone arguing with you right now than "let's resist the mob."

5. Nadia said that cuts both ways: if you can't trust her in-the-moment judgment enough to honor her refusal, you can't turn around and cite her in-the-moment fluency as proof she's competent either, since sounding perfectly coherent is exactly what mania produces. The contract exists precisely to preempt that appearance, on the say of the one person positioned to know her own pattern -- her stable self.

6. Owen said that's the scary part, not a defense of it: if no amount of present-tense coherence could ever count as evidence against the contract, there's no fact about her, right now, that could overturn it. That's less like Ulysses, who'd eventually just get released once they'd sailed past the island, and more like a standing transfer of authority with no real trigger besides whatever her husband and doctor decide counts as "mania."
