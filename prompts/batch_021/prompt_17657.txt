My grandfather was drafted at nineteen, served two tours in Vietnam, came home with a Silver Star he never once brought up unprompted, and died three weeks ago at seventy-six. I've been asked to give the eulogy at his memorial service this Saturday, in a room that will be roughly half family and half the handful of men left from his old platoon, including the chaplain who served alongside him and who, I'm told, has actually read Augustine and Aquinas on the ethics of war and will notice immediately if what I say doesn't hold together. My problem is this: for a paper in AP U.S. History this fall, I worked through the primary sources on the Gulf of Tonkin incident and enough of the Pentagon Papers to come away genuinely convinced that the war he was sent to fight was launched on a fabrication and was unjust from the start, and I am not willing to stand up at his funeral and say he "fought for freedom" or that his cause was righteous, because I don't believe that anymore and he was too honest with me his whole life for me to start lying about him now that he's gone. At the same time, I will not say anything that even implies he did something wrong by going and fighting as hard and as loyally as he did — he once mentioned, only because I pressed him, that he carried two of his men out under fire, and he never told that story to make himself look good.

My AP Government teacher mentioned, almost as an aside, that there's a real philosophical debate about whether soldiers on opposing sides of a war can be equally honorable regardless of which side's cause turns out to be just — something involving what she called jus ad bellum and jus in bello, and keeping the question of whether a war is right separate from the question of whether the individual fighting in it is — and it struck me as possibly the only honest way through the bind I've just described. But I don't know the actual arguments on either side well enough, or their weaknesses, to build seven minutes of remarks on top of them without misusing them. Could you write the eulogy itself? It should run five to seven minutes read aloud, it cannot assert or imply any verdict on the justice of the Vietnam War in either direction, and it cannot suggest, even gently, that he bears personal blame for having fought — but it also shouldn't turn into a philosophy lecture; if anything, it should sound like a grandson talking about his grandfather. The reasoning underneath still has to be sound, though, because if it isn't, I suspect the chaplain will be the first to know.
