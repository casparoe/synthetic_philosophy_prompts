At a work conference in a city none of us knew, a few colleagues and I arrived at lunchtime to find two food trucks parked side by side outside the venue, offering near-identical menus at near-identical prices. One had a long line; the other had none. I had read a favorable review of the empty one on my phone that morning, but I joined the long line anyway, and everyone who arrived after me did the same. That evening, two people in the group, Naomi and Colin, got into an argument over whether this had actually been reasonable.

Naomi opened by calling the whole thing mindless conformity: nobody in that line had any real information of their own, she said; they were simply copying whoever was ahead of them, and copying people who are themselves copying is not evidence of anything. Colin pushed back: if all you have is one middling review, and you can see that thirty people ahead of you have effectively voted with their feet for the other truck, it is rational to weight thirty revealed choices over your one review, so ignoring the line in favor of that review would have been the irrational move. Naomi granted that this might be true of whoever started the line, but said the 'thirty opinions' were an illusion: only the first couple of arrivals were actually going on their own independent read of the two trucks, and once they happened to agree, everyone else was just copying the copiers, so the line's length reflects two or three independent judgments at most, heavily amplified, not thirty. Colin answered that none of this makes joining the line irrational for any individual in it: the third person, the tenth, and the thirtieth each still faced a genuine decision given only what was visible to them at that moment, and copying was the correct call for each of them; what the line 'really' represents in hindsight is a separate question from whether anyone erred at the time. Naomi closed by saying this makes 'rational herding' a strange piece of terminology, since it requires every individual to have acted rationally while the group ends up with less collective information than if everyone had simply trusted their own read of the two trucks, which struck her as less a discovery about rationality than a reason to be suspicious of how the word is being used.

Neither of them would budge, so I would like your help doing this properly. Please go through the exchange move by move, saying who has the better of it (or whether it is a genuine wash) at each step, and then say what, if anything, you think Naomi and Colin are both still missing about how information cascades actually work and about whether 'rational herding' is a coherent idea or closer to a contradiction in terms.
