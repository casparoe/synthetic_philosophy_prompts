Having been chosen as this year's valedictorian, I will be giving the graduation speech this spring, and I want to build it around Nelson Goodman's grue paradox, which I encountered in my school's philosophy elective and have not been able to stop thinking about since. Commencement speeches usually lean on the idea that a proven track record predicts future success, but Goodman's new riddle is precisely the argument that no track record, by itself, tells you which regularity you're actually extending into the future — it is a further difficulty on top of Hume's, since it grants that induction works in general and still asks how we rule out the grue-like alternative. My philosophy teacher will be seated in the front row and will notice if I collapse that distinction or simply wave at 'entrenchment,' but most of the relatives and underclassmen in the audience will never have heard of Nelson Goodman. Please write me a five-minute speech that uses grue to say something honest and a little unsettling about trusting the future, that stays precise enough to satisfy my teacher, remains accessible to everyone else, and still ends on a note of hope.
