A friend and I spent most of a long dinner arguing about medieval political theology, and neither of us gave any ground. Citing Aquinas's discussion of tyranny in De Regimine Principum, my friend argued that Aquinas's account of political authority more or less licenses tyrannicide: since a ruler's authority is legitimate only insofar as it serves the common good, a ruler who governs purely for his own benefit has already forfeited his claim on obedience, so that any subject in a position to do so would be justified in killing him — putting Aquinas, in effect, in the same camp as John of Salisbury's more famous defense of tyrannicide a century earlier. I couldn't muster a decisive objection at the table, but the claim has kept nagging at me since. Is this actually Aquinas's considered position? If not, I'd like to understand precisely where the inference breaks down, and what Aquinas thought a subject should do instead when faced with a genuinely tyrannical ruler.
