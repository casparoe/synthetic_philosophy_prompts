So I finally got round to reading Hume's *Political Discourses* after only ever knowing him for the causation and induction stuff back in school (I spent my whole career as a research chemist, so philosophy is a very late-life hobby for me!). I was honestly kind of stunned by how modern "Of Money" and "Of Public Credit" feel - he's already arguing that money isn't wealth in itself, just a kind of lubricant that makes trade run smoother, and he's practically fuming about Britain's national debt getting out of hand, decades before it became the monster it is today. This turned into a whole argument with an old colleague of mine from the physics department over dinner about what Hume would make of our current situation, and neither of us could agree, so I thought I'd put it to you instead.

Here's what I'm curious about: if you could somehow sit Hume down today and explain to him (1) that basically every major currency on earth is now pure fiat paper with no metal backing at all, ever since Nixon closed the gold window in 1971, (2) that there's now a whole school of "Modern Monetary Theory" economists arguing that a government which issues its own currency doesn't really need to worry about debt or deficits, only inflation, and (3) that on top of all that we now have cryptocurrencies like Bitcoin - money with no government or bank behind it whatsoever, just scarcity coded into some software and a distributed ledger - what do you think he'd say to each of these? I dont just want "Hume would be horrified," I want you to actually reconstruct the argument he'd likely make in each case, tying it back to specific things he says - the specie-flow mechanism, his idea that money's value is conventional rather then intrinsic, his worries about paper credit and "public credit" turning into a kind of delusion, etc. Feel free to flag where you think his 18th century assumptions just don't map cleanly onto today's world rather than forcing an answer.
