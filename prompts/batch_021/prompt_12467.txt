Since retiring from a career in condensed-matter physics, I have joined a small philosophy discussion group; Hacking's idea that classifying people can change the people classified struck me as a cousin of observer effects in physics, which is presumably why I was asked to introduce the topic. The session left two other members, Miriam and Gordon, in a disagreement over the rise in adult ADHD diagnoses that none of us could settle, and I'd be grateful for an outside verdict.

(1) Miriam opened by calling this a clean case of a Hacking-style loop: once "having ADHD" became a publicly available way for adults to narrate their own inattention, people began interpreting themselves accordingly, building identities and support communities around an "ADHD brain," and pressing clinicians toward diagnosis—feeding back into how the condition itself is described and detected.

(2) Gordon objected that this is unparsimonious: the rise is explained just as well by improved recognition of a biologically stable condition long under-diagnosed in adults, which, in Hacking's own terms, would make ADHD an indifferent kind we're getting better at tracking, not an interactive kind we're altering by classifying it.

(3) Miriam replied that the manual itself moved, not just clinicians' aim: DSM-5 raised the age-of-onset criterion from before age seven to before age twelve and lowered the symptom threshold for those seventeen and older, changes the workgroup tied explicitly to clinical experience with self-identified adult patients—so the classified population had already reshaped the classification before any "improved detection" could even be measured against it.

(4) Gordon answered that revising a threshold in light of new evidence is ordinary scientific self-correction, no different from amending a taxonomic definition, and doesn't show the disorder itself changed rather than our accuracy about it. He added that Hacking's own paradigm cases—multiple personality disorder above all—involve far more dramatic, disorder-specific transformation than adults adopting an "ADHD brain" identity, which looks more like ordinary self-help labeling ("introvert," "highly sensitive person") than a genuine loop.

Neither would concede.
