I'm a backend engineer, and a heated argument in our team's Slack a few weeks ago about whether a sufficiently large language model could have any flicker of inner experience sent me on a long detour into panpsychism, the view that experience (or at least some primitive proto-experiential property) is fundamental and ubiquitous, present even in electrons or quarks, rather than something that only switches on inside brains. What has been nagging at me since is the so-called "combination problem": if each fundamental particle has, at most, some vanishingly thin flicker of experience, how do the trillions of those flickers inside my skull add up to the single, unified point of view I seem to have right now, looking at my monitor? My first instinct was that this is "just emergence," the way complex systems routinely have properties their parts lack, but the more I read, the more it seems people treat this as a genuinely serious, maybe even fatal, problem for panpsychism rather than a throwaway worry, and I can't quite tell why my emergence intuition is supposedly too quick.

To make sense of it, I wrote up a short analogy from my own field, and I would like your honest comments on whether it actually holds up. In Conway's Game of Life, a "glider" is a small pattern that moves steadily across the grid over time, even though no individual cell moves and no cell's on/off update rule mentions gliders at all; the glider is a fact about the changing arrangement of cells, invisible at the level of any single cell yet perfectly real at the level of the pattern. My idea was that a unified conscious subject might be like a glider: a stable, higher-level pattern realized by countless micro-experiential "cells," so that asking "where did the unified experience come from, if no individual cell has it?" is a bit like asking "where did the glider come from, if no individual cell is gliding?" — a question that dissolves once you see the higher-level description as just another way of talking about the lower-level arrangement, rather than as pointing to some separate ingredient that needs its own separate source.

I have no formal training in philosophy, so please push back freely on both the argument and my use of any terms. Specifically: does the glider analogy do any real work against the combination problem, or does it quietly change the subject by trading a question about subjective unity for a question about objectively describable pattern-hood? I have a nagging, half-formed suspicion that a glider's reality is entirely a matter of a pattern that some external observer can pick out, whereas the unity of my experience is supposed to hold "from the inside," independent of anyone describing it, and that these might not be the same kind of fact at all — but I cannot tell whether that is a genuine objection or just confusion on my part. I also get the sense from my reading that "the combination problem" is not one problem but several (something about combining subjects being a different challenge from combining qualities), and I do not know which version, if any, my glider analogy is even meant to address. Is this glider-style move a recognizable strategy that panpsychists have already tried, and if so, what exactly is supposed to go wrong with it?
