So a while back I fell down a rabbit hole reading about that Wigner thing physicists call the "unreasonable effectiveness of mathematics" -- the puzzle of why math that people invented for no reason other than that it was elegant (imaginary numbers, weird non-euclidean geometries, whatever) keeps turning out decades or even centuries later to be exactly the tool physics needed. Everyone treats this like some deep unsolved mystery about the universe, but I think I've talked myself into an explanation that makes it a lot less mysterious than people say, and I'd like someone who actually knows this stuff to tell me where it breaks.

Here's roughly what I've got written down: mathematicians have been cranking out huge amounts of math for thousands of years, almost all of it for it's own sake, with zero interest in whether any of it describes anything real. So by the time a physicist gets stuck on some problem, there's already a massive back-catalogue of pre-built mathematical structures sitting on the shelf, and with a catalogue that big, of course something in there is eventually going to fit. Riemann builds a whole theory of curved space in the 1800s purely to satisfy his own curiosity about geometry, and sure enough Einstein finds it sitting there decades later exactly when general relativity needs it. Cardano and those guys mess around with square roots of negative numbers centuries before anyone needs em for quantum mechanics. We only ever hear about the matches -- complex numbers, non-euclidean geometry, group theory -- and never about the huge pile of equally elegant math that never got used for anything, so of course it looks miraculous in hindsight. It's basically survivorship bias wearing a lab coat: physicists are good librarians, not witnesses to a cosmic coincidence.

Where I get stuck is cases like Dirac's equation basically forcing the existence of antimatter out of the math before anyone had ever seen any, and then it turns up in a cloud chamber a few years later. That doesnt feel like "browsing a library" anymore, that feels like the math knew something the library couldn't have just happened to contain, and I can't decide if that's a real problem for my library idea or if I'm just not thinking about it clearly.

Can you go through this and tell me straight: does the library/survivorship-bias argument actually hold up, is it something people already say and I'm just late to the party on, and specifically -- what should I make of cases like the Dirac one, where the math seems to predict stuff instead of just getting dug up after the fact?
