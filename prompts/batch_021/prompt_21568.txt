So this happened at a friend's birthday dinner a few weeks back and I haven't been able to drop it since. Somebody brought up our mutual friend Dave, who's sort of famous in our group for tipping exactly 20% every single time, no exceptions -- amazing service, terrible service, doesn't matter, he does the math and lands right on 20%. His reasoning is that he decided years ago that's "the right number," and he doesn't trust his in-the-moment judgment about who deserves what. My friend Nadia said this actually makes him less generous than someone who tips based on how the server actually treated them, because he's not responding to the person in front of him at all, hes just running a formula on autopilot. Her boyfriend Marcus pushed back and said that's backwards -- we usually think MORE of someone for sticking to a principle even when they're not in the mood, not less, same reason we think better of a friend who keeps a promise on a day they don't feel like keeping it than one who's only ever nice when the mood strikes. So if Dave's doing it "on principle" instead of "by feel," Marcus said that should count in his favor, not against it.

Nadia came back with: a promise is owed to a specific person who's counting on you, and Dave's rule isn't a promise to anybody, he just made it up for himself one day, so the comparison doesn't really hold. She also said if a friend ever told you the actual reason they show up for you has nothing to do with you personally, it's just "I have a rule I follow," that wouldnt exactly feel great to hear, which means the "personal" part has to matter somehow. Marcus said fine, forget promises then -- think about a teacher who grades every essay against the exact same rubric whether she likes the kid or not. Nobody thinks she's worse than a teacher who's an easier grader on her favorites; the fact that the specific student doesn't factor in is exactly what makes her fair, so "following a rule instead of reacting to the person" clearly isn't automatically some kind of moral strike. Nadia answered that grading is a fairness situation, where treating everybody identically is the whole point, but being generous or being a good friend isn't that kind of thing at all -- it's supposed to be about actually noticing the particular person and situation in front of you, so the teacher case doesn't transfer over to Dave. Neither of them budged and the conversation just kind of fizzled out there.

I don't have any background in this stuff, philosophy was never really my thing in school -- it's just something I find myself chewing on afterward. Could you go through their back-and-forth move by move and say who's actually got the stronger point at each stage, instead of just picking an overall winner?
