The idea, originally floated by Caspar Oesterheld under the name "multiverse-wide superrationality," is that if the universe (or multiverse) is sufficiently large, and my decision procedure is correlated with those of causally disconnected agents elsewhere who hold very different values, then evidential decision theory recommends I act as though I'd struck a cooperative bargain with them: compromising toward a broader mix of values rather than optimizing only my own. I find the basic mechanism genuinely compelling on paper, but the more I read around it, the more places I find where the argument could quietly fail, and I'm not sure how to weigh these failure points against one another well enough to tell my supervisor how much this idea should actually move our cause prioritization.

I've distilled the worries I keep running into down to seven: (1) the argument's reliance on evidential rather than causal decision theory, given that causal decision theorists don't obviously get the same recommendation (though it's disputed whether functional/updateless decision theory rescues something similar); (2) the difficulty of knowing whether one's own decision procedure is correlated with enough sufficiently different-valued agents elsewhere to matter, as opposed to being correlated mainly with near-copies of oneself; (3) live uncertainty about whether we actually inhabit a "large" world in the required sense (spatial infinitude, eternal inflation, many-worlds, modal realism, and so on); (4) the risk that a value-compromise aggregated over a mostly unknown population of alien or even malevolent agents could be worse by our own lights than simply acting on our own values; (5) the structural resemblance to Pascal's mugging, in multiplying poorly-bounded small probabilities by enormous stakes; (6) the charge that ECL is explanatorily idle, in that it mostly just re-derives what ordinary cosmopolitan impartiality already recommends; and (7) the worry that the argument never cashes out in any concrete, checkable behavioral upshot at all.
