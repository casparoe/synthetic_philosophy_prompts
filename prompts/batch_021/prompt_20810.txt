I've been a judge for a smallish yearly prize for debut novels for three years now -- five of us on the panel, six books on the shortlist, one winner. It's normally painless. This year it wasn't. Each judge ranks all six books top to bottom, and our coordinator turns the rankings into a winner with a points system (5 points for a judge's top pick, 4 for second, and so on down to 0 for last place, then add everyone's points up). Book A beat Book B by a couple of points. Everyone shrugged and moved on, until a week later our coordinator was tidying up the spreadsheet and, mostly out of curiosity, reran the numbers with the two books that nobody was ever seriously going to vote for removed entirely, rescoring only the remaining four. Nobody's actual preference between A and B had changed one bit. But with those two no-hopers gone, B won instead of A.

That's caused a real split on the panel. Three of the five judges want to ditch the points system entirely for straight "whoever gets the most first-place votes wins," on the theory that any method this easy to flip by deleting books nobody wanted anyway can't be trusted. The other two think that's an overreaction -- their view is that basically any voting method can probably be broken somehow if you poke at it enough, this was a one-off fluke, and we shouldn't tear up a system that's worked fine for a decade over one weird spreadsheet accident. I'm chairing the panel next year and have to circulate a memo before our first meeting in the autumn recommending how we should actually pick winners going forward (I write historical novels for a living, not policy memos, so bare with me on format). I don't want a wishy-washy "every method has pros and cons" memo -- I want an actual recommendation, a real explanation of why deleting a couple of no-hope books could flip a two-horse race like that in the first place, and something that takes the "it was a fluke, calm down" camp seriously instead of just steamrolling them.
