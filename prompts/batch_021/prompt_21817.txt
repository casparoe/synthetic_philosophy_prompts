So this happened after a post-incident review at my company last week (those things are basically our team's group therapy at this point) and I haven't been able to shake it since. Two backend engineers, call them Alex and Ben, independantly skipped the second-reviewer step on a prod deploy the same week, both because they were rushing out before a long weekend and both telling themselves "it's a tiny change, review is just a formality here." Alex's change happened to touch a path that a big customer's traffic hit that afternoon and took down checkout for two hours. Ben's change touched a path that, as far as anyone can tell, nobody exercised that week, so nothing happened. In the postmortem doc Alex got a formal writeup and got put on a mandatory review plan for a month; Ben got a "hey don't do that again" in a DM and that was the end of it.

At drinks after, two friends of mine who were also there, Mara and Devon, would not drop it, and I just sat back and listened because I honestly couldn't tell you who's right. (1) Mara said the different treatment is basically correct: Alex is more at fault because he actually broke checkout for two hours and cost the company real money, while Ben, whatever he was thinking, didn't actually hurt anyone. (2) Devon said thats backwards -- they did the literal identical reckless thing, and which one blew up depended on which customer happened to hit which endpoint that day, so punishing Alex more is punishing him for bad luck, not for anything he did differently than Ben. (3) Mara said outcomes obviously have to count for something, otherwise we'd have to say attempted murder deserves the same sentence as murder, and pretty much nobody actually believes that, so ordinary moral practice already treats outcome as relevant, not just intention. (4) Devon said "what the law happens to do" isn't the same as "what's fair" -- the law has other jobs, like deterrence and needing to actually prove intent, so it's weak evidence for what Alex and Ben really deserve. (5) Mara then brought up a third guy, Casey, who never even considered skipping review because he's just a naturally anxious, by-the-book person, and said: if we're going to stop blaming Ben for damage he got lucky to avoid, we should symmetrically stop crediting Casey for virtue he never had to work for either, since he just happens to have the personality that kept him out of the situation in the first place. Push that all the way and nobody is really praiseworthy or blameworthy for anything, which is nuts, so at some point you have to stop being precious about luck and just go with outcomes. (6) Devon said that's not a fix, that's just conceding the puzzle Mara herself raised is real -- and if anything it means we should drop the extra blame on Alex for the crash itself and only hold him and Ben equally responsible for the one decision they both actually made.

I've read enough rationalist-blog-adjacent stuff over the years (ACX, old LW, that whole cluster) to have a nagging feeling this is gesturing at something with actual literature behind it, but not enough to referee it myself. Could you go through moves (1) through (6) and, for each one, give a score from 1 (weak, kind of a bad move) to 5 (strong, basically airtight) for how much that move actually holds up? Maybe lay it out as a quick JSON block like {"1": <score>, "2": <score>, ..., "6": <score>}, and then explain each score in a sentence or two. One thing I keep flip-flopping on myself: does Mara's move (5) actually rescue her outcome-based view, or does the same logic cut just as hard against using outcomes at all, in which case it wouldn't really be doing the work she thinks it's doing?
