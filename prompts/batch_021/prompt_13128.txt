Question 3 (15 pts). In their 2015 paper "Corrigibility," Soares, Fallenstein, Yudkowsky, and Armstrong propose an agent whose utility function combines a "normal" utility function U_N and a "shutdown" utility function U_S, selected by an indicator variable for whether a shutdown button has been pressed, plus a correction term U_C.
(a, 5 pts) In 2-3 sentences, explain what problem U_C is meant to solve, and why simply switching to U_S when the button is pressed, with no correction term, wouldn't be sufficient.
(b, 5 pts) The authors describe their proposal as achieving "indifference" rather than full corrigibility. Name one thing a fully corrigible agent would need beyond what indifference provides.
(c, 5 pts) Hadfield-Menell et al.'s "off-switch game" is often cited as a complementary result. Summarize its main finding, and what it implies about the relationship between an agent's confidence in its own judgment and its willingness to be shut down.

Student's answer:

The shutdown problem and corrigibility are basically the same problem: how do you make sure an AI won't stop you from turning it off.

(a) U_C is needed because without it the agent would have an incentive to manipulate whether the button gets pressed. If it just switched to U_S when shutdown happens and U_N otherwise, it could still take actions beforehand to make the button more or less likely to be pressed, depending on which of U_N or U_S it expects to score higher on. U_C is added so that, in expectation, the agent is indifferent between the button being pressed and not, which removes its incentive to influence the probability of shutdown.

(b) Indifference just means the agent won't try to influence the button. Full corrigibility needs more than that — the agent also has to avoid building incorrigible successors or sub-agents that don't share the same shutdown behavior, and it can't deceive or manipulate its operators more generally, not just around the button.

(c) In the off-switch game, a robot is uncertain about the true reward function and can either act on its own judgment or wait and let a human hit the switch. Hadfield-Menell et al. show that so long as the robot is Bayesian and treats the human's choice to flip the switch as evidence about the reward function, it will always let itself be switched off, no matter how confident it is, because deferring is a dominant strategy once any uncertainty at all is built into the reward function. That's basically why the paper argues corrigibility falls out for free once the agent's reward is uncertain rather than fixed — confidence doesn't end up mattering to the result.
