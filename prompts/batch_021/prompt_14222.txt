I'm preparing a session for my philosophy of law seminar that pairs a historical account of constitutional interpretation with a live contemporary controversy, and I want to stress-test the pairing before it goes into the syllabus. After breaking with William Lloyd Garrison around 1851, Frederick Douglass argued that the Constitution must be read by its own text and stated purposes rather than by the private intentions of many of its framers: he made much of the fact that the words "slave" and "slavery" never appear in it, leaned on the Preamble's language of liberty and justice, and eventually called the document a "glorious liberty document." That move — privileging public textual meaning over framers' intent — prefigures a distinction constitutional theory would only make explicit generations later, in the split between original-intent and original-public-meaning originalism. Suppose Douglass were confronted with today's public-meaning originalism, including its role in a case like Students for Fair Admissions v. Harvard, where the historical record of what the Reconstruction Congress itself understood the Fourteenth Amendment to permit is genuinely contested. Would he recognize the Court's method as vindicating his own argument, or would he say that something indispensable to his reading — its unapologetic natural-law commitments and moral urgency — has been quietly dropped along the way? Lay out the strongest case on both sides before telling me where you think he would ultimately come down, and why.
