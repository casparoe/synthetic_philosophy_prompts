So I'm the student rep on my university's Honor Council this term (we're the body that actually hears academic integrity cases, mix of a few students and a few faculty), and we've got a case up on Thursday that's got the whole group kind of stuck. It's messing with my head extra because I'm also in a philosophy of language seminar right now where we just spent two weeks on Kripke's book on Wittgenstein and the rule-following paradox, and now I can't stop seeing it everywhere.

Here's the case. All the writing-intensive courses at my school share one collaboration policy: students 'may use spelling and grammar-check tools but may not use any tool that generates or substantially rewrites sentences or paragraphs.' A student fed a bunch of her own clunky sentences into an AI writing assistant, told it to 'make this flow better,' and lightly touched up whatever it gave back. She says that's basically souped-up grammar-checking. Her professor says it's obviously substantial rewriting. I went and pulled the dozen or so past cases the Council has decided under that same clause, and none of them are actually like this one -- they're all clearly Grammarly-type stuff on one side or blatant paragraph-generation on the other. So the real problem isn't a factual dispute about what she did; its that nothing about how we've actually applied the rule before seems to settle which side of the line this new case falls on. It's basically the plus/quus thing -- lots of different ways of extending the old rule agree perfectly on every past case and just disagree on this one, and whichever way we rule, it feels less like we're discovering what the policy always meant and more like we're deciding that, right now, for the first time.

Can you draft the memo I'd send around to the rest of the Council before Thursday? I need it to actually come down on a side for this specific student (not 'needs more discussion'), lay out a concrete rewrite of the policy language with a timeline for getting it approved before next semester's syllabi go out, and take seriously -- rather than dismiss as too abstract for a real disciplinary decision -- the objection that at least three Council members are going to raise: that if nothing in the rule's past use actually settles this case, fairness requires giving the student the benefit of the doubt.
