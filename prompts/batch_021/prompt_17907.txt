I'm taking a graduate seminar on comparative ethics this semester and this week's reading was Roger Ames and Henry Rosemont's stuff on "Confucian role ethics" - basically the idea that on a Confucian view persons are constituted by their roles and relations (son, father, minister, etc.) rather than being some prior autonomous self that then goes and chooses to enter relationships, the way a lot of post-Enlightenment western ethics assumes. We got to this passage from the Analects (13.18, Legge's translation) and I got kind of stuck on it:

"The Duke of She informed Confucius, saying, 'Among us here there are those who may be styled upright in their conduct. If their father have stolen a sheep, they will bear witness to the fact.' Confucius said, 'Among us, in our part of the country, those who are upright are different from this. The father conceals the misconduct of the son, and the son conceals the misconduct of the father. Uprightness is to be found in this.'"

I get that this is basically the classic go-to passage for the "Confucianism just sanctions nepotism/corruption" objection (theres a Chinese scholar, Liu Qingping I think, who wrote a whole series of papers accusing Confucian ethics of legitimating corruption off this passage plus the bit in Mencius where Shun runs off carrying his criminal father on his back rather than let him face the judge). What I can't figure out is how a role-ethics reading is actually supposed to answer that objection instead of just conceding it. Is the move that 直 (zhi, "uprightness"/"straightness") was never meant to be some free-standing, context-independent virtue you then apply impartially, so "should the son have testified" is basically a category error once your thinking in role-ethics terms instead of in terms of individual conscience and impartial rules? And if that's the move, doesn't it just relocate the corruption worry rather than dissolve it, into a question of why we should let roles fix what counts as "upright" in the first place? Would love a walkthrough of what the strongest role-ethics reading of this passage looks like and whether it actually holds up against the corruption charge or just relabels it.
