I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, but a friend from my book club recently talked me into reading Kant's "Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals," and one passage in particular has stuck with me. Most people seem to know the shopkeeper example: a shopkeeper who doesn't overcharge his customers gets no moral credit for it if his real motive is simply that honesty is good for business. That part made sense to me. What I found harder to accept is a later example in the same section, where Kant contrasts a man who is naturally so warm-hearted that he takes genuine pleasure in making other people happy with a second man whose sympathy for others has been dulled by grief of his own, but who still forces himself to help people purely because he judges it his duty. Kant seems to say that only the second man's help has genuine moral worth, since the first man is merely following inclination -- which strikes me, and apparently plenty of Kant's later readers, as exactly backwards: the theory seems to reward emotional deadness and penalize ordinary warmth. I've since seen this complaint described online as a kind of "moral fetishism," the idea being that what supposedly matters most is having the right internal thought in your head ("this is my duty") rather than actually caring about the person you're helping.

As far as I can tell, this dispute hasn't been settled. Some of Kant's defenders resist the fetishism charge by arguing that the motive of duty doesn't have to be the thought explicitly running through the warm-hearted man's mind while he acts; it only has to be a kind of standing condition that would take over and produce the same action if his sympathy ever failed or misfired, so a person acting from ordinary affection can still pass Kant's test as long as duty is quietly backing it up. Opponents reply that this concedes the substance of the dispute: once duty is allowed to sit in reserve rather than doing any actual motivational work, the account collapses into the rival view, on which moral worth simply requires that your operative reasons for acting coincide, non-accidentally, with whatever reasons actually make the act right -- with no further requirement that "duty" itself ever cross your mind at all.

I'd like you to write a dialogue between two philosophers occupying these two positions, arguing over a single running example (Kant's grief-numbed helper works fine, or you're welcome to substitute a comparable modern case, but please stay with one example throughout rather than drifting between several, so the exchange stays concrete). I want genuine back-and-forth rather than alternating summaries of each view: press the "standing condition" defender on exactly what is left of the duty requirement once she grants that duty need never be the operative motivating thought, and don't let her get away with a merely verbal answer. Likewise, press her opponent on whether "non-accidental coincidence" of reasons can be spelled out without smuggling something duty-like back in through the side door. Something like 500 to 700 words would be ideal, and I'd rather the piece end with the disagreement still substantively open than have either side concede.
