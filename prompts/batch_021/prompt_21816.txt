I'm a PhD student in philosophy working on social ontology, and this term I've been sitting in on a reading group slowly working through David Lewis's *Convention*. Last week, two of the other regulars, Nadia and Elias, ended up in a disagreement that the rest of us just watched unfold rather than settle, and it's been nagging at me since. The question on the table was whether ordinary queueing — lining up at a bus stop or a coffee counter and being served in the order you arrived — is a genuine instance of a Lewis convention, or whether it only looks like one.

(1) Nadia opened by arguing that queueing is about as clean a case as you could ask for: it is arbitrary, since a population could just as easily allocate service by lottery, by need, or by seniority; it persists purely because everyone expects everyone else to comply; and it would unravel the moment that expectation broke down — exactly the self-perpetuating structure Lewis attributes to a coordination equilibrium. (2) Elias objected that this skips over something load-bearing in Lewis's actual definition: a coordination equilibrium requires that no one would have done better by acting otherwise, given what everyone else does, and that's false of queueing, since whoever cuts to the front is always better off than if they had waited, regardless of how everyone else behaves — so "everyone queues" isn't a coordination equilibrium of the underlying game at all, and the practice holds together only because cutting gets punished with glares, confrontation, or worse. (3) Nadia replied that plenty of paradigm Lewis conventions share this feature: in the meeting-place case, I might privately prefer that you come find me rather than the reverse, and with a shared language, someone might privately gain from lying to me — yet Lewis doesn't treat a little private incentive to deviate as disqualifying, since all he requires is that coincidence of interest predominate, not that conflict be wholly absent. (4) Elias came back that the cases aren't parallel: in the meeting-place and language cases, the private incentive concerns which equilibrium obtains or how it gets used once it's in place, whereas queue-jumping is a standing incentive to defect from the equilibrium itself no matter which regularity is in force — which is exactly why theorists like Ullmann-Margalit treat sanction-backed norms of this kind as a separate category from coordination conventions proper. (5) Nadia's rejoinder was that this concedes too much to labels: a practice that is still arbitrary, still sustained by precedent, and still dependent on common knowledge doesn't stop being a coordination convention just because sanctions are doing some of the stabilizing work — for all Elias has shown, the sanctions might be how a fragile coordination equilibrium gets made stable, rather than proof that no coordination equilibrium was ever there to begin with.

Could you adjudicate this move by move? For each of the five moves, I'd like to know whether it represents Lewis's actual view correctly and whether it succeeds dialectically against what came just before it, and then a final verdict on whether queueing, as they've described it, satisfies Lewis's definition of a convention — along with anything you think both Nadia and Elias are overlooking.
