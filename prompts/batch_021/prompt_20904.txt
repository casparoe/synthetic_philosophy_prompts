Exam question (20 points): Using Clark and Chalmers's Otto/Inga thought experiment, (a) state the parity principle and the conditions under which they think an external resource like Otto's notebook counts as part of his belief system [8 pts]; (b) explain the single most influential objection to the extended mind thesis [8 pts]; and (c) briefly assess whether, if the thesis is true, it has consequences for how we think about the boundaries of the self [4 pts].

Student answer: "Clark and Chalmers ask us to compare Otto, who has Alzheimer's disease and needs a notebook to store new information, with Inga, who has an unimpaired biological memory. When both want to see the same museum exhibition, Inga simply remembers the address, while Otto looks it up in his notebook. Because the notebook plays exactly the functional role that biological memory plays for Inga — it is consulted reliably, its contents are accepted automatically rather than checked against other sources, and Otto endorsed the information when he first wrote it down — the parity principle says we should count the notebook as part of Otto's belief system rather than merely as an aid to it: if a process would count as cognitive were it done in the head, it counts as cognitive when part of it is done outside the head too.

This is really just an application of the externalism about mental content defended earlier by Putnam and Burge: just as a person's Twin-Earth counterpart means something different by 'water' despite having an identical brain, what Otto believes depends on facts about his notebook that lie outside his skull, so the extended mind thesis simply generalizes the lesson of Twin Earth from the content of thoughts to the location of the thought-process itself.

The most influential objection is the coupling-constitution fallacy, first pressed by Fodor: the fact that a process is reliably causally coupled to an external object does not show that the object is part of the process. My pen is causally coupled to my hand when I write, but no one thinks the pen becomes part of my hand. Otto's notebook may just be a coupled resource rather than a genuine part of his cognitive system.

Finally, if the extended mind thesis is correct, it bears directly on personal identity: since Locke ties personal identity to the continuity of memory, and Otto's memory now partly consists of ink on paper, losing the notebook would be, for Otto, tantamount to a partial loss of self in exactly the way that losing biological memories through amnesia would be."
