Every October, my school's nonpartisan voter-registration club, which I co-lead, holds a short assembly for the senior class the week before the registration deadline, where someone gives a few minutes of remarks meant to get newly-eligible eighteen-year-olds to actually register and show up in November. I got asked to give this year's remarks, which is a little awkward, because I'm also in AP Government with Mr. Whitfield, who spent a whole week this fall on what he calls the "paradox of voting": the observation that, for a purely self-interested and instrumentally rational person, the probability that any single ballot in an election of real size will be the one that decides it is so close to zero that the tiny expected benefit is swamped by even the small cost of registering and showing up. Mr. Whitfield is going to be sitting in the front row, and he has a running joke about how almost every "get out the vote" speech he's ever heard is secretly innumerate, because it leans on some version of "your vote could be the one that decides it" — a line I'm embarrassed to admit I used uncritically last year, before I'd taken his class. A couple of friends on the debate team told me to just say it again anyway and move on, but I don't want to hand him that satisfaction twice, and the other four hundred people in the gym have never heard of the paradox and are not going to sit through a lecture on expected-value calculations; they just want a reason to actually walk over to the registration table afterward.

So I'd like help writing those remarks: about four minutes long, meant to be delivered out loud, not read as an essay. I don't want to pretend the paradox doesn't exist, and I don't want to sneak the "decisive vote" line back in through the side door, since at this point that would basically be a lie dressed up as encouragement — but I also don't want the speech to talk people out of voting by dwelling on the math. What I'm hoping for is something that's fully honest about why the narrow, self-interested case for voting is weaker than it sounds, while still arriving at a genuine, non-cynical reason to vote anyway — something sturdier than just saying "civic duty" and hoping nobody in the audience asks what that actually means. Could you write it for me?
