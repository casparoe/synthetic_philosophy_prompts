I drive more or less the same stretch of highway to work every day, and I still remember one morning, a few years back, when I missed my exit simply because I could not tell, in the moment, whether the intersection ahead of me was the first one or the second one on that road. A friend of mine who is into recreational mathematics later told me that this exact situation has a name in decision theory: the "absent-minded driver problem." As I understand the setup, a driver on a road with two visually identical intersections needs to take the second one to get home; taking the first is the worst outcome, missing both exits entirely is a mediocre outcome, and getting off at the second is the best outcome, and the driver cannot tell which intersection he is currently at because, this being the specific feature that makes him "absent-minded" rather than merely uninformed, he retains no memory of already having passed the first intersection by the time he reaches the second. Working through the arithmetic myself with a pencil and paper, I could reconstruct why the best fixed probability of exiting, chosen once in advance, comes out to exactly one in three. What I could not shake off was the feeling that if I put myself in the driver's seat, not knowing whether the intersection in front of me is the first or the second, my instinct is to treat the two as equally likely, and I have not been able to get that instinct to square with the one-third figure.

That discomfort sent me down a small rabbit hole of forum threads and secondhand summaries of the academic exchange this problem apparently set off. I gather it was originally posed by two economists, Piccione and Rubinstein, if I am remembering the names correctly, sometime in the late 1990s, and that it immediately drew a reply arguing there was no genuine paradox at all, plus a slow trickle of further proposed fixes in the years since. From what I can piece together, the candidate resolutions include treating the puzzle as a pure modeling confusion that dissolves once you are careful about what a "probability of being at this intersection" could even mean; a "modified multi-selves" idea, on which each intersection gets its own little decision-maker rather than there being a single driver reasoning across the whole trip; arguments imported wholesale from the halfer-versus-thirder dispute over the Sleeping Beauty problem; and at least one attempt to dissolve the whole thing by switching from causal to evidential decision theory. I do not have any training in game theory or philosophy to referee this myself, which is exactly why I would value your judgment on it.
