I'm sketching a new novel built around a near-future consumer device that amounts, in practice, to Robert Nozick's experience machine: you climb into a tank wired with electrodes and remain there, floating, for as long as you like, while the machine feeds your brain a seamless stream of whatever experiences you selected in advance -- finishing the novel you abandoned, reconciling with an estranged sibling, watching your children grow up happy -- each one indistinguishable from the real thing. One of my point-of-view characters is an elderly woman who has spent her career translating nineteenth-century Russian fiction, and in the scene I'm currently drafting she can't stop turning the device over in her mind by way of Dostoevsky, whom she has lived with on the page for decades and instinctively treats as an authority on exactly this kind of question. Before I commit her reasoning to the page, I'd like to work out, properly, what that reasoning should be.

So, setting aside the small matter that Dostoevsky died in 1881, close to a century before Nozick set out the experience machine in Anarchy, State, and Utopia (1974): what do you think his actual verdict on the machine would have been, and on what grounds? I'd rather not have the one-line answer ("Dostoevsky prized suffering, so he'd refuse") -- I want the argument reconstructed properly, out of the specific places where he had, in effect, already fought this battle: the Underground Man's assault on Chernyshevsky's Crystal Palace and on rational egoism generally, the Grand Inquisitor's case that most human beings will trade freedom for bread, miracle, and authority, and the corrupted paradise of "The Dream of a Ridiculous Man." Use that material to work out, concretely, how he would engage with Nozick's own three reasons for expecting people to refuse the machine -- that we want to do things and not merely have the experience of having done them, that we want to be a certain kind of person rather than a passive vessel with no determinate character of its own, and that plugging in confines us to a reality of purely human manufacture -- and tell me plainly where he would agree with Nozick, where he would part ways with him, and where he might simply refuse the terms of the thought experiment. I'd also like your sense of what theory of well-being, if any, is doing the work in his answer: is he best read as trading hedonic well-being for some rival, freedom- or struggle-based good, or as denying outright that well-being, on any theory of it, is what a life should ultimately be judged by?
