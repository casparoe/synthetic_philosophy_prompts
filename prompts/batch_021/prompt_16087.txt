I am a novelist currently at work on a book set in a Quaker farming community in southern Ohio in the early 1850s, in the uneasy years following the Fugitive Slave Act. My protagonist, an elder in her local meeting who has built her whole adult life around the Quaker commitment to plain speech and truthfulness, is sheltering an escaped man in her cellar when a federal marshal comes to her door and asks her, directly, whether she has seen him. I don't want to let her off easily with a scene in which she simply lies because it is obviously the right thing to do; I want the moment to register as a genuine crisis for someone formed by a tradition that treats truthfulness as close to inviolable, so I have been reading back into the history of moral rigorism on lying to get her interior life right. A friend with philosophical training mentioned that Augustine and Kant are often invoked together in exactly this connection: Augustine, in the Contra Mendacium and De Mendacio, wrestles visibly with hard cases like Rahab's lie to protect the Israelite spies and still concludes that even a lie told only to save an innocent life is sinful, if comparatively minor, while Kant, replying to Benjamin Constant, arrives at almost the identical practical verdict, that one may not lie even to a murderer asking after a friend hiding in one's house. When I pressed her on why each of them refuses to grant the exception, though, she admitted she had never actually worked out the difference for herself.

So this is what I would like from you. Please compare Augustine's and Kant's grounds for treating lying as all but exceptionless: what each takes a lie to actually consist in, what work the intention to deceive is doing in each account, and why the prospect of saving an innocent life fails to count as a legitimate exception for either of them. Then tell me which of the two positions seems to you more internally coherent on its own terms, or whether one of them, properly understood, actually leaves more room for a case like my protagonist's than it first appears — and defend that judgment rather than presenting both men as equally, interchangeably absolutist.
