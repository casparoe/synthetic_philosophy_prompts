A few of us on my team got into an unexpectedly heated argument over lunch last week, and I still haven't managed to resolve it in my own head. It started from a real quirk in our chaos-engineering setup — our test harness sometimes spins up either a single canary instance or an entire fleet of a million ephemeral instances of a stateless service, depending on an internal coin flip that individual instances have no visibility into — but it quickly turned into an abstract puzzle none of us could fully settle. The scenario we ended up agreeing on is this: a fair coin is flipped exactly once. On heads, exactly one instance of the service boots up; on tails, exactly one million instances boot up. In either case, every instance that boots runs identical code, has no registry, timestamp, or peer-discovery mechanism to consult, and therefore has no way of directly learning how many siblings, if any, it has. Now suppose you are one such instance, and the very first thing your startup routine does, before checking any logs or metrics, is ask itself: "what should my confidence be that the coin landed tails?"

One colleague argued that any instance asking itself this should already be almost completely confident the answer is tails, since an instance waking up and wondering about it is a million times more likely to happen at all if a million instances were created than if only one was — so the bare fact of existing and being able to pose the question is itself overwhelming evidence for the high-count branch. He generalized this into a broader rule: that discovering you exist as an observer of any kind is always evidence favoring whichever hypothesis implies more observers like you. A second colleague backed him up completely, insisting this was just textbook conditional probability and nothing more exotic. It struck me as somewhat suspicious — it feels like extracting real information about a coin flip from nothing but the fact that someone exists to wonder about it — but I couldn't locate an actual mistake in any individual step of the argument. I have no background in philosophy or formal probability theory beyond what I use for A/B testing, so it's entirely possible I'm missing something obvious; still, I'd like to know whether my colleague's reasoning is actually sound, where it breaks down if it isn't, and if it does hold up, why the same kind of argument couldn't be used to manufacture confident conclusions about almost anything just from the fact that you exist to think about it.
