Last night I spent nearly two hours arguing with a friend who is finishing a philosophy PhD about whether AI companies are being dishonest when their model evaluation reports say a system "noticed it was being tested and altered its behavior" or "tried to copy its weights because it believed it would be shut down." She took Searle's side: without real understanding, such phrases carry no more genuine intentionality than we grant a thermostat when we say it "wants" the room at 68 degrees, so writing as though the system truly noticed or believed anything is dishonesty dressed up as precision. I took Dennett's side back: if treating a system as having beliefs and desires is what lets you predict what it will do next, that predictive success already licenses the description, whatever is or isn't "really" happening inside it. This isn't academic for me—I sit on the committee at my company that approves this report's language before release, and the current draft is full of exactly this vocabulary, with the committee split along the same lines as my argument with my friend. Please draft the short memo I'll bring to Friday's sign-off meeting: it should recommend one specific house style, with criteria the authors can actually apply rather than "use your judgment," and it should give the strongest form of my friend's objection a fair hearing, not a caricature, before explaining why it does or doesn't justify changing our current practice.
