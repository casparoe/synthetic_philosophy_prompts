I run a small podcast on ethics and practical reason, and next week's guest is a philosopher who has spent much of her career defending "parity" -- the claim that hard choices, like deciding between a stable but unexciting career and a riskier, more meaningful one, involve a fourth basic value relation over and above one option being better than, worse than, or exactly as good as the other. I've been mulling over how to interview her well ever since a long argument I had a few nights ago with a close friend, who is actually facing a version of that exact dilemma and had just watched a TED talk on the subject. I kept insisting that "on a par" is just dressed-up language for "it's vague which one is better" or "they're roughly equal," and that inventing a whole new relation to explain the phenomenon is overkill. She pushed back with the small-improvement argument: if the two careers were truly equally good, a slight improvement to one should make it the better option, and if one were truly better or worse, a slight improvement to the other shouldn't be able to erase that gap -- yet neither happens in a genuine hard choice, so the relation between them can't be equality, betterness, or worseness. When I said that just sounds like evidence the comparison is vague rather than evidence of some new relation, she accused me of quietly assuming that better, worse, and equal are the only three ways two things can relate to each other -- which is exactly the assumption parity is meant to deny.

I'd like your help turning this into eight to ten interview questions that don't just let her restate the view the way every summary of her work already does. Please include at least one question on whether the small-improvement argument really rules out a vagueness-based account or is merely compatible with it, one on the worry that repeatedly sweetening one option while it stays on a par with the other threatens to generate something like a sorites problem, or even outright intransitivity, over enough iterations, and one on whether her notion of "normative agency" -- resolving a hard choice by committing to reasons rather than discovering them -- is doing real philosophical work or simply relabels an arbitrary pick as an act of self-authorship. For each of these, add a follow-up question keyed to the answer I expect her to give: that anyone who reaches for vagueness, rough equality, or incomparability is quietly presupposing that better, worse, and equal exhaust the field, which begs the question against parity from the outset. Please close with a question about what, if anything, she thinks would count as genuine evidence against parity and in favor of one of those rival explanations.
