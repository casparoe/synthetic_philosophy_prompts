I'm several chapters into a novel set in Jena in 1924, narrated in part by a fictional young mathematician who has struck up an uneasy friendship with the elderly, by-then-retired Gottlob Frege. In the course of research for the book, I finally read the private diary Frege kept for a few weeks that same year -- the one that sat among his papers, unpublished and essentially unknown, until it surfaced decades after his death. It is a genuinely unpleasant document: contempt for the Weimar parliamentary system, hostility toward Catholics and socialists, and explicit antisemitism, including the view that Jews should be stripped of political rights. From what I can tell, even Michael Dummett, who probably did more than anyone to establish Frege's reputation within English-language philosophy, admitted years later that reading it had disturbed him badly, though he insisted it left Frege's actual arguments untouched.

I raised this at my writing group, where I've been workshopping a scene in which my narrator presses an old colleague of Frege's on exactly this question, and one member's argument has stuck with me since. Her claim was that it's too convenient to file the diary under "biography" and keep it safely away from "the philosophy": the very same cast of mind, she said, is at work in both places -- a conviction that truth is single and objective, discernible only by the rightly trained, and owing nothing to what majorities happen to believe -- and that this conviction is what generates both his contempt for parliamentary democracy and his Platonism about numbers, his lifelong war on "psychologism" in logic. If that's right, she argued, then anyone who thinks the diary should shake our confidence in Frege the political thinker owes us a reason why it shouldn't equally shake our confidence in Frege the logicist. I'd like my narrator's colleague to give a genuinely strong reply to this -- one a careful philosopher would recognize as doing real work, rather than just muttering "ad hominem" and changing the subject -- so what I'd actually like to know, before I put words in anyone's mouth, is whether my friend is onto something real, or whether the argument is weaker than it sounds, and if so, exactly where it gives out.
