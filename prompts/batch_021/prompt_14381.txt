My grandfather (a retired logician, mostly set theory and some model theory) died on Tuesday, and I've somehow ended up on the hook to give the eulogy Saturday, which is already a lot, and its made worse by the fact that he was a lifelong, very sincere Gödel person — not the incompleteness-theorem stuff everybody name-drops, but specifically Gödel's own private argument for why an afterlife basically has to exist, the one he apparently worked out in letters to his own mother. The gist, as grandpa told it to us about a hundred times over the years (always at the worst possible moment, like literally at other people's funerals): Gödel was so fanatically committed to the idea that the world is rationally constructed — same impulse behind his ontological proof, behind his whole platonism about math — that he basically reasoned his way into an afterlife, on the grounds that a mind's entire "cultivation," all that development, just switching off for no further reason would be irrational, and the world doesn't do things for no reason. Grandpa took this as his actual, literal position, completely deadpan, no priest, no soft "better place" talk, and he told my mom flat out that he didn't want his own eulogy going mushy with any watching-over-us stuff he would've found embarrassing — but he clearly wanted the argument itself respected, since he kept bringing it up unprompted at, again, other people's funerals.

Last night my friend Dev and I got into a genuinely long argument about whether the argument is even any good, or whether it's just Gödel (and then grandpa, at one remove) wanting an afterlife badly enough to dress the wanting up as logic. We never got anywhere. Now I've got two days to actually write this thing, for a room that's split down the middle — half his old math department colleagues, who know this story cold and will notice immediately if I flatten it into nonsense, and half cousins and family friends who have never heard of Gödel and just want to feel okay about loosing him. Could you write the eulogy? Something like 5-6 minutes read out loud, and it actually has to use the argument structurally — like the logic of it should be doing real comforting work for the room, not just sitting there as a fun fact about grandpa — while staying honest to how bloodless and rationalist his actual version of it was.
