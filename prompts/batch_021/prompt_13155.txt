I'm one of three members on the board of my small condominium association, and something that happened at last month's meeting has been bothering me ever since. We had to vote on a special assessment to replace the building's roof, and under our bylaws the assessment can only pass if two separate conditions both hold: a licensed inspector has to confirm that the roof genuinely needs replacing, and the contractor's bid has to come in under the spending ceiling the owners approved last year. Taken condition by condition, a majority of us agreed on both counts — our president and our treasurer both thought the roof was failing, and our president and I both thought the bid was reasonable. But when it came time to vote on the assessment itself, all things considered, it failed two to one: I voted no because I was not convinced the roof was really failing, our treasurer voted no because he thought the bid was too high, and only our president, who agreed with both conditions, voted yes. Nobody was confused, nobody misspoke, and each of us was completely consistent — yet the "yes" that seemed to follow from our own majority votes on the two conditions never actually materialized as the board's decision.

Curious whether this had a name, I did some reading and learned that it is an instance of what philosophers call the discursive dilemma, or in its original legal form the doctrinal paradox, and that Christian List and Philip Pettit in particular have written about exactly this structure turning up in courts, juries, and committees. The conclusion I have drawn is that this shows there is not really any such thing as "what the board decided," over and above our three separate votes — that saying a board, a jury, or a court "believes" or "holds" some position is just a convenient shorthand for a tallying procedure that can be pushed toward either answer depending on how the questions are divided, with no genuine group judgment underneath it at all. A friend who used to sit on the board with me thinks I am making far too much of one unlucky vote split, but I cannot find the flaw in the reasoning. Am I drawing the right lesson from this, or is there something about the underlying idea that I am misunderstanding?
