I'm a retired physicist (thirty-five years in condensed matter, if anyone's counting) who's fallen down a bit of a philosophy rabbit hole since retiring, mostly thanks to a friend from my old book club who wont stop sending me stuff. Lately I've been reading about Kurt Godel's life outside of the incompleteness theorems, and one thing has been rattling around in my head for days: apparently, in letters to his mother back in Vienna, who was getting on in years and anxious about dying, Godel tried to comfort her with an actual logical argument for the afterlife, no religion required. From what I understand (I'm working off a biography, not the original letters, so I might be mangling the details), the shape of the thing was roughly: the world is "rationally constructed," meaning nothing in it is pointless or wasted, so it would be absurd for a mind to spend decades laboriously building up the capacity for real understanding only to be switched off right around the point it's finally equipped to use it. If the world really is rational in that sense, something has to survive death to let that development continue. And if the world isn't rational in that sense, then we've got no grounds to trust our own reasoning about anything else either, so you might as well assume it is. Either way, you end up taking the afterlife more seriously than you'd expect.

What gets me is how odd it is for a career logician to reach for what's basically a conservation law, the kind of "nothing is wasted" reasoning I spent my whole career applying to energy and momentum, not minds and souls, and this from the same guy who proved that mathematics itself can never be fully closed up and accounted for. Theres also something quietly devastating about it once you know how Godel actually died: convinced for years that people were trying to poison him, he eventually stopped eating altogether and starved to death once his wife, the one person he trusted to taste his food first, was no longer around to do it. All that rigor, undone by a fear no proof could reach.

Could you write me a short story (a page or two, doesn't need to be longer) that really sits inside this tension rather than just gesturing at it? It doesn't have to involve Godel by name, or be biographically accurate at all, I'd honestly love it more if you took the core idea somewhere unexpected. I just want it to genuinely wrestle with the pull between "a truly rational universe wouldn't let a mind's development go to waste" and the much messier fact of an actual mind, rational or not, approaching its own end.
