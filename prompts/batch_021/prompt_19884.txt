I've never studied logic or philosophy formally, but I've spent a good while working through Gödel's ontological proof of God's existence on my own -- the modal-logic version built around "positive" properties, rather than Anselm's original wording. I can follow the main line of reasoning: positive properties are shown to be jointly consistent, so that having all of them at once isn't contradictory; being God-like, defined as having every positive property, is then additionally assumed to itself be positive; this is what allows God-likeness to count as an "essence" of anything that has it; and necessary existence is folded in as a positive property, so that a God-like being ends up existing in every possible world. What I can't get my head around is an objection I ran into afterward, sometimes called "modal collapse": the claim that Gödel's own axioms don't just prove God's existence, but also force the far stranger conclusion that every true proposition is necessarily true, leaving no room for real contingency anywhere. Could you walk me through exactly how the axioms generate that result, which specific assumption is really doing the damage, and whether there's a plausible way to revise the system that blocks the collapse without gutting the whole argument?
