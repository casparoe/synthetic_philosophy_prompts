Random tangent brought on by a reread of the Pensées this week, plus an argument with my reading group over drinks: everyone kept bringing up "Pascal's Mugging" (Bostrom's thought experiment, the one that's spawned a thousand LW posts including Yudkowsky's "Pascal's Muggle") like its just a cute abstract puzzle for modern decision theorists, and it struck me that its basically just Pascal's own move in the Wager pushed to a reductio -- a tiny, even vanishing, probability times a big enough payoff swamps everything else. So now I want to know what the actual 17th-century guy, not the eponym, would say if someone sat him down and explained the mugging scenario to him.

Would Pascal see it as a fatal problem for the reasoning he uses in the Wager, since that argument explicitly leans on "any finite risk is worth taking for infinite gain, however small the odds"? Or does he have real resources elsewhere in his own thought to turn the mugger away while keeping the Wager intact -- I'm thinking of his tests for telling genuine prophecy and miracles apart from imposture (which take up a big chunk of the Pensées), his insistence that the Wager isn't "any claim of infinite payoff, so weigh the odds" but one specific bet backed by fulfilled prophecy and testimony, his esprit de géométrie vs. esprit de finesse distinction, and his general suspicion of unaided reason set against "the heart has its reasons." Can you reconstruct the strongest Pascal-flavored response using only tools that would've actually been available to him (no bounded utility functions or leverage penalties -- those are 21st-century patches he never had access to), and then tell me straight whether it actually holds up, or whether he's just getting mugged in slow motion without noticing?
