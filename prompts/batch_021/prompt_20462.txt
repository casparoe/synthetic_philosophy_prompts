Augustine, in *De Mendacio* and the later *Contra Mendacium*, defines a lie as an assertion made with the will to deceive, and takes a stricter line than most Christian moralists, before or since, in holding that lying is intrinsically sinful whatever good end it might serve -- even while allowing that one may mislead through silence, evasion, or a truthful but deliberately ambiguous statement. He grades lies by gravity according to their subject matter and according to whom they harm or benefit, yet even the most sympathetic case he considers -- a lie that would spare an innocent person being hunted down for murder -- remains for him a sin, if the least grave kind: the fugitive may be hidden or misdirected, but never saved by an outright falsehood. Crucially, his case against lying does not rest solely on the wrong done to a deceived hearer: he repeatedly insists that the lie corrupts the liar's own soul by dividing heart from tongue, a wrong he takes to obtain even when the attempted deception fails or harms no one.

Now suppose you explained to Augustine the now-ordinary practice of feeding automated systems -- search engines, retailers, data brokers -- with fabricated personal details (false birthdates, decoy searches, spoofed locations) purely to frustrate the profiles they compile, with no human being individually addressed or deceived anywhere in the process. Reconstruct, as precisely as you can from his actual arguments rather than from the bare letter of his prohibition, the verdict you think he would reach, and explain whether the absence of a human interlocutor would matter to him at all, given what he takes the act of lying to corrupt in the first place.
