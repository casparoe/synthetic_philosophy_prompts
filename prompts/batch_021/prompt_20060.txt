For a seminar on the ethics of truth-telling, I've just finished reading Augustine's two treatises on lying, De Mendacio and Contra Mendacium, alongside Kant's discussion of truthfulness in the Doctrine of Virtue and his essay 'On a Supposed Right to Lie from Philanthropy.' Both writers end up defending something close to an exceptionless prohibition on lying, refusing to allow an exception even when a lie is the only way to shield an innocent person from serious harm. For my response paper, I want to compare the reasoning behind their absolutism rather than just the shared conclusion: is Kant's position essentially a more systematic, secularized descendant of Augustine's — given that Augustine ties the wrongness of lying to the sinfulness of the will to deceive and to a love of truth inseparable from love of God, while Kant grounds it in the dignity of rational agency and the demands of universalizability — or do these different foundations matter enough that the shared verdict is more coincidence than genuine convergence?
