A friend of mine and I spent much of a long dinner arguing about Plato's treatment of drunkenness in the Laws, and I came away less certain of my own position than when we started. She had read the dialogue more recently than I had, and insisted that Plato is not simply tolerating wine-drinking as a necessary social evil, but constructing a genuine argument that carefully organized, supervised drinking parties are valuable — perhaps even necessary — for cultivating self-mastery in adult citizens. The core of her case was an analogy she attributed to the Athenian Stranger: just as a lawgiver who wants to produce courage might deliberately expose people to controlled fears in order to observe and train their reactions, a lawgiver who wants to produce temperance might deliberately expose people to controlled intoxication in order to observe and train theirs, with a sober "president" of the symposium keeping the exercise from tipping over into real license. I objected that this sits very awkwardly with the far more familiar Platonic picture, from dialogues like the Republic and the Gorgias, of reason needing to govern appetite and pleasure — deliberately loosening one's rational grip seems like a strange way of reinforcing it. She also brought up something about wine being described as a gift that lets older men recover a kind of second youth and rejoin a "chorus" of poetic and musical education normally reserved for children, which I honestly could not follow at all by the end of the evening.

I would like a clearer, more textually grounded reconstruction than either of us managed to produce on the spot. Could you lay out, in some depth, the actual argument Plato gives for why regulated drunkenness serves rather than undermines the cultivation of sōphrosynē? I am especially interested in: how the courage-and-fear analogy is supposed to carry over to the case of pleasure and wine, and whether the disanalogies between the two cases (for instance, the very different risks involved in each) matter; what work the sober supervisor is doing that makes the exercise instructive rather than merely indulgent; and how the point about wine, old age, and choral education fits into the larger argument, if it does at all. Finally, I would be curious to hear whether, in your assessment, this treatment of drunkenness is a genuine exception to Plato's usual wariness of the appetites, or whether it turns out, once properly unpacked, to be continuous with it.
