My younger sister is getting married in three weeks to a philosophy PhD student whose dissertation is on Luis de Molina's account of middle knowledge, and somewhere over the course of their courtship she went from rolling her eyes at his dinner-table explanations of scientia media to being, by her own account, a fully persuaded Molinist herself. They have asked me, as maid of honor, to build my toast around that concept, on the condition that it actually do something with it rather than reduce it to a decorative line about God always knowing they would end up together. I have spent the past month working through Molina, Flint, and Craig well enough to follow the basic architecture — natural knowledge, then middle knowledge of the counterfactuals of freedom, then free knowledge of whatever actually obtains once God selects a world to create — but I am nervous about the room: our mother's side of the family is Reformed and tends to hear anything short of exhaustive divine determination of the will as a slight against God's sovereignty, while several of my sister's philosophy friends will notice immediately if the toast quietly turns middle knowledge into either simple foreknowledge or garden-variety compatibilism just to make the story easier to tell.

Could you write this toast for me? It should run about five minutes read aloud, be genuinely warm and a little funny rather than a seminar, and still put the concept to real work: I want it to explain, in terms a non-philosopher relative can follow, how my sister's marriage can be at once something God specifically foreknew and providentially built the world around, and something she and her fiancé freely chose, without letting either half of that claim soften the other. It should not collapse into "it was meant to be" fatalism, and it should not quietly resolve the tension with our Reformed relatives by sliding toward determinism either; a brief, honest nod to the fact that Molinists still owe everyone an answer to the grounding objection would land better with this crowd than pretending the view has no loose threads.
