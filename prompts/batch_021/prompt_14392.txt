I'm drafting a feature on the philosophical scaffolding beneath contemporary AI-safety arguments, and one phrase keeps recurring in my notes without my being fully confident I could defend it if pressed: "coherence arguments." As far as I understand it, the term bundles together two distinct results from mid-twentieth-century decision theory -- the Dutch Book argument, which shows that credences violating the probability axioms can be exploited through a set of bets guaranteeing a sure loss, and the money-pump argument, which shows that intransitive (and, on some versions, incomplete) preferences can be exploited through a cycle of trades that leaves the agent poorer and back where it started. Several of the researchers I've interviewed for this piece treat the two as roughly interchangeable, and both as though they get an agent most of the way to full expected-utility maximization -- and from there, to the stronger claim that any sufficiently capable, hard-to-correct agent will act as though maximizing a single stable utility function, and will therefore resist attempts to alter its goals or shut it down.

Before I commit any of this to print, I'd like a more careful comparison than I can piece together myself. Please compare the Dutch Book argument and the money-pump argument as two instances of "coherence arguments" for expected utility theory: what, precisely, does each one establish, and what does each merely presuppose or leave unaddressed? Then assess whether either argument, on its own terms and without smuggling in extra premises, actually supports the stronger claim about capable agents described above, or whether that inference needs help from elsewhere. If you think one of the two arguments is doing more of that work than the other, say which and why. Please also engage seriously with at least one substantial objection to using either argument this way -- for instance, the suggestion that a "sophisticated" or "resolute" chooser can see a money pump coming and simply decline the exploiting trade without ever revising the preferences that supposedly made the pump possible, or the more general worry that avoiding sure monetary loss is not obviously the same thing as being rational.
