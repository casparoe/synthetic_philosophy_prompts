For my formal epistemology seminar we've spent the last two weeks on Glymour's problem of old evidence and I've kind of gotten obsessed with it over the weekend, ngl. Quick recap in case it helps calibrate what I mean: Bayesian confirmation theory says E confirms H iff P(H|E) > P(H), but if E is already known for certain (P(E) = 1), conditionalizing on it can't move your credence in anything, since P(H|E) just collapses to P(H). So on the letter of the theory, the already-known anomalous precession of Mercury's perihelion couldn't have confirmed General Relativity over Newtonian gravity once Einstein showed GR predicts it exactly -- which seems nuts, since basically every historian of science treats that as one of the strongest pieces of evidence for GR, and this is well before the 1919 eclipse results even came in.

My professor ran through a handful of the responses people have given to this over the last ~40 years but never told us which one she actually buys, and now I can't stop trying to rank them myself. The ones I remember (roughly): Garber and Jeffrey's move of relaxing logical omniscience, so what really confirms H is coming to realize -- uncertainly, since none of us are perfect logicians -- that H entails E, rather than learning E itself; Howson's counterfactual strategy of asking what your credence in H would've been if you "subtracted out" your prior knowledge of E, and using that gap as the real measure of confirmation; Eells' response that a lot of the puzzle is actually about the psychology and timing of when a scientist happens to notice an entailment relation, not about confirmation itself, so it partly dissolves once you separate those two things; Earman's much more skeptical line in Bayes or Bust that none of these patches actually work and the whole episode just exposes a real limit of Bayesian confirmation theory; and Sprenger's newer counterfactual-conditionalization proposal, which is supposed to fix specific technical problems with Howson's version.
