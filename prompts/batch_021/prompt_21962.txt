So I fell down an internet rabbit hole this week that started with an AI alignment blog post about "coherence arguments" -- the claim that any sufficiently smart agent basically gets forced into acting like an expected-utility maximizer, because otherwise you can build a "money pump" that bleeds it dry over time -- and it ended up in some decision theory papers from the 90s about "resolute choice" that I'm having a hard time reconciling. The setup I get: if your preferences are cyclic (you prefer A to B, B to C, and C to A), a bookie can offer you a sequence of trades that each look like a slight upgrade but walk you right back around to your starting point minus a small fee every loop, forever. Standard moral: rationality requires transitive, or at least acyclic, preferences, on pain of getting exploited like that. But then apparently a guy named McClennen argued you can keep the cyclic preferences and still dodge the pump completely, as long as your a "resolute chooser" who commits to a whole plan up front instead of re-deciding trade by trade -- and later people (Rabinowicz? and I think a more recent guy, Gustafsson) tried to rebuild the pump so it still catches resolute choosers over "the long run," which is the part I really can't follow just from skimming summaries.

Could you write me a dialogue between two decision theorists hashing this out face to face, basically standing at the bookie's table with the trade sheet between them -- one defending the traditional money-pump argument (cyclic or incomplete preferences are irrational because they're exploitable, full stop), and one defending resolute choice as a genuine way to keep those preferences and still be pump-proof? I don't want either side strawmanned -- I want the pump-defender to actually construct a concrete cyclic example and a specific run of trades, the resolute-choice person to explain precisely how committing to a plan blocks that particular run, and then the pump-defender to come back with the strongest "okay but here's a cleverer version that gets you over the long run" move, with the resolute-choice person either finding a real answer or actually biting the bullet.
