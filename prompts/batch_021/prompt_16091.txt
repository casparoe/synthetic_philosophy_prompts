I am several chapters into a novel that follows two estranged siblings — a marine biologist and a biomedical engineer who designs prosthetic organs — who are forced back into each other's company after a research-vessel disaster leaves them stranded together on a remote station. I want the friction between them to run deeper than temperament: I would like it to trace back to a genuine disagreement over what it even means for a biological trait to have a "function," since that word is clearly doing different work depending on which of my research sources is using it. My biologist character thinks historically: a trait's function is whatever effect explains why it was retained by natural selection, so a heart's function is to pump blood because ancestral hearts that pumped blood well outcompeted those that did not. My engineer character, trained to reverse-engineer systems rather than narrate their history, thinks of function as whatever a part currently contributes to some capacity of the system it sits within, regardless of how it got there.

I gather these positions correspond, roughly, to the etiological account of function associated with Larry Wright and Karen Neander on one side, and the causal-role account associated with Robert Cummins on the other, and I would like the dialogue I eventually write to represent the strongest version of each rather than a strawman. So before drafting the scene, I would like your help getting the underlying philosophy right. Could you compare the two accounts, laying out clearly what each is actually committed to, and then test them against the hard cases that keep surfacing in my notes: the normativity of malfunction (why a heart currently failing to pump still has pumping as its function); brand-new, one-off beneficial mutations with no selective history behind them yet; structures whose current use has drifted from whatever they were originally shaped by selection to do, such as feathers or insect wings; and the standing worry that a causal-role theorist, simply by choosing which capacity and which containing system to analyze, can make almost anything count as the function of almost anything. Having worked through those cases, I would like you to reach and defend an actual verdict rather than leaving the matter open: is one of the two accounts the more defensible general theory of biological function, or is the honest conclusion that they are answering different questions and are not truly in competition — in which case I should probably write my siblings as talking past one another rather than as having a disagreement that either of them could win?
