I've been sitting in on a graduate reading group working through Spinoza's political writings this term — the political chapters of the Theological-Political Treatise, then the unfinished Political Treatise — and I've drafted the following short argument to bring to our next session. Before I do, I'd like comments on whether my reconstruction of Spinoza's position is textually fair, whether the inference I draw from it actually holds up, and whether I've simply rediscovered a point the secondary literature already treats as settled.

Spinoza's case for democracy rests on identifying natural right with power: in the state of nature, an individual's right extends exactly as far as their actual capacity to preserve themselves, and this equation does not lapse once individuals combine into a commonwealth — a sovereign's right, too, is measured by the multitude's actual collective power rather than by any further promise that could bind beyond what power secures, so that a command the state lacks the power to enforce was never really within its right to begin with. On this basis, the Theological-Political Treatise argues, in the chapter laying out its social-contract-like construction of the state, that democracy is the most "natural" of regimes, and pointedly calls it the most "absolute," since the whole multitude holds its power together rather than delegating it to some part of itself; in transferring their right to the very body of which they remain members, citizens retain more of their original equality than they would under a monarch or a ruling council. The argument seems to promise that democracy is uniquely legitimate because, alone among constitutions, it conserves rather than alienates the power each person already has.

The trouble is what happens when Spinoza actually sits down to specify who counts as a citizen. The Political Treatise breaks off unfinished at his death, by most accounts in the middle of a sentence, only a few paragraphs into its chapter on democracy — and what little he manages to write restricts full citizenship to adult men, explicitly excluding women (along with servants and anyone otherwise dependent on another's will) on the grounds that they lack the requisite power, not merely the customary status. My claim is that this is not an embarrassing lapse to be bracketed off from "the real theory," but the theory working exactly as designed: since Spinoza never lets natural right outrun actual power, any power asymmetry that happens to obtain between two classes of people — men and women in his own society, say — converts automatically, by his own principle, into a difference in natural political right. If that's correct, "right equals power" cannot be doing the normative work the naturalness-of-democracy argument needs from it, since the same principle would just as readily ratify whatever distribution of power happens to obtain, democratic or patriarchal alike.

What I'd most like pushback on: is there a resource elsewhere in Spinoza — his distinction between power grounded in the passions and power grounded in reason, say, or his account of how a multitude's power can grow precisely through rational agreement among otherwise unequal individuals — that blocks the slide from "right tracks power" to "existing hierarchies are thereby natural rights," without simply giving up the equation that made the defense of democracy work in the first place? And is this reading of the exclusion passage actually doing interpretive work, or have I just rediscovered an objection the literature already has a standard answer to?
