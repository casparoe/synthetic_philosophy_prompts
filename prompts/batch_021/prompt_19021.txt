I teach a graduate seminar on the philosophy of probability, and this term we spent several weeks on the reference class problem: the difficulty, pressed most forcefully by Alan Hájek, that assigning a probability to a single, non-repeatable event or individual requires locating it within some class of "relevantly similar" cases, yet there is generally no principled, non-arbitrary way to pick out the right class, since narrower classes are more specific but statistically thin, while broader classes are more robust but coarse-grained. We worked through the canonical illustrations at length: Reichenbach's man who may or may not live to see his forty-first birthday, depending on whether he is classified by age alone, by age and sex, or by age, sex, and occupation; the Doomsday Argument's sensitivity to whether the relevant reference class is "all humans" or something narrower tied to one's specific evidence; and the recurring worries about actuarial and algorithmic risk scores, whose outputs shift depending on which comparison population is chosen.

My students can now recite these cases fluently, which is exactly the problem I want to move past: I would like to test genuine understanding of the puzzle's underlying structure rather than mere familiarity with its most famous instances. So here is my request. Construct an entirely new example of the reference class problem, set in a domain that none of the cases above touches (nothing about mortality tables, cosmology or anthropic reasoning, and nothing about criminal recidivism or insurance underwriting). The example should feature a single, precisely specifiable individual or event that can be legitimately classified in at least two different, independently defensible ways, such that the resulting probability estimates do not merely differ numerically but flip the qualitatively correct practical verdict -- for instance, from "safe" to "dangerous," or "approve" to "deny." Please spell out explicitly what the competing reference classes are and why each has a genuine claim to relevance in this case, and then close with a brief assessment of whether your particular example admits of some principled resolution (for instance, by appeal to a narrowest-class or maximal-specificity rule) or is better read as a fresh illustration of the problem's genuine indeterminacy.
