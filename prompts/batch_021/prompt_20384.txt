I spent forty years as a field ecologist working mostly on intertidal gastropods, and it is only since retiring three years ago that I have had the time to read philosophy properly, rather than absorbing the methodological common sense the job required secondhand, at conferences and in journal clubs. This is how I have finally come to sit down with Gould and Lewontin's actual 1979 argument about spandrels and the "Panglossian paradigm," rather than the potted version of it I had carried around, unexamined, for decades. The immediate occasion is that my closest friend in the department and collaborator of thirty years, Warren, is retiring next month, and I have been asked to give the toast at his dinner. Warren has never in his life looked at a trait without finding an ingenious adaptive story for it -- cheerfully, and, in his defense, rigorously -- and somewhere around year fifteen of our friendship a postdoc who had just read Gould's essays began calling him "Dr. Pangloss" at lab meetings. The name stuck, affectionately, and rather pleased him, which strikes me as a fine irony given that a great deal of Gould's own scientific life was spent on shell variation in Bahamian land snails, marshalled expressly against exactly the kind of adaptive storytelling that is Warren's most natural mode of thought.

The dinner will be roughly half current and former colleagues, who will know precisely what that nickname is needling him about and will expect the toast to do something clever with it, and half spouses, children, and old friends from outside biology, to whom it will just sound like an odd, funny title. I would like a toast of about five minutes, built around one good joke involving the Pangloss name and what it is actually a joke about -- but I do not want that joke to concede, even by implication, that Warren's own habits of mind were the sloppy, unfalsifiable kind of storytelling Gould and Lewontin were objecting to, because they were not: he tested his adaptive hypotheses as carefully as anyone I have ever worked with, and was invariably the first person in any seminar to reach for drift or developmental constraint when the data called for it. Could you write the toast so that it earns its joke for the biologists in the room without ever tipping into a lecture on the adaptationist programme, and so that it still lands, warmly, for the people who have never heard of the dome of San Marco?
