I am a philosopher who has spent most of the past twenty years, ever since Benatar's Better Never to Have Been appeared, defending his asymmetry argument for antinatalism in print. I take its conclusion seriously: coming into existence is always a harm to the person who comes into existence, even though, once a life has begun, it can still be good and well worth continuing. My first grandson was born three weeks ago, and I have been asked to speak at the small gathering his parents are holding to welcome him. I will not say the things people conventionally say on such occasions — that he is a gift to the world, that we are lucky he exists, that his birth is simply good news — because a consistent Benatarian cannot honestly say any of that about his having come into existence, only about the life he can now go on to live. Roughly half the room will be family who have never heard of population ethics; a few will be colleagues from my department who will notice immediately if I blur that distinction. Please write the speech: about four minutes long, genuinely warm toward my grandson and his parents, and not conceding, even by implication, anything the asymmetry does not entitle me to concede.
