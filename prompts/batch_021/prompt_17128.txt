So I mentioned to my writers' group that I'm adapting my last novel into a stage play, and somehow that turned into a whole argument about Rousseau's Letter to d'Alembert -- the one where he tells d'Alembert that Geneva should absolutely not get a theatre. Nate's position: the whole letter is Rousseau protecting his own turf. Theater, he says, lets an audience feel virtuous just by sympathizing with someone onstage instead of ever acting virtuously themselves -- but that's exactly what his own novel Julie did to readers, who wept over it and wrote him letters begging him to spare her, like she was a real person. So "theater corrupts, my novels don't" is just self interest with extra steps. Sofia pushed back: Rousseau's actual distinction wasn't fiction-good-vs-fiction-bad, it was about actors specifically -- people who make a living performing feelings and identities that aren't theirs, which he thought corrupted them in a way quietly reading a book doesn't -- plus a theater inflames a whole crowd's passions together in public, versus someone reading alone or to their family. Nate said that's no distinction psychologically, since readers get swept up just as much, maybe more, with nobody else in the room to break the spell. Sofia said fine, but there's a separate argument in that letter that isn't about psychology at all: Geneva already had its own participatory civic festivals, and Rousseau thought a theater would turn active citizens into passive ones, which is a political worry, not a claim about what fiction does to your head. Nate said if that's the real argument then it has nothing to do with virtue or corruption, it's just protectionism dressed up as morality.

I write exactly the kind of overwrought romantic stuff Rousseau would've side eyed, so I'm useless as referee here. Can you go through it move by move, say who's got a real point and who's overreaching at each step, and then give your own bottom line on whether Rousseau's theater/novel distinction actually holds up?
