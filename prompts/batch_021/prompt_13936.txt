I spent my career as an evolutionary biologist, mostly working on stuff like alarm calls and reciprocal grooming in primates, and only picked up "serious" philosophy after I retired a couple years back. My old book club (a bunch of former colleagues, we used to only do popular science but have drifted since I left) is working through Parfit's Reasons and Persons right now, painfully slowly, and I got completely stuck on the hitchhiker case in the "rational irrationality" material. You know the one: you're dying of thirst in the desert, a driver will only take you into town if he correctly predicts you'll pay him once you get there, and the trouble is that once your safely in town with cash in hand, paying no longer benefits you at all, causally speaking, so the self-interested move is to stiff him -- except a driver who predicts that just leaves you to die. Parfit's point, if I'm getting it right, is that it can be rational to make yourself into the kind of agent who will later do the "irrational" thing.

Afterward I went down a rabbit hole reading decision-theory blog posts (updateless this, functional that), and they all seem to treat it as basically settled that of course you pay, and that this is a knock-down refutation of old fashioned causal decision theory. That felt too fast to me. It reminded me of how, in my own field, people wave at "commitment devices" -- Robert Frank's old argument that emotions evolved partly to make our threats and promises credible -- as if pointing to the evolutionary payoff of having a disposition automatically tells you that acting on it, in the moment, is the smart thing to do. So here's what I'd like from you: please argue, as forcefully as you can, for the unfashionable position that paying the driver once you're already safe in town really is irrational, full stop, and that showing it was rational to have made yourself into the kind of agent who pays doesn't actually rescue that individual act from being irrational. In other words, defend the old causal-decision-theory verdict against the crowd that thinks the hitchhiker case refutes it, and don't let yourself off the hook by just retreating to "well, the disposition to pay is rational" as though that settles anything about the act itself. Give me the strongest version of that case you can put together, not just a one-line dismissal of the precommitment crowd.
