Question (20 points). A hospital compares two treatments for a condition whose cases are classified as either mild or severe. The raw recovery counts are:

Treatment A: 95 of 100 mild cases recovered; 270 of 900 severe cases recovered; 365 of 1000 cases overall recovered.
Treatment B: 810 of 900 mild cases recovered; 20 of 100 severe cases recovered; 830 of 1000 cases overall recovered.

(a) [4 pts] Compute the recovery rate for each treatment among mild cases, among severe cases, and overall, and state which treatment "wins" at each of the three levels.

(b) [6 pts] Explain, using the concept of a confounding variable, why the ranking of the two treatments reverses between the subgroup level and the aggregate level.

(c) [8 pts] The hospital's data office proposes the following rule: "Whenever aggregating the data produces a reversal like this one, you should always trust the severity-stratified numbers over the combined total, since combining just conceals the confound." Is this rule reliable in general, or does its reliability depend on further assumptions about how severity relates to treatment and outcome? Defend your answer.

(d) [2 pts] This phenomenon is generally known as Simpson's paradox. Briefly note who it is named after, and whether that person is usually credited with being the first to notice it.

Student's answer:

(a) Mild cases: A = 95/100 = 95%, B = 810/900 = 90%, so A wins. Severe cases: A = 270/900 = 30%, B = 20/100 = 20%, so A wins again. Overall: A = 365/1000 = 36.5%, B = 830/1000 = 83%, so B wins. A is therefore better in both subgroups but worse on the bottom line.

(b) This is Simpson's paradox. Severity is a confounding variable here because it affects both which treatment a patient is likely to receive and how likely they are to recover regardless of treatment. In this data, Treatment A was given to 900 severe patients and only 100 mild ones, while Treatment B went mostly to mild patients (900 of 1000). Since severe cases recover less often no matter what, A's total is dragged down by having mostly been used on the harder cases, and B's total is inflated by having mostly been used on the easier ones. Stratifying by severity removes this imbalance and reveals A's genuine advantage.

(c) The data office's rule is correct. Simpson's paradox is always the product of a lurking variable correlated with both the treatment and the outcome, and stratifying by that variable is precisely how you control for the confound. Once the data is properly stratified, as it is here, the subgroup figures are the ones reflecting the real causal relationship, and the combined total is simply the artifact that should be set aside. That will hold in any case where combining the data produces a reversal of this kind.

(d) It's named after the statistician Edward Simpson, who first identified and wrote up the phenomenon, in a 1951 paper on contingency tables, which is why it subsequently became known as "Simpson's paradox."
