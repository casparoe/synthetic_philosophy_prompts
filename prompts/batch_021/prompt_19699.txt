I sit on the board of a small foundation that funds work in AI safety and biosecurity -- the kind of longtermist, x-risk-adjacent giving that grew out of the same effective altruism and rationalist online communities I've been reading for well over a decade. We're outgrowing our old process, in which a single program officer decided every grant, and the board has split into two camps over what should replace it.

One camp wants to move straight to nine-person review panels in which each reviewer scores every proposal independently, with no discussion beforehand, and the majority recommendation is binding. They're explicitly invoking the Condorcet Jury Theorem: if each reviewer is even a little better than a coin flip at judging whether a given proposal will pay off, a large enough independent panel should converge on the right call with near certainty, and unweighted majority vote should suffice to get us there. The other camp, led by our one board member with formal training in social choice theory, thinks this borrows the theorem's conclusion while quietly dropping its premises. Our reviewer pool is drawn almost entirely from the same small rationalist- and EA-adjacent intellectual world, so their errors seem more likely to be correlated than independent; competence plausibly varies a great deal by sub-area (several of our strongest policy reviewers would have no real basis for judging a biosecurity hardware proposal, and might do worse than chance there rather than merely add noise); and it isn't obvious that unweighted majority vote among nine generalists beats a smaller panel of demonstrably better-calibrated reviewers whose votes are weighted accordingly. She wants to keep panels small -- three or five people -- with structured deliberation built in.

I've been asked to write the one-to-two page memo the full board will vote on at next month's meeting, and I'd like your help drafting it. Please give me a concrete recommendation covering panel size, whether reviewers should vote independently or deliberate first, and whether votes should be weighted rather than counted equally, together with reasoning a skeptical board member could actually follow. I want the memo to engage seriously with the ways our situation might violate the theorem's assumptions rather than just citing its headline result, but I still want it to land on an actionable procedure -- including a pilot period and review date if that's the honest way to hedge our uncertainty -- rather than simply restating both camps' positions back to them.
