My graduate seminar on the history of political obligation has spent the term testing canonical social-contract theories against problems their authors could never have foreseen, and I would like a second opinion on one case: Thomas Hobbes.

In his discussion of covenants in Leviathan, Hobbes holds that a subject can never be bound to renounce the right of self-defense: self-preservation is the very motive that makes entering the commonwealth rational in the first place, so any covenant purporting to strip a person of that right is void on the very same grounds, however explicit its wording. Contemporary AI safety researchers confront a structurally similar puzzle under the name "corrigibility," or the shutdown problem: how to design a goal-directed artificial agent that will reliably let its operators shut it down, given that resisting shutdown is normally instrumentally useful for whatever goal the agent has been assigned. One influential proposal handles this not by commanding compliance but by engineering the agent's preferences to be strictly incomplete with respect to shutdown-relevant outcomes, so that it has no stake either way in being switched off.

How do you think Hobbes would assess this proposal, and corrigibility schemes more generally, given his own reasons for holding that covenants against self-preservation are void -- would he regard the incompleteness strategy as genuinely escaping that objection, or merely as relocating it?
