I spent thirty-two years running an analytical chemistry lab before retiring, and in the years since I have finally had time to read philosophy properly, mostly through a reading group that meets under the local university's continuing-education program. This term we have been working through some of the more formal literature on how a group is supposed to turn its members' separate judgments into a single collective one, and it has made me look differently at a recurring headache on the university's standing Committee on Research Integrity, on which I still sit as an emeritus member.

Under our policy, which -- like most American research universities -- tracks the federal government's own definition of research misconduct, a finding requires two elements to be affirmed independently: that the conduct was a significant departure from accepted practice in the field, and that it was committed intentionally, knowingly, or recklessly rather than through honest error. Only if both are affirmed does a finding go forward. In the file now before us, concerning a former postdoc accused of reusing and relabeling gel images across two papers, our five members split as follows: two of us think both elements are clearly met and call it misconduct outright; one thinks the images were indeed altered in a misleading way but believes it was carelessness rather than anything intentional; one thinks the intent was bad but doubts the alteration itself rises to a "significant" departure; and one denies both elements. Tallied element by element, a majority affirms the departure and a separate majority affirms the intent, so applying our own rule mechanically commits us to a misconduct finding. Yet if you simply ask each of us for a personal, up-or-down verdict on misconduct itself, three of the five say no. A colleague who has served on the committee longer than I have tells me the same reversal occurred in a case two years ago, so this does not look like a quirk of this particular file. It reminds me a little of the way a correlation can flip sign depending on whether you pool subgroups or examine them separately, though the longer I sit with it the less sure I am that it is really the same kind of problem.

Could you draft the short memo I will circulate to the committee before Thursday's meeting? Its main task should be a recommendation, as a standing matter of procedure and not merely for this one file, on whether we ought to decide misconduct element by element and then apply the rule mechanically, poll each member's final verdict directly and let that govern instead, or adopt some defensible third option if one exists -- together with what should happen to the pending file under whichever rule is adopted. Whichever way you come down, I would like the losing side's best argument made properly rather than waved away, since at least two of my colleagues hold it in good faith. A sentence or two on whether the statistical comparison I drew above is apt or beside the point would also be useful context for the committee, though that question is secondary to the procedural recommendation itself.
