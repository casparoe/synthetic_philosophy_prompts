Over the past year I've been teaching myself the fundamentals of quantum computing for work, and that led me down a rabbit hole into Bell's theorem, which turned out to be far more interesting than the "spooky action at a distance" one-liner that first caught my attention. What actually clicked for me is the precise shape of the result: measurement outcomes on entangled particles can be correlated more strongly than any theory built on local hidden variables permits (the CHSH inequality's classical bound of 2, versus quantum mechanics' bound of 2√2, sometimes called Tsirelson's bound), and yet, because of the no-communication theorem, each observer's own measurement record looks like pure noise until it's physically brought together with the other one, at ordinary sub-light speed, for comparison. That combination — a correlation too strong to have been locally pre-arranged, yet carrying no message either side could ever extract alone — feels like it should be devastating, or at least profound, in a way I haven't been able to fully articulate, and I haven't come across a piece of fiction that sits with that exact tension instead of just treating entanglement as a magic radio.

Could you write me a short story that takes this seriously? Invent whatever setting works dramatically — I keep picturing two colony ships, or two branches of a family, drifting apart across a gap that no slower-than-light reunion could ever fully close, who carry a stock of entangled particle pairs from before the separation as something between a scientific instrument and a private ritual — but I'd like the plot to turn on a specific incident (an experiment, a rumor, a desperate attempt to beat the no-communication theorem) whose result forces at least two characters into a real disagreement about what the correlation means: that locality itself fails and influences do jump the gap (something like a Bohmian reading), that the measurement choices and particle states were never actually independent, having been fixed together by some common cause long before anyone measured anything (superdeterminism), that both outcomes occur and only look singular from inside each branch (many-worlds), or that the demand for a single underlying story is itself the mistake, and only the correlations are real. I don't need the story to settle which of these is correct — physics itself hasn't — but I would like it to stay honest about the empirical core (strong correlation, zero usable signal) and to make that honesty the emotional center of the piece rather than a technical footnote.
