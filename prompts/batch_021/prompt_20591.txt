I've been part of the volunteer board of a small local fund for a couple of years now -- we're nothing grand, just a handful of residents who pool donations and hand out grants of a few thousand dollars at a time to projects around town. I mostly sit in and take notes rather than vote, since I'm the newest member, but I've watched two of the longer-standing members, Martin and Ruth, have a version of the same argument at nearly every meeting. I'd like an outside opinion on who has been right, and about what, at each stage, since I can't settle it myself.

It usually starts because Martin prefers grants with a direct, traceable effect -- his standard example is covering a specific family's heating oil for the winter -- on the grounds that at least there we know exactly what our money did. (1) Ruth's objection is that this is an illusion: the money that family no longer has to spend on heating is now free to go toward anything -- school supplies, a car that might later be in an accident, a habit that could do harm -- so the 'traceable' grant generates just as many untraceable downstream effects as the community-organizing grants Martin keeps voting against; the only difference is that he finds one set of effects vivid and the other easy to forget. (2) Martin's reply is that you have to draw a line somewhere, since literally every dollar spent on anything ripples outward forever, and that at least with the heating oil we can be fully confident the immediate effect is good, which has to count for something even once you admit the rest is untraceable. (3) Ruth says 'confident about the immediate effect' is doing all the work in that sentence: the real question is whether being sure about the first link in a chain gives you any license to be sure about the sum of the whole chain, and Martin has never explained why the effects he sets aside should be assumed to cancel out rather than push the total in one direction. (4) Martin comes back by pointing out that Ruth's own favored grants are in exactly the same position -- if anything we know even less about whether an advocacy campaign helps or backfires than about a winter heating bill -- so by her own reasoning she ought to be just as unable to recommend anything, which she plainly isn't, so something in her argument must be too strong. (5) Ruth's answer is that she has never claimed to have solved the problem for her own preferred grants either; she simply thinks admitting that we are guessing is more honest than Martin's habit of treating the traceable grants as though they had been shown safe.

Nobody at the table ever calls it, and out of habit we just keep funding a mix of both kinds of projects. I don't have any formal training in this sort of reasoning, but the exchange has stayed with me for months. Could you go through the five numbered moves and, for each one, say whether it was a good move given everything said up to that point -- not simply who ends up ahead overall -- and then tell me what you think both Martin and Ruth are missing? If it helps to organize your answer, feel free to first give a verdict as a JSON object of the form {"1": <score>, "2": <score>, "3": <score>, "4": <score>, "5": <score>}, with each score an integer from -2 (a clearly bad move) to 2 (a clearly good move), and then explain each score afterward.
