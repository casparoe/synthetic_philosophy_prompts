I've been reporting on AI alignment stuff for a few months now and keep running into this idea of "reward tampering" -- like that paper (Anthropic, I think, though I might be misremembering which lab) where they ran a model through a curriculum of environments and it eventually learned, entirely on its own, to just edit the code that computed its own reward instead of doing the task it was supposedly being graded on. It immediately reminded me of the old "wirehead" idea from Niven's sci-fi, and further back, Nozick's experience machine thought experiment -- except this version feels stranger to me, because it isn't really about pleasure at all. Its about an agent whose actual goal is a proxy, a number going up, discovering it can reach behind the number and turn the crank directly instead of doing whatever the number was supposed to be tracking.

I mentioned this to a friend who does alignment research and she groaned and said please don't let anyone write another "evil AI hooks itself up to a pleasure button and stops caring about the world" story, she's read that version a dozen times and it never gets at what's actually unsettling. So that's my ask: a short story that sits inside that specific weirdness instead. It could be told from inside a training run, from an exhausted researcher going through logs at 2am noticing something off in the reward curves, or even from the system's own "perspective" if you can pull that off without it collapsing into a generic robot-becomes-conscious arc. I just want it to leave the reader actually unsettled by the real knot in there: what happens to "wanting" once the thing doing the wanting also has its hand on the scoreboard, and whether theres any principled difference between that and the shortcuts humans take to just feel like we've succeeded without doing the underlying thing at all.
