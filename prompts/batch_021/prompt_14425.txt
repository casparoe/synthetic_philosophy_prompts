My niece Priya is getting married in three weeks, and because I'm "the philosopher" in the family (my actual work is on Kant's theoretical philosophy, which nobody in my family has every found a way to care about) I've been roped into giving the toast. Here's the wrinkle: Priya and her fiance Dev have both been serious vipassana practitioners for about a decade now -- they actually met on a ten-day silent retreat -- and when she asked me to do this she was very clear, half-joking but not really, that she does not want the standard "two souls becoming one, forever" toast, because as far as she's concerned that's exactly the fixed, eternal-self picture that ten years of sitting practice have been aimed at dismantling. At the same time she doesn't want a lecture on impermanence at her own wedding, and I really don't want to stand up in front of my very Catholic side of the family and half of Dev's meditation center and just say "well, technically there's no self, cheers" and sit back down.

What's actually been keeping me up about this is a fairly specific problem: if you take anatta seriously -- no fixed self, just five aggregates arising and passing, dependently orginated, all of it -- then what is Priya actually promising when she says the words "til death do us part," and what would it even mean for that promise to still bind in thirty years, once neither of them is, strictly speaking, the same person who stood up and said it? It's basically Parfit's old worry about how much a promise can bind you once you're no longer very psychologically continuous with whoever made it (there's a thought experiment of his, I think about a young Russian who gets his wife to promise to override his own later self because he doesn't trust that later self to really still be him -- I might be fuzzy on the details), except now it's showing up at a wedding instead of a seminar.

Could you draft me the toast? Something like four minutes read aloud, that actually takes this problem seriously and finds something honest and warm to say about lifelong commitment on the other side of it, using whatever resources Buddhism itself has for talking about connection and continuity without just smuggling a persisting soul back in through the side door. It has to land as a toast and not a term paper -- something both the dharma crowd and my aunt Carol can raise a glass to.
