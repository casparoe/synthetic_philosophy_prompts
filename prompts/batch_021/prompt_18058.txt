During a graduate seminar on the philosophy of perception this term, we spent two sessions on the disagreement over whether perceptual experience itself can be shaped by an observer's beliefs, desires, or goals (so-called cognitive penetration), or whether early vision is informationally encapsulated from such states, with any apparent influence occurring only downstream, in judgment, memory, or attention. What struck me is that both camps keep citing the very same body of evidence and simply redescribing it. Take the Proffitt lab's well-known finding that hills look reliably steeper to people who are tired, elderly, unfit, or wearing a heavy backpack, together with the related Balcetis and Dunning result that a desired object, such as a water bottle to someone who is thirsty, is judged to be physically closer than an equally distant but undesired object. Defenders of cognitive penetrability treat findings like these as showing that motivational and evaluative states reach into early visual processing and change the phenomenal character of what is seen. Critics working in the tradition of Fodor's and Pylyshyn's modularity arguments, echoed more recently in Firestone and Scholl's wholesale methodological critique of this literature, insist the same data are better explained by response bias, demand characteristics, or a genuinely cognitive but strictly post-perceptual effect on judgment, leaving perception itself untouched.

My advisor thinks the dispute can't be settled by more experiments alone, since the two sides seem to disagree about what would even count as deciding it, and I'd like to see that disagreement played out explicitly rather than just gestured at. Could you write a dialogue of roughly 700-900 words between two equally capable philosophers who disagree in just this way, using the slant-perception and wishful-seeing findings as their shared test case? One should argue, in the spirit of Siegel's or Macpherson's work on penetration, that these cases are best understood as changes in perceptual content itself; the other should argue, in the spirit of Pylyshyn, Fodor, or Firestone and Scholl, that they are not. I'd like each character to engage with the other's strongest move rather than restate their own position past one another, especially on the question of what would even count as evidence that a change is perceptual rather than merely post-perceptual, and whether that line can be drawn in a principled way at all. Please don't let either character simply concede the point by the end, since as far as I can tell the dispute is still genuinely unresolved in the literature.
