I've spent a long time reading around the rationalist blogosphere, and one argument I keep circling back to without ever fully resolving is Robin Hanson's old case for what he called "pre-rationality" -- I believe the paper is titled something like "Uncommon Priors Require Origin Disputes." The claim, as I understand it, is stronger than Aumann's 1976 agreement result, which only shows that two Bayesians who already share a prior can't knowingly agree to disagree about a posterior; Hanson wants the further, more demanding thesis that ideally rational agents are required to start from the same prior in the first place, absent some independently defensible story about why one agent's belief-forming "origin" deserves more trust than another's. The intuition pump runs through a kind of veil of self-locating ignorance: before you knew which of several equally well-specified observers you were going to turn out to be, you would want to commit in advance to updating the same way any of them would, so treating your own priors as privileged simply because they happen to be yours looks like an unprincipled indexical bias rather than a mark of rationality.

A friend of mine who works in decision theory thinks this whole line of argument is a trick. On her view, priors are exactly the sort of primitive, freely chosen inputs to Bayesian updating that orthodox subjectivism, in the tradition of Savage and de Finetti, says cannot themselves be assessed as more or less rational -- only the updating can be graded against the axioms, so "you and I should have started from the same place" isn't a rationality constraint at all, just a preference dressed up as one. On this view, persistent, sincere disagreement between equally smart, equally honest people is not, by itself, evidence that anyone has made a mistake.

I'd like you to argue the other side, seriously and to a real conclusion, rather than surveying the considerations and leaving me to adjudicate. Make the strongest available case that ideally rational agents are required to share a common prior, engaging directly with the subjectivist objection above instead of talking past it, and say something concrete about what would, and would not, count as a legitimate "origin dispute" -- a non-question-begging reason two people could give for why their starting points were allowed to differ. I'm especially interested in whether your answer leaves any room at all for the kind of everyday disagreement among careful, well-informed people that the rationalist community usually treats as at least somewhat suspicious.
