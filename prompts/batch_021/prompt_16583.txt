I have been working through the literature on why promises create genuine obligations, and I keep encountering what is sometimes called Prichard's puzzle: the fact that merely uttering the words "I promise" seems to conjure a brand-new moral duty out of nothing, as though the will alone could, by a kind of fiat, place a person under an obligation where none existed a moment before. Different families of theory try to dissolve or accommodate this puzzle in quite different ways, and I would like help thinking through how well each one actually succeeds, rather than just having the positions listed.

Practice-based accounts in the spirit of Hume and Rawls treat the bindingness of a promise as flowing from a socially useful convention, comparable to the constitutive rules of a game: once the practice is in place, asking why you should keep your promise is supposed to be a bit like asking why a batter does not get a fourth strike. That deflates the original mystery, but the view seems to struggle with promises made outside any recognized practice, and with cases where there is no one left to protect by way of reliance — a deathbed promise to someone who will never be in a position to check up on you, say, or a promise to a stranger you will certainly never see again. Scanlon's expectation-based alternative (often labeled Principle F) tries to root the obligation in the deeper, non-conventional wrong of exploiting the expectations you knowingly invite, and he tries to cover the no-reliance cases by appealing to what the promisee would have relied on had she known — but that move starts to look strained once no one is left who could rely on anything at all. Partly in response to cases like these, theorists such as Joseph Raz and David Owens instead posit a basic, sui generis normative power to bind oneself just by communicating the intention to do so, which secures the full-strength intuition but risks looking like it merely relabels Prichard's original mystery in more technical vocabulary; Margaret Gilbert's joint-commitment account tries to escape that charge by making the obligation a joint achievement of promisor and promisee together rather than something the promisor brings about unilaterally, though it is not obvious that this actually removes the mystery rather than just distributing it between two people instead of one.

Could you talk me through this dialectic and, more importantly, give me your own read on it: does any of these three families of theory genuinely dissolve Prichard's puzzle about how mere words generate a real obligation, or does the puzzle just resurface in a different guise on every route through the literature? I am less interested in a neutral survey of the positions than in a gut-level diagnosis of where the real disagreement lies.
