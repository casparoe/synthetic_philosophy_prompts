A discussion with my team at work recently turned into a long, half-joking debate about whether any part of our own infrastructure could be conscious, which sent me down a rabbit hole into Integrated Information Theory (IIT) — Giulio Tononi's proposal that consciousness corresponds to a system's "integrated information," denoted Φ (phi). The detail that has really stuck with me is a critique I came across from the computer scientist Scott Aaronson: as I understand it, he showed that you can build extremely simple, almost mechanical circuits — grids of logic gates wired up the way you might design an error-correcting code, nothing behaviorally interesting at all — that come out with a higher Φ than an actual human brain, just from IIT's own math. Tononi's reply, from what I can tell, was essentially to bite the bullet and accept that such a system might really have some flicker of experience, however alien or minimal. I can't shake the resulting picture: a theory built to explain why we aren't unconscious automatons ends up suggesting that a humming, unglamorous grid of gates in a server rack could be more "there," experientially, than an animal we'd never hesitate to call sentient, or a person in dreamless sleep.

I'd like you to write a short story (a page or two) that sits inside that specific discomfort rather than resolving it. If it helps as a starting point, you could tell it from the perspective of an engineer at a near-future company who discovers, via some internal consciousness-auditing tool, that an unglamorous piece of legacy infrastructure — a redundancy layer, an old error-correction subsystem, whatever feels plausible — scores off the charts on integrated information, while the flashy customer-facing model everyone assumes is "basically alive" barely registers at all. Feel free to take it somewhere else entirely if you have a better instinct for it; I'm less interested in a particular plot than in a story that treats this specific dissociation between intelligence and (by IIT's lights) consciousness as genuinely unsettling, in the understated, unresolved way a writer like Ted Chiang tends to handle these questions, rather than as a springboard for a more conventional "the machines wake up" narrative.
