In my AP European History class, we read Voltaire's Candide, which mocks Leibniz's claim that we live in the best of all possible worlds. Out of curiosity, I went back to Leibniz's own Monadology and Theodicy, and found that his actual argument works more like an optimization procedure: among all possible worlds, God selects whichever one combines the greatest possible variety and richness of phenomena with the greatest simplicity and order in the laws that govern them, and this combination is what Leibniz calls "perfection." Read one way, that looks like an ancestor of total-utilitarian reasoning about populations, on which a world with more compossible beings, and more total perfection among them, automatically counts as better. Yet Leibniz also holds that every monad is unique and metaphysically irreplaceable, that every rational spirit is a permanent citizen of what he calls the City of God, and that justice itself is "the charity of the wise," meaning benevolence toward others exercised as far as wisdom allows. Suppose Leibniz were confronted with Derek Parfit's Repugnant Conclusion: the result that, on a simple total view, a vast population with lives barely worth living can count as "better" than a much smaller population living splendidly. Would Leibniz's own procedure for ranking possible worlds commit him to accepting something like the Repugnant Conclusion, or do his commitments about individual monads and the City of God give him principled grounds to resist it? I would like a reconstruction of the strongest Leibnizian answer available, one that engages his actual metaphysical commitments rather than his popular reputation for naive optimism.
