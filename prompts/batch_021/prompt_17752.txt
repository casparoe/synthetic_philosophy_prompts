I am currently teaching a graduate seminar on contractarian ethics, and this week we have reached the bargaining chapter of David Gauthier's Morals by Agreement, where he argues that fully rational, mutually self-interested bargainers would settle on the principle of minimax relative concession. I have been presenting this alongside its formal counterpart in cooperative game theory, the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution, noting that Gauthier's principle turns out to pick out exactly the point that solution designates. I explained to my students that, unlike the Nash bargaining solution, the appeal of both lies in equalizing how much each bargainer actually gains from cooperating relative to the most he or she could conceivably have gained.

One of the students, who came to philosophy after a first degree in economics, raised an objection that has left me second-guessing how I presented this. Her worry was that "equalizing gains between two bargainers" is only a coherent instruction if we can measure those gains on some shared scale, whereas the whole point of casting the bargaining problem in von Neumann–Morgenstern terms, as Gauthier explicitly does, is that each party's utility function is unique only up to its own independent positive affine transformation, with no meaningful comparison across parties. On her reading, this means that either minimax relative concession secretly depends on an interpersonal comparison of utility that Gauthier never entitles himself to, which would make it no more principled than simply stipulating an equal split of utility gains, or else I was simply wrong to describe the solution as equalizing anything between the two parties at all.

I would like your help settling this before next week's session. Could you explain precisely what quantity the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution, and hence Gauthier's minimax relative concession, equalizes between the bargainers, whether that quantity remains invariant when each party's utility function is rescaled independently of the other's, and how the answer bears on my student's dilemma? It would also help to know how this compares to Kalai's own later egalitarian bargaining solution, which is likewise sometimes glossed as an "equal gains" solution but which, if I recall correctly, was explicitly framed around the question of interpersonal comparability in a way the earlier solution was not.
