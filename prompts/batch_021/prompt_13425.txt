After forty years as an experimental physicist, I have taken up Benjamin Franklin's autobiography in retirement, and what strikes me most is how closely his celebrated project of moral perfection resembles a piece of disciplined experimental design. He did not attempt all thirteen virtues at once: he gave each a full week of concentrated attention in turn, working through the whole list four times a year, and recorded every lapse as a black mark in a ruled notebook grid. Even the list itself was revised mid-experiment -- it stopped at twelve virtues until a Quaker friend told him plainly that he was widely thought proud, prompting him to add Humility, though he wryly admitted that pride in one's own humility would just be pride under a new name, and that he never did get a firm handle on that particular fault.

Suppose Franklin were transported to the present and shown our current apparatus of self-improvement: smartwatches and phone sensors that log habits automatically, dashboards that let a person monitor a dozen virtues or goals simultaneously, streak-based gamification, and AI chatbots standing in for a candid friend. Would he welcome these as a natural extension of his scheme, or would he judge that something essential to it -- the deliberate, one-fault-at-a-time discipline, or the outside perspective of a friend willing to voice an unflattering truth -- is precisely what gets lost when tracking and feedback are handed over to a machine? Defend your answer by engaging with the specific mechanics of Franklin's method, not merely his general reputation for self-improvement.
