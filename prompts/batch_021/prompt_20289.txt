A friend and I spent most of Sunday evening arguing about the centipede game and never quite reached an agreement. She maintained that backward induction is simply what rationality requires: at the very last node the player to move should obviously take the larger pile rather than pass, and once that is fixed, the player at the second-to-last node should take rather than pass as well, and so on back to the beginning, so that a rational player facing a rational opponent ought to stop the game on the very first move. I pushed back with what I understood to be the standard worry about this reasoning, sometimes called the backward induction paradox: the argument for stopping at, say, move fifty implicitly assumes that common knowledge (or common belief) of rationality is still intact at move fifty, yet the mere fact that the game got that far is already evidence that this assumption broke down earlier, since fully rational players, by the argument's own logic, would have stopped at move one. If the epistemic condition that licenses backward induction reasoning is undermined by the very history that puts a player at the node where they are supposed to apply it, it's unclear why they should trust what backward induction tells them to do from there. My friend thought this was a technical curiosity that dissolves once the game is modeled carefully enough, and kept invoking Aumann's result that common knowledge of rationality implies the backward induction outcome; I thought it pointed to something more serious about whether backward induction deserves its status as the canonical solution for games like this one.

I'd like your help settling this, in essay rather than dialogue form. Reconstruct the strongest version of each side: the case that common knowledge or common belief of rationality really does pin down backward induction as the uniquely rational way to play the centipede game, and the case -- associated with critics such as Pettit and Sugden, Bicchieri, and Reny, and with Binmore's exchange with Aumann -- that the epistemic conditions backward induction needs are self-undermining, vacuous at off-path nodes, or otherwise too fragile to bear the normative weight usually placed on them. Then take a side rather than surveying and shrugging: tell me whether backward induction is the correct prescription for an ideally rational agent in the centipede game, whether it is correct only under some restricted or non-obvious epistemic assumptions that are worth spelling out, or whether the paradox shows we should give up on backward induction as the right solution concept for extensive-form games of this kind.
