A friend and I are working through the Guide of the Perplexed together, and last week's session on the reasons for the commandments turned into an argument that neither of us managed to resolve. We had just read Maimonides' account of animal sacrifice: his claim that God instituted the sacrificial cult not because it was itself a desirable form of worship, but as a concession to the fact that the Israelites, freshly out of Egypt, knew no other way of serving a deity and could not have been weaned off sacrificial worship all at once, any more than a person habituated to one way of life can be made to abandon it overnight. My friend's conclusion was that this "accommodation" story effectively unmasks sacrifice, in Maimonides' own eyes, as a lesser and merely provisional mode of worship -- a historical concession that any sufficiently mature or philosophically self-aware Judaism should have been moving away from, if not abolishing outright. He thinks this sits very uneasily with the fact that Maimonides elsewhere codifies the sacrificial laws in full legal detail and even describes their restoration once the Temple is rebuilt in messianic times, and he takes that tension as evidence of a real inconsistency in Maimonides' thought -- or, more provocatively, as evidence that Maimonides was privately more of a reformer than his legal writings ever admit.

I was not convinced, since it seemed to me that treating a commandment's historical or psychological rationale as evidence of its provisional or diminished status assumes something about how reasons and permanence relate within Maimonides' account of divine law -- something I could not actually defend on the spot. Is there a specific argument internal to Maimonides' jurisprudence, perhaps about why a law, once legislated, must be general and fixed rather than keyed to the circumstances of the particular generation that first received it, which blocks the inference my friend is drawing? Or does he have a real point, such that the tension between the philosophical explanation of sacrifice and its permanent legal standing is a genuine and unresolved strain in Maimonides' thought that later commentators also worried about? I would like to actually understand what is going on here, rather than simply insist that my friend is being too quick.
