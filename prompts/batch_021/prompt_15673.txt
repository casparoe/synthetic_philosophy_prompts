So I've been on a bit of a physics kick lately, mostly because a coworker brought up reversible computing and Landauer's principle in a totally unrelated standup, and I fell down a rabbit hole afterward reading about entropy and the "arrow of time." Somewhere in there I kept running into this passage, which is apparently literally where the phrase comes from — it's Eddington, from The Nature of the Physical World (1928):

"Let us draw an arrow arbitrarily. If as we follow the arrow we find more and more of the random element in the state of the world, then the arrow is pointing towards the future; if the random element decreases the arrow points towards the past. That is the only distinction known to physics. This follows at once if our fundamental contention is admitted that the introduction of randomness is the only thing which cannot be undone. I shall use the phrase 'time's arrow' to express this one-way property of time which has no analogue in space."

I've read this like ten times now and I keep going back and forth on whether it's actually saying something substantive or just quietly defining "future" as "whichever direction has more randomness in it" and then acting like that's a discovery — if "the future" just means "the high-entropy direction," then sure, entropy increases toward the future, but that feels like its true by construction rather than by physics. The other thing bugging me, which I think might secretly be the same worry wearing a different hat, is: if the microscopic laws don't prefer a direction in time (apparently true for both classical mechanics and QM, or close enough), why would randomness only pile up in one direction from wherever "now" is, instead of increasing symmetrically into the past and the future both? That seems like the more naive prediction if the underlying dynamics really don't care about time's direction, and I have a vague memory of this being an actual historical objection someone threw at Boltzmann back in the day, not just something I'm making up on the spot.

So could you help me pull apart exactly what Eddington's passage is and isn't entitled to claim here, and whether my two worries above are really the same worry or genuinely seperate ones? And is there a standard reply to the second worry — something about a "past hypothesis" or a special initial condition, I think I've seen that phrase somewhere — and if so, is that considered by people who work on this stuff to actually resolve the puzzle, or is it more of a patch that just relocates the mystery to "why was entropy so low to begin with"?
