I supervise a couple of graduate students who are active in our department's effective altruism reading group, and at their last meeting they worked through Caspar Oesterheld's writings on what he originally called "multiverse-wide superrationality" and what has since become better known as evidential cooperation in large worlds (ECL): the idea that if the universe, or multiverse, is large enough to contain many agents who reason similarly to us but hold quite different values, we might do better in expectation by acting as though bound by a compromise among those values, simply because our own choice to cooperate is evidence that relevantly similar agents elsewhere are cooperating too, even though no causal contact between us is possible. One of the students then found her way to "The Evidentialist's Wager," the paper MacAskill, Vallinder, Oesterheld, Shulman, and Treutlein wrote for the Journal of Philosophy, and came away convinced that even someone who puts most of their credence on causal decision theory should still act on evidential decision theory's recommendation once enough correlated agents are at stake. Both students have since asked me, more or less in earnest, whether all this means they should redirect some effort away from the cause they actually care about most, wild animal welfare, toward whatever a hypothetical civilization-spanning compromise would recommend instead, and I would like to give them something more considered than the mixture of enthusiasm and stalling I managed on the spot.

Could you describe the most important implications of evidential cooperation in large worlds for how someone already committed to effective altruism should prioritize between causes, and for how they should weigh direct pursuit of their own values against compromise with the values of agents they could never causally interact with? I would especially like you to address how much the conclusion depends on accepting a non-causal decision theory, or on wager-style arguments for acting on evidential decision theory even under decision-theoretic uncertainty; how much weight it can bear given that the relevant "large world" assumption is an empirical claim about cosmology that we are in no position to test; and whether anything determinate can actually be said about what such a compromise would recommend in practice, or whether the whole line of reasoning, however valid in principle, is too indeterminate to change anyone's real priorities.
