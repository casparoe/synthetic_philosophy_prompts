A friend and I got into a whole argument last night that started as a joke about my dead succulent and somehow turned into two hours on causation. Backstory: she was away for a week and asked me to water her plant, I forgot, it died. She's doing a metaphysics module and now insists my not-watering it literally caused it to die, same as if I'd gone and poured bleach on it, no difference in kind. I said that's loose talk — real causation needs some actual connection, a process, between cause and effect, and an absence isn't a process of anything, so at most my omission explains why the plant died, it doesn't cause it in the strict sense. Then I hit her with the fact that like a hundred other people (her mum, her neighbours, randoms on the other side of the world) also didn't water that plant last week, and if my not-watering counts as a cause then so does theirs, which is obviously insane — and all she had was "well, it was YOUR job," which felt like she was smuggling in a moral fact to do metaphysical work. Neither of us really won, and I went and read a bit about it afterward, which is where some of the vocabulary below is coming from, even though I still don't think I have the right concepts to settle it myself.

Could you write me a dialogue between two philosophers holding those two positions — one who thinks causation requires a genuine productive or physical connection, so that omissions are at best "quasi-causes" that merely explain rather than cause; the other who thinks a counterfactual or dependence account is basically fine as it stands, and omissions can be full causes just like any positive event? Get them arguing over a case just as small and dumb as mine (doesn't have to be a plant). I want the "too-many-causes" problem pressed hard on the counterfactual side, I want that side to actually do some work with something like duty, or normal/default conditions, rather then just asserting that my omission is obviously the salient one, and then I want a real rejoinder from the other side about whether that move is doing metaphysics or just quietly doing ethics dressed up as metaphysics. Neither one should be a pushover — they should actually respond to what the other just said, not just restate their opening position, and something like eight exchanges total feels about right. They don't need to be real named philosophers.
