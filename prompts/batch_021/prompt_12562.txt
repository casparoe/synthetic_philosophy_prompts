My aunt died about two weeks ago and I'm giving her eulogy this weekend, and there's one part of it I really want to get right. She spent about thirty years working as a grief counselor, but before that she did a few years of a philosophy PhD on Wittgenstein and never really got over it — she ran an informal Philosophical Investigations reading group out of her living room for something like two decades. Her big party trick, especially a couple glasses of wine in, was explaining why the private language argument (the diary case with "S" around section 258, and the beetle-in-the-box bit around 293) was secretly the reason her whole job worked: if "grief" or "pain" named some totally private inner object that only the sufferer could ever really inspect, the way people casually assume, then two people comparing notes on loss wouldn't just be doing it imperfectly, it would be nonsense, and nobody could ever teach anybody else anything about what they're feeling, or correct them, or actually help. She used to say the standard eulogy line, "no one will ever truly know what she went through," isnt a touching thing to say, its a confused thing to say — and she told my cousin once, half joking, that if anyone said that at her funeral she'd find a way to come back and argue about it.

Problem is, I'm not a philosopher, I just picked this up over like fifteen years of Thanksgiving arguments, and now I have to write something for a room that's maybe 20% her old reading-group friends, who will absolutely notice if I mangle the argument, and 80% people who've never heard of Wittgenstein and are just there to grieve their friend or sister or aunt. Could you draft me a eulogy, something that reads out to about 5 minutes, that actually threads this needle? It should honor what she thought the private language argument showed without turning into a seminar, and it needs to avoid that "no one will ever know her pain" line for the right reason instead of just swapping in the opposite silly idea, that everyone understood her perfectly or that grief isn't really that hard. It should still feel like a eulogy, not a handout.
