Over the past couple of months I've become interested in non-classical logic, despite having no formal training in philosophy -- I work as a backend engineer. It started during a discussion at work about how our knowledge graph should handle conflicting facts pulled in from different data sources. A colleague mentioned that some knowledge-representation systems use paraconsistent logics precisely so that a single contradictory fact doesn't force the whole system to derive arbitrary conclusions -- the way it technically should under classical logic's "principle of explosion," where one contradiction entails absolutely anything. Reading further on my own, I learned that adopting a paraconsistent logic doesn't, by itself, commit you to believing that any contradiction is actually true; you can adopt one purely as a safeguard against triviality. That's different from dialetheism, the view, associated primarily with the philosopher Graham Priest, that some contradictions -- for instance, certain self-referential versions of the Liar sentence -- aren't merely tolerated by a permissive proof system but are genuinely, literally true. I don't think I've absorbed every nuance here, but I believe I've grasped the basic shape of the distinction.

I mentioned this distinction to a couple of friends over dinner, and one of them, half-joking, said that if I really understood it, I ought to be able to turn it into a joke rather than just explaining it like a textbook. So: could you write me a short joke that turns specifically on the difference between dialetheism and paraconsistent logic -- that is, on the difference between believing that some contradictions are literally true and merely adopting a logical system that doesn't collapse into triviality when it encounters one? I want the humor to genuinely depend on that distinction, so that someone who understands both concepts recognizes it as clever and accurate, rather than it being a generic "logic nerd" pun that could apply to almost any topic involving contradictions or non-classical logic. A one- or two-line joke is perfectly fine -- I'm not looking for an elaborate scene.
