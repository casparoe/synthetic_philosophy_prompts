I'm assembling a small cycle of speculative short stories, each one built around a different pathology of rational choice -- the kind of thing that lives in game theory seminars rather than in most readers' mental furniture. I already have pieces anchored to Newcomb's problem and to the unexpected hanging paradox, and the one I'm currently stuck on is meant to dramatize what's sometimes called the commitment races problem in discussions of bargaining and advanced decision theory: the idea that when two parties can each make credible, irrevocable commitments -- the old Schelling image of the driver in a game of chicken who visibly tears out his own steering wheel and throws it out the window, so that the other driver has no choice but to swerve -- the incentive isn't to commit wisely but to commit first and hardest, before you've had time to think it through. Deliberation, on this view, is exactly the thing your side can't afford to finish, since every extra hour spent reasoning is an hour in which the other side might commit first and lock in the advantage. I find this genuinely more unsettling than the usual thought experiments about rational agents, and I want a story that makes a reader feel that vertigo rather than just have it explained to them.

Please write me a short story of around 1,500 to 2,000 words built around this dynamic. I'll leave the setting to you -- I've gone back and forth between something small and human (a custody dispute, a hostage standoff, two family firms fighting over the same contract) and something vast (rival star-faring civilizations, or automated negotiators bargaining on their creators' behalf), and I suspect either register could carry the idea, so choose whichever lets you dramatize the logic most precisely rather than defaulting to whichever is easiest to describe. The one thing I don't want lost is the actual shape of the problem: it should be the capacity for binding commitment itself, not mere stubbornness, misunderstanding, or malice, that drives events toward their outcome, and somewhere in the piece -- in a character's thought, in an exchange of dialogue, in the narration itself -- I'd like the story to register the paradox that thinking longer, becoming wiser, or waiting to be sure is a competitive liability once the other side knows you're capable of it. Don't feel obliged to resolve the dilemma; it doesn't have a clean solution in the literature it comes from, and I'd rather the ending sit honestly with that than manufacture false comfort.
