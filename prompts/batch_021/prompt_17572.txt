Ok this has been bugging me all week. My philosophy of AI seminar is reading Turing's original 1950 paper and theres this bit near the end where he goes through objections to "machines can think," and the last one is the argument from extrasensory perception (telepathy, clairvoyance, precognition, psychokinesis, the whole package). He literally says the statistical evidence for telepathy "is overwhelming" and worries it could let a human player cheat in the imitation game, so his fix is to stick everyone in a "telepathy-proof room." This turned our discussion section into a whole argument between two people in my cohort, Leo and Nadia, and now I've got a reaction paper on this exact bit due Friday so I actually need to figure out who had the better of it.

Leo's position: this isn't a cute historical footnote, it wrecks the argument. If Turing thinks a non-computational channel like telepathy is real enough that you need to physically shield against it, the imitation game was never cleanly testing "thinking," just text-indistinguishability-given-whatever-shielding-you-remembered-to-add, and "put them in a telepathy-proof room" just stipulates the problem away instead of showing it can't arise. He also pointed out this is the same paper where Turing confidently predicts machines with about 10^9 bits of storage will, within fifty years, play the game well enough that an interrogator won't have better than a 70% chance of correctly identifying them after five minutes of questioning -- and said if this is a guy calling flimsy card-guessing data "overwhelming," we should discount that prediction too.

Nadia's comeback: Rhine's experiments were taken seriously by plenty of mainstream scientists in the 1940s, so Turing was just reporting the consensus of his moment, not being a crank, and it's anachronistic to dock him for replication failures that came out decades later. She also said the telepathy-proof room is just good experimental control, same idea as blinding a trial, not a cop-out. Leo replied that "an effect exists at all" and "how large or robust is this effect" are different statistical questions, so being wrong about the first tells you nothing about his judgment on the second. Nadia didn't really have a clean comeback to that one but still thinks Leo's bigger conclusion -- that this undermines the imitation game itself -- is way too strong. Neither of them budged before section ended.

Who's actually right at each step here, and is there something bigger both of them are missing?
