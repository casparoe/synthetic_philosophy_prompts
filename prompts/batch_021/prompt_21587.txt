Since retiring from a career in vertebrate paleontology, I have taken up philosophy, and I now sit on my local natural history museum's collections committee. Our most popular exhibit, a mounted sauropod skeleton assembled in the 1930s from the bones of at least three individuals plus several sculpted replacement parts, has always been cataloged and labeled as a single specimen; our curator wants to keep it that way, citing standard practice for legacy mounts. A donor will fund its overdue restoration only on that condition, while two board members instead want the composite origin disclosed on the label, or the mount recataloged as an assemblage rather than a specimen. Reading van Inwagen lately, it struck me that we are really litigating a live instance of the special composition question -- under what conditions do several things compose a further thing -- and that neither unrestricted composition nor his own restriction to living organisms settles it, since on his view the bones compose nothing at all once the animal is dead, whether from one individual or several. Please draft the memo I will circulate before the board votes on the donor's offer: recommend a specific cataloging and labeling policy, give reasons the curator and the two board members would each have to take seriously, and include a candid statement of the strongest objection to whatever you recommend.
