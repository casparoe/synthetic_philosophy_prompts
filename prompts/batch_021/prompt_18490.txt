Here is the question as it appeared on the exam (20 points total):

"(a) [5 pts] State the four pramāṇas recognized by the Nyāya school and briefly define each.
(b) [5 pts] Using the standard case of fire inferred from smoke on a mountain, lay out the Nyāya five-membered syllogism (pañcāvayava), naming and illustrating each of the five members.
(c) [5 pts] The Prābhākara Mīmāṃsakas hold that a fifth pramāṇa, arthāpatti ("postulation"), is needed to account for cases like inferring that a man who is fat but does not eat during the day must eat at night. How do the Naiyāyikas respond to this proposal, and what does their response show about how Nyāya individuates the pramāṇas in the first place?
(d) [5 pts] Contrast the Nyāya account of how universals (jāti) figure in perception with the rival Buddhist epistemologists' account of the same question."

And here is the student's answer, verbatim:

"(a) Nyāya accepts four pramāṇas. Pratyakṣa is perception, direct sensory contact with an object. Anumāna is inference, knowledge derived from a reason (hetu) via its invariable concomitance (vyāpti) with what is to be proved. Upamāna is comparison, the pramāṇa by which we learn to apply a word to an object on the basis of a stated similarity, as when someone who has never seen a wild ox learns to recognize one from being told it resembles a cow. Śabda is verbal testimony, knowledge gained from the statement of a trustworthy speaker.

(b) The syllogism runs: (1) pratijñā, the thesis, 'the mountain has fire'; (2) hetu, the reason, 'because it has smoke'; (3) udāharaṇa, the example stating the general rule together with a supporting instance, 'whatever has smoke has fire, as in a kitchen'; (4) upanaya, the application, 'the mountain has smoke of that kind'; (5) nigamana, the conclusion, 'therefore the mountain has fire.'

(c) The Naiyāyikas deny that arthāpatti is a distinct pramāṇa, holding instead that it is a disguised case of upamāna. Just as upamāna lets us extend a word to a new object on the strength of a previously stated similarity — as when the forest-dweller, told only that a gavaya resembles a cow, becomes able to recognize a gavaya on his own — so too here: once we grasp, from other cases, that unexplained daytime fasting together with weight gain resembles the pattern found in known nocturnal eaters, we can apply that same recognition to Devadatta's case without needing any additional pramāṇa. Since the cognition can already be produced this way, positing arthāpatti as a further, distinct pramāṇa is explanatorily idle. This is characteristic of how Nyāya draws the boundaries of the pramāṇa list generally: a candidate is admitted only if there is some cognition that it — and no other recognized pramāṇa — can produce.

(d) For the Naiyāyikas, as for the Buddhist epistemologists such as Dignāga and Dharmakīrti, a universal like cowness is not an extra-mental reality but a construction of thought: what perception strictly delivers is only the unique particular (svalakṣaṇa), and the appearance of a feature shared by all cows is generated by the mind's exclusion of what is other (apoha) rather than read off the world as it is. Where the two schools part ways is on what perception itself involves: Nyāya distinguishes an initial, non-judgmental grasp of the bare object (nirvikalpaka) from a subsequent determinate perception (savikalpaka) in which the object is cognized as qualified by such features, whereas for the Buddhist epistemologists any awareness answering to a general term is, strictly speaking, never perception at all but conceptual construction (kalpanā)."
