I am working on a piece about how philosophers of physics think about the reality of spacetime, and I keep running into Einstein's "hole argument" -- an argument that worried him during the years he was developing general relativity, and that philosophers revived in the 1980s as a challenge to "substantivalism." I understand the basic mechanics: because the field equations of general relativity are invariant under diffeomorphisms, one can take any solution and construct a second, mathematically distinct solution that agrees with the first everywhere outside some bounded "hole" yet diverges inside it, so that the physical situation everywhere outside the hole seemingly leaves what happens inside it undetermined. What I have not been able to get an intuitive grip on is why this is supposed to be a distinctive problem specifically for substantivalists, who hold that spacetime points are real, mind-independent individuals, rather than a general worry about the theory itself, and how so-called "sophisticated substantivalism" claims to let someone keep believing spacetime points are real while denying that the two solutions describe genuinely different possibilities. Could you walk me through the intuition behind both the threat and that reply, in a way that makes clear why everything turns on the identity of spacetime points across models, rather than requiring me to follow the differential-geometry formalism directly?
