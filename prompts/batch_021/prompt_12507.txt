I serve on my university's student conduct board, and this week's docket includes a case that has split us more than anything we've handled in the two years I've been a member. During a heated argument in a dormitory common room, a second-year student shouted a series of humiliating insults at his roommate in front of several other residents, reducing the roommate to tears and prompting a request for an emergency room change. The student who did the shouting has a documented dissociative disorder, and a letter from his treating psychiatrist states that he was in a dissociative episode at the time — brought on, in her assessment, by the sudden death of a parent earlier that week — during which he had markedly diminished awareness of, and control over, what he was saying. Our conduct code lists documented psychiatric conditions as grounds for "mitigation" of a sanction but says nothing about whether mitigation can shade into withholding any finding of responsibility at all. Roughly a third of the board wants to apply our standard sanction for this kind of harassment regardless of the letter; a third wants to close the disciplinary matter entirely and route the student instead toward a support and accommodation plan; the rest sense that both positions are too tidy for the facts but can't quite say why.

Rereading Strawson's "Freedom and Resentment" over the weekend, it struck me that our disagreement tracks his distinction between excusing and exempting conditions — and that this case may be exactly the kind that resists sitting cleanly in either category. Read one way, the psychiatrist's letter offers an excuse: like "he didn't mean to" or "it was an accident," it denies that the outburst expressed the ordinary disregard that makes resentment apt, without saying anything general about the shouting student's standing as a responsible agent. Read another way, it is doing something stronger — treating the episode the way Strawson thinks we treat serious psychological abnormality, where the point isn't that this particular act failed to express ill will but that, for its duration, the student wasn't functioning as the kind of agent to whom reactive attitudes like resentment and indignation properly apply at all, and toward whom something more like the objective attitude is called for instead. If the board is persuaded of the second reading, does the resulting verdict have to be as global and forward-looking as that sounds, or can the board suspend judgment about this incident specifically while continuing to hold the student to ordinary expectations of accountability afterward — without thereby implying that the roommate's distress was somehow an inappropriate reaction?

Please draft the opinion I will present to the board at Thursday's hearing. It should recommend one concrete disposition of the case, including any conditions attached to it and a timeframe for review, and it should engage seriously with whichever side of the board's split — full sanction or full accommodation — your recommendation ends up rejecting, rather than treating Strawson's framework as something that mechanically settles the question.
