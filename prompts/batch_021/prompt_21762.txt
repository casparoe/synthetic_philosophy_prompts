The simple conditional analysis of dispositions holds, roughly, that an object is disposed to give response r to stimulus s just in case it would give r if it underwent s. C. B. Martin's finkish counterexamples showed that this biconditional fails whenever something would remove or install the disposition at the very moment the stimulus occurs. David Lewis's "reformed" conditional analysis, which requires that the disposition's intrinsic causal basis persist through the stimulus and combine with it to produce the manifestation, is widely credited with defusing finks. Mark Johnston and Alexander Bird, however, describe a further phenomenon -- masking (Johnston's term) or antidotes (Bird's term) -- in which the causal basis persists undisturbed and the stimulus occurs, yet something else intervenes downstream to block the manifestation. Write an essay assessing whether Lewis's reformed analysis, or some principled extension of it, can also accommodate masking cases, or whether masking instead shows that no conditional analysis of dispositions can succeed, so that dispositions should instead be treated as metaphysically primitive. Weigh the relevant considerations, but do not end on a non-committal note: defend a specific verdict.
