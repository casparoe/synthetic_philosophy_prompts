I'm working on a piece about why "technically true" statements feel so slippery to us -- the whole genre of corporate non-denial denials and political answers that are factually accurate but obviously built to make you believe something false. There's even an academic term for it now, "paltering." Everyone I talk to for this eventually brings up Augustine as the original hardliner who thought lying is always wrong, full stop, no exceptions even to save a life, so this week I actually went and read "On Lying" (De Mendacio) instead of just the two-line summary you get in every ethics textbook, figuring the primary text would have more in it then the Cliff Notes version. Turns out there's a second, later treatise, "Against Lying," which I didnt know until a few days ago and which is exactly the kind of thing that makes me feel like I'm always the last to find out.

Anyway, pretty early on (it's section 4 in the translation that's up on newadvent.org, which I think is the old 19th-century Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers one), right after he's defined lying as a matter of having a "double heart" -- saying one thing while your mind holds another, or as he puts it, a person "has one thing in his mind and utters another in words" -- he sets up this example that I've now read about six times and still can't fully untangle:

"So that it may be inquired, which rather lies: he who says a false thing that he may not deceive, or he who says a true thing that he may deceive? [...] The one, who knows or thinks he says a false thing, and says it on purpose that he may not deceive: as, if he knows a certain road to be beset by robbers, and fearing lest some person for whose safety he is anxious should go by that road, which person he knows does not trust him, should tell him that that road has no robbers, on purpose that he may not go by it, as he will think there are robbers there precisely because the other has told him there are none, and he is resolved not to believe him, accounting him a liar. The other, who knowing or thinking that to be true which he says, says it on purpose that he may deceive: for instance, if he tells a person who does not believe him, that there are robbers in that road where he really knows them to be, that he to whom he tells it may the rather go by that road and so fall among robbers, because he thinks that to be false, which the other told him. Which then of these lies? The one who has chosen to say a false thing that he may not deceive? Or the other who has chosen to say a true thing that he may deceive?"

So here's what I can't shake: by Augustine's own "double heart" definition, the FIRST guy (false statement about the robbers) is the one whose words don't match his belief, so technically he's the liar -- while the second guy, the one actively trying to get someone killed, said exactly what he believed to be true, which by that same definition should make him innocent of lying. That feels completely backwards. Does Augustine actually resolve which one "lies" in his own framework, or is he just letting the tension sit there as a hard case? And zooming out -- does this passage basically show that his own definition of lying (mismatch between inner belief and outer statement) and the thing we actually care about morally (intent to deceive or harm) can come apart, in a way that's genuinely awkward for a guy about to spend the rest of the treatise arguing that no lie, ever, is justified?
