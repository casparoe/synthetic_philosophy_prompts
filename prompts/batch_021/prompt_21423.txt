I've been rereading the opening books of Plato's Laws for a seminar on ancient moral psychology, and one passage keeps nagging at me. The Athenian Stranger surveys Greek practice and sides with Sparta and Crete, concluding that a well-governed city should ban wine-drinking among its citizens outright, since intoxication dissolves exactly the rational self-command that Plato's political philosophy prizes above everything else. This sits oddly with the emphasis Plato places elsewhere on habituating each part of the soul through graduated exposure: he has the Athenian insist, for instance, that courage can only be trained by deliberately exposing the young to controlled fear, never by shielding them from danger altogether. Does Plato ever confront this apparent inconsistency, or does he simply take it for granted that, unlike fear, pleasure is too dangerous ever to be safely trained rather than just suppressed?
