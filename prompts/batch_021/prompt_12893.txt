I'm a retired astrophysicist who's fallen down a Newton rabbit hole lately, and its messing with how I see him. Turns out he spent more energy on biblical prophecy than on the Principia, and for him the theology and the physics were never really separate projects. Here's the bit that's stuck with me: Newton argued the solar system's long-term stability wasn't just brute mechanics. In the Opticks he says the mutual tugging of planets and comets piles up small irregularities until, as he put it, the system basically "wants a reformation" -- he took that as evidence God has to keep stepping back in, not just wind the thing up once and walk away. Leibniz tore into this in his letters to Clarke (who was fighting Newton's corner), saying it made God look like a shoddy watchmaker who cant even build a watch that keeps its own time. A couple generations later Laplace shows, with perturbation theory, that the wobbles are self-correcting after all, no divine tune-ups required. Except that's not even the end of it: we now know, from Laskar's work in the 80s and 90s, that the solar system really is chaotic over long enough timescales, with something like a 1% shot that Mercury's orbit goes unstable before the sun even burns out. So Laplace wasn't the final word either. If you walked Newton through that whole 300-year saga, what do you reckon he'd actually conclude -- would he take the chaos result as some kind of vindication, or is that giving him way too much credit?
