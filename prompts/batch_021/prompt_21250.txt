I've been working through Rousseau's Letter to d'Alembert on the Theatre on my own, and I keep wondering how his objections to Geneva building a playhouse would translate to today's entertainment habits. As I understand it, his case rested on a few distinct claims: that playwrights can only flatter an audience's existing tastes rather than reform them; that watching virtue rewarded and vice punished on stage lets people discharge their moral feelings cheaply, feeling virtuous without having done anything virtuous; that acting itself, as a trade built on convincingly displaying feelings one doesn't have, corrodes the performer's sincerity; and that all of this is especially damaging in a small, relatively equal republic like Geneva, since it imports luxury and a hierarchy of paying spectators in place of the communal festivals he favored, where citizens were performers and audience for one another rather than divided into passive viewers and paid professionals.

Suppose Rousseau were shown how people now spend their evenings: bingeing shows on streaming services, scrolling algorithmically curated feeds, or posting short videos of themselves for strangers to watch and rate. Reasoning from his own arguments rather than a generic "screens corrupt us" reaction, how do you think he would judge this shift? I'm especially curious what he'd make of the interactive, self-broadcasting side of social media, since everyone acting as both performer and audience sounds superficially close to his festival ideal, yet seems very different in spirit. Could you also flag any point where his framework strains once pushed this far past anything he could have anticipated?
