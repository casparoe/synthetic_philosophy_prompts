I'm the president of my high school's Effective Altruism Club, and I need help drafting a memo ahead of the vote we're holding at next Thursday's meeting, where we decide how to disburse the roughly $2,900 we raised this year through a bake sale, a trivia night, and a matching pledge from a local tech company. For the past two years we've given everything to the Against Malaria Foundation, on the reasoning that its cost-effectiveness estimates are about as well-evidenced as anything in this space. This year one of our officers proposed redirecting a third of the funds to a pooled fund that grants to AI safety and biosecurity organizations, on the grounds that even a very small reduction in existential risk outweighs any near-term health gain once you multiply through the size of the future at stake. That proposal stalled at our last meeting when another member, who'd been reading Global Priorities Institute material, objected that we can't really run this comparison at all: distributing bednets and funding safety research both set off long causal chains — effects on population growth, local governance, who ends up shaping future AI development — that fan out further than anyone can track, so whatever "expected value" either side quotes is more confident than the evidence warrants. She called this "cluelessness," a term she traced to a paper by James Lenman and developed further by Hilary Greaves, who distinguishes cases where the evidence about some long-run effect points equally in both directions and so washes out from cases where we have real, asymmetric evidence on both sides that we have no principled way to weigh against each other. My first instinct was that this just counsels sticking with the well-evidenced near-term option, but she pointed out that saving lives via bednets changes population size too, which is exactly the sort of large, unpredictable long-run effect the objection is supposed to be worried about, so the "safe" choice doesn't obviously dodge it either.

Our by-laws require a two-thirds majority to change the existing allocation, or we default to last year's split, and our faculty advisor has said she'll sign off on whatever we decide as long as the process is fair, so this really is our call to make. Could you draft the short memo I'll circulate before Thursday's meeting? I'd like it to recommend a specific allocation — a straight split, a full redirection, or keeping things as they are — together with a timeline for transferring the funds, and to actually engage with the cluelessness worry rather than setting it aside: if you think it should change how much weight we put on expected-value comparisons here, say what should supplement or replace them and why; if you think there's a satisfying reply that lets us go on comparing the two options in those terms, give that reply rather than just assuming one is available.
