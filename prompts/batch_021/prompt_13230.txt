My advisor's retiring at the end of this semester after 33 years in the econ department, and because I'm supposedly "good with words" (her phrase, and I am now regretting every legible referee report I've ever written in front of her) I got roped into giving the toast at her retirement dinner in two weeks. Her whole research program, going back to like her second year of grad school, has been arguing that Berkeley -- yes, the esse est percipi, is-there-a-tree-in-the-forest Berkeley -- basically had it right about money in "The Querist," that strange 1735-37 book of his that's just several hundred numbered questions about Ireland's economy. She'll talk your ear off about how he broke the conceptual link between money and metal a good two centuries before "fiat currency" was a phrase anyone used, insisting the "true idea of money" is a ticket or counter for a more basic thing -- power to command other people's labor -- rather than wealth in itself, and about how his national bank proposal never even got a floor debate in the Irish parliment. Shes spent her whole career picking fights with the metallist "sound money" crowd over this (she owns a book literally titled "Exciting the Industry of Mankind" about Berkeley's monetary theory, which is a great way to find out which visiting speakers have actually read her stuff), and I've personally watched her get heated at a conference dinner over whether Locke or Berkeley had the better theory of money, which is a sentence I did not expect to type as a grad student.

The dinner's going to be maybe half current and former econ / history-of-thought people who'll definitly catch a Querist reference, and half family and non-academic friends who, if they know Berkeley at all, know him as the tree-falls-in-the-forest guy. Could you write me a five-minute toast where her actual Berkeley-on-money stuff is the spine of the speech and not just a bit of trivia she happens to be into? I'm picturing one running joke built off "esse est percipi" -- to be is to be perceived -- applied to money itself: a currency's only worth what people are willing to perceive/trust it as being worth, so what would Berkeley say about a bill nobody will accept. I want that joke to actually land for both the theory people and the civilians in the room.

The one thing I really care about getting right is that it shouldn't flatten her life's work into "money's not real, maaan" -- that's the lazy misreading she's spent three decades arguing against -- so the sentiment needs to actually track what she thinks (money as a social/institutional sign pointing at something real, namely labor and industry, not mere paper illusion), while still reading as warm and personal rather than a seminar on eighteenth-century Irish monetary policy.
