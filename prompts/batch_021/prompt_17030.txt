I'm at work on a novel whose central relationship is between two people who spend a long, close summer circling the same disagreement without either of them winning it. Elena trained as a psychoanalyst in a fairly orthodox Freudian mode; Marcus is a philosophy dropout who abandoned half a dissertation on Sartre and has never quite let the material go. The scene I'm currently stuck on is the one where the disagreement finally surfaces on its own terms, rather than through the usual displacement of arguing about something else entirely. What actually divides them is a question about the architecture of self-deception: when someone is furious, or grieving, or in love, and genuinely doesn't know it, is that best explained by repression — a dynamic unconscious policed by something like a censor — or by what Sartre calls bad faith, a single, undivided consciousness that manages, somehow, not to know itself?

I know enough about this to know it isn't a fair fight to just stage as "Freud says the unconscious, Sartre says freedom." Sartre's actual argument in Being and Nothingness is more pointed than that: he thinks Freud's censor doesn't so much solve the paradox of self-deception as relocate it, since a censor that can selectively repress has to already grasp, in some fashion, exactly what it's hiding and from whom — which makes the censor itself into a little bad-faith consciousness, deceiver and deceived in one. And Sartre's own alternative isn't simply an assertion of freedom either; it leans on a distinction between reflective, positional knowledge and the kind of non-positional self-awareness he thinks consciousness always has of itself, plus the more general ambiguity of being both facticity and transcendence at once. What I can't reconstruct with any confidence is how a serious Freudian would actually answer the censor charge, or where the real pressure points are in Sartre's own solution once someone leans on it.

Could you write that scene as a real dialogue between Elena and Marcus — a page or so, no stage directions needed beyond the bare minimum — in which each argues the strongest version of their position and directly answers the other's specific objections, rather than delivering parallel monologues? I'd like it to stay down in the actual mechanics of the argument (the censor, the unity of consciousness, what "knowing" would have to mean for either side's account to work) rather than drifting up into generalities about determinism versus freedom, and I'd rather the scene end with the disagreement sharpened than smoothed over, since an unearned resolution is exactly what the novel doesn't need here.
