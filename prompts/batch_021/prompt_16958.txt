I spent my working life as an experimental physicist, and only since retiring have I started reading philosophy in earnest -- lately the foundations of decision theory, where Ellsberg's paradox has become something of a fixation. I follow the formal point well enough: no single subjective probability over the unknown mix of black and yellow balls can rationalize both preferring the known bet on red over black and preferring the known bet on black-or-yellow over red-or-yellow; anyone who chooses that way is violating Savage's sure-thing principle. What I haven't settled for myself is whether that violation is a mistake decision theory is entitled to correct, the way it is entitled to correct an incoherent set of betting odds, or whether it is better evidence against the sure-thing principle than against the chooser. Please write a dialogue between a strict Bayesian decision theorist, committed to the sure-thing principle no matter what, and a proponent of an explicitly ambiguity-sensitive alternative such as Gilboa and Schmeidler's multiple-priors model, with the two of them standing at the urn trying to talk each other out of their bets. I'd like them to get past merely re-narrating the paradox and fight over two specific points: whether it matters that the ambiguity-averse pattern tends to survive being shown the inconsistency, unlike most reasoning errors people get talked out of, and whether the aversion is really just rational suspicion of whoever fixed the black-yellow ratio rather than a response to ambiguity as such. Both sides should be argued with real technical conviction -- no straw Bayesian who has never heard of non-additive probabilities, and no straw ambiguity theorist who has never heard of a Dutch book.
