Kurt Gödel was, in his private correspondence, far more than a logician: in letters to his mother he argued that the rationality of the world guarantees an afterlife, since it would be irrational for a mind's hard-won development to be simply extinguished at death. That conviction coexisted with his admiration for Leibniz's monadology, in which souls are simple, indestructible substances immune to decomposition, and with the disjunction he proposed in his 1951 Gibbs lecture: that either the human mind's mathematical powers outstrip those of any finite machine, or some mathematical truths are absolutely undecidable by any mind or machine. Suppose Gödel had lived long enough to encounter today's transhumanist proposal that a person might achieve effective immortality by having their mind scanned and "uploaded" onto a computer. Reasoning specifically from the metaphysical commitments above, rather than from generic reverence for his genius, what do you think Gödel's verdict on mind uploading would have been: a genuine technological realization of the immortality he defended to his mother, or a confusion of something mechanizable with something he held could never, even in principle, be captured by a machine?
