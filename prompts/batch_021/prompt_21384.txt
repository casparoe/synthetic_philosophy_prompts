I help put together a small philosophy podcast that grew out of a rationalist meetup group, and next month's guest is a philosopher of mind who has built her career defending the claim that perception is genuinely cognitively penetrable -- not merely that attention or eye movements can be steered top-down, but that the conscious contents of what we see can be altered by antecedent beliefs and desires, and that a perceptual state produced this way can be epistemically downgraded (rendered unjustified) in something like the way a belief inherits the defects of a biased prior. I first got interested in this question by way of Scott Alexander's old review of Andy Clark's "Surfing Uncertainty": if perception just is hierarchical prediction-error minimization all the way down, top-down penetration by priors looks trivially true, which left me puzzled about why "is perception cognitively penetrable" is still treated in the literature as a genuinely open, hard-fought question rather than a settled yes.

Reading further into it, I've come to appreciate how much weight rests on the older Fodor/Pylyshyn picture of early vision as informationally encapsulated, and on the methodological broadside from Firestone and Scholl, who argue that essentially every canonical demonstration of cognitive penetration -- from color perception to apparent distance to seeing threat in ambiguous objects -- can be redescribed as a downstream shift in judgment, memory, attention, or response bias, leaving the perceptual module itself untouched. I don't want the interview to just let her restate the thesis for an audience that already agrees with it, so I'd like help drafting eight to ten questions that actually engage that tension.

Could you make sure there's at least one question that forces her to say how she'd tell genuine content-level penetration apart from the deflationary alternatives Firestone and Scholl offer, at least one that presses on how the "epistemic downgrade" claim is even supposed to work given that perceptual states aren't usually thought to answer to reasons the way beliefs do, and at least one on whether predictive processing actually helps her case or instead undermines it by making penetration so ubiquitous that it stops doing any interesting philosophical or moral work? For each question I'd like a short, specific follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give rather than a generic prompt for elaboration, and I'd like the sequence to end on a question about what finding -- empirical or philosophical -- would actually lead her to give up the view.
