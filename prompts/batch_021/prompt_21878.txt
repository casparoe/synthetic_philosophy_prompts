A friend of mine, who taught classical Chinese philosophy for almost thirty years and directed my dissertation long before that, died two weeks ago after a short illness. She had spent most of her career working on the Zhuangzi, and the instructions she left in writing for her memorial were pretty transparently modeled on the philosopher she'd devoted her life to: cremation, the ashes scattered informally with no headstone, no black clothing, and explicitly no one is permitted to say that she is "watching over us" or "in a better place." She used to close her seminar every year on the "Zhile" ("Perfect Happiness") chapter with the scene where Huizi arrives to console the newly widowed Zhuangzi and instead finds him sitting sprawled on the ground, drumming on an upturned basin and singing; her point was always that Zhuangzi had not argued himself out of loving his wife, only out of the assumption that his wife's death was a misfortune happening to some self of hers that persisted afterward to be pitied. She also had a real fondness, which I remember mainly because it always got a laugh, for the scene near the end of the book where the dying Zhuangzi waves off his disciples' plans for an elaborate burial, tells them heaven and earth will do for an outer coffin and the sun and moon for a pair of jade discs, and then, when the disciples worry that crows will eat him if he's left exposed, asks why being eaten by worms underground is supposed to be the better deal.

I've been asked to give the eulogy at the memorial, and the room is going to split cleanly in two: former students and colleagues who will recognize exactly which arguments sit behind every one of her instructions and will notice immediately if I get them wrong or soften them into mush, and family and old friends who have never read the Zhuangzi and are there because they loved her and are simply grieving. Could you write the eulogy? It needs to let the philosophy do real work in shaping what can and can't be said, rather than showing up as decoration -- no talk of her watching over anyone, since that's closer to what she spent her career arguing against than to anything she actually believed, but also nothing so serenely detached that it comes across as telling the grieving half of the room that their grief is a mistake. I'd also like it to find an honest way of touching on that deathbed scene, since it was more or less the blueprint for what she asked us to do with her own remains, without letting it land as a cute literary allusion, or reproducing the joke about the crows and the worms in a way that scandalizes people rather than comforting them.
