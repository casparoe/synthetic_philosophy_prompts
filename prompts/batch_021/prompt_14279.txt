While reporting a piece on comparative ethics, I've been struck by how two canonical passages get pitted against each other in debates over Confucian "role ethics" (in the sense developed by Roger Ames and Henry Rosemont). In the Analects, Confucius tells the Duke of She that true uprightness lies not in a son testifying against his father for stealing a sheep, but in father and son concealing such things for each other; in the Mencius, when a disciple asks what the sage-king Shun should do if his own father committed murder, Mencius has Shun's minister of justice arrest the father unimpeded, while Shun himself abdicates -- tossing aside the throne like a worn sandal -- and flees with his father into hiding, rather than either obstruct the law or abandon him. Critics take the first passage as evidence that role ethics is nepotism in philosophical dress; defenders take the second as proof that the theory never lets family loyalty override public justice. Please argue for a reading that reconciles the two using resources internal to role ethics itself -- one that explains why concealment suits a private citizen while abdication, not obstruction, is required of a ruler -- without that distinction collapsing into an ad hoc rule bolted on from outside the theory.
