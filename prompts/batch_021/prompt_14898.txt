Had dinner with two friends this weekend and somehow we ended up 45 minutes deep into Fitch's paradox of knowability, of all things. It started because Theo claimed there's an actual proof that anti-realism about truth (the view that every truth has to be at least knowable in principle) is just self-contradictory. (1) His argument: since nobody's omniscient, there's got to be at least one true thing, call it p, that literally no one knows right now. So the statement "p, and nobody knows that p" is true. If EVERY truth is knowable, that statement has to be knowable too. But the second anyone knew it, they'd know both halves — including "nobody knows p" — except that half would then be false, since they'd have just come to know p. So that statement can never actually be known, which (by the same knowability principle) means it can never actually be true either. But we just said it was true. So the only way to dodge the contradiction is if there's no unknown truth in the first place, i.e. literally everything true is already known by somebody — which is nuts, so anti-realism refutes itself.

(2) Nadia's response was to just restrict the knowability principle so it doesn't apply to weird self-undermining statements like "p and nobody knows p" — keep it for ordinary claims about the world, and the proof never gets off the ground, without giving up anything a sane verificationist actually wanted.

(3) Theo wasn't having it: convinient that the one case you're carving out happens to be exactly the case that breaks your theory, and she hadn't said what's principled about the exclusion beyond "it causes trouble." He also pointed out that nothing else in the proof is even distinctively anti-realist — it only uses that knowledge is factive (if you know something, it's true) and that knowing "A and B" means knowing A and knowing B, both of which realists accept too. So carving out the instance doesn't diagnose the problem, it just hides the body.

(4) Nadia's comeback: there's a principled version of the restriction — only exclude statements that themselves are about what is or isn't known, a real independently-statable class, not just "whatever breaks the proof." And separately, verificationists already have independent reasons to doubt classical logic's law of excluded middle, and if you drop that, the last step of Theo's argument (going from "it's not true that p is both true and unknown" to the full "so if p is true, p is known") doesn't actually go through anymore — a second way out that isn't ad hoc at all, since its the logic anti-realists wanted regardless of this paradox.
