I'm the president of my high school's philosophy club, and over the last few meetings we've been working through decision theory, starting with Newcomb's Problem and then moving on to a case I stumbled across while reading further on my own: the "Death in Damascus" puzzle, originally due to Gibbard and Harper. It's a decision-theoretic version of the old fable about a servant who flees a startled glance from Death in a crowded marketplace, only to discover that Death already had an appointment with him in the very city he fled to. In the decision-theory version, Death is a perfect predictor who will always be wherever you actually end up going, so fleeing from Damascus to Aleppo does nothing if Death has already predicted that you'll flee there.

What I found most interesting wasn't the setup itself but the wrinkle that neither city turns out to be what decision theorists call a "ratifiable" choice. The moment you become confident you'll pick Aleppo, that very confidence should make you suspect Death is waiting there, which makes Damascus look safer again — and the reasoning just runs back and forth forever, so you can never settle on a choice that still looks best once you're sure you've made it. From what I can tell, the standard way out is to say that only a mixed strategy, randomizing between the two cities, can be stable in the way neither pure choice can be, a bit like a mixed-strategy equilibrium in a game against an opponent who's trying to match you move for move.

For our next meeting, I'd like to open with something a little lighter than our usual close reading, so instead of re-explaining the puzzle, I want to present a short joke about it. Could you write one for me? I'd like the punchline to genuinely depend on the ratifiability problem, or on the mixed-strategy fix, rather than being a generic joke about Death, fate, or the futility of running away from something inevitable — the kind of joke that would land just as well even if nobody in the room had ever heard of decision theory. A few lines are plenty; I mainly want the people who sat through the technical material with me to feel like the setup was worth it.
