A friend and I spent most of dinner last night arguing about Ruth Chang's treatment of hard choices. I pressed an objection that she wouldn't concede, and I'd like a second, better-informed opinion on whether it actually works. Chang holds that alongside "better than," "worse than," and "equally good as," there is a fourth basic value relation, parity, which can hold between two options that are comparable yet related by none of the first three. Her main argument for treating parity as sui generis, rather than as a loose way of describing vague or approximate equality, is what's sometimes called the small-improvement argument: if two options A and B were merely equally good, a slightly improved version of A (call it A+) would have to be better than B, since exact equality is so finely balanced that any improvement tips it. But when A and B are intuitively "on a par" — to borrow the flavor of one of Chang's own examples, a career as a lawyer versus a career as a clarinetist — the improved A+ does not become better than B; it still seems to occupy the same rough neighborhood as B. Since ordinary equality can't survive a small improvement like that, Chang infers that A and B must instead stand in a distinct relation that, unlike equality, tolerates small improvements without flipping into "better than."

Here is the objection I couldn't let go of. If parity really does tolerate small improvements, it should tolerate them cumulatively, up a whole chain of successive small improvements — A, A+, A++, and so on. But at the top of a long enough chain we can stipulate a version of A, call it A*, that is flatly, non-controversially better than B (an extraordinarily distinguished legal career against a strictly amateur clarinet hobby, say). Somewhere in the chain, then, parity has to give way to "better than." Locating that transition seems to demand either a sharp cutoff — implausible for a relation Chang insists is not a species of vagueness — or an admission that parity itself has fuzzy boundaries, which threatens to collapse it back into exactly the vagueness-based view she rejects. My friend's best reply was that Chang can accept fuzzy boundaries at the edges of a parity range without conceding that parity is nothing but vagueness at its core, since the fourth relation is still doing real work in the interior of the range even if its edges are soft. I wasn't sure this fully defuses the worry, but I couldn't articulate why.

Could you tell me whether this sorites-style objection actually damages the small-improvement argument for parity, or whether I'm missing a standard response from the literature? I'd also value a blunt assessment of whether the argument is even valid as I've reconstructed it, independent of whether it ultimately succeeds against Chang.
