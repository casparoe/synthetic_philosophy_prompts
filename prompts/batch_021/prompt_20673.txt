I am several chapters into a novel set at a small cryonics outfit in the Mojave, and one of my recurring characters, Eleanor, is a retired classicist who let her tech-optimist son talk her into signing the paperwork for suspension, then spends every subsequent dinner scene undermining the whole arrangement by quoting Epicurus at everyone else. I want her objections to hold together as actual Epicurean philosophy rather than collapsing into the bumper-sticker version of "death is nothing to us," because the book's momentum depends on the other characters not being able to wave her off easily. A friend of mine who teaches ancient philosophy warned me over drinks that cryonics might not map onto Epicurus's own concepts as neatly as I had been assuming, and that I should work out the mapping properly before putting speeches in Eleanor's mouth rather than paper over the gaps with a punchy line.

So here is what I would like help thinking through, working strictly from Epicurus's own position: the argument in the Letter to Menoeceus that death is nothing to us because, so long as we exist, death is not present, and once death is present we no longer exist to be troubled by it; the symmetry Lucretius later draws between that thought and the nonexistence that preceded our birth; and the underlying physics, on which the soul is a structure of unusually fine atoms distributed through the body that can sustain sentience only while the body contains it. First, does someone vitrified within minutes of cardiac arrest, before any cellular structure has broken down, actually count as dead by Epicurus's own criteria -- which seem to turn on the dispersal of the soul's atoms rather than on the mere cessation of metabolism -- or does cryonic suspension fall into some third state his framework was never built to classify? Second, if it is not equivalent to death in his technical sense, does the symmetry argument even get any grip on it? Third, arguing strictly within Epicurean premises rather than importing later objections, should a consistent Epicurean regard signing up for suspension as pointless, as a sensible hedge, or as a project that quietly reintroduces the very assumption Epicurus denies -- that permanent nonexistence is a determinate evil worth engineering around? I would like the reasoning spelled out step by step rather than handed to me as a bare verdict, since I need to know which moves are sturdy enough to survive a sharper character needling Eleanor in the scene that follows.
