Now that I'm retired from a long career teaching and doing research in physics, I've finally had time to read the philosophy of quantum mechanics properly, rather than mining it for exam questions. Working through Maudlin's Quantum Non-Locality and Relativity this winter, I've come to realize that the single word "locality" in Bell's theorem is carrying far more weight than the standard graduate treatment -- derive the CHSH inequality, show that quantum mechanics violates it, conclude that local hidden variables are dead -- ever let on. Could you walk me through the decomposition, first identified by Jarrett, of Bell's locality condition into the two logically independent halves usually called, following Shimony, "parameter independence" and "outcome independence": what each actually forbids, why only one of the two is equivalent to the no-signalling requirement that keeps the resulting nonlocality compatible with relativity, and why Bohmian mechanics and an orthodox collapse picture are usually described as violating different halves of the pair. It's the conceptual picture I want, not the algebra -- that part I can still do in my sleep.
