While reading around the value-of-information literature for my dissertation, I've run into a tension I haven't seen squarely resolved anywhere. I. J. Good's 1967 theorem, from "On the Principle of Total Evidence," shows that for a Bayesian expected-utility maximizer who updates by conditionalization, the expected utility of observing a costless, relevant signal before acting is never lower than that of acting without it: a rational agent should never refuse free evidence. Yet Hirshleifer's 1971 discussion of the private and social value of information shows that when information resolving a future risky outcome — such as which farms' crops will fail — is revealed publicly before agents can arrange risk-sharing contracts, that very information can leave every party worse off in expectation, by destroying the shared uncertainty on which mutually beneficial insurance depended. On its face, one result says information is always weakly good, and the other produces a case in which it makes everyone worse off.

Write an essay establishing whether Good's theorem and the Hirshleifer effect are genuinely in tension, or whether the appearance of conflict trades on an equivocation — about whose expected utility is being evaluated, what is held fixed, or whether we are inside a single fixed decision problem versus a setting where the information itself reshapes other agents' opportunities. Weigh this carefully, but do not end on a non-committal note: defend a specific verdict on how the two results should be reconciled, and state exactly what qualification it forces on the slogan that information is always valuable.
