I'm one of the student members of my university's Honor Council, and, as it happens, the case I've been assigned to write up this term overlaps rather neatly with the reading list for the Philosophy of Mind seminar I'm taking alongside my Council duties: we just spent two weeks on Clark and Chalmers's "The Extended Mind" and the replies it provoked. The case is straightforward on the facts: a junior was flagged during a closed-notes, no-electronic-devices logic midterm for consulting a notes app on her phone, specifically a page of inference rules she keeps there. She doesn't dispute any of this.

What she disputes is that she violated the rule's spirit rather than merely its letter. Her written statement to the Council points out that she's kept the same running notes file for over three years, edits it almost daily, never fact-checks an entry once it's recorded, consults it constantly and without any sense of deliberately "looking something up," and that retrieving from it feels to her exactly like remembering rather than searching. She argues that this satisfies, more or less to the letter, the standard criteria that are supposed to make Otto's notebook part of Otto's memory rather than a tool he merely relies on, and concludes that treating her phone as a prohibited "device" -- rather than as part of her memory -- is exactly the kind of brain-bound assumption the extended-mind literature was meant to unsettle. She isn't requesting a disability accommodation; her claim is that the rule simply doesn't reach her case at all.

The Council is split three ways, and not productively. Some members think this is just a philosophically dressed-up confession and want the standard sanction applied; others think she's identified a genuine hole in the policy and want the charge dismissed outright; the rest want some kind of middle finding but admit they can't articulate a principled line between her case and any student who might claim that looking things up simply "feels like remembering" too. I've been asked to circulate a short written opinion before we vote next Tuesday, and I'd like your help drafting it. It should settle on one specific disposition of the case -- uphold, dismiss, or a clearly defined middle path -- together with a concrete timeframe for whatever follows from it (an appeal window, a policy review, or the like), and it should engage her extended-mind argument seriously on its own terms, including its strongest form, rather than resolving the case by simply reading the exam rule back at her.
