I've spent the last few weeks reporting a piece on the psychedelic-therapy boom - MDMA and psilocybin trials, the Johns Hopkins and Imperial College centers, all the FDA drama - and a classicist I roped in for a sidebar quote sent me down a total rabbit hole into Plato's Laws that I haven't been able to shake.

Turns out the same guy who in the Republic wants the guardians upbringing scrubbed of any poetry showing gods or heroes losing themselves to food, drink, or sex spends a huge chunk of Laws Books 1 and 2 mounting this weirdly clinical defense of regulated drinking parties, against the Spartans and Cretans who just banned them outright. His argument is basically that wine is a test of the soul the same way facing danger tests courage - so a properly run symposium, with a sober president in charge, can actually reveal and train self-control instead of just wrecking it. He even grades it by age: nothing for kids, fairly moderate for young men, and then old men allowed to really let go, because (if I'm remembering this right) Dionysus supposedly gave humans wine partly as medicine for the stiffness of old age, so that cranky old guys would loosen up enough to sing and dance in the civic choruses instead of sitting there embarrassed.

That's suspiciously close to the pitch behind supervised psychedelic therapy now - controlled setting, sober guide, the whole thing framed as self-knowledge and psychological training rather than recreation. So I'm genuinely curious: if you sat Plato down in front of an actual psilocybin or MDMA therapy session today, would he recognize it as basically his own proposal with better pharmacology, or is there something load-bearing in the actual argument - the specific role of Dionysus, the fact that it's a state-run civic institution tied to choral education, or just the total cultural gulf between a fourth-century-BC symposium and a clinical trial - that would make him say this isn't the same thing at all? I'd rather have a real, specific answer than just "he'd probably have had mixed feelings."
