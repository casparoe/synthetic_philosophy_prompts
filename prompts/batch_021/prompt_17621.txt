At my company we've spent the last few months prototyping autonomous negotiation agents — LLM-based services that make deals on our behalf over things like spot compute pricing and short-term supply contracts with other companies' equivalent agents — and one recurring design question is whether it's worth building in genuine mutual source-code or weight inspection as a trust-and-cooperation mechanism, rather than just leaning on reputation scores and repeated interaction. Chasing that question down sent me into the game-theory literature on "program equilibrium," starting with Tennenholtz's 2004 result (building on earlier work by Howard and McAfee) that if players submit programs rather than actions, and those programs are given mutual read access to each other's source, then a program that cooperates only against a byte-for-byte copy of itself and defects otherwise forms a stable equilibrium of that induced program-submission game — so rational cooperation becomes possible even in a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma. That trick is obviously brittle: because it depends on exact syntactic identity, even a functionally irrelevant rewrite (a different variable-naming convention, a different Gödel-numbering scheme) is enough to make two "essentially identical" agents defect against each other.

That fragility is apparently what motivated a follow-up line of work using provability logic, out of a MIRI workshop in 2013: agents like "FairBot," which cooperates exactly when it can find a formal proof, in Peano arithmetic, that its opponent cooperates with it, with mutual cooperation between two instances of FairBot established via Löb's theorem; and refinements like "PrudentBot," which keeps that same cooperative relationship with FairBot and with copies of itself while also defecting against an unconditional cooperator, rather than wastefully cooperating with (and forgoing the extra payoff of defecting against) an agent that was never going to punish it anyway. There's apparently a further, more practically-minded line of successors that swaps out the formal proof search for direct simulation of the opponent's code (I've seen this called an "epsilon-grounded" approach). I can more or less follow the mechanics of the Löbian proofs, but I don't have the logic or philosophy background to know how much weight to put on any of this once you leave the toy setting of formulas in Peano arithmetic and start asking about real, messy, weight-based systems like the ones we're actually building.

Could you lay out what you see as the most important implications of this program equilibrium literature — Tennenholtz's original construction plus the Löbian-cooperation refinements and their simulation-based successors — for a decision like ours: whether to invest real engineering effort in mutual code or weight transparency as a cooperation-inducing mechanism between interacting AI agents, versus just doubling down on more conventional mechanism-design fixes like reputation systems or escrow? I'm especially interested in which of the obstacles this literature runs into — the undecidability of general program equivalence, the need for a shared trusted proof system, reliance on bounded proof search, brittleness to exact-match requirements — strike you as artifacts of the toy formal setting that would go away for real, learned agents, versus ones that would bite just as hard in practice, and in whether the added robustness the provability-logic approach buys over Tennenholtz's original syntactic-matching trick amounts to a real step toward something implementable, or is closer to a mathematical curiosity that even its own authors seem hesitant to extend very far beyond that toy setting.
