I'm a second-year MA student in philosophy, and this term's seminar on modal metaphysics has spent the last two weeks on de re modality: specifically the disagreement between David Lewis's counterpart theory, on which nothing exists in more than one possible world and modal claims about an individual are made true by how that individual's counterparts fare elsewhere, and views on which the very same individual literally exists in more than one possible world (transworld identity, the position Kripke is usually read as defending against Lewis). Two people in my cohort, Daniel and Sasha, spent most of a coffee after seminar arguing about it, and by the end they had wandered somewhere our syllabus doesn't actually go, so I'd like a more careful referee than I could manage on the spot.

It started with (1) Daniel running the classic Kripke line: if "Humphrey might have won the election" is made true by some merely similar other person winning in another world, that seems to change the subject, since Humphrey himself presumably has no stake in what happens to a qualitatively similar stranger. (2) Sasha answered that Humphrey's cares aren't the test of a correct semantics, and that in any case people do extend concern to their counterparts derivatively all the time -- buying insurance or writing a will only makes sense on the assumption that we care what happens to people relevantly like us in nearby scenarios. (3) Daniel granted the point but argued it shows the transworld-identity theorist is still better off, since they can simply stipulate that the winning candidate in the counterfactual scenario is Humphrey, full stop, with no similarity metric required at all. (4) Sasha replied that stipulation isn't free: it owes some account of what makes a barely-described individual in some other world Humphrey rather than somebody else, on pain of collapsing into Quine's old mockery of the "possible fat man in the doorway" with an extra stipulation stapled on. (5) Daniel came back with Chisholm's paradox: since Lewis cashes out cross-world sameness in similarity, you can chain together a sequence of worlds, each barely different from the last, that counterpart-connects Humphrey to someone wildly unlike him, forcing Lewis to either accept that absurd-looking result or deny that "could have been" composes across iterated steps -- a real cost, not a free lunch. (6) Sasha's last word was that Lewis bites that bullet on purpose and isn't uniquely burdened by it, since Kripke's own concession that an object's actual origin could have been slightly different without it being a different object opens exactly the same sorites chain for the identity camp -- so both sides owe some non-transitive or vague relation somewhere, and the real question is only which one pays less for it.

At that point the coffee shop closed and nobody had actually answered Sasha's closing question. Could you go through the six moves and, for each one, say who had the better of it, or whether it's a genuine standoff -- being honest about the strongest consideration on the losing side rather than just calling a winner? I'd also like to know whether there's a consideration neither of them raised that bears on the overall dispute, and I'd especially like your own answer to the "who pays less" question at the end, since I suspect that's where the real philosophical work is, and neither Daniel nor Sasha got there.
