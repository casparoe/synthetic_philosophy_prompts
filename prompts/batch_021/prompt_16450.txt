I've been teaching Plato's Laws in my graduate seminar on ancient ethics this term, and my students are always a little startled -- and clearly delighted -- to discover how much space Plato gives over to the regulation of drinking parties in Books I and II. It isn't the temperance sermon they expect from the author of the Republic. The Athenian Stranger argues, against the wine-suspicious Spartans and Cretans in the dialogue, that a properly supervised symposium is really a controlled experiment in self-mastery: a sober president -- what later writers would call a symposiarch -- presides over the party the way a technician presides over an experiment, watching to see whose sophrosyne survives contact with the very intoxicant designed to strip it away, on the theory that if fear can be used to train courage, pleasure can be used the same way to train moderation. Then, almost as if setting up its own punchline, Book II turns around and insists that men well past their prime should be all but required to get drunk, since only wine can dissolve the shame and stiffness that otherwise keep them from joining the choral singing and dancing Plato thinks is indispensable to a well-tuned soul.

Write me a joke that turns on this material -- the wine-as-diagnostic-test idea, the office of the sober supervisor, the age-graded license to drink, the thought that inhibition itself is the thing virtue has to defeat -- rather than a generic "philosopher walks into a bar" line that would work equally well for any thinker you plugged into it. It should be intelligible enough that someone who only knows Plato wrote something odd about drinking parties can still follow the logic of the punchline, but it should get funnier, not less funny, the better the listener actually knows Books I and II of the Laws.
