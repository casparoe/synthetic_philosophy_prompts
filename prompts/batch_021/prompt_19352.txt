For a dissertation chapter on cross-modal perception, I keep running into a methodological worry about the main empirical evidence used to adjudicate Molyneux's question, and I haven't found a treatment that addresses it head-on.

Molyneux's original question, put to Locke in 1688, asks whether a person born blind who has learned to distinguish a cube from a sphere by touch could, immediately upon having her sight restored, tell which is which by sight alone, before touching either object. Locke and Molyneux answered no; Leibniz answered yes, but only once enough time had passed for the subject's sight to settle, explicitly rejecting Locke's requirement that the test be run at first sight.

The best-known modern attempt to settle the question empirically, Held et al.'s 2011 study of five congenitally blind subjects who gained sight through cataract surgery, splits along exactly this line. Tested immediately after surgery, all five failed to match a felt shape to its seen counterpart, performing at essentially chance level despite controls showing intact shape matching within each modality alone; retested a few days later, after ordinary seeing, three of the five succeeded.

Here is the worry. Tested immediately, a failure seems at least as consistent with a visual system too immature for fine shape discrimination as with any specific absence of cross-modal transfer. Tested after a delay, a success seems at least as consistent with ordinary associative learning from handling objects while looking at them as with a transfer that owes nothing to that intervening experience. Write an essay that reaches and defends a definite verdict on whether this worry is ultimately correct -- that is, on whether any empirically realizable version of the Molyneux experiment, run at any delay, could in principle tell us what Molyneux wanted to know.
