During my working life I was a field geologist, spending a good many seasons mapping river terraces and stream deposits, and it is only since retiring that I have finally had time to read philosophy in any serious way. At present a small reading group made up of former colleagues is working through Wittgenstein's On Certainty, and given my background it was probably inevitable that I would get stuck on remarks 96, 97, and 99, where, quoting from my copy, he writes:

"It might be imagined that some propositions, of the form of empirical propositions, were hardened and functioned as channels for such empirical propositions as were not hardened but fluid; and that this relation altered with time, in that fluid propositions hardened, and hard ones became fluid." (96) "The mythology may change back into a state of flux, the river-bed of thoughts may shift. But I distinguish between the movement of the waters on the river-bed and the shift of the bed itself; though there is not a sharp division of the one from the other." (97) "And the bank of that river consists partly of hard rock, subject to no alteration or only to an imperceptible one, partly of sand, which now in one place now in another gets washed away, or deposited." (99)

What I cannot work out is how this image of a shifting river-bed is meant to sit alongside the much better-known "hinge" passage considerably later in the same book, where Wittgenstein says that our doubts depend on certain propositions being exempt from doubt, standing like hinges on which the doubting turns, and that "if I want the door to turn, the hinges must stay put" (341-343). If the very propositions that have hardened into hinges can themselves turn fluid again over historical time, and if, as he also says nearby, one and the same sentence can at one moment be tested against experience and at another serve as an unquestioned rule for testing, does that not reduce hinge-certainty to an extreme, historically local degree of confidence rather than a genuine difference in kind from ordinary empirical belief? And does it not then commit the book, despite Wittgenstein's evident resistance to the idea, to some form of relativism about our most basic certainties? Alternatively, does the further contrast he draws within the river-bed image itself, between immovable "hard rock" and shifting "sand," give him a principled way of letting some certainties drift while others stay fixed, so that the two passages turn out to be compatible after all? I would be grateful for a close reading that takes both passages fully seriously rather than quietly explaining one away in favor of the other.
