I teach our department's jurisprudence seminar, and this term we spent two whole sessions on Hart and Fuller's dueling readings of 'no vehicles in the park' -- which is annoying timing, because I've also just landed on the Graduate School's Academic Standards Committee, and this month we've got an honest-to-god case that's basically their argument wearing a name tag. The handbook says, flatly, 'All requirements for the doctoral degree must be completed within seven (7) years of matriculation.' A student in linguistics took an approved one-year medical leave -- she has an email from the Dean's office saying her 'progress clock is paused for the duration' -- and by the calendar she's now hit year seven with about a year of dissertation work left. Here's what makes it an actual fight and not just a sad case: the handbook explicitly lists two leave types that don't count against the seven years, military service and parental leave, and says nothing about medical leave at all. Half the committee reads that silence as deliberate -- classic expressio unius, if the drafters had wanted medical leave excluded too they knew how to write it in -- and doesn't think it's our place to go rewrite the list just because one case is sympathetic. The other half thinks that's a ridiculous way to read what's probably just a gap left over from before medical leave was its own formal category, and that treating the omission as meaningful gets the whole point of the tolling provision backwards.

Could you draft the memo I'll bring to committee on Thursday -- recommending grant, deny, or grant-with-conditions (a partial extension, say, or a going-forward amendment that actually names medical leave) -- with a real argument behind it rather than a 'textualists would say X, purposivists would say Y' survey? I especially want you to take the loosing side -- the 'the silence was deliberate' reading -- seriously rather than strawmanning it: there's a genuine cost to a committee like ours deciding that unwritten purposes can override an enumerated list whenever the facts pull at us, namely that the one thing a bright-line rule was supposed to buy us, that petitioners can't just argue their way into being the exception, quietly stops being true. Two more leave-related petitions are already sitting in the Dean's inbox citing this one, so whatever we decide Thursday is going to function as precedent whether we mean it to or not.
