Okay, this is a bit of a rabbit hole but I've fallen into it anyway. For a science-and-ideas column I write, I've been digging through the old finks-and-masks literature on dispositions -- C.B. Martin's electro-fink example, and the whole Johnston/Lewis/Bird back-and-forth over what people now call "masking" or "antidotes." I think I've got the textbook contrast down: a fink is when whatever triggers the disposition also switches the underlying causal basis on or off right at the crucial moment, so the thing never gets to manifest (or manifests when by rights it shouldn't). A mask (or antidote) is different -- the causal basis stays fully intact the whole time, its just that something else steps in and blocks the manifestation once the trigger happens. My problem is that every writeup uses the exact same two illustrations, the live wire wired up to a fink-device, and the poisoned drink with an antidote slipped in, and I don't feel like I've really internalized the line between the two concepts until I can spot it somewhere else entirely.

So here's my ask: invent two brand new examples for me, from a domain that has nothing to do with wires or drinks (software, animal behavior, immune systems, office politics, whatever fits), one that's a genuine fink and one that's a genuine mask. Walk through why each one lands where it does, and then, for bonus points, tell me what the smallest possible change to your own example would flip it from a fink into a mask (or the reverse) -- that boundary is exactly the part I keep loosing my grip on.
