For a graduate seminar on philosophy of language, write a dialogue between two philosophers standing at the back of a classroom, watching a student work out an addition problem of a kind she has never been asked to solve before. One holds, with Kripke, that the rule-following paradox admits only a skeptical solution: no fact about her past use of "plus" fixes which function she has been computing all along, and our calling her new answer "correct" is sustained only by a communal practice of endorsement and correction, not by any truth-condition she satisfies. The other insists that some straight solution must be available, on pain of making arithmetical correctness illusory. Have them press each other on the hardest version of the challenge: the skeptical-solution advocate should explain what, if anything, stops the community's practice from being indeterminate in exactly the way the individual's was, while the straight-solution advocate should say, concretely, what fact could settle the extension of "plus" for cases the student has never faced. Neither should be allowed to win by default.
