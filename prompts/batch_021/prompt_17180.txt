So I'm a philosophy PhD student working mostly on formal epistemology, and something happened at our game theory reading group last week that I haven't been able to let go of. We'd just finished going through Aumann's backward induction paper, and afterward two people in the group, Dan and Mei, ended up arguing about the centipede game for like 40 minutes while the rest of us just sat there eating leftover pizza. I wrote down roughly how the argument went because I wanted to ask someone about it, and I honestly still can't tell who came out ahead.

(1) Dan opened with what he called the "boring but correct" reading: if it's common knowledge that both players are rational, backward induction has to go through. At the very last node the mover strictly prefers to take rather than pass, so a rational player never passes there; anticipating that, the second-to-last mover should take too, and this unravels all the way back, so two commonly-known-rational players playing centipede should end the game on move 1.

(2) Mei said that's exactly the paradox Binmore raised: the whole derivation talks about what a rational player would do at, say, node 50, but if its really common knowledge from the start that both players are rational, node 50 is never reached. So you're using "common knowledge of rationality" to license claims about a situation that, according to that very same common knowledge, can't occur. That's not a proof, that's smuggling the conclusion in the back door.

(3) Dan came back with the Aumann line: in his framework rationality and knowledge attach to a whole strategy -- i.e., a disposition at every node -- not to the path that actually gets played, so saying "she's disposed to take at node 50 too" is no different from any ordinary counterfactual, like "if you'd touched the stove you'd have gotten burned," which is perfectly meaningful even though the stove never got touched. So there's no real incoherence, just people getting uncomfortable with counterfactuals.

(4) Mei granted that this answers the "incoherence" charge, but said it doesn't touch a seperate point, which she credited to Stalnaker: Aumann's theorem never tells us what a player should believe about her opponent once an actual deviation happens. If we're somehow at node 6, something has already gone wrong with "common knowledge of rationality held from the start" -- so why think the mover there still knows the other is rational going forward? Backward induction needs belief in the other's rationality to survive an observed deviation, and nothing in common knowledge of rationality by itself forces that.

(5) Dan said fine, that's a real distinction, but it's an objection to how strong the premise is, not a flaw in the theorem itself: the hypothesis is logically consistent and it entails backward induction; if you think it's a bad model of how actual people revise their beliefs mid-game, that's a complaint about realism, not about validity.

Could you go through (1) through (5) one at a time and tell me whether each move actually holds up on its own terms, before it gets rebutted -- not just who ends up winning? And then give me your overall read: does Mei have a genuine, unanswered objection left standing after (5), or has the disagreement basically dissolved by that point into a dispute about how much idealization we're willing to swallow, where they're no longer really disagreeing about the theorem itself?
