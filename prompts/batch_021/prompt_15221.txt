random rabbit hole from this week: rereading some Gregory Chaitin essays and remembered that Leibniz basically scooped algorithmic information theory like 300 years early. In the Discourse on Metaphysics he's got this bit about how a geometer can always fit *some* equation to any random scatter of points, but we only say the points "follow a law" when the fitting curve is way simpler than just listing the points out -- and that God picked our world because its the one thats simplest in hypotheses and richest in phenomena. which is... basically just a description of Kolmogorov complexity, no? a law only means something if it compresses the data.

so here's what I keep chewing on: if you gave Leibniz a crash course on Solomonoff induction -- the whole formalize-Occam's-razor-as-2^-(complexity), update-via-Bayes-over-all-programs thing that Yudkowsky writes about in the Sequences -- would he recognize it as "oh that's the thing I was gesturing at" and love it, or would he actually have real objections as a committed rationalist? two snags I can't get past: (1) Kolmogorov complexity is only defined up to an additive constant depending on your choice of universal machine, so the "simplicity" ranking isn't fully principled -- doesn't that kind of brute arbitrariness offend the Principle of Sufficient Reason the same way he thought absolute space did in the correspondence with Clarke? and (2) this is a guy who dreamed of a calculus ratiocinator that could settle any dispute by calculation ("calculemus!"), and the closest thing anyone's actually built to an ideal universal inductive calculus turns out to be formally uncomputable -- does he bite the bullet on that somehow, or is it a genuine defeat for the whole rationalist project? curious what you actually think here, not the "he liked simple explanations so obviously he'd approve" version.
