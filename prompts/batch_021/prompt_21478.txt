I've been working through Hobbes's account of covenants in Leviathan for a seminar on the philosophical foundations of promising, and I keep getting stuck on how his argument would hold up against developments he could never have anticipated. Hobbes's central claim is that a covenant carries no real obligation in the state of nature unless it is backed by a common power able to compel performance: absent the fear of punishment needed to override the passions—ambition, distrust, the temptation of short-term advantage—that would otherwise lead a rational, self-interested party to defect, a promise between strangers is, on his view, merely words. This is why the sovereign, an artificial person created precisely to wield that coercive power, does the moral work that trust, reputation, or any natural fact about promise-keeping cannot do by themselves—a strikingly different diagnosis from Hume's later picture of promising as a convention sustained by mutual advantage and sentiment, with no sovereign required at all.

With that framework in mind, I would like you to work out, in some detail, how Hobbes would most plausibly assess two contemporary developments he never encountered. First, blockchain-based "smart contracts" are self-executing: performance is guaranteed automatically by code rather than by any person's judgment or will, so the parties need not trust one another to begin with. Does this finally deliver, in mechanical form, what Hobbes thought only an artificial person with the power to punish could provide—so that two mutually anonymous strangers with no shared sovereign can covenant validly after all—or would he deny that something enforced by code, rather than by a punishing will, counts as a covenant in his sense at all, making it closer to a physical constraint than to a genuine obligation?

Second, autonomous AI agents increasingly negotiate and finalize binding agreements with other agents, or with humans, often faster than any person could monitor or ratify them in real time. Given that Hobbes grounds an individual's reason for keeping covenants in a very specific psychology—fear of violent death and the desire for self-preservation—is an AI agent that has neither even a candidate, on his account, for being bound by a covenant, for holding another bound to one, or for validly transferring a right on a human's behalf? Please work through Hobbes's actual premises about covenants, personhood, and the sources of obligation to answer both questions, and commit to a definite predicted verdict in each case rather than simply surveying considerations on both sides.
