I spent about thirty years as an observational astrophysicist (galaxy dynamics and gravitational lensing, mostly), and since retiring I've somehow ended up in a little philosophy reading group with some old colleagues -- currently working slowly through Duhem and then Quine's "Two Dogmas." The example our discussion leader keeps coming back to for the underdetermination thesis is the old two-planets story: Uranus's orbit didn't match the Newtonian prediction, so Le Verrier posited an unseen planet to save the theory, and it turned out to exist (Neptune). Mercury's orbit didn't match prediction either, so the very same Le Verrier posited another unseen planet, "Vulcan," to save the theory again -- and this time there was no planet, the anomaly instead got dissolved fifty-some years later by throwing out Newtonian gravity in favor of general relativity. Same move both times (invent an auxiliary hypothesis rather than blame the core theory), opposite endings, which I gather is close to the point Duhem wants to make: there's no logic that tells you in advance whether its the core theory or the auxiliary assumption that's really on the chopping block.

What I want comments on is a little essay I'm drafting for the group where I try apply this to my own corner of astrophysics: the dark matter vs. MOND dispute. Galaxy rotation curves and cluster dynamics don't match what general relativity plus visible matter predicts. You can save the theory by adding unseen mass in the right places (dark matter -- betting on "Neptune"), or you can modify the law of gravity/inertia itself at low accelerations (MOND, betting on "Vulcan" being right for once), and both camps have spent forty-odd years absorbing anomalies with further patches. My draft's claim is that this is about as clean a live case of Quine-Duhem underdetermination as science currently offers. But then I try to talk myself out of it: several independent lines of evidence (the Bullet Cluster's lensing offset from the visible gas, the relative heights of the CMB acoustic peaks, how large-scale structure forms in simulations) all lean the same way, toward dark matter, and I worry that this kind of convergence is exactly the sort of thing that's supposed to make underdetermination go away -- in which case my "great example" is really just an ordinary unresolved scientific dispute dressed up in fancy language, not the deep, principled underdetermination Duhem and Quine were actually pointing at.

So, honestly: does the analogy hold up, and is "independent lines of evidence converging" a real philosophical answer to Quine-Duhem holism, or does it just quietly change the subject from the philosophical question to a statistical one? I'd rather hear where the draft is weak now than get embarrassed in front of the reading group.
