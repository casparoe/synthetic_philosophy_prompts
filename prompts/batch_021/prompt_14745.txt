I'm at work on a novel that follows Einstein through his last years in Princeton. One scene I've drafted tries to dramatize the thinking behind his 1949 essay "Why Socialism?", written for the first issue of Monthly Review, in which he explains to a young left-wing editor, who has come to ask him for a contribution, his reasons for calling himself a socialist. Here is the passage:

Princeton, May 1949. The editor sat across from the cluttered desk, notebook open, waiting.

"You keep asking why a physicist should have anything to say about socialism," Einstein said, waving a hand as if brushing off smoke. "As though the ends we choose for ourselves were the kind of thing science could discover, the way it discovers a particle. They are not. Science sharpens our means. It cannot choose our ends. For that, every one of us must answer as a human being, not as a specialist."

The editor smiled, unconvinced, and let him go on.

"Consider what unrestricted competition asks of a man," Einstein said. "It does not simply fail to reward the cooperative feeling in him; it starves it, until what is left is a kind of crippled social sense, dressed up as ambition. And consider what unrestricted accumulation does to a society: capital, once concentrated in a few hands, buys not only factories but newspapers, and not only newspapers but, in time, the parties that are supposed to hold it in check."

"So: a planned economy," the editor said.

"So: a planned economy," Einstein agreed. "But planning is not yet the cure. It is only the instrument. Hand that instrument to a bureaucracy answerable to no one, and you have merely traded one master for a more thorough one. That is the problem I do not know how to solve. I only know that it must be solved, or the remedy will be worse than the disease."

Before I go further with the book, I would like a hard-nosed read on this scene. First: does the dialogue actually track the shape of his real argument in that essay, or have I smoothed over a move he makes that shouldn't be skipped? Second: I gave him the worry about bureaucracy as the closing beat because it struck me as the most self-undermining, and therefore the most interesting, part of his position — the thing that keeps him from sounding like a mere partisan. Is that a fair emphasis, or am I inflating something closer to an aside into a centerpiece? Third, apart from the question of accuracy: does this read as two people in genuine disagreement, or does Einstein's half amount to a lecture with dialogue tags attached? I would rather cut something true than keep something that flattens him into a mouthpiece.
