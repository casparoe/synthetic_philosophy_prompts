I've been part of a small in-person reading group that grew out of our local rationalist/EA meetup -- we've been meeting every other Sunday for about three years now, slowly working through papers people keep citing on the forums but that almost nobody in the group had actually read start to finish. This term's run was on aggregation and rescue cases: Taurek's "Should the Numbers Count?", Parfit's reply to it, and a bit of Scanlon on how contractualism is even supposed to handle numbers at all. The session on Taurek ended with two regulars, Marisa and Dev, in a real argument that then dragged on in the group's Discord for two more days. I sat out of it completely and would like an outside read on who actually had the better of it, move by move, since I keep changing my mind about who won.

The case that kicked it off was a variant of Taurek's own example that our facilitator made up to strip away distractions: a coast guard cutter picks up two distress calls at once during a storm, one from a single sailor whose dinghy has capsized, one from a life raft carrying five people; the storm means the cutter can only reach one boat before both would otherwise be lost, no other rescue is possible in time, and nothing else distinguishes the people involved -- no one is crew, no one caused the storm, there's no debt or relationship on either side. (1) Marisa said the cutter obviously heads for the raft: five deaths are a worse outcome than one, and refusing to act on that just refuses to take seriously that four extra people, each as real as the lone sailor, would die for nothing. (2) Dev answered that "obviously" was smuggling in exactly what was at issue: the five don't compose one entity whose death is five times as bad as the sailor's death; each of them, like him, stands to lose only their own single life, and none of them loses more than he does. Since nothing else picks out either side, Dev said the fair procedure is to give everyone an equal shot -- flip a coin between the two boats -- rather than write the sailor off for the crime of being outnumbered. (3) Marisa answered that this can't be right, because it proves too much: if the raft held five hundred people instead of five, Dev's rule would still hand the sailor a fifty-fifty shot against them, which no coast guard would ever actually run, and a principle that can't distinguish five from five hundred is exactly the kind you should throw out. (4) Dev conceded the large-numbers case is the hard case for his view, but said it doesn't rescue Marisa's original reasoning -- it only shows that if numbers are going to matter, they can't matter by directly summing welfare across people as though they were one big person. He said the more defensible fix is to first ask what each individual could reasonably object to, notice that this alone doesn't settle a tie between equally strong claims, and only then let numbers break the tie -- not because five hundred lives outweigh one on some cosmic scale, but because between claims that are otherwise equal, favoring the side with more valid claims is the only tie-break that isn't arbitrary. (5) Marisa said that was a distinction without a difference: if numbers get to break ties, they're doing exactly the work she wanted them to do from the start, and Dev had smuggled aggregation back in through the side door after loudly refusing it at the front. Dev's last word was that it isn't the same move at all, because a tie-break only ever fires between claims that are already equal and can never let trivial harms to a huge crowd outweigh a severe harm to one person, whereas Marisa's original reasoning has no such limit built into it anywhere.

Could you go through moves (1) through (5) and say who has the better of it at each step, not just who wins overall?
