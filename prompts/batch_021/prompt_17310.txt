ok so this is going to sound niche but its been bugging me. in stats class our teacher threw out bertrand's paradox as a random aside — the "pick a random chord of a circle, what's the probability it's longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle" thing, where depending on how exactly you define "random chord" you can get 1/2, 1/3, or 1/4, and all three ways of picking seem equally "random." she just said "weird right" and moved on to the actual lesson, but two people in my group, Sam and Theo, turned it into a whole hallway argument afterward.

Sam's take: this proves the "principle of indifference" (assume equally likely outcomes when you've got no reason to prefer one over another) is basically empty, since it never tells you WHAT to spread your uniformness over, and here you get three totally diffrent "no reason to prefer" answers that all sound equally reasonable.

Theo said not so fast — some ways of picking are more natural than others, because whatever "random chord" means, the answer shouldn't flip if you rotate the circle, slide it somewhere else on the page, or draw it bigger. apparently some guy named Jaynes tried to actually run with this and claimed it narrows things down to one answer, so the paradox doesn't kill indifference, it just means the original question was underspecified.

Sam clapped back that rotating the circle can't be the thing doing any work, because all three classic methods already don't care which way you spin the circle (each one gives back the exact same distribution either way) — yet they still disagree. so "invariant under rotation" can't be the filter Theo thinks it is, and the second you add MORE invariance requirements to actually break the tie, you have to choose which extra ones count and what exactly they apply to (the circle? the whole page? something else), which is just smuggling the answer in through a side door.

Theo's comeback was that it's not arbitrary because you should think of it physically: it's not the circle being randomized, it's a straw getting tossed onto the page from far away without anyone aiming it at the circle at all — so the sliding/resizing invariance is a fact about the toss, not a free choice about the circle, and that's a real constraint, not a disguised preference.

they wanted me to declare a winner and I bailed. can you go through it move by move and say who's actually right at each step, and whether there's some issue with all this that neither of them spotted?
