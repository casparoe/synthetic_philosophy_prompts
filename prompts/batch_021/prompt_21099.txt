So a few of us run a small side-project podcast on formal/social epistemology -- started it during the pandemic as an excuse to keep talking shop with people from grad school -- and next month's guest is a philosopher whose whole research program is built around a pretty strong claim: that collegial bodies like appellate courts, corporate boards, or expert panels are literal believers in thier own right, with judgments that can outrun anything any individual member believes, and that the discursive dilemma (the old three-judge duty-and-breach panel everyone trots out) is proof of this rather than an embarrassment for it. Her signature move is arguing that any body that wants to count as a genuine rational agent has to aggregate issue-by-issue on the premises rather than voting on the bottom-line outcome, even in cases where the resulting "verdict" is one that literally nobody on the panel individually endorses.

I want to push her past the version of this she's given in every book talk. Can you draft me 8 or 9 interview questions that get into the actual mechanics rather than just having her restate "the group can believe things no member believes"? I want at least one question on why premise-based aggregation should be privileged over conclusion-based aggregation at all, given that List and Pettit's own impossibility result says no procedure can satisfy universal domain, anonymity, systematicity, and collective rationality simultaneously without collapsing into a dictatorship; one on the agenda-manipulation worry, i.e. whoever gets to frame the sequence of sub-questions can flip the final outcome, so isn't "the group's judgment" just a redescription of whoever set the agenda; one on responsibility -- if the panel believes something no member believes, does that let the individual judges off the hook when the verdict turns out unjust; and one asking her to square her view with the fact that most real collegial courts vote on the disposition rather than issue-by-issue, if issue-by-issue really is what rationality demands. For each one, add a quick follow-up keyed to whatever deflection she's most likely to reach for, and close with a question that asks what would actually count as evidence against strong group agency rather then just being compatible with the view either way.
