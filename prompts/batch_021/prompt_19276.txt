Question: "Explain and compare Peirce's and James's accounts of truth. Your answer should address (i) how each thinker characterizes what makes a belief true, (ii) the differing roles played by the community of inquiry and individual experience in the two accounts, and (iii) the dispute between Peirce and James that eventually led Peirce to rename his own position 'pragmaticism.'"

Student answer:

"Peirce and James both reject the idea that truth is a static correspondence between a proposition and some antecedently fixed fact, tying truth instead to the practical workings of inquiry. For Peirce, truth is 'the opinion which is fated to be ultimately agreed to by all who investigate': a belief is true just in case it is the belief that indefinitely continued, properly conducted inquiry would converge on in the long run. This is why no currently held belief, however well confirmed, simply is the truth for Peirce — present science remains fallible, and truth is fixed by the ideal limit toward which inquiry tends, not by any actual stage of consensus. Peirce's picture is thoroughly social: it is the community of investigators, not any single knower, to which truth answers. Like James, Peirce was at bottom a nominalist, treating general laws as convenient shorthand for regularities among particular experiences rather than as themselves part of the furniture of the world, which is one reason he could insist that truth, though independent of what any of us believes today, only emerges through the collective process of inquiry rather than sitting ready-made.

James, by contrast, cashes truth out in terms of an idea's workability for the individual believer: an idea is true if acting on it pays off, if it satisfactorily links our experiences together — if it has good cash value, in James's phrase. Truth on this view is made rather than found: it happens to an idea in the course of its verification, rather than being a static property the idea had all along. Where Peirce ties truth to what a community would converge on given unlimited time, James is willing to let truth be more local and plural, varying with what actually proves satisfactory to particular inquirers in their own circumstances — part of why James felt comfortable extending pragmatist reasoning to defend the legitimacy of religious faith in a way Peirce found distasteful.

This difference in temperament came to a head over the word 'pragmatism' itself. James put the term into circulation first, introducing it in the Metaphysical Club discussions in Cambridge in the early 1870s and continuing to use it as the banner for his own, more expansive doctrine over the following decades. Peirce, who had developed a similar but more narrowly logical maxim about the meaning of concepts around the same time, grew uneasy watching his own thinking travel under a label he felt he had never quite chosen, stretched to cover questions — such as the rationality of religious belief — that his maxim was never meant to settle. In 1905 Peirce announced he was leaving the word 'pragmatism' to James and Schiller and coining a new term, 'pragmaticism,' for his own stricter position — a word he described as 'ugly enough to be safe from kidnappers.'"
