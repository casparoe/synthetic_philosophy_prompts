Had a long, kind of heated argument with my friend Jae last night about the Wilt Chamberlain bit in Nozick's Anarchy State and Utopia (the one where a million people each drop a quarter to watch him play and he ends up mega rich, so you go from distribution D1 to this unequal D2). We argued for like an hour about whether D2 is actually just, and we eventually landed on this shared read: D2 is fine because Wilt deserves the money, he's freakishly talented and worked way harder then everyone else, so its only fair the market funnels cash toward him. Then we got into a side argument about whether that logic extends to, say, someone who just inherits a fortune, or wins the lottery, or a totally average player who happens to be popular for dumb reasons (cute nickname, whatever) and also gets a million people paying quarters to see him play. Jae's position was that entitlement theory would treat the inheritance/lottery/mediocre-player cases as less just than Wilt's case, because there's no desert story backing up the transfer the way there is with Wilt's talent and effort.

Now that I'm typing this out I'm not 100% sure "he deserves it" was ever actually the mechanism Nozick was going for, but I couldn't pin down what else would be doing the work if not desert, and neither could Jae. So: is Jae right that the entitlement theory needs Wilt's talent/effort to be morally weighty for the argument to succeed, and that it would treat those other cases (the heir, the lottery winner, the arbitrarily-popular mediocre player) differently from Wilt's? Or is there some way the argument is supposed to go through regardless of whether the recipient deserves anything at all, in which case I feel like we were kind of missing the point this whole time.
