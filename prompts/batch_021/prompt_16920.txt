I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, but over dinner a few weeks ago a friend who is finishing a PhD in philosophy of physics got me hooked on a debate I hadn't previously known existed: what it actually means for something to be a "law of nature," as opposed to merely a pattern that happens to hold. As I understand it, there are two broad camps. Humeans hold that a law is nothing more than a generalization that earns its place in the simplest, most informative deductive system fitting everything that actually happens in the universe — the total, unstructured pattern of particular facts sometimes called the "Humean mosaic" — with no further glue connecting events beyond the pattern itself. Anti-Humeans think laws involve some further, more robust relation, such as a genuine necessitation between universals, or a primitive fact about which regularities "govern," that explains why the pattern holds in the first place rather than merely redescribing it. My friend walked me through both sides well enough that I can restate them, but whenever I try to work out which position is actually more defensible, I get stuck, because each side seems to have a serious objection ready for the other, and I don't trust my own reconstruction of how a sophisticated defender on either side would respond in real time.

Could you write me a dialogue between a committed Humean about laws (something like David Lewis's best-system analysis) and a committed anti-Humean (something like an Armstrong-style necessitation view, or a primitivist who takes lawhood itself to be fundamental and unanalyzable) in which they argue about whether the laws of nature could have been different from what they actually are, and about what, if anything, explains why the universe displays any regularity at all rather than none? I'd like this to be a genuine back-and-forth between two equally sharp interlocutors, not a case of one side quietly winning or the other being reduced to a strawman — so the Humean should press the anti-Humean on whether positing a necessitation relation actually explains the regularities or merely relocates the mystery one level up, and the anti-Humean should press the Humean on whether laws that are ultimately just a summary we happen to find convenient can really be said to govern anything, or to support the counterfactuals we ordinarily expect laws to support. Please have each side respond to the other's actual objections rather than talking past them, and don't feel obliged to resolve the disagreement neatly by the end — from what I can tell, neither camp considers the matter settled.
