Had a whole argument with my friend last night over whether commitment devices are just straightforwardly good in game theory (his whole argument was some version of "Ulysses tying himself to the mast, what's not to like"), and now I've got a real version of it on my plate. I sit on the funding board of a small AI alignment nonprofit, and a team wants a grant to open-source a "commitment module" — code that lets an AI agent credibly and irreversibly lock itself into a bargaining stance before a negotiation even starts, so it can never bluff or back down later. Their pitch is basically my friend's argument: credible commitment is how you escape prisoner's-dilemma-style standoffs instead of bluffing forever. Half the board thinks this is exactly how you get a "commitment race" instead: once the capability exists, every lab's agents have an incentive to commit first and hardest, before anyones actually worked the values out, so you get a race to lock commitments in fast rather than a race to get them right, and two agents committing to incompatible demands means mutual ruin instead of a deal — basically the AI version of both drivers chucking the steering wheel out the window in chicken. Can you draft the board's decision memo for next weeks meeting — approve, reject, or approve with conditions — with real reasons, and make sure it actually takes the "commitment solves cooperation" side seriously instead of just waving it off?
