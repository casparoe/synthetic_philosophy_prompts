In *Knowledge and Its Limits* (2000), Timothy Williamson argues that no non-trivial mental condition is "luminous": there is no condition such that, whenever it obtains, one is thereby in a position to know that it obtains. His argument applies a "margin for error" principle governing inexact but reliable judgments (such as how cold one currently feels) across a sorites-style series of closely related cases, purporting to show that any putatively luminous condition would have to persist even in cases where it manifestly does not — a result Williamson extends to refute the KK principle, that knowing that p entails knowing that one knows that p. Write an essay assessing whether this anti-luminosity argument succeeds against the strongest replies available on behalf of introspective self-knowledge (for instance, that direct acquaintance with one's own current mental states differs relevantly from the comparative, discrimination-based judgments for which margin-for-error principles were designed). Weigh the considerations on both sides and defend a definite verdict on whether any interesting class of mental states is luminous, and whether the KK principle survives in at least some restricted form.
