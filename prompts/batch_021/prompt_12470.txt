In a letter written to his mother, Marianne, in the early 1960s, Kurt Gödel offers an argument for the persistence of the person after death that he is careful to distinguish from any appeal to religious doctrine or "superstition." (a) [8 pts] Reconstruct the premises of this argument, including what Gödel means, in this context, by calling the world "rational." (b) [6 pts] Explain how the argument connects to the broader set of philosophical convictions Gödel later dictated to Hao Wang — for instance, the theses that "the world is rational" and that "materialism is false." And here, verbatim, is the student's answer:

Gödel's argument to his mother is best understood as a rationalist descendant of the traditional argument from design, translated from nature as a whole onto the development of a single human life. The premise that the world is "rational" means, for Gödel, that nothing in it happens without a sufficient reason — a principle he inherits from Kant's rationalist metaphysics and puts to a new use. Given that principle, it would be irrational for a world to produce beings whose intellectual and moral faculties visibly continue to develop and mature right up until death, only for that development to be cut off just as it becomes most fully realized. Gödel's own illustration is biological: an organ like the eye develops before birth in anticipation of a use it will only have later, and by the same logic our "higher" faculties must be developing toward a use that a single lifetime is too short to supply, which points to some form of continued existence after death.

This fits naturally with two of the theses Gödel later dictated to Hao Wang. "The world is rational" is essentially the premise of the mother-letter argument restated as a general metaphysical commitment, and "materialism is false" is doing important background work: if a person just were a physical organism, there would be no faculty left over to persist once the organism dies, so Gödel needs materialism to be false before the afterlife argument can even get started. Taken together, the theses show that Gödel wanted immortality to be a conclusion of philosophical reasoning rather than an article of religious faith.

The question is right to warn against treating this as a mere corollary of the ontological proof, but the two arguments are closer in spirit than they might first appear, since Gödel drafted both in the same period, the early 1960s: the mother letter states informally the same conviction — that a sufficiently rational world must actually contain whatever pure reason shows must exist — that the ontological proof later tries to demonstrate rigorously for the special case of a maximally perfect being. Still, the arguments are not logically dependent on one another: one could accept the mother letter's conclusion while rejecting the ontological proof, or vice versa. So they are best read as two expressions of a single rationalist conviction Gödel held throughout his life, rather than as unrelated products of entirely different periods of his thinking.
