I'm on the HR analytics team at a mid-sized software company, and last week I put together a short internal memo after noticing something strange in our hiring-channel data. Before sending it up the chain, I wanted an outside sanity check on the reasoning, since my statistics background stops at one undergraduate course. Here is the memo, lightly edited:

Over the past two years we hired 400 people through two channels: employee referrals and public job-board postings. Looking only at overall 12-month retention, referrals clearly outperform job-board postings: 168 of 200 referral hires (84%) were still with the company after a year, versus only 82 of 200 job-board hires (41%). On the strength of this, I was ready to recommend shifting hiring budget toward referral bonuses and scaling back job-board spending.

Then I broke the same numbers down by department, since Engineering and Sales have very different attrition profiles. Within Engineering, 162 of 180 referral hires (90%) stayed a year, versus 19 of 20 job-board hires (95%). Within Sales, 6 of 20 referral hires (30%) stayed, versus 63 of 180 job-board hires (35%). So in both departments taken separately, job-board hires actually retained slightly better than referrals — the opposite of the company-wide pattern. This looks to me like a textbook case of Simpson's paradox: referrals skew heavily toward Engineering (high retention regardless of channel) and job-board hires skew heavily toward Sales (low retention regardless of channel), so "channel" in the aggregate figures is really just standing in for "department."

My tentative conclusion is that the department-level numbers are the ones that matter, that there's no real advantage to referrals once you control for department, and that we should stop citing the 84%-vs-41% company-wide gap as if it were meaningful for budgeting purposes. A couple of colleagues have pushed back, arguing that the overall number is still what the board and finance team look at, so it shouldn't just be discarded. Thinking about it further, I'm honestly not sure what it would even mean for one channel to be "really" better here — the department-level breakdown feels more trustworthy to me than the aggregate, but I can't fully articulate why one slice of the data should count as more real than the other.

I'd appreciate comments on a few things: whether the reasoning in the memo actually holds up, including whether I've drawn the right lesson from the reversal and whether the arithmetic itself is correct; whether my claim that the aggregate figures are "not meaningful for decision-making purposes" is too strong, too weak, or about right; how you'd respond to the colleagues who want to keep citing the company-wide numbers; and, if you have a view, what you make of my closing puzzle about whether the aggregate or the by-department breakdown is the one that reflects what's "really" going on with these two hiring channels.
