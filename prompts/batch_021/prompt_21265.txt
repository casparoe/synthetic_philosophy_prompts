I'm prepping a session on Pascal for my early modern political philosophy seminar and I've gotten stuck chewing on one of the Pensées fragments — the "justice, force" one. (I can never keep straight whether it's Brunschvicg 298 or Lafuma 103 or what, the numbering across editions always trips me up.) The bit I mean opens something like: "Justice, force. — Il est juste que ce qui est juste soit suivi, il est nécessaire que ce qui est le plus fort soit suivi. La justice sans force est impuissante ; la force sans justice est tyrannique... La justice est sujette à dispute ; la force est très reconnaissable et sans dispute... Et ainsi ne pouvant faire que ce qui est juste fût fort, on a fait que ce qui est fort fût juste." For anyone in the group who's French is rusty, the gist is: justice unbacked by force is powerless, force unbacked by justice is tyranny, and since justice is endlessly disputable while force just settles things on its own, rather than manage to make the just side strong, people made the strong side "just."

What I keep flip-flopping on is how cynical this is actually supposed to be, and I was hoping you could help me pressure-test a reading. Taken on its own the fragment sounds like flat-out proto-Hobbesian debunking — justice just IS whatever force manages to stabilize, no remainder left over. But Pascal's got this whole apparatus elsewhere (the three orders of body/mind/charity, the "raison des effets" bits where the truly wise consciously go along with customs they see straight through) that seems designed to leave room for some further, truer justice that's simply out of reach on the plane of force and bodies. There's also the neighboring fragment about truth on one side of the Pyrenees being error on the other, which pulls in the same relativizing direction but via Montaigne rather than Hobbes. A colleague of mine says I'm overreading it and it's just cynicism all the way down, but I'm not convinced.

So: is the force/justice fragment best read as a limited claim about justice-as-it-functions-politically, with a theological escape hatch left open elsewhere in the text, or does Pascal's underlying Pyrrhonism mean natural justice really does bottom out at convention, full stop, with grace doing completely separate work that isn't in the business of rescuing justice at all? I'd love it if you could walk through how the pieces are supposed to fit (or don't), ideally engaging with the actual mechanics of the fragment rather than just reaching for the "Pascal is basically a relativist" one-liner.
