A friend and I both sit on the five-member grant committee of a small arts foundation, and this week we had a long argument about how the committee should have handled a contested application. Funding requires an affirmative verdict on both of two separate criteria, artistic merit and financial feasibility, and when we tallied the committee's majority view on each criterion separately, the result implied that the project should be funded; yet when everyone was polled directly on the bottom-line question of whether to fund it, a majority said no. My friend maintains that only the premise-based procedure—deciding criterion by criterion and then applying the funding rule—treats each criterion with the seriousness it deserves, while I think there is no principled reason to prefer it over simply going with people's considered overall verdicts, and that our disagreement is really just an instance of the discursive dilemma studied in judgment-aggregation theory. Please explain the most important implications of the discursive dilemma and the associated impossibility results in judgment-aggregation theory for how small deliberative bodies—committees, boards, juries, and the like—should combine members' judgments on logically connected questions, and say whether either the premise-based or the conclusion-based procedure can be defended as a principled default.
