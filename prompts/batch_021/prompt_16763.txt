And here's the student's answer:

"A disposition like fragility is standardly analyzed as a conditional: something is fragile iff it would break if struck. Finks are cases where this analysis fails because the disposition itself switches on or off exactly when the triggering condition occurs. C.B. Martin's classic example is the electro-fink: a wire hooked up to a device that senses an incoming conductivity test and makes a dead wire live the instant a tester touches it, so the wire counts as disposed to conduct even though it isn't actually live beforehand. Masks work differently: masking doesn't change whether the object has the disposition, it just blocks the disposition from manifesting. The go-to example is a fragile glass that's been wrapped in bubble wrap - the glass is still fragile, it just won't break when struck because the wrap absorbs the impact.

Lewis's reformed conditional analysis fixes the fink problem by tying the truth of the conditional to the object's intrinsic causal basis in the moment just before the stimulus, plus a requirement that this basis persist for a short interval afterward. That handles Martin's fink because in the finked case the intrinsic basis (being dead) doesn't survive through the stimulus - it flips right at the moment of testing - so the analysis correctly says the wire lacks the disposition until the flip happens. The same fix can't rescue the analysis from masking though, because masking works the same way finking does, just slower: the bubble wrap gradually alters the glass's molecular structure over the course of the impact, so the glass stops being fragile in Lewis's sense right as it's struck. That's exactly why Lewis himself eventually gave up on the conditional analysis altogether and moved to a dispositional essentialist view of properties instead."
