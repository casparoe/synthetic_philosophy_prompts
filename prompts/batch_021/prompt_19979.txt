In a philosophy, politics, and economics workshop in my department, I recently gave a talk on Robin Hanson's proposal for futarchy: the idea that a society should fix its welfare metric by ordinary democratic vote and then let conditional prediction markets determine which policy is implemented, based on which policy the markets expect will maximize that metric — vote on values, bet on beliefs, as Hanson puts it. It set off a disagreement between two colleagues who were in the room, Kai from economics and Nora from political theory, which has continued by email ever since; I've been copied on the thread and would like a more disinterested reading of it than I can manage myself. The case they keep arguing about is a live one: a mid-sized city is deciding whether to close a downtown street to cars and convert it into a pedestrian plaza, and a local reform group wants the decision handled by futarchy rather than referendum. Under their proposal, the council would first vote on a weighted index of nearby retail revenue, measured foot traffic, and 311 complaint volume (the city's non-emergency service line) as the official metric of "neighborhood welfare" for that stretch of street, then run two conditional prediction markets — one paying out on the index if the plaza is built, one paying out on the index if it isn't — and implement whichever policy the market-implied conditional expectation favors.

(1) Kai's opening argument was that ordinary voting is reasonably good at aggregating what people want but bad at aggregating dispersed factual knowledge about what will actually work, so splitting the decision into a values stage settled democratically and a beliefs stage settled by market should beat both pure referendum and handing the decision to planners outright. (2) Nora objected that whoever controls implementation — here, council members and well-connected landlords — could place large bets on the plaza and then simply vote it through regardless of what the market "really" thinks, cashing in on a self-fulfilling prophecy that has nothing to do with genuine information. (3) Kai answered that this is less damning than it sounds: a trader who moves the price away from the informed consensus for reasons other than genuine belief hands everyone else a profitable arbitrage, and it is exactly this corrective pressure that keeps manipulation from persisting in real markets — so the possibility of manipulation shows only that the market needs enough independent liquidity to function as advertised, not that the scheme is conceptually broken. (4) Nora replied that this concedes the less important half of her worry: even a manipulation-proof market only tells you what maximizes the chosen index, and a composite of retail revenue, foot traffic, and complaint counts is not a neutral stand-in for "neighborhood welfare" — it favors commercial vibrancy over quiet residential use, and whatever merchants notice over whatever renters and pedestrians notice. So the beliefs stage never actually operates on top of a settled, prior values stage; the values fight just migrates into the design of the index, where, unlike the referendum futarchy was meant to replace, it is never itself put to a vote.

Neither of them will budge. Could you go through moves (1)–(4) one at a time and say who has the better of it at each step? Please give your verdicts as a JSON object of the form {"1": ..., "2": ..., "3": ..., "4": ...}, with each value one of "right," "partly right," or "wrong," followed by a few sentences justifying each verdict in turn. Then, separately, tell me whether Nora's point in (4) strikes you as a decisive objection to futarchy as such or only to careless choices of metric — and if the latter, what, if anything, would stop a more careful metric from running into the same problem one level up.
