After nearly forty years running a pharmacology lab, I retired three years ago and have spent much of the time since finally reading the philosophy of science I never had leisure for as a working researcher -- Mayo and Royall on statistical evidence, and behind them the older argument between Bayesians and frequentists that goes back to Fisher, Neyman, and Savage. I mention this only because it has left me less settled, not more, on a live question before the small foundation whose Methodological Standards Committee I now sit on. We fund preclinical and translational research, and like most such bodies we tightened our rules after the reproducibility crisis of the last decade: grantees must fix and preregister their sample size in advance and are barred from examining their data, in any analyzable form, before that number is reached.

A subcommittee has now proposed an exception, and the full board votes on it at next month's meeting. Under the proposal, researchers who want to monitor an experiment as the data accumulate -- to end it early, say, if the animals are suffering or a costly reagent is running low -- may do so without penalty, on one condition: they report a Bayes factor rather than a p-value. The argument, pressed hard by two of our more theoretically inclined members, is that this is not a loophole but a straightforward consequence of the likelihood principle: since what the data say, on a Bayesian reading, depends only on the likelihood actually observed and not on the rule that stopped collection, there is simply nothing left to correct for, unlike the frequentist case, where the long-run error rate really is inflated by peeking. Our working statisticians remain unpersuaded. They suspect that a sufficiently patient and motivated researcher can nearly always wait for a Bayes factor that looks respectable, and that recasting the stopping problem in the vocabulary of "evidence" rather than "significance" only gives old-fashioned data-dredging a more respectable name.

I have been asked to draft the committee's recommendation memo, and I would value your help with it. Could you write the memo I should bring to the board: a specific recommendation -- adopt the exception, reject it, or adopt some qualified version of it -- together with reasoning that actually engages the losing side's strongest argument rather than politely restating the winning side's? Whatever we decide, I would like the colleagues on the losing end of the vote to at least recognize their own position in what I've written.
