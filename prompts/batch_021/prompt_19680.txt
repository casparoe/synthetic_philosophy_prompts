Every semester I convene an informal reading group with colleagues from philosophy, mathematics, and economics on topics in formal epistemology, and a dispute broke out a few weeks ago that I have not been able to settle to my own satisfaction, even after mulling it over since. We had just finished a session on Aumann's agreement theorem, and two of the regulars — a decision theorist I will call Nadia and a philosopher of science I will call Owen — fell into an argument that started as banter and turned into a real disagreement. A few weeks earlier, each had independently stated a credence that a contested psychology finding would replicate: Nadia said 0.75, Owen said 0.3. When this came up again over coffee, the exchange went roughly as follows.

(1) Nadia said that since they were both trying to be good Bayesians, and now had common knowledge of each other's number — each knew the other's estimate, knew that the other knew theirs, and so on — Aumann's theorem shows they cannot rationally go on disagreeing: one or both of them has to move. (2) Owen replied that the theorem only forces convergence when the two parties share a common prior over the relevant possibilities, and nothing requires two people to have started out with the same prior, so their disagreement, even after full disclosure, is not automatically irrational. (3) Nadia answered that the real question is not whether their priors were numerically identical, but whether the residual gap tracks any actual asymmetry in evidence; if it doesn't, the difference in priors is exactly the sort of thing rational people ought to revise away, on pain of the disagreement being arbitrary. (4) Owen said this proves too much: the theorem's proof pins down posteriors given a common prior and says nothing about any obligation to harmonize priors in the first place, and reasonable people can ground their priors in different background commitments without thereby being irrational. (5) Nadia responded that if Owen is right about that, the theorem is toothless in practice, since it lets any two disputants simply attribute their gap to "differing priors" and stop there, which cannot be what made agreement theorems seem philosophically interesting in the first place. (6) Owen conceded that this is a genuine limit on how much the theorem tells us about disagreements between real, non-ideal people, but insisted that the limit lies in the theorem's idealized assumptions, not in anything mistaken about how he was using it.

I would like your help adjudicating this properly, move by move rather than by simply declaring an overall winner, since my sense is that each of them is onto something and also missing something. Please work through moves (1) through (6) and report your verdict on each as a JSON object of the form {"1": <score>, "2": <score>, ..., "6": <score>}, where each score is an integer from -2 (clearly mistaken) to 2 (clearly right); then, separately, briefly justify each score and say what, if anything, you think both Nadia and Owen are still overlooking about what Aumann's theorem does and does not establish.
