I'm TAing a graduate seminar on decision theory this term, and I've been asked to do a first pass on grading one of the take-home questions before the instructor reviews it. The question was:

"Lara Buchak's risk-weighted expected utility (REU) theory allows an agent to rationally prefer a certain outcome to a risky gamble with equal or higher expected value, without appealing solely to a concave utility function over outcomes.

(a) State the formal definition of REU for a gamble with outcomes ranked from worst to best, and explain what the risk function r contributes that the utility function u does not.
(b) Explain why Buchak thinks this framework accommodates Allais-style preferences while conceding less than the alternative of simply assuming diminishing marginal utility of money (cf. Rabin's calibration argument).
(c) Relate REU theory to Quiggin's rank-dependent utility theory and to Yaari's 'dual theory of choice under risk.'
(d) Does an REU-maximizer with a convex risk function ever violate stochastic dominance? Explain why or why not, and say why the answer matters."

Here is the student's answer in full:

"(a) REU is defined for a gamble g with outcomes o1 ≤ o2 ≤ ... ≤ on (worst to best) and associated probabilities p1,...,pn as REU(g) = u(o1) + Σ_{i=2}^{n} [u(oi) − u(oi−1)] · r(pi + ... + pn). The risk function r is a strictly increasing function from [0,1] to [0,1] with r(0) = 0 and r(1) = 1, applied to the decumulative probability of getting an outcome at least as good as oi. If r is the identity function, REU collapses to ordinary expected utility. What r adds is a way to weight the probability of ending up at or above a given rank, independently of how much the agent values the outcomes themselves; a convex r puts extra weight on the worse ranks and represents a global taste for caution, while a concave r represents a taste for gambling.

(b) On Buchak's diagnosis, standard EU theory has only one dial to capture risk attitudes — the curvature of u — which forces a single psychological fact (how much extra money is worth to you) to do two jobs: explaining diminishing returns to money and explaining your attitude toward risk as such. Rabin's calibration argument shows that if mild, plausible aversion to a modest 50/50 bet is explained purely through a concave utility function, the implied risk-aversion over larger stakes becomes absurd. Because REU has a second free parameter, ordinary risk-aversion can be located in r instead, so u can stay close to linear at the relevant scale and the Rabin problem doesn't arise. Buchak thinks this is also the theoretically cheaper way to accommodate Allais-style choices: since the certainty effect in Allais's paradox is a failure of the independence axiom, and independence is really just a special application of transitivity to compound gambles, REU theory secures the Allais pattern by relaxing transitivity itself rather than by tinkering with u — a smaller concession than it sounds, since it is only relaxed for gambles that share a common sub-outcome.

(c) REU belongs to the same family as Quiggin's rank-dependent utility theory: both replace linear probability-weighting with a function applied to decumulative probabilities. Yaari's dual theory of choice under risk is the fully general member of this family, allowing both the utility function over outcomes and the probability-weighting function to be nontrivial simultaneously; Quiggin's theory, and Buchak's REU along with it, is the special case of Yaari's framework obtained by additionally requiring u to be linear, so that all risk-attitude is pushed into the weighting function alone.

(d) No — an REU-maximizer never violates stochastic dominance, however convex r is, because r is applied to cumulative (decumulative) probabilities rather than to the probability of each outcome taken separately: shifting probability mass from a worse outcome to a better one can only raise the decumulative probabilities r is applied to, never lower them, so REU can only go up. This is exactly where the original Kahneman-Tversky prospect theory ran into trouble, since its weighting function was applied outcome-by-outcome rather than cumulatively and could therefore rank a dominated gamble above the gamble dominating it — part of the motivation for the later cumulative version."
