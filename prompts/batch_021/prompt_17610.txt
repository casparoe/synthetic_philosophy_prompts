After a chamber-music recital last week, a friend of mine — a professional violist who has little patience for philosophy — and I ended up in a two-hour argument over drinks about Schopenhauer's philosophy of music, which I had been reading for a seminar on nineteenth-century aesthetics. My position was that, whatever one makes of his larger metaphysics of the Will, most of his specific claims about music hold up remarkably well when considered on their own terms; she thought I was being far too charitable, and that several of these claims are little more than flourishes he needed to make his case for music's supremacy among the arts. We went through them one by one with her battered copy of The World as Will and Representation open on the table, but by closing time we had only managed to agree that we disagreed.

Below are six of the specific claims that came up, restated in my own words rather than quoted directly, since neither of us trusted our memories enough to reconstruct his exact phrasing:

- Direct copy of the Will: unlike the other arts, music does not objectify the Platonic Ideas at all; it is a direct copy of the Will itself, bypassing the Ideas that every other art must pass through.
- Four voices as grades of nature: in a harmonized piece, the bass, the inner voices (tenor and alto), and the melody correspond respectively to the lowest (inorganic, gravitational), middle (plant and animal), and highest (human, self-conscious striving) grades of the Will's objectification.
- Melody as the will's craving: melody's constant wandering away from, and eventual return to, the keynote depicts the endless craving, digression, and only ever temporary satisfaction of the will, before the cycle begins anew.
- Suspensions as delayed desire: dissonances that suspend and postpone an expected resolution are pleasurable precisely because they enact, in miniature, the delay and eventual satisfaction of desire.
- Abstract yet concrete universality: because music expresses the inner nature of feeling wholly apart from any particular occasion or object, it reaches a universality higher than that of abstract concepts, while remaining perfectly concrete and determinate in a way concepts never are.
- Purity of absolute music: because text and any imitative tone-painting (of storms, battle sounds, birdsong, and the like) tie music to the representation of particular phenomena, Schopenhauer treats such uses as a degradation of music's proper vocation, and regards the wordless symphony as its fullest expression.
