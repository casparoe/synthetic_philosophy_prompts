So my friend Dana and I had an argument that just wouldnt die. We spent a whole evening debating whether the housing market here is about to crash, laid out literally all our reasoning to each other, and still disagreed enough at the end that we put fifty bucks on it. We shook hands, called it "agreeing to disagree," and moved on -- until I made the mistake of reading about Aumann's 1976 paper, literally titled "Agreeing to Disagree," which apparently proves that two Bayesians who share the same prior can't actually end up with different opinions once those opinions are common knowledge between them, plus there's a related "no-trade" result saying rational people shouldn't take opposite sides of a bet on pure difference of opinion either. Dana's comeback was that this whole thing leans on the common prior assumption, which she thinks is just a modeling convenience with zero real justification -- nothing says two people have to start out believing the same things about anything. I said no, there's a real argument behind the so-called Harsanyi doctrine: probability is supposed to track the world, not the person, so two equally rational people reasoning from scratch about the same situation shouldn't diverge before any evidence even comes in. Neither of us backed down. Can you write a dialogue between two people defending our two positions -- one a committed defender of the common prior assumption, one a skeptic who thinks it's smuggling in something unearned -- and have them really engage with what it would mean for two rational, equally informed people to still end up on opposite sides of a real bet?
