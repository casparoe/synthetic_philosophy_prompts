A friend and I spent most of a long dinner arguing about Gottlob Frege's 1924 diary: the one, discovered among his papers and published only decades after his death, full of nationalist, antisemitic, anti-parliamentary invective, which famously unsettled Michael Dummett -- the philosopher most responsible for canonizing Frege as a founder of analytic philosophy -- when he first encountered it. My friend's position was that the diary is simply irrelevant to the philosophy: the soundness of the context principle, the sense-reference distinction, or the logicist reduction of arithmetic can't depend on the character of the man who formulated them, any more than a valid proof becomes invalid because its author was unpleasant. I argued there's at least a real tension worth taking seriously -- between Frege's official doctrine in "Der Gedanke" that thoughts inhabit an objective "third realm" (his own phrase was literally "drittes Reich") binding on every rational being regardless of nation or race, and the ethnic-particularist politics of the diary -- even if that tension ultimately cuts against, rather than for, any deep connection between the philosophy and the politics. Neither of us budged. Write an essay that lays out the strongest version of each side, engages seriously with whether and how Frege's anti-psychologism, his logical objectivism, and his elitism about who is capable of grasping abstract thoughts bear on his politics, and then reaches and defends a clear verdict of your own.
