I've spent years reading Overcoming Bias, LessWrong, and Slate Star Codex/Astral Codex Ten, and one specific pattern keeps striking me: the rationalist and EA communities' normalization of polyamory tracks a particular argument from Bertrand Russell's *Marriage and Morals* far more closely than it tracks generic progressive rhetoric about relationships. Russell's claim there, resting partly on the anthropological evidence of his day for wide cross-cultural variation in sexual jealousy, was that jealousy is substantially a product of upbringing and property-based marriage customs rather than a fixed instinct, and that a differently raised population could largely do without it. These communities now amount to something like a live trial of that claim — open relationships, relationship anarchy, polycules, and explicit practices for cultivating "compersion" instead of jealousy — and yet jealousy keeps turning up in their own internal discussions as a serious, recurring problem rather than dissolving the way Russell's diagnosis implied it should. Bracketing the book's more dated material, how do you think Russell would react to this specific track record if he could see it: would he treat it as confirming his account (badly implemented, still too close to conventional upbringing) or as genuine evidence against his central empirical bet, and what would it force him to revise? Answer in terms of how his own reasoning would most plausibly run, not with a generic "ahead of his time" verdict.
