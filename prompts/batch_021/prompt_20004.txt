I'm working on a piece about philosophy departments experimenting with AI grading assistants, and a friend who teaches a seminar on philosophy of religion agreed to let me use one of her actual exam questions, together with a student's answer to it, as a test case for the story. The question comes from a unit on David Lewis's short paper "Do We Believe in Penal Substitution?," in which he tries to work out whether the doctrine that Christ was punished in the place of sinners can be made philosophically coherent by treating punishment on the model of a debt that a third party can simply pay off. Below are the question, worth 15 points, and one student's answer, transcribed exactly as submitted with the name removed.

Question: Lewis asks us to imagine an offender who has been fined for some offense, and a friend who wants to cover the cost for him. (a) [5 pts] Explain what work this example does in Lewis's argument: why does the fact that a fine, unlike punishment as such, can apparently be paid off by a third party matter for the coherence of penal substitution? (b) [5 pts] Identify at least one respect in which Lewis thinks the fine-paying case fails to capture something essential to punishment, and explain why that residue matters for the doctrine. (c) [5 pts] Overall, do you read Lewis's paper as vindicating penal substitution, refuting it, or landing somewhere in between? Defend your reading in a few sentences.

Student answer: Lewis's example is meant to expose an assumption that defenders of penal substitution often help themselves to without noticing, namely that punishment is basically a kind of debt, so that it doesn't matter who ends up paying it as long as it gets paid. A parking fine can be settled by a friend, a stranger, or the offender, and the state doesn't care who hands over the money; the debt is simply discharged either way. If punishment really worked like this, it would not obviously be unjust for Christ to be punished in our place, since the "debt" incurred by sin would just be getting paid by a different party, much as Anselm already argued in Cur Deus Homo, where Christ's death repays the debt that sin incurred against God's honor.

Lewis, however, notices that the fine case does not fully bear this out. Even when a friend pays your fine for you, you do not walk away completely clean: you still get the opprobrium of having been fined, even though the money came from someone else. So at least one part of what we mean by punishment, the attaching of blame to the offender specifically, stays exactly where it was and cannot be handed off the way money can. That matters for penal substitution, because it suggests that even if Christ could absorb the cost of sin, our own guilt might not thereby be erased, which looks like a real problem for the doctrine.

Even so, I do not think Lewis means this as a takedown of penal substitution so much as a way of being fair to both sides. He is, after all, working very much in the tradition of C.S. Lewis, who thought Christians should not get too attached to any one theory of exactly how the atonement works, so long as they agree that it does work. Read that way, the fine example is not really an objection at all; it is Lewis clearing the ground so that some less literal-minded version of the doctrine can still do the job. On balance, I would say the paper ends up closer to a qualified defense of penal substitution than a refutation of it.
