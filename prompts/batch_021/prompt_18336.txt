I run a small reading group with a few of my grad students on early modern moral and political philosophy, and last week's session on Pascal turned into a real argument that I'm still turning over. We'd gotten to that stretch of the Pensées where he says justice without force is powerless and force without justice is tyrannical, and where he basicaly argues that since people couldn't manage to make what's just also strong, they settled for making what's strong count as just instead -- plus the nearby material on custom standing in for justice in the eyes of ordinary people, and his "reason of effects" idea that there's a naive level (people who respect rank because they confuse it with real merit), a half-clever level (people who see through that confusion and despise the whole practice), and a fully wise level that circles back to the very same outward deference as the naive, but for a completely different, second-order reason.

Here's roughly how it went. (1) Ines said this is just Thrasymachus in nicer sentences: strip away the aphorisms and Pascal is telling us "justice" just names whatever force happens to have entrenched, dressed up so the ruled don't get any ideas. (2) Marco said that can't be right, because calling naked force "tyrannical" only makes sense against some standard of justice that force can fail to meet -- so Pascal isn't debunking justice, he's explaining why a real standard can't get traction in politics. (3) Ines replied fine, maybe there's some "true" justice hovering out there for the handful of people who reach it, but every actual piece of political advice Pascal gives amounts to "don't tell people the law is arbitrary, just obey what's established," which is indistinguishable in practice from her original reading, whatever metaphysical footnote he attaches. (4) Marco came back with the three-level business, arguing that the fully wise circling back to endorse the same practices as the naive, but for a different reason, is exactly what lets Pascal draw a line between legitimate order and pure tyranny that a flat conventionalist could never draw, and that Ines was collapsing that distinction by treating all deference to established power as equivalent.

Neither of them budged and it's not really my place to referee my own group, so I thought I'd ask you instead. Could you go through the four moves one at a time and say who's got the better of it at each stage (not just an overall winner), and then say what you think both of them are missing about what Pascals actually up to in these fragments? A short final verdict at the end is fine too, but the move-by-move part is what I actually want.
