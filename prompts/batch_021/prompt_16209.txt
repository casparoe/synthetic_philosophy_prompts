Ok so a few weeks back I went down a total rabbit hole reading about Thomas Hobbes (the Leviathan guy, not calvin and hobbes lol) and there's this argument of his that's been stuck in my head since. Basically he thought geometry was the one science we can be totally, 100% certain about, and his reason was kind of wild when I first read it: he said its because WE draw the lines and construct the figures ourselves, so there's nothing hidden about a triangle or circle, we already know its whole "cause" since we made it happen with our own hands (kind of ironic given he also spent like 20 years convinced he'd squared the circle and actual mathematicians kept publicly destroying him for it, but that's a whole other story). Anyway, he doesn't stop at geometry - he uses that exact same reasoning to argue that civil philosophy (basically the study of the state) can be just as certain, because a commonwealth is also something people build themselves, through the whole social contract/covenant thing.

I brought this up with a friend and she pointed out it sounds a lot like a bigger idea she'd heard about called something like "verum factum" - that we can only truly know what we ourselves make - which apparently comes from an Italian guy named Vico who was writing a bit after Hobbes, and who took basically the same thought and ran with it way further, applying it to all of human history and society in general, not just geometry and government specifically.

So what I'm actually curious about is: how special is Hobbes's version of this idea, really? Is he basically just stumbling onto a narrow, early case of what Vico later generalized into a whole theory of knowledge, or is there something about Hobbes's specific reasoning (the geometry + civil philosophy combo) that makes it a genuinely different kind of argument, not just a smaller-scale one? And as a side question - any thoughts on why Hobbes, given what a hardcore materialist he was about literally everything, never took the leap of applying this same "we can only know what we make" logic to physics or nature more broadly, the way you'd almost expect him to?
