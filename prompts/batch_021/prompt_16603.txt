I've been reading Parfit's discussion of what's sometimes called "rational irrationality" in Reasons and Persons for an independent decision-theory project with my school's philosophy club, and I keep getting stuck on one question: does his Hitchhiker case actually show that causal decision theory is mistaken, or just that being rational can sometimes cost you?

Quick recap of the case: dying of thirst in the desert, you can only get a ride into town from a driver who is an excellent, though not infallible, judge of whether people are telling the truth; she will take you only if she predicts, before setting off, that you will pay her $1,000 once you're safely in town, and she has no way to force payment afterward. Causal decision theory seems to say that once you've arrived safely, paying can no longer affect whether you got the ride, so a rational agent shouldn't pay — and a driver who foresees this simply leaves you to die.

Could you write a dialogue between a causal decision theorist who bites that bullet and insists that not paying is the rational choice once you're in town, and a proponent of functional decision theory who holds that rationality should be assessed at the level of the decision procedure fixing both your choice and, via "subjunctive dependence," the driver's earlier prediction of it, rather than at the level of an isolated act performed after the prediction is already settled? I'd like the causal decision theorist to press hard on whether that subjunctive dependence is really anything more than backward causation in disguise, or evidential correlation wearing a new name, and I'd like the functional decision theorist to give a substantive answer rather than just pointing back to the outcomes. Please make both sides equally sharp, with genuine back-and-forth rather than either one simply restating their position.
