Two people in my Frederick Douglass reading group got into a disagreement last week that the rest of us never managed to settle, and I'd like an outside read on it.

We had just discussed Douglass's mature position -- laid out most fully in his 1860 Glasgow address -- that the Constitution, read according to its actual text, is an anti-slavery document: it never once uses the words "slave" or "slavery," and clauses like the three-fifths and fugitive-slave provisions are written in deliberately neutral language ("other Persons," "Person held to Service or Labour"). (1) Callum said this vindicates Douglass's break with Garrison: interpret a legal document by what it says rather than by what some of its framers privately wanted, and the Constitution comes out on the side of liberty -- Garrison's "covenant with death" was a moral verdict dressed up as a textual one. (2) Noor answered that this gets the euphemisms backwards: those clauses only make sense as protections for slaveholders, everyone at the Convention understood as much, and neutral wording chosen to disguise a compromise isn't the same as a silence that leaves room for a better reading. (3) Callum replied that meaning isn't intention -- what matters is what the words commit the country to, and Douglass's whole point was to hold future generations to the document's stated promises rather than to the private hopes of the men who wrote it. (4) Noor said that proves too much, since the very same words were read as straightforwardly pro-slavery by the courts of Douglass's own lifetime, in Prigg v. Pennsylvania and then Dred Scott -- so "what the words commit the country to" turns out to be whatever they were actually used to enforce, which makes Douglass's reading wishful rather than principled. (5) Callum answered that this conflates meaning with enforcement: collapsing the two is Garrison's mistake in reverse, letting bad rulings stand in for bad law. (6) Noor closed by saying Callum still owes an account of why a hopeful minority reading should outrank the reading shared by the framers, the slave states, and the Supreme Court, when what was at stake was people's actual freedom rather than textual elegance. Neither budged, and the meeting ended there.

Please go through the exchange move by move and say who has the better of it at each numbered point, not just who wins overall. I'd also like to know what you think both of them are missing -- including anything from Douglass's own actual arguments, as distinct from Noor's and Callum's reconstructions of them, that would strengthen or undercut either side.
