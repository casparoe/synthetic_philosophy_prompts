Okay so for my philosophy of language class we just wrapped up Frege and Russell on definite descriptions and now we're a few lectures into Kripke's Naming and Necessity. I got kind of hung up on the bit in (I think) lecture two where he brings up the old definition of the meter as the length of that platinum-iridium bar in Paris, which he calls stick S or something like that. His whole point is supposed to be that "S is one meter long at t0" is knowable a priori, since that's literally just how we fixed what "one meter" means -- you don't need to go measure anything.

But that's the part that's bugging me: if a sentence is true purely because of how a term got defined/stipulated, isnt that just the standard case of a necessary truth? Like, an analytic statement can't turn out to be false, that's kind of the whole point of calling it analytic. So when Kripke turns around a couple pages later and says the exact same sentence is contingent, it really feels like he's either contradicting himself or quietly using "necessary" to mean something different than what everyone assumed going in, just so the rigid designator stuff works out cleanly.

I brought this up with a couple people from my study group and nobody had a great answer, and my TA sort of brushed past it in section without actually engaging with the definitional-truth part of the objection. Is this a real problem with the lecture, or is there some standard move here that I'm just not seeing?
