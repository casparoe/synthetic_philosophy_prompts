I'm a PhD student in psychology working on judgment and decision-making. Our lab runs a forecasting tournament in which members privately estimate the probability that specific published findings will replicate. Two labmates, Elena and Marcus, are still arguing about this week's round: Elena put 70% on a social-priming study replicating, Marcus put 35%, and their Brier scores this year are statistically indistinguishable, so neither has a track-record edge going in.

Elena's view was that since neither of them has any independent reason to think themselves the better forecaster here, both numbers should move toward their average. Marcus said his 35% wasn't a hunch: he'd run a power analysis on the original study, and that shouldn't get diluted just because Elena feels confident. Elena answered that she had her own reasoning too -- a base-rate calculation for how often findings like this one replicate -- and that if 'a reason I find convincing' were enough to justify holding firm, no disagreement could ever rationally move anyone. Marcus replied that this actually cuts the other way: her figure rests on a base rate he never used, and his rests on a calculation she never ran, so they were never working from the same evidence to begin with -- averaging the two numbers would launder that gap rather than close it.

Could you go through the exchange move by move and say who has the better of it at each step, rather than just declaring an overall winner? I'd also like to know whether there's a consideration about responding to a disagreeing peer that both of them are missing.
