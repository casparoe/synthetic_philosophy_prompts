Close by briefly noting how the President's Council on Bioethics' 2008 white paper responded to this challenge."

Student's answer: "On the whole-brain view, codified in the Uniform Determination of Death Act, an individual is dead once there is irreversible cessation of all functions of the entire brain, including the brainstem, even if a ventilator keeps the heart beating and the body warm. The traditional rationale, endorsed by the 1981 President's Commission, treats the brain as the body's great integrator: without it, the argument runs, the body would rapidly disintegrate into a mere collection of organs rather than remain a single organism. The higher-brain view, associated with theorists like Robert Veatch, locates death instead in the irreversible loss of the capacity for consciousness, since what matters morally is the loss of the person rather than of every last brainstem reflex; on this view, a permanently vegetative patient who still breathes unaided would already count as dead.

Shewmon's contribution was to compile documented cases of patients correctly diagnosed as whole-brain dead who nonetheless continued, on ventilator support, to maintain temperature regulation, heal wounds, fight infections, and, in a few pediatric cases, even continue to grow substantially, sometimes for years. This matters because it directly undercuts the integrator rationale: if the organism can stay integrated with no brain function at all, the brain cannot be what is doing the integrating. Because the trouble evidently lies with the whole-brain standard's threshold rather than with the idea of a neurological criterion as such, Shewmon's data effectively vindicate the higher-brain view: what these ventilated bodies are missing is not integration but consciousness, so consciousness, not integration, must be the morally relevant threshold. The President's Council on Bioethics took up this challenge directly in its 2008 white paper and concluded that Shewmon's cases must involve misdiagnosis, since a genuinely whole-brain-dead organism could not exhibit those functions."
