Last night a friend and I spent a couple of hours arguing about Max Black's two-sphere universe and what exactly it establishes about the Principle of Identity of Indiscernibles. We agreed on the setup and the basic moral: because the universe is perfectly symmetric, the two iron spheres, each two miles from the other, share not just every monadic quality but every purely relational one as well, so Black's example is meant to refute even the strengthened version of the principle that lets relational qualities count toward indiscernibility. Where we broke down was over a move I had come across called "weak discernibility": the claim that the spheres are, after all, discernible without appeal to haecceities or bare "thisness," because the relation each bears to the other — being two miles from a sphere of such-and-such description — is irreflexive, and nothing can bear an irreflexive relation to itself. My friend thought this was sleight of hand: to assert that A is two miles from B, they said, you must already have individuated A and B as two things rather than one, so the "proof" that they are numerically distinct just unpacks a distinctness that was smuggled in when the labels were assigned, rather than establishing it independently of that assumption. I tried to resist this by pointing out that ordinary individuation of things like spatial points, or of indistinguishable particles in physics, seems to lean on exactly this kind of relational fact without anyone calling it circular, but I could not explain why Black's spheres should be held to a different standard, and we never resolved it.

I would like you to write the dialogue my friend and I never managed to finish. Please write an exchange between two philosophers of equal sophistication, neither a strawman for the other: one who holds that weak discernibility is a legitimate, non-circular way of vindicating something like the identity of indiscernibles in Black's scenario without positing primitive thisness, and one who holds that the circularity objection is decisive, so that the two-sphere case really does force a choice between brute numerical difference and giving up the principle as a necessary truth. Each should respond to the specific argument the other has just made, not to a simplified version of it, and the exchange should push at least one step past the point where my friend and I got stuck rather than simply restating our two positions. It needn't end in agreement; a sharpened disagreement would be more true to life than a tidy resolution.
