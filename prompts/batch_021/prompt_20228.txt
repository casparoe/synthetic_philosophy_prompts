I've gotten pretty into forecasting stuff over the last year or so — I'm one of those people who forecasts on Manifold and argues with friends about calibration, nothing formal, just for fun. Somebody in a discord for that stuff mentioned "Van Fraassen's Reflection Principle" as basically the reason you're not allowed to flip-flop your forecast for no reason, and once I got the basic idea it kind of stuck with me: if you know for a fact that tomorrow you'll be 90% confident in something, then today, right now, before you've seen whatever it is you're going to see, you should already be around 90% confident too — like your future self counts as an expert witness on your own beliefs and you owe them deference in advance. Seemed almost obviously right at first.

Then I went down a rabbit hole (mostly stanford-encyclopedia-type pages and a couple philosophy blogs) and found theres a whole bunch of "gotcha" cases that are supposed to sink it. Like: I know that if I get properly drunk tonight I'll be 90% sure I'm fine to drive home, but that obviously shouldn't nudge my current sober credence in "I'm fine to drive" up even a little. Or: I know that right after I go under for surgery tomorrow, the anesthesia is going to leave me groggy and 100% sure the operation went perfectly regardless of how it actually goes, so is my current belief about that really supposed to defer in advance to my future doped-up certainty? Feels like a hard no. From what I can tell, the standard fix is to only require deference to future selves who got there by picking up ordinary evidence, not by getting drugged or brainwashed or drunk — but that move smells a little circular to me, like it's just defining "counts as evidence" so as to exclude every case that was ever a problem, which would mean the principle never really gets tested by anything.

So here's what I want: an actual essay, not a "well, some philosophers think this, others think that" summary. Make the strongest case you can that something like Reflection really is binding on rational agents, then make the strongest case that the exception-cases above (or better ones, if you know of some) sink it for good, and then actually tell me where you land. I'd take a hedged answer like "it only holds for changes that come from genuine learning, and here's a principled way to say what that means" way more seriously than a flat yes or no — I just don't want it to end on a shrug.
