Two months ago I joined the reproductive ethics advisory committee at the fertility clinic where my partner and I did our own IVF cycle with preimplantation genetic testing the year before (screening for an unrelated inherited condition on my side of the family). The clinic wanted a patient representative sitting alongside the physicians and the staff bioethicist, and I did not expect to be handed something this difficult so soon.

A couple currently in treatment has several viable embryos. Testing done for an unrelated reason happened to show that two of the embryos carry a gene for a moderate, non-life-threatening skeletal condition affecting height and joint mobility, which runs in the male partner's family; he has the condition himself and is active in an online community of people who share it. Rather than asking us to screen the affected embryos out, as most couples in this situation do, the couple has specifically requested that we transfer one of the affected embryos ahead of the unaffected ones, saying they want a child who will grow up sharing this part of his life and his extended family's history with it rather than feeling like the odd one out at reunions. They raised this preference before testing even began, so nobody on the committee thinks it's a rationalization invented after the fact, and this is likely their last funded cycle, so a denial would effectively end their options with us.

The committee is split roughly down the middle. One group wants to treat the request as asking us to knowingly cause a disability in a future child and is pushing for a flat rule against ever transferring an affected embryo when unaffected ones from the same cycle are available. The other group, including our genetic counselor, argues that this framing quietly assumes something false: that transferring an unaffected embryo instead would spare this couple's child the condition, when it would actually just result in a different child being born, since the embryos are separate individuals from the start. They also point out that we approved elective sex selection for family balancing last year on autonomy grounds and want to know why this case should be treated any more strictly. Because I'm new and not yet identified with either camp, the chair has asked me to draft the memo the committee will vote on this coming Thursday, and I would rather not hand them something that just restates one side's talking points. Please draft that memo: it should recommend, concretely, whether we approve the transfer, deny it, or approve it subject to specific conditions, propose a standard general enough to guide comparable requests in the future, and engage honestly with the strongest argument on whichever side it ultimately doesn't adopt.
