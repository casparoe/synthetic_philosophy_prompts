Most of what I write for a living is about replication crises in psychology and fights over how to regulate machine learning, so I don't often get asked to write about love. But two old friends of mine, Priya and Marcus, both historians of early-twentieth-century philosophy who met in a seminar on the outer circle of the Bloomsbury Group, are getting married next month, and they've asked me — half as a joke I don't think is really a joke — to build their wedding toast around Bertrand Russell's *Marriage and Morals*.

They were very specific that they don't want the version of Russell that gets trotted out in undergraduate lectures, where he's just the "free love" philosopher whose own chaotic personal life supposedly refutes him. They want the actual argument: that possessive jealousy is less a brute instinct than something inflated by property law, anxieties about paternity, and inherited religious guilt, and that what a marriage is really for, once you strip away those historical accretions, is a durable and honest partnership, organized above all around raising children well. They're also entirely comfortable with, and want the toast to reckon with, how that argument sits next to Russell's own record: his second marriage, to Dora Russell, began as a good-faith experiment in exactly the kind of openness his philosophy recommended, and came apart in part because he found he could not extend to her relationship, and eventually two children, with another man, the American journalist Griffin Barry, the equanimity he had spent an entire book insisting reasonable people could achieve.

The trouble is the room. Something like a third of the guests will be philosophers and historians of ideas who will notice immediately if the toast either flattens Russell into a hypocrisy joke or borrows his ideas as decorative name-dropping without engaging their actual content; the rest are family who have never heard of *Marriage and Morals* and, since Priya and Marcus are having an entirely conventional marriage, shouldn't come away thinking the toast is some kind of coded statement about the couple's own arrangement. I'd like a five-minute toast, formal enough for the occasion but warm rather than academic, that uses the real substance of Russell's position — including its friction with his own life — to say something true about what these two are actually committing to, in a way that lands for both halves of the room.
