Here is the question as I set it:

"Consider a six-move centipede game between A and B, who alternate moves starting with A (so A moves at 1, 3, 5 and B moves at 2, 4, 6). At each move the player to act may Take, ending the game, or Pass, continuing it. If the player to move Takes at move k, the resulting payoffs (A, B) are: move 1: (4,1); move 2: (1,6); move 3: (9,2); move 4: (2,14); move 5: (17,4); move 6: (4,22). If both players Pass all six times, the game ends with payoffs (16,16).
(a) Find the subgame-perfect equilibrium by backward induction, and state what your answer implies (if anything) about the uniqueness of equilibrium in this game.
(b) Explain, on behalf of a critic, why some have called backward induction 'paradoxical' rather than merely at odds with experimental behavior — i.e., why the assumptions needed to run the argument are alleged to undermine themselves once a Pass actually occurs.
(c) Does Aumann's (1995) common-knowledge-of-rationality result settle this dispute in backward induction's favor, or does it just relocate the disagreement? Explain briefly."

And here is the student's answer:

"(a) Solving from move 6 backward: B prefers Take (22) to Pass (16), so B takes at move 6. Anticipating this, A at move 5 prefers Take (17) to Pass, which would yield only 4 once B takes next. So A takes at 5. The same logic carries back through moves 4, 3, and 2: at each node the mover prefers Take now to Passing into a continuation where the other player takes next. So the unique subgame-perfect equilibrium has A taking immediately at move 1, for a final payoff of (4,1). And since at every node the Take payoff strictly exceeds what Passing would yield once the other player best-responds, no player can ever profit from deviating regardless of what is specified off the path of play — so this isn't just the unique subgame-perfect equilibrium, it is the unique Nash equilibrium of the game.

(b) The complaint critics raise isn't just that people don't play this way — it's that the reasoning is alleged to eat itself. The whole backward-induction chain is licensed by each player's common knowledge that the other will act rationally at every future node, however far off. But if the game actually reaches move 3, that already shows somebody deviated from the 'rational' plan at move 1 or 2. So the very thing that's supposed to license A's reasoning about what a rational B would do later — common knowledge that B is rational — has, by the time move 3 is reached, already been falsified by the history of play itself. Pettit and Sugden pressed exactly this: common knowledge of rationality does all the work in the backward-induction argument, yet reaching almost any node but the first is itself evidence that this common knowledge has failed, which leaves it unclear what a player is entitled to believe, or why Take is still uniquely rational, once that happens.

(c) Aumann's (1995) result is usually glossed as showing that common knowledge of rationality is actually inconsistent in a game of this length: since the game could only proceed past move 1 if that common knowledge had already broken down, Aumann concluded that the backward-induction outcome is the only one compatible with fully rational players, treating this inconsistency as evidence for Take-immediately rather than against it. On this reading, Aumann settles the dispute in backward induction's favor rather than merely relocating it. Binmore's published response pushes back on exactly this move: an assumption that is only ever consistent with a version of the game that stops at move 1, he argues, was never a realistic idealization of two ordinary, boundedly rational players to begin with, so if anything Aumann has sharpened the original objection in (b) rather than answered it."
