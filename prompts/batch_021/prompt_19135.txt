The question, from an upper-level undergraduate metaethics exam, read as follows:

Expressivist (non-cognitivist) theories in metaethics — emotivism, prescriptivism, and their more sophisticated descendants — hold that moral sentences do not describe facts but instead express the speaker's non-cognitive attitudes, such as approval or disapproval. Peter Geach argued that this view runs into serious trouble once moral sentences are embedded in more complex constructions. Consider the following argument:

(1) Cheating on your taxes is wrong.
(2) If cheating on your taxes is wrong, then talking your accountant into cheating on your taxes is wrong.
(3) Therefore, talking your accountant into cheating on your taxes is wrong.

(a) Explain what the Frege–Geach problem is and why this argument brings it out.
(b) Explain, in your own words, how Simon Blackburn's quasi-realism and Allan Gibbard's norm-expressivism each try to solve the problem.
(c) Which of the two solutions, if either, do you find more successful, and why?

Here is the student's answer, reproduced exactly as submitted:

Expressivism says that when I say "cheating on your taxes is wrong," I'm not describing the world the way a scientist describes an atom — I'm doing something more like venting a certain attitude. For an expressivist like Ayer, to call an act wrong is, in effect, to say "I disapprove of this act" in a slightly disguised form; there's no further moral fact being tracked, just a report of the speaker's own reaction. This is opposed to descriptivist or cognitivist theories, on which moral sentences are ordinary truth-apt claims, about natural or non-natural properties.

The trouble Geach pointed out is that sentence (1) also shows up, unasserted, inside the antecedent of (2). If all "cheating on your taxes is wrong" does is express disapproval, it's hard to see what job it's doing inside (2), since nobody who asserts (2) is thereby expressing disapproval of tax cheating — you can accept the conditional while being personally indifferent to tax fraud, so long as you think there's a general connection between an act being wrong and getting someone else to do it being wrong. But if "cheating on your taxes is wrong" means something different in (1) than it does inside (2), the argument is really trading on an ambiguity, the way "a feather is light; what is light cannot be dark; so a feather cannot be dark" trades on two senses of "light." Yet (1)–(3) is clearly not equivocal in that way; it is a straightforward instance of modus ponens. So expressivists owe us an account of what stays constant across the two occurrences, without simply helping themselves to the cognitivist's answer that both occurrences express the same proposition. This is why the problem is worse for expressivism than for descriptivism: a descriptivist can say that the very same proposition is asserted in (1) and embedded, unasserted, in (2), exactly as with any ordinary factual claim, and ordinary propositional logic goes through untouched.

Blackburn and Gibbard both try to answer this by getting more precise about what noncognitive state is attached to complex sentences like (2), and not just to simple ones like (1). Gibbard's proposal is that accepting (2) is a matter of holding a higher-order attitude: disapproving of the combination of not disapproving of tax cheating together with disapproving of talking your accountant into it. He builds these up into a kind of tree of nested attitudes, so that accepting (1) and (2) while rejecting (3) turns out to be holding a set of attitudes that cannot jointly be satisfied, which is the expressivist's substitute for inconsistency. Blackburn's version instead treats a moral judgment as expressing acceptance of a norm, which he models as a pair of a possible world together with a system of norms; a normative claim rules out certain world-norm pairs in much the way an ordinary factual claim rules out possible worlds, and embedded occurrences then get a systematic value in a way that mirrors possible-worlds semantics for modal claims. Both are meant to show, against Geach, that there is a determinate noncognitive state attached to the complex sentence, so the argument doesn't equivocate after all.

Of the two, I find Blackburn's more satisfying, since tying moral commitment to something as structured as a system of norms makes it easier to see how large-scale moral reasoning could work, whereas the tree-of-attitudes approach seems to need a new, more baroque attitude for every additional logical construction you throw at it — long conjunctions, multiple embeddings, and so on — an objection along these lines is pressed by Mark Schroeder. Still, most commentators think the technical embedding problem has, by now, been given at least a workable solution, even if the further, more explanatory question of whether these attitudes are psychologically real remains contested.
