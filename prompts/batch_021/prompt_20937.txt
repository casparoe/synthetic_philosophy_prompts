For an ethics elective, I've been reading G. E. M. Anscombe's 1956 Oxford speech opposing Harry Truman's honorary degree, later published as the pamphlet "Mr Truman's Degree," where she argues that deliberately killing civilians is murder no matter the consequences. In her later essay "War and Murder," she extends this to nuclear deterrence itself, holding that merely forming the conditional intention to annihilate an enemy's population if deterrence failed is already a moral corruption, whether or not the threat is ever carried out. Anscombe died in 2001, before the U.S. Navy fielded the "low-yield" W76-2 warhead on Trident submarines, a weapon defended as more ethical precisely because its smaller blast allows a limited strike instead of automatic city-annihilation. Reasoning strictly from Anscombe's own distinction between intending and merely foreseeing a death, would she treat a "more discriminating" deterrent like this as a genuine moral improvement, or would she say it rests on exactly the same murderous conditional intention she condemned in Truman's case?
