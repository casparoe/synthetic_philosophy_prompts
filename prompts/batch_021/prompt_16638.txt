I'm about two-thirds into a novel with a side character loosely based on Henry Sidgwick -- the Victorian utilitarian who helped found the Society for Psychical Research and personally investigated mediums for something like twenty-five years despite being a professional sceptic through and through. I mentioned this to my writing group last week and two of them got sidetracked into an argument about whether the guy was actually being rational, and I still can't settle it in my head.

Elena's opening claim was that the whole thing was motivated reasoning wearing a rigor costume: Sidgwick had basically argued in his big ethics book that without immortality, or some moral government of the universe, self-interest and duty can never be fully reconciled, so practical reason is left arguing with itself. Naturally a guy staring down that conclusion spends the next two decades hunting ghosts -- that's not open inquiry, that's a man trying to rescue his own system.

Marcus said that gets the psychology backwards. A motivated believer finds what he needs and stops looking. Sidgwick kept exposing cheating, including in subjects his own circle had initially vouched for, and kept publishing the bad news instead of quietly shelving it. If he needed it to be true and still wouldnt call it, that's evidence he wasn't fooling himself, not evidence that he was.

Elena came back with: fine, but he also never gave the negative verdict. Twenty-five years and he never once published 'there's nothing here, I was wrong to look.' He just kept looking indefinitely, and 'I'll keep searching forever' is exactly what you'd expect from someone who can't accept the answer he's actually getting, not from someone dispassionately following the evidence.

Marcus's reply was that this isn't fair to the shape of the problem: if the phenomenon, supposing it exists at all, is rare and actively hidden by fraud, a long run of null results doesn't earn you a confident 'it doesn't exist' any more than decades of failing to spot a rare particle lets a physicist declare it fictional. Continuing to look isn't bias -- refusing to ever stop would just be the same caution pointed the other way.

Elena's last word was that this supposed even-handedness was awfully one-sided in practice -- he was quick to expose fraud in mediums but never applied anything like that same speed to the possibility that the whole project wasn't worth another year. That asymmetry, she said, is the tell.

Nobody backed down and I told them I'd think about it, which was a lie -- I'm putting it to you instead. Can you go through the exchange move by move, say who's got the better of each point rather than just who wins overall, and flag anything you think both of them are missing?
