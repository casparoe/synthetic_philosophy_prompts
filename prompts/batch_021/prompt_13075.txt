I organize my department's colloquium series, and next month's invited speaker — the capstone event for the graduate seminar on fundamentality I'm teaching this term — defends a version of priority monism: the view, developed out of Jonathan Schaffer's work, that the cosmos as a whole (what she, following the old monist slogan, calls the One) is the single fundamental thing there is, and that every part of it — quarks, tables, us, the Many — exists only derivatively, as something the whole grounds rather than as something that helps compose it. She is a formidable debater with a signature move: whenever someone objects that a fundamental whole must already contain all the complexity of its parts, and so is no more unified than pluralism's swarm of basic particles, she replies that unity doesn't require simplicity, only priority, and moves on before anyone can ask what "priority" is doing there that isn't just restating monism. My students have all worked through Schaffer's papers already, so generic "why do you believe this" questions will bore them and embarrass me.

I'd like eight to ten interview questions for her, numbered, each followed by a short note on the reply she is likely to give and a sharper follow-up keyed to that reply. At least one question should press on whether her view simply presupposes that the world has a maximal whole to serve as the fundamental One, given that she has elsewhere taken so-called junky worlds — ones with no all-encompassing whole, where every object is a proper part of some larger one — seriously as a live metaphysical possibility. At least one should test whether her constraint on which decompositions of the One into the Many are admissible (something like a tiling requirement, that the Many must jointly and exactly cover the One, with no gaps or overlaps) actually explains why the parts have the properties they do, or just redescribes the pluralist's picture in grounding vocabulary. And at least one should ask what becomes of her argument from quantum entanglement if some future physics turned out to be fully separable after all — whether she takes monism to be a contingent upshot of how physics happens to have turned out, or a necessary truth that the entanglement evidence merely illustrates. Close with a question that asks her to specify, concretely, what she would need to see to give up the One for the Many.
