I don't have any formal background in philosophy or decision theory, but I follow a fair amount of AI-safety-adjacent writing, and a disagreement broke out between two coworkers of mine (both ML engineers) at lunch that I haven't been able to settle. It grew out of a claim that keeps coming up in things we read: that 'coherence arguments' force any sufficiently capable agent toward expected-utility maximization, and therefore toward having some fixed goal it pursues ruthlessly. Roughly, here's how the exchange went:

(1) He said the argument is basically airtight: if an agent's preferences ever let it be walked in a loop back to where it started while paying something at every step along the way (a 'money pump'), it is leaking resources for nothing, so any agent capable of noticing and correcting this will end up behaving exactly like an expected-utility maximizer. That's why he thinks it's wishful thinking to hope a sufficiently capable AI won't have 'real' goals.

(2) She replied that 'behaves like an expected-utility maximizer' is close to an empty claim, since literally any sequence of choices can be rationalized after the fact by some utility function or other -- even a thermostat, or a rock that never does anything, technically 'maximizes' something -- so showing that an agent can't be pumped doesn't yet show it's chasing any particular real-world objective in the sense that actually worries people.

(3) He answered that this is fair enough for a rock, which never faces an actual trade-off, but not for an agent that keeps making sequential choices as circumstances change: for such an agent, the utility function that rationalizes its choices has to stay fixed across all of them on pain of exploitability, and that's a substantive constraint, not a vacuous one.

(4) She said that's fine, but all that rules out is reversing a comparison the agent has already committed to; it doesn't rule out the agent having large swaths of options it simply never ranks against one another, plus a policy of refusing any trade that would return it to its starting point at a loss. That's enough to dodge every money pump without ever amounting to a complete preference ordering, let alone a fixed goal -- so the argument still doesn't get you from 'can't be exploited' to 'will act like a maximizer of some fixed objective.'
