Over the past year I've been sitting with something that recently acquired a technical name I wasn't expecting: "divine hiddenness." I was raised Catholic, was confirmed, have never seriously doubted that some form of theism is true — and yet since my mother's death last spring, prayer has gone from feeling like a conversation to feeling like leaving messages nobody checks. The term surfaced a few weeks ago in a philosophy of religion seminar I'm taking, when we read J.L. Schellenberg's argument that a perfectly loving God would not permit "nonresistant nonbelief" to occur. Something about it stopped me short, because the argument is explicitly about people who don't believe at all, and I still believe — I just feel cut off from the object of that belief. Trying to work out whether that distinction is as clean as it first looks turned into the seminar paper below, which is due Friday, and I'd value your comments before I hand it in.

I'd rather you be genuinely critical than encouraging: tell me where I've flattened a position into a caricature, where the central move doesn't actually hold up, and whether the closing paragraph should be cut. This is closer to notes than a finished essay, but it has the shape of one:

1. Schellenberg's argument, as I understand it: if God exists, God is perfectly loving; a perfectly loving God, wanting personal relationship with every capable creature, would see to it that none of them fails to believe God exists through no fault of their own — that "nonresistant nonbelief" simply does not occur; but such nonbelief plainly does occur; so no perfectly loving God exists, and hence no God of the traditional sort exists. The whole weight of the argument sits on that second premise: the claim that this kind of love requires making belief available, not merely sustaining a relationship once belief is already secured.

2. What I want to press on is a move I recall from Michael Rea's book on hiddenness (I may be misremembering the details, so flag it if I've got him wrong). Rea treats Schellenberg's argument — which is about the nonbeliever — as one problem, and the believer's felt experience of God's absence (the lament psalms, John of the Cross's "dark night," Mother Teresa's decades of private darkness despite unwavering practice) as a second, existential problem, handled separately, mostly by analogy with ordinary loving relationships that survive silence and withdrawal. My question is whether that division is as clean as it needs to be for the two treatments to work independently, or whether both problems are actually pressure on the same underlying premise from two directions at once.

3. The standard replies to Schellenberg — skeptical theism (we aren't positioned to know God's reasons for permitting this) and the soul-making or epistemic-distance replies associated with Hick, Swinburne, and Murray (too much evidential pressure would crowd out free, uncoerced response) — are pitched at explaining why belief might reasonably be withheld from the nonresistant nonbeliever. Moser's alternative is pitched differently: what's withheld isn't primarily evidence a neutral spectator could assess, but a kind of knowledge — he has a specific term for it, "filial" something, though I'm not certain I have the label exactly right — available only on the far side of a will already willing to be transformed rather than merely informed. My tentative claim is that Moser's account, unlike the other two, extends fairly naturally to my own situation, because it was never really about clearing an evidence threshold in the first place. But I don't think the essay has reckoned with the cost of that extension: it implies that ongoing felt absence in someone already believing and practicing is itself diagnostic of insufficient surrender, which is a fairly brutal thing to imply about a person's grief.

4. (Possibly cut) I don't have a tidy conclusion. If Moser is right in the way I've described him, hiddenness stops being a puzzle to be solved before belief and becomes a feature of belief that keeps getting renegotiated — which is either the most honest thing I've read on this all year, or a way of making the position unfalsifiable from the inside. I'd rather the paper admit that uncertainty than resolve it artificially.

Specifically: is premise 2 of Schellenberg's argument as strong as I've made it sound, have I been fair to skeptical theism and to Moser, and does the link I'm drawing between the two "hiddenness" problems amount to a real philosophical point or just a conflation I'm motivated to believe?
