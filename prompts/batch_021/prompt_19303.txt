I spent my working life as a physicist, mostly on problems in statistical mechanics, and in retirement I've been teaching myself decision theory -- which is how I stumbled on a dispute that borrows my own field's vocabulary to challenge a piece of economic orthodoxy. The Kelly criterion recommends maximizing expected log-wealth rather than expected wealth when facing repeated favorable gambles; a simple illustration is a fair coin-flip that multiplies your wealth by 1.5 on heads and 0.5 on tails, which leaves expected wealth unchanged forever while driving almost every actual trajectory to ruin. Ole Peters and the "ergodicity economics" literature use examples like this to argue for something considerably stronger than Kelly himself claimed: that maximizing long-run geometric growth is not merely one defensible preference among others, but the objectively correct standard of rational choice whenever a process's time average and its ensemble average diverge, quite apart from anyone's attitude to risk. This revives, in sharper form, the objection Samuelson pressed for decades against growth-optimal investing: that almost-sure dominance "in the long run" is not the same as expected-utility dominance, and that privileging log-wealth still just smuggles in one utility function among the many a rational agent could hold. Could you write an essay that reconstructs this dispute with some precision and then defends a definite verdict on whether "geometric rationality" really does displace expected-value maximization as the objectively correct standard for this class of problems, rather than retreating to "it depends on your utility function" -- a qualified verdict is fine (e.g., restricted to a specific class of gambles), provided you commit to it and argue for it.
