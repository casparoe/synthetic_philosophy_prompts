I'm the secretary of my school's Honor Council, a group of three student members (plus a non-voting faculty advisor) that hears academic integrity cases and decides on sanctions. I don't get a vote myself; I just keep the minutes, and that's how I ended up in the middle of an argument between two of the actual voting members, Marcus and Priya, that I haven't been able to settle in my head since. The case we heard involved a copied lab report, and our handbook says a student can only be expelled if the Council finds both (a) that the student plagiarized, and (b) that the student had already received a prior formal warning for a separate academic integrity violation earlier in the year. Privately, the three members' views were all different: Jenna thought both (a) and (b) were true, so her own verdict was "expel"; Marcus thought (a) was true but (b) was false, so his own verdict was "don't expel"; and Priya thought (a) was false but (b) was true, so hers was "don't expel" too. Our bylaws require us to vote on (a) and (b) separately rather than asking each member for an overall yes-or-no, so that's what we did: (a) passed 2-1 (Jenna and Marcus voting yes, Priya no), and (b) also passed 2-1 (Jenna and Priya voting yes, Marcus no) -- which meant that, by the handbook's own rule, the official result was "expel," even though, verdict for verdict, two of the three members had personally said "don't expel."

Walking out to the parking lot afterward, Marcus and Priya kept arguing about it, and I wrote down the gist because it bothered me that I couldn't tell who was getting the better of it. (1) Marcus said the whole point of having three people decide is to find out what the Council as a body actually thinks, and since a plain majority said "don't expel" when asked for their real verdict, overruling that with a question-by-question tally produces a result that isn't really anyone's judgment -- just an artifact of how the vote happened to be sliced up. (2) Priya said that gets the handbook backwards: the rule is written as a conjunction of two specific factual findings, and the Council's actual job is to find those facts, not report a gut feeling about the punishment, so voting issue-by-issue is the only way to really apply the rule instead of letting members quietly smuggle in their own view of what the sanction should be. (3) Marcus came back with this: notice that Jenna is the only person on the Council who actually believes both (a) and (b) together -- Priya only signed onto (b), he only signed onto (a) -- so the "majority" behind each half of the rule is stitched together out of different people each time, and nobody except Jenna actually holds the combination of beliefs that supposedly commands majority support. (4) Priya answered that this is true of just about any decision made question-by-question -- majorities on separate questions almost never line up with any single voter's full set of views, and that's simply unavoidable when you combine opinions -- and that it's not obviously worse than her alternative, because if the Council instead let each member vote their overall gut sense, the actual content of the rule becomes optional, and whoever gets to decide whether the Council votes issue-by-issue or all-at-once effectively decides the case in advance, which struck her as the much bigger problem.

This whole thing has been bugging me partly because it reminds me of something we covered in AP Government, about legislatures sometimes amending bills clause-by-clause instead of voting the whole package up or down, though I'm honestly not sure that's the same issue or just a surface resemblance. Could you go through Marcus and Priya's exchange move by move? Then, all things considered, tell me whether the Council's official "expel" finding should actually stand, whether Marcus has a real complaint about it, and whether there's some important consideration neither of them raised. If you think there's a cleaner way to run this kind of vote that sidesteps the whole problem, I'd be curious to hear that too.
