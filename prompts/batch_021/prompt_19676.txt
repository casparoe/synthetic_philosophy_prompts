I don't have any background in philosophy -- I play viola in a community orchestra, and I first got curious about Schopenhauer after reading how completely Wagner had been won over by him. Over the past year I've worked through *The World as Will and Representation* on my own, and the treatment of music in Book Three, section 52, together with the supplementary chapter on music in the second volume, is the part I keep coming back to, because I've never been able to settle for myself whether it actually holds together.

The headline claim, as far as I can tell, is that every other art -- architecture, sculpture, painting, even tragedy -- presents the will only indirectly, by copying the Platonic Ideas that are themselves the will's first objectification, whereas music alone dispenses with the Ideas altogether and counts as an equally direct objectification of the will, on a level with the Ideas rather than one step beneath them. That is supposed to explain why one and the same piece of music can underlie almost any text, action, or mood without strain: it never depicts a particular grief or triumph, only the inner, universal nature that any grief or triumph shares, so it is never tied to one Idea rather than another. Yet when Schopenhauer actually explains how music pulls this off, he gets remarkably specific: the bass voice, he says, corresponds to the lowest, unorganized grade of nature, the middle voices to the intervening plant and animal kingdoms, and the melody -- the single connected, wandering top voice -- to man's self-conscious, deliberating will, while suspended dissonance and its resolution figure desire and satisfaction, with the endless deferral of a final cadence figuring the endlessness of human striving. That four-part correspondence looks to me like exactly the graded, content-specific mapping onto the Ideas -- mineral, plant, animal, human -- that the claim of music as a direct copy of the will, prior to and independent of the Ideas, was supposed to rule out.

Please argue for the position that these two halves of Schopenhauer's account of music are in genuine tension, and that the four-voice correspondence cannot be rescued simply by calling it an illustration rather than a representational claim. I'd like the strongest version of this argument you can construct, one that engages with the actual structure of the correspondence rather than summarizing his philosophy of music in general terms, and that takes seriously -- and then answers -- the most obvious reply available to him: that the mapping is offered only as an aid to the reader's understanding of music's effect, not as a claim about what music, on his own official terms, represents.
