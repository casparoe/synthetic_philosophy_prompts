My aunt Thandeka taught African philosophy for over thirty years, first at a university in South Africa and then, after she emigrated, at a small liberal arts college in the US, and the debate she cared about most — the one she would happily derail Christmas dinner to relitigate — was the disagreement between Ifeanyi Menkiti and Kwame Gyekye over what "a person is a person through other persons" actually commits you to. She sided with Menkiti: personhood, on her view, is not a fixed metaphysical fact you either have or lack from birth, but something achieved in stages, through moral maturation and progressive incorporation into community, such that the community doesn't merely recognize an already-complete person but partly constitutes personhood as such. She also took seriously the obvious objection, associated with Gyekye and others, that an achievement view like this seems to put infants, and more uncomfortably, anyone who loses the capacities that let them participate in communal life, at risk of counting as lesser persons. Her own answer, worked out over several papers, was that the community doesn't only certify the personhood of those who can hold up their end of the relation; it can also carry someone's personhood for them once they can no longer reciprocate, much as it already does for infants.

She spent her final three years in a memory-care facility, unable to reliably recognize her own children, after a fast-moving dementia. My cousin and I are both speaking at the memorial next week, and I've been stuck on mine, because the room will hold people who have never heard of Menkiti and a handful of her former doctoral students who will absolutely notice if I flatten her actual, fairly technical position into the greeting-card version of ubuntu, or worse, into something that quietly implies she "wasn't really there" for those last three years — exactly the implication her own scholarship spent decades trying to close off. I've read enough Overcoming Bias, Less Wrong, and Parfit at this point to be entirely comfortable with the idea that questions about what personhood consists in can be genuinely unsettled rather than just a matter of tact, which is probably why I can't bring myself to paper over it with something generic.

Could you write the eulogy for me? Something in the five-to-seven-minute range, built out of a couple of small, human details rather than a list of accomplishments — she was a cheerfully terrible singer who never once let that stop her, and took in two foster children in her forties alongside her own — but resting, underneath, on the relational view of personhood she actually held, in a way that does the real work of comforting a mixed room rather than sitting on top of the eulogy as decoration.
