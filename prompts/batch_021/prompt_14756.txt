I'm reporting a long-form piece on the philosophy of moral responsibility, and my research has carried me past the point where most popular treatments of the subject stop. Every accessible account of Frankfurt-style cases begins the same way: Black, a neuroscientist, wants Jones to go through with shooting the senator and will intervene only if Jones shows signs of changing his mind; since Jones goes ahead entirely on his own, he seems fully responsible even though, thanks to Black, he couldn't have done otherwise. That much I can put in a sentence for readers. What I can't yet summarize confidently is the argument specialists are still having about it, which turns out to hinge on one very load-bearing detail: the "prior sign" Black is supposedly watching for. From my interviews, I gather that defenders of the principle of alternative possibilities press a dilemma here -- either the sign deterministically guarantees Jones's choice, in which case the whole scenario just smuggles in determinism and begs the question against them, or it doesn't, in which case there's some window, however thin, in which Jones could have done otherwise, restoring exactly the "flicker of freedom" the case was supposed to rule out. I understand that a newer family of "blockage" cases tries to dispense with the prior sign altogether, and that committed Frankfurtians think this finally shuts the door, while the other side maintains it only relocates the same dilemma to wherever indeterminism now enters the story.

I'd like help rendering that dispute the way it actually feels to the people having it, rather than in the flattened, textbook-summary form. Could you write a dialogue between a philosopher who still defends the principle of alternative possibilities by way of that dilemma, invoking the flicker-of-freedom point where it's actually needed, and a semi-compatibilist who thinks guidance control -- not the ability to do otherwise -- is what responsibility requires, with the two of them working through a specific blockage-style case in real time? Neither should be allowed to simply restate their opening position: each move should respond to what the other has just said, and the semi-compatibilist in particular shouldn't get to win by stipulating the case away if there's a serious reply available. If it helps to picture them somewhere, imagine the two of them cornered at a conference reception, unable to let it drop even after everyone else has gone up to bed.
