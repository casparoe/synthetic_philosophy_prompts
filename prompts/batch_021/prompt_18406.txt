I need an outside perspective on something that happened on my apartment co-op board (I'm treasurer this year, still not totally sure how that happened). We've got a recurring water infiltration problem hitting about six units unevenly, and the board brought in a structural engineer who did a full assessment and came back with a pretty specific, technical remediation plan - rerouting drainage, redoing waterproofing on particular units, that sort of thing. Two board members who actually work in construction argued we should just ratify the plan at next week's meeting and get moving: the engineer already crunched the numbers, none of the rest of us knows anything about drainage systems, and an open floor discussion would just be a bunch of uninformed people arguing past each other for months while the damage gets worse. I pushed back and said we should hold at least one open meeting with the affected residents before voting, since the plan involves cutting into some units way more than others and there's already tension brewing over who should absorb the cost. But honestly, in the moment I couldn't really say why that mattered more than just implementing the correct fix quickly, especially since I don't even disagree that the engineer probably knows best on the technical merits.

A friend in the building who's an econ postdoc told me afterwards that I was being sentimental about "process" for its own sake, and that inviting non-experts to weigh in on a technical question just adds noise and delay without improving the outcome. Here's the embarassing part: I'm doing a PhD in political theory, so you'd think I'd have a ready answer for exactly this kind of situation, but everything I've read on the subject suddenly feels weirdly hard to apply directly. So - is my friend basically right that the open meeting is wasted motion dressed up as principle, or is there something genuine that gets lost if we skip straight from the expert's diagnosis to implementation?
