After forty years as a physicist working mostly on nonlinear dynamics, I retired three years ago and have since been attending a small philosophy discussion circle that meets monthly at our local library, currently by video call. We spent the autumn term on free will, and I found myself unexpectedly gripped by Galen Strawson's Basic Argument against moral responsibility: something about tracing responsibility back through a regress of prior character formation felt uncannily like tracing a physical system back through its initial conditions, except that here the chain is supposed to make room for something our ordinary causal picture has no place for. Next month our circle is hosting, for a recorded conversation, a philosopher whose recent book defends the Basic Argument in close to its original form, and since I am the one who lobbied for the topic, I have been handed the job of drafting the questions.

I do not want to spend the hour having her simply restate the argument's steps, so I would like eight to ten questions that push on where I take the real pressure points to lie. At least one should test whether the regress is genuinely vicious, given compatibilist replies that try to block it at the "you must be responsible for the way you are" premise rather than deny that self-creation is impossible -- Frankfurt-style mesh theories, say, or Fischer and Ravizza's idea that an agent can come to "take responsibility" for a character she did not originally choose. At least one should address her insistence that the argument goes through whatever the truth about determinism, including what she says to a libertarian who thinks some form of agent-causation breaks the regress rather than merely relocating it. And at least one should press on the distinction between "true" or "ultimate" responsibility and the everyday, desert-light responsibility compatibilists are happy to grant -- I want to know whether that distinction is doing real philosophical work or is simply a stipulation that makes the argument true by definition. For each of these three, please add a natural follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give, so the conversation does not let her off with the first reply. Finally, I would like a question or two connecting the argument to the practical unavoidability of the reactive attitudes -- guilt, resentment, gratitude -- given that her position, like that of P. F. Strawson before her, seems to be that we cannot simply reason our way out of these attitudes even once we accept the argument; I want to know whether she thinks that concession leaves the Basic Argument practically idle however sound it may be, and what, if anything, she thinks should change in how we punish, praise, or blame people if we took it fully to heart.
