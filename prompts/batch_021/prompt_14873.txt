I'm a senior backend engineer, and I sit on my company's Incident Review Board -- the small group that has to sign off on the official "Root Cause" field in every postmortem after a major incident, which in turn determines which team gets assigned mandatory remediation work and, less officially, which team looks bad in front of the VPs. Last month, during a flash-sale traffic spike, our checkout service went down for forty minutes. It turned out two independent problems were live at the same time: the Gateway team had disabled a rate limiter on the checkout API during a routine migration and never turned it back on, and, separately, the Platform team had a slow connection-pool leak in a shared caching layer they'd shipped weeks earlier. Our SRE group replayed that day's real traffic against a staging replica twice -- once with only the rate-limiter problem present and the pool bug fixed, and once with only the pool bug present and the limiter intact -- and both replays independently reproduced the full outage.

That finding has produced a genuine deadlock, and the disagreement has turned out to be less technical than I expected going in. Gateway's lead argues their misconfiguration wasn't "the" cause, since the replay proves the outage would have happened even if they'd never touched the limiter; Platform's lead makes the exact mirror argument about the pool bug. Both are using the same "would it still have happened without X" logic, and by that logic nobody caused an outage that very much happened and very much needs fixing on both sides. Our data-science lead, who's been on a Judea Pearl kick lately, thinks the fix is to stop asking the "would it still have happened" question and instead ask which single change we could have intervened on to prevent the outage -- but when I tried actually running that test myself, holding everything else exactly as it really was, I got the same non-answer for both teams, which makes me suspect the "just intervene on one thing" framing is missing some step. A third camp wants to just declare both teams jointly responsible and move on, which feels intuitively right here but seems ad hoc, and I'm not sure it survives contact with the next incident, where the two factors won't be so cleanly symmetrical.

I have no formal background in philosophy, but I'm now fairly convinced we're arguing about what "cause" means rather than about any remaining fact of the incident, and I don't trust myself to referee that. Our postmortem template only has room for a single "Root Cause" field, and the write-up is scheduled to go out company-wide, with leadership cc'ed, next Wednesday, with next quarter's remediation budget riding on it -- so the board needs a position we can actually defend in writing, not just a vibe. Could you draft the memo I'll circulate before Tuesday's board meeting? It should recommend, concretely, how this specific incident's causation should be written up -- including whether the template itself needs to change to allow more than one cause -- and it should propose a general rule the board can reuse the next time this happens, rather than deciding it ad hoc all over again. Please don't just split the difference: tell me which way of thinking about causation the board should actually adopt, and be honest about the strongest objection to it, since I'd rather hear that from you now than get blindsided by it in the room.
