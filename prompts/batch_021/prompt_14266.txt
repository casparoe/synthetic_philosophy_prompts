I've been slowly working my way through a secondhand book on the history of astronomy, and one thing about Johannes Kepler keeps nagging at me. Every account treats him as the man who gave us the laws of planetary motion and helped bury the old earth-centered picture of the cosmos, then almost apologetically adds, as a kind of footnote, that he also cast horoscopes for a living and wrote entire treatises defending astrology, with titles like Tertius Interveniens and De Fundamentis Astrologiae Certioribus. None of the books I've come across really explain what he thought he was doing there, beyond the throwaway line that he needed the money, which strikes me as too easy an answer for someone who spent that much effort on the subject.

What I would really like is a proper comparison between Kepler's own, reformed astrology and the mainstream Ptolemaic astrology of his day: the kind organized around the twelve zodiac signs, the astrological houses, and detailed predictions about a person's character and fate drawn from their birth chart. From what I can piece together, Kepler rejected most of that apparatus as groundless, yet still held that the angles, or "aspects," between planets as seen from earth had some genuine effect on the weather and on human affairs, connected to his wider theory of cosmic harmony, the very same mathematical ratios he thought explained musical consonance, and even to the idea that the earth itself possesses a kind of soul that senses these harmonies. Could you set out, on Kepler's own terms, exactly what he preserved from the older astrology, what he discarded, and why he drew the line precisely where he did? And once that is clear, I would value your own judgment on the matter: does what remains still deserve to be called "astrology" in any meaningful sense, or had Kepler, in practice, worked out a different theory altogether, one that kept the old name only out of caution or habit?
