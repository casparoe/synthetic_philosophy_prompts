So this is a dumb family thing but it's been rattling around in my head all week and I think there's an actual question buried in it. Every Christmas Eve my extended family plays the gift-stealing game -- White Elephant, Yankee Swap, whatever your family calls it, where once a gift's been opened anyone can steal it, and whoever it gets stolen from either steals someone else's or opens a new one. We've played it the same way for something like fifteen years and never once written a rule down anywhere. Nobody's ever disagreed about a call in the moment, we just all "know" how it works.

This year, for the first time ever, my cousin's gift got stolen, then stolen right back by the person she'd taken it from, then stolen back again -- three steals in a row between the same two people -- and the whole table split down the middle. Half the family is positive the rule has always been "you can't steal a gift back immediately from whoever just took it from you," the other half (me included, apparently) is just as positive it's always been "any opened gift is fair game, no restrictions, thats the whole point." Nobody's lying and nobody's misremembering some past Christmas, because this exact situation has genuinely never come up before -- every single game we've ever played is completely consistent with both versions of "the rule," they only pull apart on this one case. My uncle, who's a lawyer, says there's obviously a correct answer and we just need to work out what we all actually meant this whole time, the same way you'd find an implied term in a contract.

I'm not so sure, and it's bugging me way more than a party game should. I already tried explaining the whole thing to my partner, who just said "this is exactly why I don't play games with your family" and went to bed, so now I'm bringing it to you instead. The longer I sit with it, the less it feels like we're recovering something we all forgot, and the more it feels like we're about to decide something for the first time and then dress it up as memory. I cover science and ideas for a living, which makes it extra annoying that I can't work this out myself: is my uncle right that there's a fact buried somewhere in fifteen years of Christmases waiting to be dug up, or does the whole idea of "what the rule really meant all along" quietly stop making sense once a case like this actually happens? Who's actually right, him or me, and how would we even settle it before next Christmas?
