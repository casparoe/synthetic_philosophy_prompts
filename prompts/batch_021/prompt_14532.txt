So I've been rereading the bit in Franklin's autobiography where he lays out his whole "project for arriving at moral perfection" — the 13 virtues, the little book with a page for each one where he'd put a black mark every time he slipped that day, doing one virtue at a time on a weekly rotation so he cycled through the whole list four times a year. This came up because a friend of mine is way into habit-tracking apps and I showed her that section as basically the OG version of what she's doing, except Franklin is also kind of wierdly self-aware about the whole project — he only adds Humility to the list after a friend tells him he's generally thought proud, then jokes that even if he beat every other fault he'd probably end up proud of his humility. And by the end he admits he never got close to perfect (Order in particular seems to have beaten him for good), but decides he was a better and happier man for having tried anyway, comparing himself to someone whose handwriting never matches the fancy copybook examples they're practicing from but improves from the attempt regardless.

What I can't stop thinking about is how he'd react to two things that obviously didn't exist in his day. First, there's now a whole body of psychology research (self-compassion people like Kristin Neff, plus a lot of the habit-formation literature) arguing that exactly his method — daily self-surveillance, tallying your own lapses, treating slip-ups as failures — tends to backfire, since shame and self-criticism erode motivation rather than sustain it, and that people actually stick to change better when they're gentler with themselves. Given that Franklin's own conclusion already sounds pretty forgiving of himself, would he take that research as basically vindicating the gentler reading of his project, or would he think it's soft modern stuff that ignores that his method got him real results? Second, what do you think he'd make of an app like Habitica, which turns the exact same virtue-tracking idea into an RPG with points and levels — is that just his system with better UX, or does gamifying it lose something he'd have thought actually mattered?
