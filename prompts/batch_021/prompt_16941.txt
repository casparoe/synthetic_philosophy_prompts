I sit on my university's Committee on Academic Freedom and Responsibility, which has been asked to resolve a complaint before it escalates to the provost's office. I need a draft of the decision memo to circulate before we meet this Thursday.

A lecturer teaching a graduate seminar on the semantics and pragmatics of slurs wrote two of them out in full on the whiteboard, unredacted, to make a point within the very debate the seminar covers: that a slur's derogatory force survives even a clearly flagged, non-endorsing, third-person mention, unlike an ordinary conversational implicature, which can be cancelled by explicit context. Several students complained that hearing and seeing the words caused the same injury as an ordinary use, and that "it was only a mention" is cold comfort to a listener who cannot verify the speaker's intentions in real time. Roughly half the committee wants to dismiss the complaint outright, since treating mention as equivalent to use would make it impossible to teach philosophy of language, literature, or law honestly; the other half wants to uphold it, since whatever the semantics, some word-forms are close to socially unmentionable regardless of framing, and the lecturer could have made the identical theoretical point by redacting the words or spelling them only by initial.

Please draft the memo: recommend that the committee uphold the complaint, dismiss it, or dismiss it with specific guidance for future teaching; propose a standard the department could actually apply next time; and give the strongest form of the losing side's argument the weight it deserves rather than gesturing at it.
