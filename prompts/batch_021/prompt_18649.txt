A. J. Ayer spent his career defending a verificationist theory of meaning on which claims about the soul surviving bodily death are not merely unconfirmed but strictly meaningless, since no observation could count for or against them. In 1988, while hospitalized, he choked on a piece of smoked salmon, went into cardiac arrest, and was without a heartbeat for several minutes before being resuscitated. He later described what happened to him in an essay pointedly titled "What I Saw When I Was Dead": a painfully bright red light that he took to be governing the universe, and two of its ministers, charged with the upkeep of space, who had recently bungled the job and left it to him to put things right. Ayer never renounced his atheism, and he insisted that the episode was no more than a material brain flickering back into activity, not evidence that anything survives bodily death; yet he also conceded, in print, that it had somewhat weakened his confidence that his own approaching death would truly be the end of him, even as he went on hoping that it would be.

Write a short story that dramatizes this exact predicament rather than resolving it. You may write about Ayer himself or invent a philosopher of comparable temperament and commitments, but the character's verificationism should emerge from the encounter shaken rather than either confirmed or overturned, so that the story's tension comes from the gap between what can be verified and what has, unverifiably, been felt.
