As a journalist who writes about science and ideas, I've spent the last few months reporting on the "disease model" of addiction and on diminished-capacity defenses in criminal court, and I keep running into the same unacknowledged philosophical move: the claim that a wrongdoer should be met not with blame but with treatment, management, or cure. That move is essentially P. F. Strawson's distinction, in "Freedom and Resentment," between excuses and exemptions. Excuses ("he didn't mean to," "he was pushed," "he didn't know") concern a particular act and leave the agent fully inside the circle of people we hold responsible; exemptions ("he's a hopeless psychopath," "he's only a child," "he was acting under post-hypnotic suggestion") concern the agent as a whole and shift us into what Strawson called the objective attitude, where resentment and indignation give way to something closer to clinical management. I'd like an example that shows this tidy-looking distinction is less stable than it appears — describe one specific, realistically detailed case of wrongdoing, rooted in something like compulsive behavior, addiction, or a personality disorder, where the very facts that constitute the strongest possible excuse for the act are, on Strawson's own criteria, exactly the facts that would justify exempting the person altogether. Then explain precisely where, in his own terms, the case forces excuse and exemption to collapse into each other rather than yielding two different verdicts.
