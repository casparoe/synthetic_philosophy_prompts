I am a novelist a little more than halfway through a book whose central figure, a paintings conservator named Ingrid, spends the better part of a year authenticating a small ink drawing. On grounds of paper, ink, and pentimenti she can defend line by line, she becomes convinced it is an unrecorded study by a major Northern Renaissance master -- the very attribution her mentor had been pursuing when he died. Just before she is due to announce the finding, the museum's own conservation-science unit shows her an internal study indicating that conservators who commit to an attribution within a year of losing a mentor in the field have a measurably elevated rate of false positives, with their confidence no lower than anyone else's. She spends that evening going back through every plank of her argument and still cannot find the flaw.

I recognize this as my own amateur version of a higher-order evidence case, the kind that produces what epistemologists call epistemic akrasia: believing something at the first order while also believing, at the second, that this is not what your evidence actually supports. I want to say Christensen and Horowitz are among the philosophers who have written on it, though I have only read around its edges. What I have not been able to settle, and need to settle before I can write Ingrid's reasoning that evening rather than just summarize it as "she had doubts," is whether there is any stable rational position for her to occupy. If she keeps believing the attribution while also believing, on the strength of the study, that this is not what her evidence supports, that looks like the textbook akratic combination that most of the literature treats as a mark of irrationality rather than a real option. But neither tidy alternative looks much better: revising the attribution purely because of a statistic about people in her situation, without locating any actual defect in her reasoning, seems to let a fact about a reference class override a specific, checkable argument; discounting the study instead, so as to keep her confidence and her sense of its rationality in line, looks suspiciously like the self-immunizing move we distrust in anyone who says "I have checked my work" no matter what they are told about their own reliability.

Could you write me an essay -- not a scene, not dialogue, but the kind of argumentative essay I would write for myself if I had the training -- that takes on this question directly: is it ever rational to remain knowingly akratic in a case like this, or does rationality require bringing the two levels into agreement, and if the latter, in which direction? I would like it to make the strongest case for each of the three responses I have sketched -- stay akratic, defer to the higher-order evidence, discount the higher-order evidence -- and then actually settle on one of them as the right general policy, with an argument for why the other two cost more than they save. A conclusion that reasonable people simply disagree would be the one outcome that leaves me no better equipped to write the scene.
