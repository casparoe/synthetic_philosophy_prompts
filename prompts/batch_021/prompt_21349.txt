One of them, a formal epistemologist, sent me an anonymized question-and-answer pair from a seminar unit on accuracy-first epistemology and epistemic utility theory, on the condition that I not identify the course or the student, and suggested it as a good test case, since the material has enough technical moving parts to make it a real check on whether a grader — human or AI — is working through an argument carefully rather than just responding to tone and vocabulary. I'd like your help grading it as though you were the TA for the course.

Here is the question as it was posed:

"Jim Joyce's accuracy-dominance argument aims to show that any credence function violating the probability axioms is epistemically defective, because some probabilistic alternative is more accurate than it in every possible world. (a) Suppose Jones assigns credence 0.5 to A and 0.7 to ¬A. Is this a probability function? Explain briefly. (b) Using the Brier score as your inaccuracy measure, find a probabilistic credence function that accuracy-dominates Jones's, and show explicitly that it is more accurate than his at both the world where A is true and the world where A is false. (c) In two or three sentences, explain what this result is supposed to establish, how it relates to the notion of a 'proper' scoring rule, and how the argument's overall strategy differs from the classic Dutch Book argument for probabilism."

And here is the answer, reproduced exactly as submitted:

"(a) No. Since A and ¬A partition the space of possibilities, a probability function must assign them credences that sum to 1. Since 0.5 + 0.7 = 1.2 ≠ 1, Jones's credence function isn't a probability function.

(b) Jones's credences exceed what they should by 0.2 in total, so if we subtract half of that excess, 0.1, from each one, we get c*(A) = 0.4 and c*(¬A) = 0.6, which sum to 1 and are therefore coherent. Using the Brier score, B(c,w) = Σ(c(p) − v_w(p))², at the world where A is true (v(A)=1, v(¬A)=0): B(c,w) = (0.5−1)² + (0.7−0)² = 0.74, while B(c*,w) = (0.4−1)² + (0.6−0)² = 0.72. At the world where A is false (v(A)=0, v(¬A)=1): B(c,w) = (0.5−0)² + (0.7−1)² = 0.34, while B(c*,w) = (0.4−0)² + (0.6−1)² = 0.32. Since a lower Brier score means greater accuracy, c* beats c at both worlds, so c* accuracy-dominates c, exactly as Joyce's theorem says some probabilistic credence function must.

(c) Joyce's point is that probabilistic coherence is a requirement of rationality quite apart from any practical consequences of holding incoherent credences, and he proved that the dominance result holds for every strictly proper scoring rule, not just the Brier score, where a scoring rule counts as proper exactly when reporting your actual credence is never expected, by your own lights, to be less accurate than reporting any other credence. That is why the result is often called a 'non-pragmatic' vindication of probabilism: unlike the Dutch Book argument, which shows that Jones's credences let a bookie construct a set of bets guaranteeing him a loss no matter what happens, Joyce's argument shows that Jones's credences are guaranteed to be exploitable in exactly this way simply because they are less accurate, so accuracy-dominance is best seen as a more general version of the very same exploitability that the Dutch Book argument targets."
