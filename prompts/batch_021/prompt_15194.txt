My PhD advisor is retiring after basically her whole career in computational complexity theory, and I got roped into giving the toast at her retirement dinner next month (I'm her most senior remaining student and everyone else in the lab would rather defend a thesis again than speak in public). She made her name on interactive proofs and zero-knowledge protocols back in the 80s/90s, but the last decade or so she's become almost as well known for arguing that this stuff actually has philosophical teeth — not just cute metaphors, but that the gap between finding a proof and checking one tells you something real about mathematical creativity, or that zero-knowledge is a genuinely useful model for what testimony and trust require epistemically. Problem is, half the room will be complexity people who worked with her and will absolutely clock it if a joke about reductions, or about Impagliazzo's whole "which possible world are we actually living in" thing (Algorithmica, Cryptomania, and the rest), is even slightly off. The other half is family and older colleagues from completely outside theory who just know she's brilliant and a bit obsessed.

I ended up in a genuinely heated argument about all this with my friend Dan over beers on friday — he thinks the entire "complexity theory has philosophical implications" program is dressed-up analogy with zero actual content, since asymptotic worst-case claims about infinite families of instances can't literally say anything about whether one particular guy finds it easier to recognize a good idea than to have one, and that she's smuggling in whatever's philosophically interesting by metaphor and then getting credit as if she'd derived it from a theorem. I think that's too fast — plenty of formal ideas structure real insight without applying "literally," and that doesn't automatically make the insight fake, any more than it does for, like, evolutionary metaphors in other fields. We did not resolve this and I'm still a bit annoyed about it.

Anyway — could you write me the toast? Roughly five minutes read aloud, built around one actual joke that lands for the theorists without needing a footnote and is still funny to the non-technical half, and which — without turning into a lecture or relitigating my argument with Dan in front of her family — comes down somewhere on whether her "philosophical" side is the real deal or just very good marketing for her theorems. I'd rather it take a gentle, genuine position than play it safe and dodge the question entirely.
