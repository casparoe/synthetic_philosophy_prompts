I'm putting together a collection of short stories about historical thinkers whose private obsessions quietly expose the cracks in their public life's work, and I'm stuck on Henry Sidgwick. Everyone treats his decades spent chasing mediums through the Society for Psychical Research as a funny footnote to an otherwise dry utilitarian philosopher, but I think its actually the same wound as the "dualism of practical reason" that he can never fully close in the Methods of Ethics -- if reason alone can't guarantee that doing your duty and doing well for yourself come to the same thing, you go looking for a universe that will guarantee it for you instead. Can you write me a short story (~1500 words) where that's the real engine of the piece, not just a fun biographical detail? I don't want the easy version where he catches a fraud and feels vindicated, or witnesses something spooky and feels comforted either way -- have him end up more or less where he actually did, still stuck between the two, and make the reader feel why that's genuinely unbearable for him instead of just quaint.
