I'm plotting a historical novel that follows Pyotr Kropotkin through his last years in Dmitrov, and one pivotal chapter imagines him being handed a stack of twentieth-century biology journals by a young visitor -- a scene that never happened, but one I want to render as intellectually honest as possible. Kropotkin, as you may know, wrote Mutual Aid: A Factor of Evolution partly as a rebuttal to Thomas Henry Huxley's "gladiatorial" picture of nature, arguing from his own observations as a young geographer in Siberia and Manchuria that cooperation within and even between species was as genuine an evolutionary factor as competition. A friend who works in evolutionary biology read an early draft of the chapter and told me it flattens Kropotkin into either a naive precursor of group selection or a lucky anticipator of everything that came later -- neither of which, she thought, did justice to how much his actual argument would have had to bend to survive contact with the theory that followed him.

So I'd like your help reconstructing, in some detail, how Kropotkin himself might have reasoned his way through that later theory. Specifically: would he have read Hamilton's kin selection and inclusive fitness theory, Trivers' reciprocal altruism, and Dawkins' gene's-eye view as quietly vindicating his lifelong case against the competitive, individualist reading of Darwin -- since cooperative behavior turns out to be evolutionarily well-founded after all -- or would he have felt that explaining "mutual aid" away as a by-product of selection on individuals and genes actually concedes the deeper point to his opponents, by denying that groups or species can be beneficiaries of selection in their own right? And where would he plausibly have landed on the later revival of multilevel selection theory associated with David Sloan Wilson and E. O. Wilson, given that its critics (biologists like Stuart West and Andy Gardner) argue it is mathematically just kin selection relabeled, while its defenders treat group-level selection as a genuinely distinct causal story?

I'm not looking for a tidy verdict -- I'd like the tensions and false starts in the reasoning laid out as well, including anywhere his own framework in Mutual Aid seems to pull in two directions at once, so that the chapter's central argument reads as something a sharp, still-thinking Kropotkin could actually have worked out, rather than a caricature bent to fit modern biology.
