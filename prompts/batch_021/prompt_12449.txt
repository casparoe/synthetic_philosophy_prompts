Scott Alexander's essay 'Epistemic Learned Helplessness' argues that most people are right to distrust even an apparently airtight argument on unfamiliar terrain, since a sufficiently skilled arguer can construct a compelling case for almost anything, which makes a policy of always updating on the best argument you've heard exploitable rather than rational. A superintelligent AI advisor would be a far more skilled arguer than any human debater Alexander had in mind. Write a dialogue between two people on a rationalist forum arguing about how such a system should handle its own persuasive advantage. One holds that the AI should deliberately throttle its persuasive force, even when arguing for conclusions that are true, since unthrottled persuasion makes human epistemic autonomy meaningless regardless of the AI's intentions. The other holds that arguing below its actual ability is itself a form of manipulation, since it means the AI is deciding on our behalf which true things we get to be efficiently convinced of, and argues instead for structural safeguards, such as mandatory cross-examination by a second AI arguing the opposite conclusion, that don't require weakening any individual argument. Have the two press each other on concrete cases rather than talk past one another, and let the disagreement remain genuinely unresolved.
