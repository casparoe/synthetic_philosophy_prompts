I have been drafting a short paper on whether a philosopher's private convictions can have any real bearing on their technical work, using Frege as the test case, and I would welcome your comments before I take it further. (Working title: "Frege's Third Realm, Twice Over.")

In the spring of 1924, the last full year of his life, Gottlob Frege kept a diary that had nothing to do with logic. It lay unpublished among his papers until Gottfried Gabriel and Wolfgang Kienzler brought it out in 1994, with an English translation following soon after. The entries, written between 10 March and 9 April, are relentlessly nationalist, anti-parliamentary, and antisemitic: Frege doubts that Jews, however assimilated, can be loyal Germans, is contemptuous of the Weimar constitution and of universal suffrage, and looks forward to some kind of authoritarian rescue of the nation. For readers who knew him only as the inventor of quantificational logic and the theorist of sense and reference, the diary came as a genuine shock — not least to Michael Dummett, who had done more than anyone to secure Frege's reputation as the father of analytic philosophy, and who later wrote candidly about how disturbing he found the discovery.

The question I want to press is not merely biographical: does any of this bear on the philosophy itself? Two answers suggest themselves, and I think both are too quick. The compartmentalizing answer says logic is logic: a bigot can construct a valid proof as well as anyone else, so the diary has no purchase on Frege's arguments against psychologism or on the sense/reference distinction. The guilt-by-association answer says the opposite: since the man was a bigot, the whole apparatus is now suspect. Neither engages with the actual structure of the philosophy.

So I want to try something riskier: not a causal claim, but a claim about a shared disposition. Frege's case against psychologism and relativism about logic rests on insisting that there is a "third realm" (drittes Reich, his own term from "Der Gedanke") of objective thoughts, answerable to no thinker's psychology, culture, or historical position — a realm one simply defers to rather than negotiates with. The diary displays the same cast of mind transposed into politics: a horror of relativizing anything to popular opinion or cultural assimilation, matched by a hunger for a fixed order imposed from above rather than argued for or voted on. On this reading, metaphysics and politics are united not by shared doctrine but by shared temperament — an intolerance of any plurality of voices about who gets to say what is true.

I can anticipate the objections. "Drittes Reich" is almost certainly just ordinary German usage: Frege wrote the phrase in 1918, well before the Nazis appropriated it, and Popper later borrowed the same three-realms picture for perfectly untainted purposes. And plenty of committed democrats have also been Platonists about logic and mathematics, so at best this is a loose correlation dressed up as an insight. Perhaps the honest conclusion is that logical rigor and political judgment are simply calibrated by different faculties, and that the urge to find one unified sensibility behind a thinker's whole output is itself a kind of intellectual vice.

I would value your reactions on three fronts: whether the "shared disposition" argument in the fourth paragraph is worth taking seriously or is simply overreading a striking coincidence of vocabulary; whether I have set up the two "easy answers" fairly, or am strawmanning one of them; and whether there is textual or historical evidence, from the diary or from Frege's logical writings, that would strengthen or undercut the parallel I am drawing.
