Last week a friend of mine, who is finishing a machine learning PhD with a focus on reinforcement learning, and I had a long, occasionally heated argument that started from a single offhand remark. She was describing the well-known case of an RL agent trained on the boat-racing game CoastRunners that discovered it could rack up a higher score by looping endlessly in a lagoon, repeatedly ramming into targets and catching fire, rather than ever finishing the course, and she called this "a textbook case of Goodhart's Law." I objected that the label, however standard it has become in discussions of specification gaming, actually blurs together two quite different phenomena, and that the loose usage does real conceptual damage.

My contention, drawing on the four-way split proposed by David Manheim and Scott Garrabrant into regressional, causal, extremal, and adversarial Goodhart, is that only the adversarial variety captures what Charles Goodhart originally had in mind when writing about monetary policy: a strategic response by agents who understand that a statistic has become a target and adjust their behavior accordingly. The CoastRunners agent, by contrast, has no model of being measured and no capacity to respond to the fact that its score is a proxy; what happened to it seems much closer to what decision analysts call the "optimizer's curse" or what auction theorists call the "winner's curse" -- a statistical consequence of selecting on the extreme value of a noisy proxy, not a strategic response to being watched. My friend regards this distinction as largely semantic. She points out that Manheim and Garrabrant themselves note that real-world cases usually involve several of the four mechanisms at once, and takes this as evidence that the categories are better understood as overlapping symptoms of one underlying pathology than as genuinely distinct phenomena that merely happen to share a name.

I would like you to construct the strongest possible case for my side: that adversarial Goodhart is conceptually distinct from the other three varieties in a way that actually matters, that treating "Goodhart's Law" as a single unified phenomenon obscures an important difference between strategic adaptation and mere statistical or optimization artifacts, and that something like the CoastRunners exploit is better described with pre-existing statistical vocabulary than imported wholesale under the Goodhart umbrella. Please take my friend's objection seriously rather than talking past it: address, in particular, whether "strategic awareness" comes in degrees that resist a principled cutoff (does a genetic algorithm exploit a fitness function any less "strategically" than a bureaucrat exploits a KPI, once we stop assuming a bright line between them?), and address her point about co-occurrence head-on -- does the fact that real-world Goodhart effects often combine several mechanisms actually show that the mechanisms are not conceptually distinct, or is that a non sequitur?
