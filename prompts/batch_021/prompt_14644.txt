I have spent a good deal of time reading about the replication crisis on my own, and there is one recurring point I have never seen explained to my full satisfaction. Among the "questionable research practices" blamed for inflated false-positive rates is optional stopping: monitoring results as data come in and halting collection the moment p drops below 0.05. I can see why this distorts the true error rate under standard frequentist reasoning, since it effectively gives chance repeated opportunities to get lucky. Yet I have also come across the claim that a Bayesian analysis of that very same final dataset would be completely untouched by this practice, on the grounds of the likelihood principle: the idea that all the evidential meaning in data is carried by the likelihood function and does not depend on the stopping rule that produced it. This strikes me as almost too convenient. How can one and the same sequence of observations count as badly biased evidence under one framework, yet as perfectly legitimate evidence under another, apparently just because of the researcher's private intentions about when to stop collecting data? I would like a genuinely careful, mechanistic explanation of what is actually going on here, together with an honest assessment of whether Bayesians' claimed immunity to optional stopping truly holds up, or whether it is more of a theoretical idealization that quietly depends on assumptions practicing researchers rarely satisfy.
