I'm teaching a graduate seminar on reference and modality this semester, and we've just spent two weeks working through Kripke's arguments against descriptivism in *Naming and Necessity* before turning to Chalmers's and Jackson's epistemic two-dimensionalist machinery for handling the necessary a posteriori. The usual selling point, as I present it in lecture, is that a term like "water" gets assigned both a secondary intension (a function from counterfactual worlds to extensions, evaluated by holding fixed what water actually turns out to be, which is why "water is H2O" comes out necessary) and a primary intension (a function from epistemically possible scenarios, or centered worlds, to extensions, supposed to be a priori accessible to any competent thinker, and which is what explains why the identity nonetheless feels contingent and informative before any chemistry gets done).

One of my students pushed back hard on this in discussion, and I haven't been able to shake her objection since. She pointed out that specifying the primary intension of "water" at all seems to require crediting speakers with something like a general, a priori reference-fixing condition -- "whatever dominant, clear, drinkable liquid fills the oceans and lakes hereabouts," or some more idealized rational reconstruction of it -- that picks out an extension in each scenario. But, she argued, that condition just is a description, however dispositional or unarticulated, and once the theory is committed to it, all three of Kripke's classic arguments against descriptivism -- the modal argument, the epistemic argument, and the semantic, Gödel-Schmidt-style argument -- resurface against primary intensions with essentially undiminished force, since none of the three actually turned on the relevant descriptions being simple, consciously entertained, or common knowledge among speakers. I'd like to give the objection its due before I respond to her in next week's session. Could you construct, as rigorously and charitably as you can, the strongest version of the argument that epistemic two-dimensionalism earns no real explanatory advantage over classical descriptivism about names and natural kind terms, because its primary-intension apparatus is best understood as a notational variant of a reference-fixing description theory, and so inherits every one of Kripke's original objections to descriptivism?
