So I'm reporting out a longer piece on the "agent foundations" wing of AI safety -- the MIRI-style work that was happening years before anyone was talking about RLHF or interpretability -- and I keep getting stuck on this idea of "embedded agency" that Scott Garrabrant and Abram Demski wrote up back in 2018. The setup, as I understand it, is that pretty much every formal model of a rational agent we actually know how to build -- argmax, AIXI, all of it -- quietly assumes the agent sits outside the environment its optimizing, feeding in observations and reading out actions through a clean little interface, like the game controller in their example of "Alexei," their stand-in for that kind of agent. Once you actually put the agent inside the environment its supposed to be modeling (their other stand-in, "Emmy"), a bunch of things apparently don't just get harder, they stop making sense: you can't treat the world as a fixed function from your actions to outcomes anymore, and you can't fit an accurate model of the whole environment inside something that's only a small piece of that same environment.

I get why this ends up mattering for something like a self-modifying superintelligence trying to trust a future, smarter version of itself -- I gather this is where the Löb's theorem stuff comes in, about why a successor agent apparently has to run on strictly weaker axioms than its predecessor, which seems like it should bottom out somewhere but I don't quite see why. What I don't have is a gut-level feel for why "embeddedness" is a difference in kind and not just in degree -- why you can't get most of the way there by just bolting a self-model and a bit of noise onto the AIXI-style picture. Could you walk me through, in fairly plain language, what actually breaks once the agent-environment boundary disappears, and why the people working on this treated it as a genuinely deep problem rather than an engineering detail you sort out later?
