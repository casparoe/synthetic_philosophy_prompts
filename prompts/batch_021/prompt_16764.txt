I defended my PhD thesis two weeks ago and, like pretty much every dissertation ever written, stuck a line in the acknowledgements saying something like "despite my best efforts and my committee's, errors surely remain, and they are entirely my own." At the pub after, this turned into a whole argument between two friends of mine, Colin and Dana, and its been bugging me since.

Colin said what I wrote is a textbook preface paradox: I believe every claim in the thesis individually, but I also believe (and stated in writing!) that at least one is false -- an outright contradiction if belief is closed under conjunction. Dana said sure, that's the setup, but it's not actually troubling, because nobodys obligated to believe the conjunction of everything they believe -- closure is what should go, not her confidence in chapter 3.

Colin shot back that dropping conjunction-closure isn't free: it's literally Kyburg's move on the lottery paradox, and it costs you the ability to rationally believe P, rationally believe Q, and still not be allowed to infer "P and Q" from them -- reasoning you're not even allowed to do out loud.

Dana said the lottery and the preface case aren't even the same problem, so his "cost" doesn't automatically transfer: a lottery gives you a precise, symmetric, known per-ticket probability, but nobody can put a number on "the odds claim 4.2 is false" -- it's a vague, holistic hunch about authors in general, so maybe conjunction fails in one case and not the other.

Colin's comeback was that this actually cuts against her, not for her: if she can't assign a number to her confidence in any single claim, that undermines the premise that each individual belief was justified to begin with, so her "disanalogy" is really just precision-envy dressed up as a distinction.

Neither of them budged. Can you go through this move by move and say who's got the better of it at each step, rather than just an overall winner, and flag anything important both of them are missing? I'm especially curious whether Dana's right that the lottery and preface paradoxes actually need different solutions, or whether Colin's right that you can't pull them apart like that.
