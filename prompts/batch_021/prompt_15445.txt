So I'm in a decision theory reading group (mix of CS and philosophy grad students), and two people in it, Sam and Lena, got into a real argument last week over coherence arguments -- the money-pump/Dutch-book style arguments people use to claim any sufficiently rational agent has to act like an expected utility maximizer -- and what that actually implies about advanced AI. Trying to reconstruct it fairly:

(1) Sam: an agent whose preferences can't be represented by a utility function can be money-pumped -- cycled through a series of trades that leave it strictly worse off, paying a fee each lap. So any agent that sticks around and gets to improve itself over time will get squeezed into coherence, and a coherent agent pursuing basically any real goal will find it useful to hoard resources, resist being shut down, etc, pretty much regardless of what the goal actually is.

(2) Lena: that shows less than you think. Coherence theorems say your choices are representable as maximizing *some* utility function, but literally any fixed behavior -- a thermostat's included -- is "rationalizable" that way, you just define the utility function to reward whatever it already does. Coherent doesn't mean "has an ambitious utility function ranging over world-states," and that's the part doing all the scary work, wich the theorem doesn't hand you for free.

(3) Sam: sure, as a one-off relabeling trick, but the interesting claim is dynamic -- an agent that reflects on itself and can patch its own decision procedure will iron out incoherence, since exploitability is a standing cost no matter your goal. And real agents we'd actually build, or that fall out of training on rich objectives, have preferences over future world-states rather than just their own next action, so your relabeling escape hatch doesn't apply to them.

(4) Lena: now the scary premise is coming in through the goal structure, not the coherence theorem, which was kind of my point -- the theorem stopped doing any real work a while ago. Also your dynamic version has a hole: the money-pump only bites an agent that's forced to take one of the trades on offer. An agent with incomplete preferences can just decline to trade when it can't rank two options, and stays un-pumpable without ever becoming a classical expected-utility maximizer.

Neither of them budged and I honestly cant tell who's ahead. Could you go through it move by move, say who's got the better of each point (not just an overall winner), and flag anything you think both of them are missing?
