I'm several drafts into a novel with a subplot set among the "left wing" of the Vienna Circle in the late 1920s, and to keep the philosophical texture honest I've spent this term auditing a graduate seminar on logical empiricism at a university near me. The professor who runs it knows I'm not there for credit, but last week, buried under a stack of take-home essays, she half-jokingly asked whether I wanted to try grading one myself, purely as an exercise, so she could compare it against her own marking afterward. I agreed before thinking it through, and now I'd like a more expert second opinion before I hand anything back to her, since I don't yet trust my own instincts about where an answer's philosophical weight is real and where it's just dressed up to sound weighty.

The question the students were given was: "Carnap's Vienna Circle colleagues were divided over how far logical empiricism should be tied to a political program. Explain the political outlook associated with the Circle's 'left wing,' how it was connected—both in the Circle's public self-presentation and in Carnap's individual case—to the fact/value distinction and the unity-of-science program, and whether Carnap's practice of advocating for the rational planning of society is actually consistent with his own meta-ethical commitments. Use specific figures and episodes, not just generalities." (20 points.)

Here is the answer I've been asked to grade:

"The left wing of the Circle—Neurath above all, with Carnap and Hahn closer to him than Schlick ever was—saw logical empiricism as part of a broader Enlightenment project of social reform, not a politically neutral technical exercise. This comes out in the 1929 manifesto 'The Scientific Conception of the World,' which frames the rejection of metaphysics as clearing away the intellectual props of reactionary and nationalist politics, and which Schlick reportedly found too programmatic for his taste despite the dedication to him. Carnap's own socialist sympathies went back to the German youth movement, and he carried into his mature philosophy the conviction that questions of social organization should be handled with the same rigor as questions in physics; it was Carnap, drawing on his stint running the Zentralwirtschaftsamt, the central planning office of the Bavarian Council Republic, in 1919, who championed 'full socialization' as an alternative to piecemeal reform. Philosophically, the interesting tension is with the fact/value distinction: Carnap held that value statements are not cognitively meaningful, merely expressive of attitudes, which looks hard to square with his own passionate advocacy. But his position is actually consistent, because for Carnap the case for a planned society was itself established by logical and empirical analysis rather than resting on an arbitrary preference—planning wasn't Carnap's personal taste, it was the rationally demonstrated conclusion of applying scientific method to social ends, which is exactly why he felt entitled to argue for it rather than merely confess to liking it."

Could you grade this out of 20 and give me a point-by-point breakdown—ideally structured as a JSON object with one entry per distinct claim in the answer (a verdict of correct, incorrect, or partially correct, plus a one- or two-sentence justification for each), followed by a final numeric grade and a two- or three-sentence overall comment? I'd rather hand the professor something I can set against her own marking than a vague impression of whether the answer "sounds right."
