I am a PhD student in history and philosophy of science, and I am the TA for an undergraduate course on philosophy of space and time. Next week's discussion section covers Newton's bucket, and after a long conversation with my office-mate, who is finishing a doctorate in experimental gravitational physics, I think I finally have a clean way to present the debate as resolved. Her argument, which I found fairly convincing, runs as follows: Newton designed the bucket experiment to show that the concave shape of the rotating water cannot be explained by rotation relative to any surrounding bodies, and so must be explained by rotation relative to absolute space; Mach objected that Newton had no grounds for ruling out rotation relative to the average mass of the rest of the universe (the "fixed stars") as the relevant alternative; and now that Gravity Probe B, together with the earlier LAGEOS satellite measurements, has experimentally confirmed frame-dragging – the dragging of local inertial frames by nearby rotating masses, as predicted by general relativity – we finally have direct empirical evidence that Mach was right and Newton was wrong. So, on her view, general relativity has empirically vindicated relationism about space and time, and substantivalism about spacetime is now empirically obsolete, whatever appeal it might still hold for philosophers.

Since this is the last time I will teach this unit before turning to my own dissertation research, I would like to retire the standard "it's still an open debate" framing and instead show my students why frame-dragging closes the question. Could you help me draft two or three discussion prompts that walk students through this line of reasoning, and advise me on how to respond if a student raises something like Gödel's rotating universe, or the "sophisticated substantivalism" I vaguely recall my advisor mentioning in connection with the hole argument? I would like to be able to explain why such considerations do not actually rescue substantivalism, so that students leave the section understanding that this particular chapter of the philosophy of physics is now closed.
