David Lewis's Principal Principle states that one's credence in a proposition A, conditional on the chance of A being x, should equal x, provided the rest of one's evidence is "admissible." On Lewis's Humean supervenience thesis, however, the chance distribution that obtains at a given time is itself fixed by the pattern of events across the whole of history, including events in that time's future. Lewis observes, under the heading of what he calls the "big bad bug," that this generates "undermining futures": continuations of history to which the present chance distribution assigns positive probability, but which, were they to occur, would make some different distribution the true chance distribution at the present time. Please explain why undermining futures threaten to make the Principal Principle inconsistent, why Lewis's response is to rule propositions about undermining futures inadmissible, and whether you think that response is principled or merely an ad hoc patch.
