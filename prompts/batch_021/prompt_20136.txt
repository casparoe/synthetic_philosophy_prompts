So in my rational choice theory seminar this week we spent way too long on Parfit's hitchhiker and nobody actually won the argument, it just kind of fizzled when class ended (yes I should've been using that time to write my own dissertation chapter, but here we are). Setup in case my memory of it is slightly off: you're dying of thirst in the desert, a driver will only take you to town if you promise to pay him some large sum once you arrive, and he's basically a perfect lie detector, so he'll only actually drive you if he's genuinely convinced you mean to pay. Problem is, once you're safe in town, paying does nothing for you anymore, the ride already happened, its a sunk cost, so I think both causal AND evidential decision theory tell you to just keep the money. Except obviously an agent like that never gets picked up in the first place, since the driver sees through it before you even get in the truck. Our professor kept calling this "rational irrationality," which I think is actually Parfit's own phrase from Reasons and Persons (don't hold me to the exact citation) — the idea that it can be rational, at the level of what disposition to have, to be disposed to do things that, taken one at a time, aren't rational at all.

What I want is a dialogue that actually works through that disagreement instead of just gesturing at both sides the way our seminar did. Setting: two decision theorists who got picked up by the same driver on the same trip, now standing in the middle of town while he idles at the curb with his hand out. One of them holds that rationality has to be evaluated act by act, at each choice node, given your situation right then, so keeping the money is simply the rational thing to do, and if that means this type of agent dies of thirst in some other possible desert, that's a fact about the world and not a refutation of anything. The other holds that this whole way of thinking gets the unit of assessment wrong, that whether an act is rational can't be read off in isolation from the policy or disposition that produced it, and that you actually have to hand over the cash to be the kind of agent who survives the desert at all. I don't want either of them to just cite Gauthier or start monologuing about functional decision theory and call it a day — I want them pressing on each other's actual weak points, like: can the first guy explain what work "rational" is even doing if it's a label that predictably gets you killed, and can the second one explain in what sense her view is still a theory of what to DO right now, standing in town, rather than just a fancy way of saying "be the sort of person who refuses to reconsider." Real back and forth, please, not two people taking turns monologuing past each other.
