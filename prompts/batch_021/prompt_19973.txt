I'm a software engineer who has implemented minimax and alpha-beta search for game-playing bots, so backward induction always struck me as an unproblematic algorithm rather than a subject of philosophical dispute. That impression didn't survive my reading about Robert Aumann's result that common knowledge of rationality among the players formally forces the backward-induction outcome in any finite game of perfect information, such as the centipede game. There's also a well-known rebuttal — associated with theorists like Ken Binmore and Philip Reny — holding that this argument is incoherent, since it asks what a "rational" player would do at a decision node that could only be reached if common knowledge of rationality had already broken down earlier in the game. Write an essay reconstructing both sides of this dispute as rigorously as you can, and then take a side: does common knowledge of rationality really pin down backward induction as the uniquely rational way to play such games, or does the argument collapse at exactly the off-path nodes it depends on? Defend your verdict against the strongest form of the opposing position rather than settling for an on-the-one-hand-on-the-other-hand answer.
