I'm a philosophy major taking a seminar on vagueness this term, and I sit on my university's Graduate Emergency Fund Committee, which awards small grants to students facing "severe financial hardship" -- a phrase our bylaws never define. This cycle's hardest case is a doctoral student whose $2,200 emergency car repair has to come out of a $28,000 stipend and about $600 in savings; she's clearly worse off than applicants we routinely deny, but nowhere near the zero-savings, facing-eviction cases we've funded before. The committee has split along lines that echo our readings: three members are, in effect, epistemicists who think "severe hardship" has some determinate cutoff we're simply ignorant of and want us to approximate it using benchmarks like the federal poverty line and comparable funds at peer schools; the other three sound like supervaluationists who think the phrase has no sharp extension at all, that her case is true under some equally admissible precisifications and false under others, and that we should stop pretending to discover a hidden number and just stipulate one. One member has further muddied things by doubting we can even agree this is a clear borderline case rather than a clear denial. Applications close in ten days, and the board wants a standing policy before next cycle. Draft the memo I'll circulate: a recommendation on her application with a timeline, and a recommendation for handling future borderline cases -- take seriously the strongest objection to whichever approach you end up recommending.
