So my partner and I have been in hardcore "FIRE" mode (financial independence, retire early) for about four years now — living pretty lean, maxing out every tax-advantaged account we can find, tracking our net worth in a spreadsheet like it's a leaderboard (I'm a software engineer, this tracks). Every non-trivial decision gets run through "does this get us to our number faster or slower." Last weekend my sister, who has never once made a budget in her life, said something that's been bugging me ever since: that by treating literally every day right now as a stepping stone to some future payoff, I've basically arranged my whole adult life so that none of it is actually for anything — it's all just fuel for a day that, by definition, hasn't happened yet. I laughed it off at the time (delayed gratification is just math, compounding is real, etc.) but it's stuck with me more then I'd like to admit.

Here's the part I can't shake: she wasn't making the standard "money can't buy happiness" or "you should relax more" argument, which I could brush off pretty easily. It felt more specific than that, almost like she was saying something is structurally off about a life plan built this way, no matter how big the eventual payoff turns out to be. I don't have any philosophy background at all (closest thing is arguing about simulation theory in a work Slack channel at 1am), so I genuinely can't tell if there's a real argument hiding in what she said, or if she's just needling me for being frugal. Is she onto something real about why organizing your whole life around a future payoff might be a bad structure regardless of the payoff's size, or is this just a garden-variety "workaholic vs. free spirit" gripe and I should get back to my spreadsheet in peace?
