I've been reading through John Stuart Mill's writings on socialism for a term paper in a graduate seminar on nineteenth-century political economy, and I've run into an interpretive puzzle I can't quite resolve on my own. In "On the Probable Futurity of the Labouring Classes" — the chapter Mill substantially rewrote for the third edition (1852) of the Principles of Political Economy — he writes with real warmth about cooperative associations of workers gradually displacing the ordinary arrangement of capitalist employer and hired labourer, and in the Autobiography he even suggests that his and Harriet Taylor's mature economic outlook would have placed them under the general heading of socialism. Yet the unfinished Chapters on Socialism, apparently drafted in the late 1860s and published only after his death when Helen Taylor brought it out in the Fortnightly Review in 1879, reads far more defensively: it dwells at length on the practical difficulties facing socialist and communist proposals — incentives to labour, control of population, the mechanics of distributing output without private property — and insists that any departure from the existing economic order be gradual, voluntary, and tested on a small scale rather than adopted wholesale.

Could you help me think through whether this amounts to a genuine change of mind on Mill's part, or whether the apparent hardening is better explained by something else — for instance, the two texts having different targets (specific continental schemes of revolutionary communism versus socialism as a long-run ideal), the fact that Chapters on Socialism breaks off before reaching any constructive proposal of its own, or the fact that what survives is an incomplete draft that someone other than Mill selected and arranged for publication? I'd like you to lay out the strongest version of both readings — real intellectual retreat versus a divergence that is more superficial or context-dependent than it looks — and then say which one you think the textual and biographical evidence actually favors, and why.
