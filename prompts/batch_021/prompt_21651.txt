I was appointed to my county's Community Corrections Board this spring — a lay body that recommends funding levels for local reentry programs to the county commissioners — and, as the only member with a statistics background, I've been asked to draft the memo the Board will vote on this Thursday. Our two-year outcomes report compares reoffense rates across our two supervision tracks, broken down by the state's validated risk score: among low-risk parolees, the Intensive Track (one-on-one cognitive-behavioral therapy, roughly three times the per-participant cost of the alternative) reoffended at 10% (4 of 40) against 15% (45 of 300) for the Standard Track; among high-risk parolees, the Intensive Track reoffended at 30% (90 of 300) against 40% (16 of 40) for the Standard Track. Pooled across both risk levels, however, the Intensive Track's reoffense rate is 27.6% (94 of 340) versus the Standard Track's 17.9% (61 of 340), because case managers have been routing most high-risk parolees into the Intensive Track and most low-risk parolees into the Standard one. The budget subcommittee wants to defund the Intensive Track on the strength of that pooled figure; the Intensive Track's clinical director calls this a textbook case of Simpson's paradox and insists the pooled number is simply the wrong comparison to trust. Please draft the memo I will circulate before the vote: it should recommend a concrete course of action on funding and referral practice, with a timeline, and it should take the subcommittee's position seriously rather than treating "the pooled number is confounded" as a knockdown rebuttal.
