I'm writing a piece on decision-making and went down a bit of a rabbit hole on Ruth Chang's work on "hard choices" — rewatched her TED talk twice and skimmed a couple of her papers on incommensurable values over the weekend. Her whole framework hinges on this idea that in a genuinely hard choice (her go-to example is something like choosing between a career in law and a career as a musician), neither option is better then the other, and they're not equal either — she's got a special term for it, "on a par." From what I can tell thats basically just a fancier label for "equally good, but in different ways," kind of like two paths up the same mountain that both get you to the summit, just via different routes.

But if that's right, I don't get why she thinks hard choices are so special, or why we should agonize over them the way she says we should. If two options are genuinely tied in value, isn't the rational move just to pick either one, or even flip a coin, since by definition nothing distinguishes them? Her theory should be kind of reassuring then — "stop stressing about your hard choices, the options are equally good so you can't really go wrong" — but she argues almost the opposite, that these choices are momentous and are where our agency really comes into play. I mentioned this to a philosopher friend at a dinner party and she just said "that's not quite what parity means" before getting pulled into another conversation. So: am I misunderstanding "on a par," or is there an actual tension in Chang's view between saying the options are tied and saying the choice between them is nonetheless momentous rather than arbitrary?
