Ok so this has been a running argument at work for a while now and I want an actual steelman written out, not just people going back and forth in Slack threads. Background: our team owns a recommendation feature, and pretty much every quarter, whatever "north star" engagement metric we pick gets gamed within like two sprints — by users, by our own team subtly overfitting to it, sometimes even by other teams whose metrics interact with ours in weird ways. We've cycled through session length, click-through rate, some composite "satisfaction" score built off a survey, etc, and the same pattern keeps happening no matter wich one we pick. This sent me down a rabbit hole a while back reading about Goodhart's law, and I stumbled on some writeup (LessWrong I think, could be misremembering) that splits it into four flavors: "regressional" (your proxy and the real goal are only correlated, not identical, so pushing hard on the proxy just selects for noise/luck), "extremal" (you optimize so hard you shoot into weird extreme territory where the correlation that held under normal conditions just breaks down), "causal" (the proxy is correlated with the goal but doesn't cause it, so moving the proxy does nothing to the actual thing you care about), and "adversarial" (there's an actual strategic agent on the other end — a user, a model, a rival team — deliberately routing around your metric because it has its own agenda). That last one is basically what ML people call "specification gaming," like that classic story of the boat-racing RL agent that figured out it could rack up a way higher score looping in circles hitting the same reward pickups forever instead of ever finishing the race.

Here's what I want you to argue for, as hard as you can, no wishy-washy hedging: that the first three flavors are "merely" hard statistics/engineering problems which better measurement design, more data, or smarter causal modelling can push arbitrarily close to zero — but adversarial Goodhart is a categorically different beast that can never be closed no matter how clever your metric design gets, because the instant you patch a metric to catch the last exploit, you've just created a new, higher-order target for that same adversarial process to route around next. So there's a real, structural line between "our measurement is imperfect" (fixable in the limit) and "something is actively optimizing against me" (never fixable in the limit, just an endless arms race). I know an economist type would probably object that mechanism design gives us genuinely strategy-proof mechanisms in plenty of real settings, so adversarial goodhart isnt obviously different in kind from the others, just in degree — I want you to take that objection seriously and tell me whether it actually sinks my claim or whether the claim survives it anyway.
