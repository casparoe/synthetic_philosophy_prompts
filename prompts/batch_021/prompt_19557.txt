I've somehow become "the identity guy" on our university's Art & Monuments Committee -- I teach in the philosophy department, and made the mistake of giving an after-dinner talk to some donors a couple years back that name-dropped the Ship of Theseus -- and now they've handed me a case that's a lot more precise (and annoying) than that old chestnut. Our bronze war memorial, which has stood in the main quad for about ninety years, got hit by lightning during a storm last month, and a good chunk of it actually melted and re-hardened into a lumpy mass. A foundry we consulted says essentially all the original bronze is recoverable as scrap and could be re-melted and recast -- using photogrammetry plus the original plaster maquette we've still got in storage -- into something visually indistinguishable from the original. Our insurer's adjuster is treating this as a routine repair claim ("the material survives, restore the appearance, we cover the recasting cost"), but our policy actually values monuments and single-edition artworks not by replacement cost but by whether "the extant original work" survives, a phrase nobody thought to define when they drafted it. The donor's descendants, who still fund a scholarship tied to the memorial, insist a recast statue would be a faithful copy wearing the original's face, not the original restored, and that we're owed the full original-work valuation instead.

I have to bring the committee an actual recommendation on Friday -- total loss, repair, or some negotiated middle position -- and I'd like it as a short memo I could actually read into the record, not a list of pros and cons. Don't just assert an answer or hide behind the repair-cost-percentage rule insurers use for ordinary property: I want it to genuinely work through whether "all the original matter, just reshaped" is enough for this to still be the same statue, or whether the statue -- as opposed to the bronze -- was already destroyed the instant it lost it's shape. And whichever side the memo doesn't land on, the adjuster's or the descendants', represent their strongest case rather than steamrolling them. Keep it something a busy committee and an impatient claims adjuster could both actually get through.
