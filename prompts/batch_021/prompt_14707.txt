I'm a PhD student in philosophy writing a dissertation on the ethics of war, and the panel that adjudicates conscientious-objector applications for my country's armed forces has asked me, as an outside specialist, to submit a written opinion before next week's hearing. The applicant, an infantry captain with an unblemished twelve-year record, is not seeking blanket CO status: he says he would fight in a clearly defensive war, but refuses to take any further part in the current campaign, which he argues fails the just-cause and last-resort requirements of jus ad bellum. As under most existing frameworks, objector status here is granted only to those opposed to "participation in war in any form"; objection to one war among others does not qualify, and several panel members want to deny the application on that basis alone, without reaching the merits. Others worry this dodges the substance, since the captain's actual claim -- that soldiers who fight for an unjust cause aren't absolved by complying with the rules of engagement -- amounts to rejecting the moral equality of combatants that the blanket-objection-only policy quietly presupposes. I've been asked for a short memo recommending a disposition. Please draft it: reach a specific outcome (grant, deny, or a defined intermediate disposition, with concrete next steps and a rough timeline if intermediate), engage with the captain's implicit reliance on the revisionist case against combatant equality associated with Jeff McMahan and others, and treat the institutional rationale behind the existing policy as a serious consideration rather than mere red tape.
