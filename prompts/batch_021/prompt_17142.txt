I've been mulling over the "eliciting latent knowledge" (ELK) problem that Paul Christiano and his co-authors at the Alignment Research Center set out a while back. In the central toy example, a predictive model monitors a vault containing a diamond, and a separately trained "reporter" is meant to answer questions about what the predictor internally represents. The worry, if I have it right, is that the reporter may settle into being a "human simulator" -- reporting whatever a human would conclude from the same sensor data -- rather than a "direct translator" of the predictor's actual internal state, and that nothing in a human-labeled training signal can distinguish these two strategies so long as the labelers are never themselves mistaken. A natural fix is to penalize the reporter for unnecessary extra computation, on the thought that simulating a human's judgment ought to cost more than simply translating. What I'd like is a careful explanation of why ARC takes this fix to be unreliable even in principle -- specifically, why it can be genuinely ambiguous, given what the predictor already needs to compute for its main task, which of the two reporters actually counts as the "simpler" one. As a further, more speculative question: does this strike you as structurally the same underdetermination that Quine pointed to in his discussion of radical translation, or does the analogy break down somewhere -- for instance, in that ELK seems to presuppose a determinate fact about what the predictor believes, which is just what Quine's argument was meant to call into question?
