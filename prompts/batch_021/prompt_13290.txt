so this argument happened after our philosophy of medicine seminar last week and i still can't decide who actually had the better of it. we'd just finished the naturalism vs normativism unit (Boorse vs the normativists) when someone brought up that the DSM added "prolonged grief disorder" as an actual diagnosis a couple years back, and it turned into a whole thing between my friends Ben and Kayla that never got resolved.

Ben said PGD is a real dysfunction on totally naturalist, value-free grounds: its statistically atypical for grief this intense to still be going a year out (i think thats literally the cutoff, something like 12 months for adults and 6 for kids), and by that point its wrecking the exact functions grief is supposed to eventually let you get back to, like sleep, work, relationships. no value judgment required, he said, just biology.

Kayla shot back that the 12-month line was picked by an APA committee, not discovered in nature - they decided when intervention seemed warranted and then called the result "atypical." that's the normativist point exactly, she said: the "normal function" baseline already has a value judgment baked into it, it's just dressed up as a number so you don't notice.

Ben's comeback was that blood pressure and A1C cutoffs for diabetes are also set by committees, and nobody concludes from that that high blood pressure isn't a real condition - drawing an approximate line across a continuum isn't the same as inventing the category out of values.

Kayla said the cases aren't actually analogous: blood pressure tracks stroke and heart attack risk regardless of what anyone values, but with grief the only "harm" on offer is that it's unpleasant and disrupts work and relationships, and deciding how much disruption counts as impairing already presupposes some standard for how much grieving is reasonable - you can't read that off statistics alone.
