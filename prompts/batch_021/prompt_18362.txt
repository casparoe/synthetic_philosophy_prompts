My grandfather died two months ago, and I've only recently realized that one of his last conditions on me was, in effect, a real-life version of a philosophical puzzle I'd never heard named. In his final year he told me he would leave me the family cabin only if, before he died, he became genuinely convinced that I intended to scatter his ashes at the lake he loved; he was explicit that he wanted the intention itself, formed and recognized while he was still alive, and not a promise I would actually be held to afterward. I apparently succeeded, because the cabin is now mine outright, and there is no one left to check whether I follow through. I've since learned that this is essentially Kavka's toxin puzzle, and what I would like explained is the precise structure of Kavka's argument that such an intention could not have been rationally and sincerely formed in the first place, as distinct from the weaker and more familiar point that I would simply have had no reason to follow through once the cabin was secured. My understanding is that the argument turns on some principle tying sincere intention to a belief that one will actually comply, and I would like to know exactly how that principle is supposed to work, and how far it really succeeds. I would also like to understand how "resolute choice" theorists such as McClennen would diagnose my situation differently from "sophisticated choice" theorists, and whether either view implies that I actually managed to form a genuine intention, or only something that merely resembled one well enough to convince my grandfather.
