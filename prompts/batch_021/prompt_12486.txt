I'm a retired comparative physiologist -- spent most of my working life on fish and cephalopod nervous systems -- and since retiring I've fallen into a philosophy of mind rabbit hole, slowly working through Godfrey-Smith, Chalmers, and that crowd on animal consciousness. I vaguely remember reading that a bunch of consciousness researchers signed some kind of joint statement not too long ago, urging people not to write off invertebrate consciousness too quickly, and it's gotten me thinking about how strange this whole debate is compared to ordinary biology. Normally in science, if two competent people disagree about whether some animal has trait X, you go collect more data and the disagreement eventually closes. But with consciousness it seems like you could hand two serious, well-informed theorists the exact same complete dossier on a creature -- full connectome, every behavioral study on record -- and they'd still flatly disagree about whether anyone's home in there, because they don't even agree on what's supposed to count as the marker of consciousness in the first place.

Could you give me a concrete example that really brings this out? I don't mean the tired "is a thermostat conscious" line -- I mean an actual or carefully-built hypothetical creature (an octopus, a bee, a fish, whatever works best -- I know just enough about octopus arm neurons and insect midbrains to be dangerous) that plausibly satisfies some of the standard markers researchers use for sentience while just as plausibly failing others, such that two specific, serious theories of consciousness -- say global workspace theory versus integrated information theory, or a higher-order theory versus a first-order one -- would flatly contradict each other about it, even given total agreement on the underlying biology and behavior. Then walk me through why thats a genuine in-principle conceptual problem, about how we even individuate "a subject of experience" or decide what's allowed to count as evidence at all, rather than just a gap that better brain-scanning tech could eventually close.
