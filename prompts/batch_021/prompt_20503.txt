I'm putting together this week's session for a seminar on early modern political thought, where we pair Hobbes on sovereignty with a handful of notes from Pascal's Pensées that tend to catch students off guard. Everyone arrives already knowing the Wager and assumes Pascal has nothing to say about politics, so they're unsettled by how flatly unsentimental he sounds once he turns to law and authority. I'd rather send them into class with the primary text itself than a paraphrase, but before I do, I want a second reading of it, partly because a colleague in the politics department dismissed the fragment as "just Thrasymachus with better prose," and that strikes me as too quick.

Here is the note as Pascal wrote it (usually numbered 298 in Brunschvicg's edition, though the numbering shifts across editions), followed by a fairly literal working translation of my own, since the published translations smooth over exactly the ambiguity I want the students to sit with:

« Justice, force. — Il est juste que ce qui est juste soit suivi ; il est nécessaire que ce qui est le plus fort soit suivi. La justice sans la force est impuissante ; la force sans la justice est tyrannique. La justice sans force est contredite, parce qu'il y a toujours des méchants ; la force sans la justice est accusée. Il faut donc mettre ensemble la justice et la force ; et, pour cela, faire que ce qui est juste soit fort, ou que ce qui est fort soit juste. La justice est sujette à dispute ; la force est très reconnaissable et sans dispute. Aussi n'a-t-on pu donner la force à la justice, parce que la force a contredit la justice, et a dit que c'était elle qui était juste. Et ainsi, ne pouvant faire que ce qui est juste fût fort, on a fait que ce qui est fort fût juste. »

("Justice, force. — It is just that what is just should be followed; it is necessary that what is strongest should be followed. Justice without force is powerless; force without justice is tyrannical. Justice without force is contradicted, because there are always wrongdoers; force without justice stands accused. We must therefore join justice and force together, and, to that end, make what is just strong, or what is strong just. Justice is subject to dispute; force is very easy to recognize and is not disputed. And so it has not been possible to give force to justice, because force has contradicted justice, and has claimed that it was force which was just. And thus, unable to make what is just strong, we have made what is strong just.")

What I'd like help with is the actual mechanics of the argument in that second paragraph, not just its rhetorical force. Is the slide from "justice is disputed, force is not" to "we have made what is strong just" meant to be valid reasoning, or is Pascal deliberately staging a sleight of hand — and if the latter, where exactly does it occur: an equivocation between justice as a standard and justice as whatever gets socially certified as such, or something else? I'd also like your view on whether the fragment is best read as normative (endorsing might as the real ground of right), purely diagnostic (describing how justice comes to be settled among quarrelsome, self-interested creatures), or only fully legible against the surrounding material Pascal's editors group under headings like "the reason of effects" (raison des effets), where he repeatedly argues that customs looking arbitrary to naïve reason turn out to have a hidden second-order rationale. And since Pascal was such a close reader of Montaigne, I'm curious whether this fragment does any philosophical work beyond compressing Montaigne's own skepticism about the conventional basis of law — and, coming back to my colleague, whether the Thrasymachus comparison actually holds up or misses something distinctive about the move Pascal is making.
