For a philosophy elective project on intrinsic value, I've been reading the final chapter of G. E. Moore's *Principia Ethica* ("The Ideal"), and I've gotten stuck trying to reconcile two things he argues there. Here is the passage that's giving me trouble:

"Let us imagine one world exceedingly beautiful. Imagine it as beautiful as you can; put into it whatever on earth you deem loveliest—mountains, rivers, the sea; trees, and sunsets, stars and moon. Imagine these all combined in the most exquisite proportions, so that no one thing jars against another, but each contributes to increase the beauty of the whole. And then imagine the ugliest world you can possibly conceive. Imagine it simply one heap of filth, containing everything that is most disgusting to us, for whatever reason, and the whole, as far as may be, without one redeeming feature... The only thing we are not entitled to imagine is that any human being ever has, or ever, by any possibility can, live in either, can ever see and enjoy the beauty of the one or hate the foulness of the other. Well, even so, is it irrational to hold that it is better that the beautiful world should exist than the ugly one? ... Certainly I cannot help thinking that it would; and I hope that some may agree with me in this extreme instance."

Moore takes this thought experiment to show that beauty is intrinsically valuable even when no one ever perceives it. But not much later in the same chapter, he argues that the best things of all are "organic unities": complex wholes combining a correct perception of some beautiful or admirable object with the emotion appropriate to it, where neither the perception nor the emotion is worth much on its own. It's specifically this combination that lets him rank mutual personal affection - roughly, the kind of appreciation friends or lovers have for one another's characters - as at least as valuable as, and maybe more valuable than, contemplating beauty in art or nature.

What I can't work out is how these two arguments are supposed to fit together. The organic-unity story seems to make some actual mind, holding both the perception and the feeling, essential to the highest goods. But the uninhabited beautiful world contains no perception or feeling at all, and Moore still wants to say it is better than the uninhabited ugly one. So is he committed to two different, seemingly inconsistent, pictures of where value comes from - one on which consciousness is required and one on which it isn't? Or is there a way of reading his "isolation" method for testing intrinsic value that keeps these two arguments consistent with each other? I'd really appreciate help figuring out whether this is a genuine tension in Moore's position, and if so, how someone sympathetic to him might try to resolve it.
