"The Principle of Alternative Possibilities holds that an agent is morally responsible for what she has done only if she could have done otherwise. Frankfurt attacks this principle with the case of Jones and Black. Jones deliberates and decides, entirely on his own, to perform a certain action; unbeknownst to him, Black has implanted a device that monitors his mental states and would force him to perform that very action if, and only if, Jones showed some sign of deciding otherwise. Because Jones goes ahead and decides on his own, the device never activates and plays no role in what actually happens. Frankfurt concludes that Jones is morally responsible even though, thanks to Black, he could not have done otherwise, since the device closes off every alternative in advance; what matters for responsibility is only the actual sequence issuing in the action, not whether some counterfactual sequence was available. This is meant to sever the link between being able to do otherwise and being responsible that motivates incompatibilist arguments like the Consequence Argument. Critics have replied that Jones is not really deprived of every alternative. Fischer's own flicker-of-freedom defence points out that there must be some prior sign in Jones's brain that tips Black off, and that Jones's failure to exhibit that sign was itself open to him, so a thin alternative possibility survives after all and PAP is vindicated in a suitably weakened form. I think this defence succeeds: because the flicker is an indeterministic event occurring in Jones's own brain rather than anything imposed on him by Black, it is exactly the kind of thing an agent needs to be in control of her action, so Frankfurt's counterexample shows only that PAP must be restated in terms of these flickers, not that alternative possibilities are dispensable altogether."
