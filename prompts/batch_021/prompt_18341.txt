A few of us who never studied philosophy or history formally have been running an informal online reading group for about a year now — we started with pop histories of science and have slowly worked our way up to primary sources, which is exciting but also a little nerve-wracking, since it's easy to misread a seventeenth-century text when you have no training to fall back on. Last cycle everyone read Galileo's 1615 "Letter to the Grand Duchess Christina," and as the person who organizes things I set the group this question to answer in writing:

"Explain the argument Galileo makes in the Letter to the Grand Duchess Christina for why the Copernican theory does not contradict Scripture. Your answer should address: (a) the principle of accommodation and the patristic source Galileo invokes for it; (b) the distinction Galileo draws between what has been strictly demonstrated about nature and what is merely a matter of scriptural interpretation, and which of the two he thinks should give way when they conflict; and (c) how Galileo's position compares to the one Cardinal Bellarmine lays out in his own 1615 letter to Foscarini on the same subject."

One member — very enthusiastic, but as self-taught as the rest of us — turned in this answer:

"Galileo wrote to the Grand Duchess to defend his fellow Copernicans against the charge that their astronomy was heretical. His central move is to insist that Scripture and nature are both truths that flow from God, so properly understood they cannot really contradict one another; if they seem to, either our science is not yet proven or our reading of the text is wrong, but the text itself cannot be at fault. For this he leans heavily on Augustine, particularly the 'De Genesi ad Litteram,' arguing that Scripture is written in language accommodated to ordinary people rather than as a treatise on astronomy, so it often speaks as though the earth stood still and the sun moved simply because that is how things appear to us. Augustine's caution, as Galileo reads it, is that Christians who insist too rigidly on the literal sense of such passages risk embarrassing the faith once independent demonstration reveals the physical facts — and it's in this spirit that Galileo repeats Augustine's own memorable line that the intention of the Holy Spirit is 'to teach us how to go to heaven, not how the heavens go.' On the second point, Galileo argues that where a conclusion about nature has actually been demonstrated by necessary proof and the senses, it is the scriptural interpretation, not the demonstration, that must be revisited, since Scripture was never meant to settle such questions; but where no such demonstration yet exists, he agrees one shouldn't rashly abandon the traditional reading. Finally, Bellarmine's letter to Foscarini lands in basically the same place as Galileo's, just delivered more cautiously and diplomatically, since Bellarmine as a cardinal had to be careful about appearances: he too says that if Copernicanism were ever really proven, the Church would have to revisit the relevant verses rather than deny the proof."
