Me and my friend Priya got into it again last night over Galen Strawson's Basic Argument -- it was the reading for our philosophy of mind group this week and we ended up still arguing about it in her kitchen till almost 1am. Her position is basically that the whole thing is just hard determinism with extra steps: you act from your character, you never chose your character (regress, regress, regress, you'd have to be causa sui, which is impossible), so no one is ever truly responsible for anything. Fine, I get that part. But then I pushed back with what I thought was actually a pretty good move: if the argument is really just leaning on determinism the whole time, it should stop working the second you introduce genuine indeterminism somewhere in the chain. Like if some of the neural stuff behind my decisions is truly probabilistic, not just unpredictable-to-us but actually undetermined at bottom, then the "you didn't choose that, which flowed from stuff you didn't choose, which flowed from..." chain has an actual break in it somewhere, and that gap seems like exactly the kind of thing that could ground real responsibility, since not everything about me traces back cleanly to factors outside my control.

Priya kept saying "Strawson already deals with this" but couldn't actually explain how, and neither of us had the essay open anymore to check, so we just went around in circles until we both had to give up and go to bed. So now I want an actual answer: is there some reason already baked into the Basic Argument for why my indeterminism move doesn't work, or did I actually find a real gap in Strawson's case? Basically, does bringing in quantum-style randomness get us off the hook where the Basic Argument is concerned, or not?
