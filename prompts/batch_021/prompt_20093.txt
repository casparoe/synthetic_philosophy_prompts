Over dinner last night, a friend convinced me that the argument from illusion settles the question empirically rather than merely philosophically: a straight oar half-submerged in water looks bent, the oar is plainly not bent, so whatever I am directly aware of in that moment must be some other, actually-bent thing rather than the oar itself, and since ordinary, non-illusory seeing feels no different from the inside, the same conclusion should extend to veridical perception generally. I know disjunctivists such as McDowell and Martin are supposed to have a response to this, but after an hour of back-and-forth I could not reconstruct what, exactly, they are entitled to deny, since it seems churlish to insist the oar does not look bent. Once that much is granted, what is actually left in the argument for a disjunctivist to reject?
