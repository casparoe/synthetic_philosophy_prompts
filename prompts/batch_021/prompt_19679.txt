I'm the sophomore representative on my high school's student council constitution committee. Last spring the council passed a "phones away during lunch" policy by a narrow 12–10 vote; a year later, it's genuinely popular. A senior on the committee wants to entrench the policy in our constitution so that repealing it would require a two-thirds vote, arguing that a slim, mood-driven majority nearly killed it in its first month, before the benefits were visible, and that next year's council -- elected by students who never experienced lunch without the rule -- shouldn't get to undo it on a whim. Another member objects that the entrenchment clause itself would only need a simple majority to pass, so this is really just one bare majority tying the hands of every future one, and she points out that a future council could sidestep the whole arrangement anyway by repealing the entrenchment clause with a simple majority right before repealing the policy itself. I need to write the committee's recommendation memo for Monday's full council meeting: whether to adopt the entrenchment clause as proposed, adopt some modified version of it (a sunset date, a higher bar for the clause's own passage, something else), or reject it outright -- and why, engaging the fairness objection seriously rather than just asserting that binding our future selves is obviously wise.
