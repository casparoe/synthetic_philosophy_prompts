I spent thirty years as a geophysicist, and only in retirement have I taken up philosophy in earnest. I now chair my scientific society's honors committee, which is deadlocked over this year's nomination for its highest award, given annually 'for outstanding lifetime contribution to the field.' Our leading nominee is a much-loved science communicator whose work has done more for the society's public visibility and finances than anyone in a generation; the development office has signaled that a substantial pending bequest is far likelier to close if she wins this year. Judged by the citation record and peer esteem we've always applied to past winners, though, her research is respectable but clearly below our historical bar, and two quieter nominees have stronger claims by that standard. Half the committee treats institutional benefit as reason enough to honor her now; the other half insists that would corrupt what the award is meant to certify, even though no outsider would ever learn of the bequest. Draft the short memo I'll circulate before our final vote: recommend honoring her, deferring, or choosing another nominee, and directly confront the strongest argument on the side you reject.
