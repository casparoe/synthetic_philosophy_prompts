I've spent the past few weeks reporting on the flood of AI-generated music, film, and visual art now appearing on streaming platforms and social feeds, and a philosopher I interviewed for the piece kept steering our conversation back to Tolstoy's late treatise "What Is Art?" rather than anything more contemporary. As she explained it, Tolstoy's account of art as "infection" — his claim that a work counts as genuine art only to the extent that it transmits a feeling the artist has actually lived through, with the strength of that transmission depending on the individuality, clarity, and above all sincerity of the feeling conveyed — gives us unusually sharp tools for thinking about art with no feeling human being behind it at all. It is a demanding enough theory that Tolstoy used it to disqualify much of the Western canon he had grown up revering, notoriously including his own novels, alongside works by Shakespeare, Beethoven, and Wagner, while defending simple, broadly accessible pieces that the critics of his day considered beneath serious attention.

I would like to build part of the article around this idea, but before I put words in anyone's mouth, I want a clearer sense of what actually follows from it. Could you spell out the most important implications of Tolstoy's infection theory, together with his related notion of "counterfeit" art manufactured through borrowed subject matter, technical effect, or sheer intellectual interest rather than real feeling, for how we ought to evaluate art produced by large generative models — whether closely directed by a human with a genuine feeling in mind, generated with no particular human intention behind it at all, or optimized mainly to maximize audience engagement? I am especially interested in whether the theory turns out to be more permissive of some AI-generated work than defenders of "real" art might expect, and in the places where it runs into problems Tolstoy himself never had to consider.
