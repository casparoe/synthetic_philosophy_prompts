I'm a junior double-majoring in biology and philosophy, and my philosophy of biology seminar just spent three weeks on what my professor calls the species problem: the biological species concept, the phylogenetic species concept, and half a dozen ecological and cohesion-based rivals routinely sort the very same organisms into different taxa, and a paper we read by the philosopher of biology Marc Ereshefsky argues there is no principled way to crown any one of them the single correct concept. I also work part-time for our university's Natural Areas Program, and my supervisor has asked me to draft the memo our five-person Natural Areas Advisory Committee will vote on this Thursday, the last meeting before Facilities Management's six-week deadline, because the question that has landed on the committee's desk is exactly the kind of case we have been picking apart in class.

Here is the situation. A graduate student's survey of the woodland salamanders in the preserve's north bog turned up a population that might be more than a local variant of the common Desmognathus species found everywhere else on the property. Mitochondrial sequencing puts the bog population roughly 4% diverged from the common one, and it forms its own well-supported clade in every phylogenetic tree the student has built so far, which is exactly the evidence a phylogenetic-species-concept taxonomist wants before splitting a population off as its own species. But along the half-mile ecotone where the bog population's range meets the common species, the two interbreed freely and produce fertile, apparently healthy offspring, which is exactly what a biological-species-concept taxonomist treats as decisive evidence that they are one species and not two. The stakes are concrete: a grant-funded boardwalk trail, already engineered, runs straight through the bog population's only known habitat patch, and rerouting it means significant added cost and a real chance of forfeiting matching funds. One committee member has proposed sidestepping the taxonomic question entirely by designating the bog population an "evolutionarily significant unit" for management purposes regardless of what we ultimately call it; another thinks that just renames the problem instead of answering it.

Could you draft the memo I will circulate before Thursday's meeting? It should recommend one specific option -- reroute the trail, build it as planned, or something else -- on a concrete timeline, and it cannot resolve the dispute by quietly picking a favorite species concept and moving on, since at least two committee members know enough biology to ask why that concept and not another. I need the memo to take the pluralism seriously and still explain how the committee can reach a defensible decision even if the question of whether this is really a separate species turns out not to have a single correct answer.
