I am preparing a graduate seminar on Frege for next term, and I keep going back and forth on how much space to give to the political diary he kept during the final year of his life. In the entries he wrote across March and April of 1924, Frege set down a string of virulently antisemitic, anti-parliamentary, and anti-egalitarian convictions: he wanted Jews stripped of full political rights in Germany, and at several points he seems to welcome the prospect of their removal from the country altogether, alongside a general contempt for the Weimar Republic's commitment to universal suffrage and party pluralism. His family kept the diary out of circulation for decades, and it reached the English-speaking philosophical world only through translation in the 1990s; Michael Dummett, on reading it, wrote that he could no longer regard Frege with the unqualified admiration he had once felt.

What I would like from you is an essay that takes a direct position on the following question: does this diary bear on the philosophical assessment of Frege's work, or is it simply an unrelated biographical fact about a great logician who happened to hold repugnant political views? Take seriously the obvious genetic-fallacy worry -- the soundness of the reduction of arithmetic to logic, and the cogency of the sense/reference distinction, cannot depend on the character of the man who first stated them -- but take equally seriously the less obvious possibility that there is a substantive continuity between Frege's insistence that logical laws, numbers, and "thoughts" (Gedanken) inhabit a mind-independent "third realm," untouched by any individual or communal perspective, and the hierarchical, anti-pluralist temper of his politics. (I will flag, since some sharp student is bound to raise it, that the German phrase Frege himself uses for this third realm is drittes Reich; you are welcome to decide how much, if anything, should be read into that echo.) I want an actual verdict, not a balanced survey: tell me whether, and in exactly what sense, the diary should change how a contemporary philosopher engages with Frege's technical philosophy, and defend that verdict against the strongest objections you can bring against it. You may qualify the verdict -- for instance, by distinguishing its bearing on the validity of the logicist project from its bearing on how we teach or canonize Frege -- but you may not simply conclude that reasonable people disagree.
