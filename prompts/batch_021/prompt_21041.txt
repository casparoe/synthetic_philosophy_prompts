Here's my classmate's answer, copied over exactly (I only fixed two or three typos, didn't touch the content):

"Aumann's theorem says that if two people share a common prior probability distribution over some state space, and they each update on their own private information to get a posterior for some event E, then if their posteriors are common knowledge, the two posteriors must actually be equal. The key assumptions are: a common prior (they start from the same probability function before seeing their private evidence), that they're both Bayesian updaters, that they're both rational and not being strategic about what they report, and that it becomes common knowledge what each person's posterior actually is — it's not enough that they've each privately computed one.

Common knowledge is stronger than Alice and Bob just each knowing the other's posterior — if that's all we had it'd only be mutual knowledge. What actually matters is common knowledge: Alice knows Bob's posterior, Bob knows Alice's, and on top of that each of them knows that the other one knows their posterior too. It's this extra layer — each of them being aware that the other is "in the loop" — that lets each person treat the other's posterior as real evidence to condition on rather than noise, and that's what forces the two numbers to converge.

This is exactly why the theorem doesn't make real disagreement irrational: outside of toy models we basically never get common knowledge of each other's actual posteriors. When my uncle and I argue about politics at Thanksgiving, I have no guarantee he's being fully sincere or that he's actually updated on all the evidence rather than being motivated to believe what he wants, and neither of us can iterate "I know that you know that I know..." more than a round or two before the conversation moves on to something else. There's also no particular reason to think we share a common prior in the first place, since our priors were shaped by totally different upbringings — so even if we somehow did reach common knowledge of our posteriors, the theorem's conclusion isn't guaranteed to kick in anyway.

This connects to what's sometimes called the "no-trade theorem," which is really a direct corollary of the agreement theorem rather than a separate result needing its own assumptions: if it's common knowledge that I want to buy your side of a bet and you're willing to sell at that price, then my knowing you're willing to sell is itself evidence about what you know, and factoring that in should bring our valuations back into line by the same logic as before, so there's nothing left to trade on. Wanting to take the other side of a bet with someone rational who shares your priors is really just disagreeing with them about a probability wearing a different hat, so the same proof goes through unchanged — no extra premises needed beyond common priors and common knowledge that both sides want the trade."
