I'm a senior currently drafting my IB Extended Essay in Philosophy, and I've chosen Sartre's concept of bad faith (mauvaise foi) from Being and Nothingness as my focus. In the course of researching, I noticed that before Sartre lays out his own account, he spends several pages attacking Freudian psychoanalysis, arguing that the model of a repressing "censor" standing between the id and the ego doesn't really explain self-deception at all — it just pushes the same problem down a level, since the censor would have to already recognize the very drive it's supposed to be hiding from consciousness in order to know what to repress. When I brought this up with my philosophy teacher, she asked a question I haven't been able to answer on my own: does Sartre's own alternative, in which a single, undivided consciousness must somehow both know and not know the truth it is fleeing, actually avoid this same paradox, or does it just lose the (admittedly artificial) division into separate mental systems that at least let Freud localize the contradiction somewhere?

For my essay, I'd like to work through this properly rather than just asserting that "Sartre solves the problem Freud couldn't." Could you compare the two accounts of self-deception in detail — Freud's repression model and Sartre's bad faith — specifically on how each one tries to handle the basic paradox that the deceiver and the deceived seem to have to be the same person? I'm especially interested in whether Sartre's appeal to the difference between pre-reflective, non-positional self-awareness and explicit reflective belief genuinely gives him a way out that Freud's picture lacks, or whether it's mostly a relabeling of the same difficulty in new vocabulary. If you think one account is clearly more successful than the other, please justify that judgment rather than just describing both views side by side.
