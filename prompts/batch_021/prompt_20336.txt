I'm working through Spinoza's political writings for a dissertation chapter on early modern theories of natural right, and I keep getting stuck on a tension I'd like help thinking through.

In the Theological-Political Treatise, Spinoza argues that democracy departs least from the state of nature: transferring one's right to the whole body of citizens, rather than to a monarch or a smaller council, is said to preserve something close to natural equality, since natural right is itself nothing more than the extent of one's power. Yet in the unfinished Tractatus Politicus, the final chapter on democracy restricts full citizenship to those who are sui iuris (literally, "in their own right"), and the surviving fragment argues on naturalistic, not merely conventional, grounds that women should be excluded because they are not men's equals in strength of mind — before the manuscript breaks off entirely.

Given that Spinoza's whole political theory is meant to derive its normative force from facts about power and conatus rather than from any independent moral standard, it seems too easy to just write off the exclusion as an unprincipled prejudice grafted onto the theory from outside. Can the argument of that final chapter be reconstructed as a genuine, if empirically dubious, application of his own premises — an extension of how he generally treats natural inequalities of power as right-conferring — or does it expose a fault line that a purely power-based theory of right cannot absorb on its own terms? I'd like to see the strongest case for each reading before I settle on one myself.
