I'm a philosophy PhD student working on formal epistemology and decision theory, and I'd like a dialogue to use as a discussion-starter for my reading group. Set it in Gibbard and Harper's "Death in Damascus" puzzle: a man in the Damascus marketplace learns that Death — a near-perfect predictor — has already settled on a city, Damascus or Aleppo, in which to meet him tomorrow, and he must decide where to be, knowing he dies if and only if he picks Death's city. Write a dialogue in which a causal decision theorist and an evidential decision theorist, both called in to advise him, end up arguing with each other instead. I'd like the exchange to work through why a payoff structure in which matching the predictor is fatal inverts the usual Newcomb-style incentive to align with its prediction; why neither staying nor fleeing turns out to be ratifiable for the causal theorist, and what it costs her view to concede that only a mixed strategy is fully rational; and whether the evidential theorist's own verdict is really more stable, or only looks that way for reasons that would embarrass her in a case like the smoking lesion. Have the two rebut each other's actual moves rather than simply restate their general credos, and the exchange need not resolve into agreement by the end.
