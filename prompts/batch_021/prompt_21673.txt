I'm taking a seminar this term on the history of political thought, and for the past two weeks we've been closely reading Edmund Burke's *Reflections on the Revolution in France* before moving into a unit our instructor has, only half-jokingly, titled "prudence and its discontents," which pairs Burke with some contemporary readings on existential risk and longtermism. Sitting with these texts side by side left me with a question I haven't been able to resolve on my own: what would Burke actually have made of strong longtermism? The view has roots in Nick Bostrom's earlier writing on existential risk and was formalized more recently by philosophers such as Hilary Greaves and William MacAskill; on this view, positively influencing the far future is among the very most important things we can do. As I understand the argument, it trades heavily on the sheer number of people who could ever exist: since that number so vastly exceeds the present population, even a minuscule probability of shaping the far future can carry more expected value than nearly anything we could do for people alive today.

On one hand, Burke's own description of the political community as a partnership stretching across generations, binding the dead, the living, and the unborn, seems like exactly the intuition longtermists want to generalize and place on a rigorous footing. On the other hand, so much of the rest of Burke's outlook cuts against the way longtermists actually arrive at that conclusion: his hostility to abstract, quasi-mathematical reasoning about morals and politics conducted at a distance from lived experience and local circumstance, his preference for inherited prescription over first-principles calculation, and his insistence, in the "little platoons" passage, that our wider public affections must grow out of small, concrete attachments rather than being derived directly from an impartial view of humanity as a whole, let alone of humanity's entire future.

So here is what I would like help thinking through, reasoning as consistently as possible from Burke's own premises rather than from generic "conservative" instincts: on balance, would he have regarded strong longtermism as a natural extension of his intergenerational partnership, or as precisely the sort of dangerous, hubristic rationalism he built his career opposing? I suspect the honest answer is not a flat yes or no, so I would be especially grateful if you could consider whether Burke might have drawn a real distinction between longtermist arguments for guarding against concrete, irreversible catastrophes, such as nuclear war or engineered pandemics, which seem to sit fairly naturally alongside his own emphasis on prudence, and the more ambitious longtermist project of deliberately steering civilization's long-run trajectory for the sake of vast, speculative future populations, which seems much harder to square with the rest of his thought. Please give a considered verdict rather than simply listing considerations on both sides.
