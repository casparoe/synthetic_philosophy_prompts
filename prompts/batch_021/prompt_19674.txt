I spent about thirty years as an operational meteorologist before I retired, and one thing that never left me is the Brier score -- we used it constantly to check whether our probability forecasts were honest ones. Lately I've been going down a bit of a philosophy rabbit hole (there's a program for retirees that lets us sit in on philosophy of science seminars at the university near me), and a couple weeks back we landed on "accuracy-first epistemology," where it turns out the Brier score, or a close cousin of it, does some heavy lifting in an argument for why credences ought to obey the probability axioms in the first place. The argument (I beleive it's originally Jim Joyce's) is supposed to show that any credence function that isn't a genuine probability function gets "accuracy-dominated" -- there's always some coherent alternative that beats it on accuracy in every single possible world, whichever one turns out to be actual. As someone who spent a career worrying about forecast skill, that struck me as a wild claim, and I'd like to actually understand why it's true instead of just taking it on faith the way the seminar leader seemed to want us to.

Here's my specific hangup, though. I looked at a writeup with all the formal conditions on "legitimate" measures of inaccuracy spelled out, and it didn't help me one bit -- too much notation, not enough picture. What I want is the gut-level version: why does stepping outside the probability axioms guarantee that you can be beaten everywhere like that? I have a hunch it's something about the coherent credence functions forming a convex set, with the scoring rule acting like a kind of distance to the nearest "corner" (the all-or-nothing truth assignment), so that anything sitting outside that set can be swapped for something inside it that's closer to every corner at once -- but I can't get that picture to actually hold together in my head. I'd also like to understand why the rule specifically has to be strictly proper for the argument to go through, rather than just any old sensible-seeming way of penalizing wrong credences -- we cared a lot about propriety in forecast verification too, precisely to stop forecasters from hedging, but I don't have a feel for why strictness is the load-bearing part here. And, if it's not too much of a tangent: I have this nagging half-memory that the original version of Joyce's argument had some kind of gap in it that somebody else found later, which eventually got patched up with a cleaner, more geometric proof -- if that rings a bell at all, I'd genuinely like to know what the gap actually was and whether closing it changed the substance of the result or just tidied up the bookkeeping. My daughter's doing a PhD in statistics, and when I described all this to her on the phone she said it sounded like it was really about "Bregman divergences," which meant nothing to me at the time -- feel free to bring that in if it's actually doing real work here rather than just adding more jargon on top of jargon.
