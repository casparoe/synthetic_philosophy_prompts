I've noticed that arguments about bad behavior often turn into arguments about whether the person raising it even has the right to do so, and I've never been sure how much that second question should really count. A clean example of this unfolded in front of me last week, between two friends of mine, Marcus and Dana.

Marcus told Dana he was tired of her canceling on him twice that month. Dana replied that he had no standing to complain, since he had canceled on her three times the previous year and never once apologized. Marcus answered that the two situations weren't really comparable: he had always texted a couple of hours ahead so she could still salvage the evening, whereas she simply didn't show up and explained herself afterward. Dana said that was a difference in style rather than substance: either way she ended up with no plans that evening, and a couple hours' notice barely counts as consideration once someone was already looking forward to something. Marcus responded that even if he really were just as guilty as she claimed, that still wouldn't make her canceling on him acceptable -- it would only mean that they were both open to criticism. Dana countered that this missed her point entirely: she had never denied that canceling on people is bad in general, only that he, given his own record, had no right to be the one lecturing her about it.

Neither was willing to give ground, and I have gone back and forth on it myself ever since. Could you go through their exchange move by move and say who has the better argument at each step, rather than simply declaring an overall winner? I would also like to know whether either of them is missing something important about when a person's own past conduct does, or doesn't, undermine their right to call out the same conduct in someone else.
