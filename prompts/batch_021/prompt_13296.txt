Since retiring from a long career as an experimental physicist, I have drifted, somewhat to my own surprise, from puzzles about hidden variables and the observer's role in quantum measurement toward what turns out to be a closely related family of puzzles in decision theory. My current preoccupation is Gibbard and Harper's "Death in Damascus" scenario: a man in Damascus receives an unmistakable, meaningful look from Death, who is (he is sure) a near-perfect predictor and already has an appointment fixed for the next day, either in Damascus or in Aleppo, according to wherever Death predicted the man would be. If he stays and Death guessed Damascus, he dies there; if he flees to Aleppo and Death guessed Aleppo, he dies there instead; only a mismatch between his choice and Death's prediction lets him live. As I understand the textbook moral, this case is meant to count decisively against evidential decision theory and in favor of the causal variety: the evidentialist's deliberation is said to chase its own tail, since settling on "flee" immediately becomes evidence that Death foresaw flight, which should push him back toward staying, and so on without end, whereas the causal theorist can calmly observe that the man's choice has no causal bearing on a decision Death has already made, and can then invoke Richard Jeffrey's notion of a "ratifiable" choice to recommend randomizing fifty-fifty between the two cities.

What nags at me is that ratifiability, as far as I can tell from my reading, was not devised as a distinctively causal tool at all; Jeffrey introduced it to patch up his own evidential theory against exactly this sort of self-undermining deliberation. If that is right, it looks as though both the evidentialist and the causal decision theorist need the same fixed-point maneuver to arrive at the fifty-fifty verdict, which would make Death in Damascus a good deal less discriminating between the two theories than it is usually advertised to be. I would like you to argue for the position that Death in Damascus does not, in fact, establish the superiority of causal over evidential decision theory once ratifiability is properly credited to both camps rather than treated as a causalist's private rescue device. Please first reconstruct the strongest version of the standard argument for why the case favors causal decision theory, so your rebuttal has something substantial to push against, and then state plainly whether you think any genuine asymmetry between the two theories survives once each is allowed to help itself to ratifiability.
