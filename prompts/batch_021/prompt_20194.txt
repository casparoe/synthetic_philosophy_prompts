Last week I received a mild shock from a wire I had not realized was live; the electrician called in to investigate explained that the shock was so brief only because the circuit was fitted with a GFCI breaker, which cuts the current within milliseconds of sensing that some of it is leaking through a person to earth. Watching him later work on a neighboring line that he left fully live throughout, relying instead on insulated gloves, I found myself thinking back to a university seminar on the metaphysics of dispositions I had taken years ago. My hunch is that the breaker illustrates what is usually called a "finkish" disposition, while the gloves illustrate "masking" instead, though I would like this checked by someone who recalls the literature better than I currently do. Compare finks and masks as distinct ways of generating counterexamples to the simple conditional analysis of dispositional predicates (e.g., "x is live iff x would shock anyone who touches it"), and explain whether Lewis's reformed analysis — which rescues the conditional analysis from finkish counterexamples by requiring the disposition's causal basis to persist for a moment past the onset of the stimulus — handles the masking case equally well, or instead leaves it as a distinct, harder problem.
