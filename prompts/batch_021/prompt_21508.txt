I'm a junior at a high school that offers a small Enlightenment history elective taught almost like a college seminar, and it has just eaten my entire weekend. In 1757, d'Alembert's Encyclopédie article on Geneva praised the city's pastors as more tolerant than their reputation suggested and, almost as an aside, argued the city should finally permit a public theatre, since a well-regulated playhouse would refine manners rather than corrupt them. Rousseau, who had recently reclaimed his Genevan citizenship, answered the following year with a much longer and angrier letter insisting theatre was exactly wrong for a small republic like his home city, and the controversy drew in Voltaire, who was by then living just outside the city walls and took the letter as a personal attack on his own private theatricals.

For our final project, our teacher has asked us to treat Geneva's tangled actual government as a single body, which she calls the "Small Council," and to imagine that d'Alembert's suggestion has landed on its desk as a formal petition to license a public theatre — something she admits never happened quite this cleanly in real life. I've been assigned the councillor responsible for drafting the body's written opinion ahead of Monday's mock session. She was explicit in class that she doesn't want "theatre is frivolous" or "theatre is harmless fun." She wants the specific arguments engaged: Rousseau's claims about what acting does to an actor's own sincerity, about the citizen amusements Geneva already had that he thought a theatre would crowd out, and about mixed seating in the audience — set against whatever d'Alembert and Voltaire could actually say back to those particular points, not a generic defense of the arts.

Could you draft that opinion for me to work from? I'd like it to state plainly whether the Council should grant the license, refuse it, or grant it with specific conditions and a defined trial period; to build that recommendation out of the specific arguments I just described rather than either side's slogans; and to say honestly what the losing side gets right, rather than making the decision look easier than it actually was. A page or so is plenty.
