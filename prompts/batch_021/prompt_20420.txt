The question was: "Molina distinguishes three logical moments in the divine mind. Explain what work middle knowledge is meant to do that simple foreknowledge cannot do, state the grounding objection to it, and say whether Molinism succeeds in securing meticulous providence over a world of free creatures without compromising libertarian free will. Support your answer with reference to at least one objection and one Molinist reply."

Here is the student's answer in full: "Molina distinguishes three types of knowledge in God. Natural knowledge covers all necessary truths and every metaphysically possible state of affairs, and does not depend in any way on what God wills. Middle knowledge covers contingent truths about what any possible free creature would freely choose if placed in a given set of circumstances -- the so-called counterfactuals of creaturely freedom. Free knowledge is God's knowledge of what will actually happen in the created order, and depends on which world God has decided to bring about.

On the older 'simple foreknowledge' picture that Molina was trying to improve on, God simply foresees what free creatures will do once he has already decided to create them, so this foreknowledge becomes available only after the creative decree and cannot itself guide that decree -- providence ends up looking more like educated hope than control. Molina's innovation is to hold that once God, drawing on his natural knowledge of every possible world, settles on the created order he means to bring into being, his middle knowledge of the relevant counterfactuals tells him exactly how each free creature within it will respond, which is what lets him actualize a world that fulfills his providential purposes without himself determining any creature's choice.

The most influential challenge, the grounding objection, holds that counterfactuals of creaturely freedom cannot be true prior to creation because nothing grounds them: they are contingent rather than necessary, so natural knowledge does not cover them; they are not fixed by God's will, since that would make the creature's choice unfree; and the creature does not yet exist to settle the matter by actually choosing. Alvin Plantinga pressed this worry hardest in the course of developing the Free Will Defense: because his notion of transworld depravity requires counterfactuals of freedom to be true of every possible creature prior to God's decision about whom to create, Plantinga is committed to the same apparatus Molina needs, and so inherits the grounding problem along with it. The Molinist reply is to deny that every truth needs an independent truthmaker in this sense, treating some counterfactuals of freedom as primitive contingent facts about possible creatures -- no stranger, on this view, than treating other modal facts as ungrounded. Whether that is a satisfying answer or just a relabeling of the mystery is, I think, the crux of the debate, but it at least shows that the Molinist is not obviously worse off than opponents who also must accept some brute contingent truths somewhere in their system."
