I'm a novelist finishing a book whose central relationship is between a mother and a synthetic replica of her teenage son, built after his death from years of his texts, voice recordings, and home video; the plot deliberately withholds any authorial verdict on whether "he" has an inner life or is simply a very convincing arrangement of matter. To mark the book's publication, my publisher has arranged a public conversation with a philosopher who has built her reputation on a rigorous defense of the conceivability argument against physicalism: the claim that because a being physically and behaviorally identical to a conscious person, yet utterly without experience, is conceivable, such a being is metaphysically possible, which in turn shows that consciousness cannot be a purely physical matter. I've done enough reading in the primary and secondary literature to follow the argument comfortably, and I would rather not spend our stage time letting her simply restate it for an audience that has already read the proof copy.

Could you draft eight to ten questions for me to put to her on stage, each keyed to a specific pressure point in the debate rather than an invitation to summarize the view? I want at least one question on the paradox of phenomenal judgment: the worry that if a being physically identical to us would sincerely report being conscious for exactly the reasons we do, then our own reports about our qualia can't be caused by those qualia either, which seems to undercut the very testimony the thought experiment treats as reliable evidence. I want at least one on whether the two-dimensional-semantics machinery she presumably relies on to license the move from conceivability to possibility actually survives the "strong necessities" objection, given that the primary/secondary-intension distinction was designed for easier cases like water and H2O. And I want one that puts to her the fact that Robert Kirk, who introduced the zombie idea into this debate in the first place, later recanted and argued at length in his 2005 book Zombies and Consciousness that the beings aren't coherently conceivable after all. For each of these three, add a brief follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give, and close the sequence with a question that's genuinely hard to deflect: since a zombie is stipulated to be indistinguishable from the rest of us in both behavior and physical makeup, what, if anything, could count as evidence against her position?
