So in my seminar on 19th-century Black political thought we just spent a week on how much Frederick Douglass's view of the Constitution flipped over his life, and it's kind of messing with my head. Early Douglass is basically on the Garrison train - the Constitution is a "covenant with death," made by slaveholders for slaveholders, and real abolitionists shouldn't even vote or hold office under it. Then by that 1860 Glasgow speech (something like "is it pro-slavery or anti-slavery," I don't remember the exact title) he's doing this very deliberate move where he reads the text on its own terms, separate from framers intent and separate from how slaveholding courts actually apply it - and on that reading there's nothing pro-slavery in the document at all, the pro-slavery reading is basically an interpretive con that abolitionists themselves had been buying into for years.

A couple weeks later on the syllabus there's also that speech of his about America as a "composite nation," where he makes this very universalist, natural-rights case for why Chinese immigrants belong in the country just as much as anyone, at a moment when anti-Chinese sentiment on the west coast was getting pretty ugly. Which got me thinking about something the syllabus doesn't touch at all: Douglass died in 1895, a few years before Wong Kim Ark (1898) settled that a kid born in the US to Chinese immigrant parents who couldn't themselves naturalize was still a citizen under the 14th amendment. He never got to weigh in on that case, and obviously never got to weigh in on the current fights over birthright citizenship for kids of undocumented immigrants and what "subject to the jurisdiction thereof" is supposed to mean - the executive order stuff that's currently working its way through the courts.

So for my term paper: given his specific interpretive method from the Glasgow speech (text over framers intent, text over how power actually applies the clause) plus the universalist commitments from the composite nation speech, can you actually reconstruct the argument he'd have made about the current birthright citizenship fight, instead of just giving me a generic "he'd obviously side with immigrants" answer? I'm mainly interested in whether his own framework, taken seriously, forces a determinate answer here, or whether there's a point where it just runs out and anything past that is basically us guessing.
