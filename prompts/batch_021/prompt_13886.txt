Last night a friend and I had a long argument about whether Hilary Putnam's model-theoretic argument against metaphysical realism does anything genuinely different from Skolem's Paradox, or whether it is simply Skolem's 1922 result transposed from set theory into philosophy of language. My friend maintained that once you accept the lesson of the downward Löwenheim–Skolem theorem — that any consistent first-order theory with an infinite model has a countable one — you have already granted Putnam everything he needs: anyone who takes Skolem's result seriously as a genuine puzzle about set theory has no principled basis for resisting Putnam's conclusion that reference is radically indeterminate. I argued that Putnam needs considerably more than Skolem ever claimed, including a permutation argument, the "just more theory" maneuver against causal or operational constraints on reference, and an application of the Löwenheim–Skolem theorem that Timothy Bays has argued is technically mistaken, though Tim Button thinks Putnam's conclusion survives regardless. Compare the two arguments directly and tell me who had the better of it: explain what work the theorem is actually doing in each case, whether Skolem's own conclusions about the "relativity of set-theoretic notions" already commit anyone who accepts them to something like Putnam's antirealism about reference, and whether one can coherently treat Skolem's Paradox as a genuine mathematical result while rejecting Putnam's extension of it into semantics. I want a real verdict, not just a summary of the existing debate.
