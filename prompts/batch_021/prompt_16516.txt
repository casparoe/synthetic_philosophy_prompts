So in my epistemology seminar this week we've been slogging through higher-order evidence and I think I've fully melted my brain on the akrasia part of it. The basic setup makes sense to me: you form a belief in P off of totally solid first-order evidence, and then you get some (maybe misleading) higher-order evidence suggesting your reasoning about P specifically is unreliable right now — like the classic case where you're at a party, you nail some bill-splitting calculation through careful reasoning, and then someone tells you the punch might've been spiked with a drug that messes with people's logical/arithmetical reasoning about half the time, even though it feels totally normal from the inside. The puzzle is: what happens to your confidence in the original calculation once you learn that? Stay just as confident and you look dogmatic, like you're ignoring real evidence about your own reliability. Lower your confidence while your first-order evidence for the calculation hasn't budged an inch, though, and you end up "epistemically akratic" — believing P while also believing (or at least suspecting) that you're not rational to believe P.

My prof said there's a genuine split on this in the literature, though I'll admit I might be garbling names at this point. I think it's Horowitz who has this accuracy-based argument that akrasia is always irrational full stop, because the akratic state is somehow always dominated by some other, non-akratic state. Then apparently there's a newer line (Worsnip? pretty sure that's right) claiming that in misleading-higher-order-evidence cases, the requirement to follow your first-order evidence and the requirement to stay coherent across levels can just flat out conflict, so you get a genuine rational dilemma instead of a clean answer, meaning the "never be akratic" principle is actually too strong. And apparently some people just deny the whole setup and go "level-splitting," i.e. there's no general requirement linking first-order and higher-order beliefs in the first place.
