For my philosophy of physics seminar this term, we've spent the last few weeks on the statistical-mechanical account of the thermodynamic arrow of time, and this week's reading finally got to Boltzmann's own defense of the H-theorem against Loschmidt's reversibility objection and Zermelo's recurrence objection. What struck me most was Boltzmann's eventual fallback position: rather than giving up on deriving a one-directional increase in entropy from time-symmetric microdynamics, he proposed that the universe as a whole is overwhelmingly likely to be in thermal equilibrium at any given moment, and that creatures like us simply happen to inhabit a rare, localized, low-entropy fluctuation away from that equilibrium. The added twist is that any such fluctuation defines its own local "arrow of time" for whatever beings exist inside it, pointing away from the fluctuation's entropy minimum in whichever direction that happens to lie, regardless of how it relates to any external, universe-wide notion of before and after.

Reading ahead in the syllabus, I noticed that our final unit turns to the "Boltzmann brain" problem in contemporary cosmology: the worry that in a universe (or region of an eternally inflating multiverse) that persists forever with a positive cosmological constant, rare thermal fluctuations should eventually generate vastly more isolated, momentarily-conscious observers with false memories of an orderly past than they generate whole low-entropy histories like the one we seem to remember, which several physicists treat as a serious problem for, or even a de facto refutation of, cosmological models that allow it. It occurred to me that this is essentially Boltzmann's own nineteenth-century fluctuation move, just relocated to de Sitter space and given his name back as a label.

I'd like help thinking through how Boltzmann himself, based on the actual physical and philosophical commitments he displayed in his exchange with Zermelo, would most plausibly have reacted to this modern descendant of his idea. Would he likely have regarded it as an expected, even welcome, extension of the statistical worldview he was already defending, or are there disanalogies between a fluctuating classical gas and a fluctuating quantum de Sitter vacuum (say, concerning what kind of "equilibrium" is doing the work, or how seriously to take an infinite future) that would plausibly have given him pause, or at least demanded a different sort of response than the one he gave Zermelo? I'd appreciate a fairly detailed, textured reconstruction rather than a generic remark about how a nineteenth-century physicist would marvel at modern cosmology.
