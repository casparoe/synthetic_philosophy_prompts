Here's their answer, copied exactly:

"Illusions like the straight stick that looks bent when it's half in water, or a round coin that looks elliptical from an angle, are the starting point. The naive realist thinks perceiving is just a direct relation between a subject and mind-independent objects and their properties, with nothing "mental" getting in the way. But the argument from illusion says: the stick looks bent, and by the phenomenal principle, if something looks F there must be something that actually is F. Since the stick itself isn't bent, there must be some other item, a sense-datum, that is bent and that we're really aware of. Because the illusory experience can be subjectively indistinguishable from a veridical one, the same story is supposed to generalize to ordinary "good case" perception too, so we end up always aware of sense-data rather than external objects directly.

Disjunctivism resists this by denying that the good case and the illusory case share a common underlying mental element (the "Highest Common Factor"). Instead, "S looks F" can just mean S looks the way Fs typically look, without requiring any actual F item to be present, so no sense-datum is needed. This view was first put forward by McDowell in the 1980s specifically to get around the Gettier problem, by treating veridical perception itself as a distinct, factive mental state rather than a justified true belief that could turn out to be accidentally false.

I think disjunctivism actually has an easier time with hallucination than illusion, since in hallucination there's simply no object there at all, so the disjunctivist can just say nothing perceptual is going on, whereas illusions involve a real object being misperceived in a way that seems to invite a common causal story after all."
