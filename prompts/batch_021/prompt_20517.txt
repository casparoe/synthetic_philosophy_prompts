I'm a retired geophysicist (spent about 35 years doing seismology, mostly on subduction zones) who finally started reading Thomas Kuhn this year, mostly out of embarrassment that I'd spent a whole career throwing around the phrase "paradigm shift" at conferences without ever reading the book it comes from. It's a strange experience, because I actually lived through the tail end of one of the standard modern illustrations people use for his idea -- when I was a grad student in the early 70s a couple of my advisor's colleagues still wouldn't say "plate tectonics" out loud in seminar, they'd talk around it like it was a bit embarrassing. Anyway, that's the background to something I'm actually dealing with right now.

I sit on the small scientific advisory panel of a private foundation (won't name it) that hands out maybe half a dozen "high-risk" seed grants a year, specifically aimed at proposals that don't fit the normal NIH/NSF review categories -- the whole premise of the fund is to catch things ordinary peer review supposedly cant. This cycle we've got a proposal from an independent researcher, previously a postdoc in a solid mainstream lab, asking for two years of support to develop an alternative account of what actually drives plate motion, one that doesnt just tweak the standard slab-pull/ridge-push/mantle-convection picture but redefines several of its core terms outright, such that -- he argues -- the usual tests of the mainstream theory can't even be brought to bear on his version without begging the question. Our two mainstream reviewers both wrote scathing reports along the lines of "this doesn't engage with the literature because it structurally can't, it isn't describing the same entities anymore" -- and the applicant's response, approvingly, is "yes, exactly, that's what Kuhn says revolutionary science always looks like from inside the old paradigm, you're just proving my point for me."

Half the panel wants to fund him mostly because the mainstream reviewers so obviously can't get any traction on him, which they take as a good sign in itself. The other half thinks "you can't judge me, we're incommensurable" is just a costume bad science learned to wear after reading the same book I just did, and that Kuhn's own picture gives us no principled way to tell the two apart from inside the room, in real time, before history's had a chance to sort it out for us. I have to write the panel's decision memo for next week's meeting -- fund, decline, or fund with conditions -- and whichever way it comes out I'd like it to actually engage with that second worry rather than wave it away, since that's the thing that's actually got us stuck, and it needs to be something I can put in front of the room and defend on the spot.
