I cover science and ideas for a living, which is apparently enough to get you roped onto committees like this: I sit on the ethics and standards committee for a science writers association I've belonged to for years, and we've been stuck on the same case for two meetings running. A member's piece, based on the usual university trial writeup, included the line "Patients who received the compound were symptom-free after eight weeks." That's true, it's lifted almost word for word from the paper's own results section. What she left out, which only turns up if you actually dig into the supplementary tables, is that the placebo arm hit almost the identical symptom-free rate over the same stretch, so the drug never actually beat placebo. Her defense is that she never asserted it did, and that if a reader had emailed her point-blank asking whether the drug outperformed placebo, she'd have said no -- so nothing false was technically claimed. About half the committee finds that persuasive. The other half thinks its beside the point, since nobody writes a line like that in a "promising new treatment" story without expecting readers to walk away thinking the drug worked, and our accuracy policy right now only bars statements that are "false or fabricated," full stop.

I mentioned the case to a philosopher friend over drinks and he went off about Grice for a solid twenty minutes -- the said-versus-implicated distinction, the cooperative principle, some test about whether an implication can be "cancelled" without contradicting yourself -- and it genuinely clarified the disagreement more than either meeting did. It also opened up a new front, though: some members now want the ruling to hinge on whether she actually intended readers to draw the false conclusion, which nobody can prove either way, while others think intent is a red herring and all that should matter is what an ordinary reader comes away believing.

Could you draft the memo I'll circulate before our next call in a couple weeks? I need it to rule on this specific case -- sanction, no sanction, or something in between, with an actual timeframe attached -- propose wording for the accuracy policy that would keep us from relitigating this from scratch every time some version of it comes up again, and take the "it's true, so it's not our problem" camp seriously rather than wave it off, since a couple of them raised a real worry about what happens to careful reporters once we start policing implications instead of statements.
