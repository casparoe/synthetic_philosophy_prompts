I'm a PhD student working on the epistemic foundations of game theory, and I keep running into a dispute that textbook treatments tend to resolve too quickly. Aumann (1995) argues that common knowledge of rationality (CKR), formalized in the standard partitional model of knowledge, forces the backward-induction outcome in any game of perfect information: since a strategy is a complete contingency plan, rationality can be required to hold even at nodes the equilibrium path never reaches, and common knowledge of this is enough to rule out every other outcome. Stalnaker's belief-revision-based reply denies that this is really forced by CKR alone. On his reconstruction, once play reaches a node a rational opponent "should" never have reached, what a player is entitled to infer about that opponent's continued rationality depends on a substantive, independently chosen revision policy — and under equally defensible policies, CKR turns out to be compatible with outcomes other than backward induction.

Compare these two treatments of off-path reasoning. Does Stalnaker's diagnosis show that Aumann's result quietly smuggles in a contestable assumption under the guise of pure epistemic logic, or does Aumann's static semantic framework genuinely avoid needing any belief-revision policy in the first place? Defend whichever side you find more convincing.
