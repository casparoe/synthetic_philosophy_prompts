So I fell down a rabbit hole this week reading old EA forum posts about "moral weight" and animal sentience (the Rethink Priorities stuff, plus a bunch of Brian Tomasik essays from like a decade ago), and predictably someone in the comments brought up Kant as the historical villain of the piece -- the guy who supposedly thought animals are basically biological automatons that dont actually have any inner experience, Descartes with extra Prussian rigor, and thats why he said we have no direct duties to them. Fine, I can buy that as a weird 18th century prior about animal minds even if it's obviously wrong now.

Except that's the exact same guy whose whole argument for why cruelty to animals is still bad is that it dulls your sympathy and makes you more likely to be cruel to people later (theres that famous bit from the ethics lectures about the man who shoots his worn-out dog). And that just seems straight up incoherent to me? If animals literally can't suffer in any meaningful sense, what does "cruelty" toward one even consist of, and why would doing it to what's essentially a biological machine corrode anything in your character? It's like Kant wants to deny they have inner lives when thats convenient for denying them moral status, then quietly smuggle real suffering back in the moment he needs a story for why we still shouldn't be jerks to them. Is this an actual contradiction that people just paper over because "indirect duties" sounds sophisticated, or am I misremembering the setup somewhere?
