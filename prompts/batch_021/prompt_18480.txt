I've spent the last few weeks reporting a piece on symmetry in physics and metaphysics, and one thread of it — why physicists insist that two electrons are not merely practically but fundamentally indistinguishable, with no hidden "identity tag" separating them even in principle — led me, over dinner with two friends, straight back to Max Black's old thought experiment about two iron spheres. For context: Black asks us to imagine a universe containing nothing but two spheres of equal size, floating exactly two miles apart, and alike in every quality you could specify without already presupposing which one you mean — same mass, same temperature, same chemical composition, same age. He uses the case, in a paper that appropriately enough takes the form of a dialogue between two speakers, to argue against Leibniz's principle of the identity of indiscernibles: the claim that no two distinct things can share literally all of their properties. If the spheres really are two, and really share every property, the principle is false. My friends Dana and Theo spent most of the meal arguing about whether that's actually right, and I haven't been able to settle it myself.

Dana, who has a physics background, opened with (1): there are two spheres, by hypothesis they are exactly alike, so the principle simply fails, end of story. Theo, who minored in philosophy, answered with (2): the case only looks like it involves two objects because we default to picturing ordinary flat space, but the very same scenario could be redescribed as a single sphere in a space that loops back on itself, so that travelling two miles in any direction just returns you to the sphere you started from; if that redescription fits exactly the same qualitative and relational facts, there's no basis for insisting there are two things rather than one, and Leibniz survives. Dana pushed back with (3): the natural reading of Black's universe is ordinary, unbounded space, not the closed-up kind Theo's redescription requires, and in any case a thought experiment only has to describe one genuinely possible case to refute a principle that's supposed to hold with necessity — Theo would have to show that two spheres in flat space is impossible, not just that some other, topologically strange case can be redescribed. Theo shifted ground with (4): fine, grant flat space and two distinct spheres — but then each one is discernible after all, via a relational fact like "the one two miles from that other sphere"; the only way to keep them truly indiscernible is to also throw out ordinary relational facts like "two miles from an iron sphere of the same mass," and if you're willing to throw out that much, you've excluded far more than mere identity-facts, so Dana still owes him a principled, non-question-begging line between the relations that are supposed to count and the ones that aren't. Dana's answer was that a relation like "stands two miles from some sphere of such-and-such mass" doesn't name or point at either sphere and so can be true of both without presupposing which is which, whereas something like "identical to that one" can't be stated without already picking one out. Theo wasn't convinced this fully met his challenge, and by the time dessert arrived neither of them had budged.

I'd like help adjudicating this properly — move by move, rather than just declaring an overall winner. Could you go through (1) through (4) and say who has the better of the argument at each step? I'm especially interested in whether Theo's redescription move in (2) genuinely threatens Black's case or merely relocates the problem, and whether Dana's closing distinction actually answers the challenge Theo raises in (4) or just restates which side she's on. Finally, is there some assumption you suspect both of them are relying on without ever really defending it?
