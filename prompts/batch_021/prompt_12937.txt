My younger sister is getting married in a few weeks to a guy, Daniel, who's finishing a classics PhD, and his dissertation has a whole chapter on the wine-drinking material in Plato's Laws -- like Books 1 and 2, not the Symposium, which is the one everbody actually knows about. He explained the gist to me at their engagement party after a couple of glasses of wine, which felt very on-the-nose, and I haven't been able to stop thinking about it since: apparently the Athenian Stranger spends ages arguing AGAINST the Spartan and Cretan guys in the dialogue, who think drinking parties should just be banned outright, insisting instead that getting people a little drunk under the eye of a sober elder is a genuinely useful test of character, because the wine loosens people up enough that you can see who they really are and gently correct them. There's also this whole other bit about how it helps rigid, self-conscious old men soften up again, like iron does in a fire, so they can be reshaped and actually sing and dance without shame. I cover science and ideas for a living and this is exactly the kind of thing that makes the job fun.

I'm giving the toast at the reception and want to build it around this material properly, not just drop "haha the Greeks liked wine" as a throwaway line. The tricky part is the room: half the guests are Daniel's classics department, including his advisor, who will absolutely clock it if I mangle the philosophy, and the other half is our extended family, who will glaze over the second it turns into a lecture on ancient Greece. Could you write me the toast -- something like three to four minutes out loud -- that actually uses the logic of Plato's argument to say something true about what marriage is (the "testing and reshaping each other's character over decades, gently, with someone sober-ish keeping watch" angle seems like it's sitting right there for the taking, though I'm open to being wrong), lands at least one line that works as a real laugh for the classicists and as pure warmth for everyone else, and please, for the love of god, doesn't lean on "in vino veritas" as the punchline. It's been done to death.
