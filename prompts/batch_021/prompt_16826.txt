I've been down a Galileo rabbit hole lately (started as a history-of-science podcast thing, then I actually went and read the primary texts), and I keep getting stuck on the 1615 Bellarmine-Foscarini-Galileo triangle. Everyone tells this story as "dogmatic churchman vs. reasonable empiricist," but when you read the Letter to the Grand Duchess Christina closely, Galileo's own rule for when scripture has to be reinterpreted is actually pretty demanding: he keeps insisting it should only happen once there's a "necessary demonstration," not just a nice or plausible-sounding account. Which makes me wonder if he actually met his own bar. No stellar parallax had been detected yet (and wouldn't be until Bessel in 1838), the telescope discoveries killed naive Ptolemaic geocentrism but left Tycho's geo-heliocentric hybrid completely untouched (Venus shows the same phases either way in that system), and the one physical argument for the earth's motion Galileo actually produced, the tides business in the Dialogue, is now considered just wrong.

So here's what I want: actually argue for a specific thesis, don't hedge into a "both men had a point" conclusion. The thesis is that, judged by Galileo's own hermeneutic standard from the Christina letter, Bellarmine's cautious "fine as a calculating device, not as physical truth until its really proven" position in the letter to Foscarini was the more consistent application of that very standard than Galileo's own conduct was. Commit to it and build the strongest case you can, engaging with the actual texts and the evidential situation (the demonstration requirement, the accommodation doctrine, the tides argument, the Tychonic alternative) rather than just re-running the generic "science vs. religion" framing everyone reaches for.
