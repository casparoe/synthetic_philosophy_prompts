I've been reading up on the history of celestial mechanics for a side project involving n-body simulations, and I ran across a detail about Newton that I hadn't expected. I'd like your help thinking through it.

In the concluding Queries of the Opticks, Newton argued that the solar system is not really self-sustaining: mutual gravitational perturbations among the planets and comets would, left alone, gradually throw the orbits into disorder, so God had to step in every so often to "set the system right" again. Leibniz mocked this in his correspondence with Samuel Clarke (writing on Newton's behalf), comparing Newton's God to a watchmaker too incompetent to build a clock that doesn't need constant repair. Laplace's perturbation theory later seemed to side with Leibniz: it showed that, to the order he calculated, the classical orbital elements merely oscillate rather than drift, so the system looked stable on its own, no divine patching required. But twentieth-century numerical work (Laskar and others) complicated that tidy resolution yet again: it turns out the solar system is mildly chaotic, with a Lyapunov time of only a few million years for the inner planets, and long-run simulations give something on the order of a one-percent chance that Mercury's orbit becomes unstable enough within the next few billion years to risk a collision.

Given what we know of Newton's actual theological commitments — an interventionist, voluntarist God who actively governs rather than setting up the system and stepping back, his rejection of Leibniz's principle of sufficient reason, and his professed distaste for speculative "hypotheses" — would Newton have read this final twist as vindicating his providential argument, or would his own methodological commitments have forced him to reject that reading? Please argue for one answer rather than just surveying the possibilities.
