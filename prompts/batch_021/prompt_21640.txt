I write about science and ideas for a magazine, and I've spent the last few weeks reporting a feature on how the AI-safety and rationalist communities invoke "Solomonoff induction" as though it finally puts Occam's razor on rigorous footing. The pitch, as I understand it, goes like this: Solomonoff's universal prior weights each computable hypothesis at roughly 2^-K(x), where K is the hypothesis's Kolmogorov complexity, and Solomonoff proved that a predictor built this way converges on the true, computable data-generating process as evidence accumulates, provided that process gets some nonzero weight in the prior to begin with. This is the same apparatus behind Marcus Hutter's AIXI, which some in AI-safety circles treat as a model of what ideal, unboundedly rational inference actually looks like — so the claim that it vindicates Occam's razor is supposedly doing real argumentative work, not just dressing up an old intuition in mathematics.

Two philosophers of science I interviewed for the piece both resisted that claim, but for what struck me as quite different reasons. One said the whole thing is subtly circular: which hypotheses count as "simple" depends on a choice of universal Turing machine, and although the invariance theorem guarantees that any two such machines will assign a given string complexities differing by no more than some fixed constant, that constant depends on the pair of machines and can be made arbitrarily large — so for any two actual hypotheses a scientist would ever compare, a suitably perverse choice of machine can reverse their complexity ranking, making "machine-independence" a much weaker result than it sounds. The other largely granted that point but thought it missed the sharper problem, which is that Kolmogorov complexity is provably uncomputable — so nothing anyone could actually run, on any machine, is "Solomonoff induction" — which makes it strange to cite the theory as an account of why simplicity is, or ought to be, truth-conducive in the bounded, approximate reasoning real scientists do.

I'd like help thinking through who has the better of this. Please write an essay that takes a real position: does the Solomonoff-induction/Kolmogorov-complexity framework give Occam's razor a genuine, non-circular justification, or does it just re-describe the preference for simplicity in more technical language while leaving the original epistemological problem exactly where it was? Engage specifically with the invariance theorem and the machine-dependence worry, with the uncomputability objection, and with what Solomonoff's convergence theorem does and doesn't show (tracking the truth in the limit is not the same claim as "simpler hypotheses are more often true"). A hedged verdict is fine — e.g., that the framework answers one objection but not the other — but don't just list considerations on each side; commit to a specific, defended conclusion.
