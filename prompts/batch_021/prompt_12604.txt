In the seminar on the ethics of war I'm taking this term, our TA posed a hypothetical that turned into a real argument between two classmates. The case: State A invades State B with no credible justification whatsoever (no self-defense claim, no Security Council authorization — nothing of the sort). Consider one conscript in A's army: he never wanted to fight, was falsely told B struck first, and once deployed fires only on B's uniformed combatants while observing the same rules of engagement his counterpart defending on B's side does. Are the two soldiers equally justified in killing enemy combatants, given that both stick to the same rules? Roughly, the exchange went like this:

(1) Marcus said yes: the A soldier is doing exactly what the B soldier is doing — firing on people who are firing on him, under identical constraints — and blame for the war belongs to the leaders who started it, not to a conscript with no real way of checking his government's claims.

(2) Elena disagreed: the B soldier is resisting an unjustified attack while the A soldier is carrying one out, and that asymmetry doesn't vanish just because their conduct looks identical from outside. A mugger who politely observes the "rules" of a knife fight isn't thereby the moral equal of the person defending themselves.

(3) Marcus replied that the A soldier's ignorance of the war's real cause is exactly what should excuse him — which is why international law prosecutes leaders for launching aggressive wars, not ordinary troops for fighting in one.

(4) Elena answered that an excuse isn't a justification — ignorance can reduce blame without making the killing permissible — and besides, it says nothing about soldiers who do suspect, or know, their cause is unjust and fight regardless.

(5) Marcus said that denying equality altogether guts the whole logic of having reciprocal laws of war — POW protections, surrender norms, restraint — since none of it works unless both sides are treated symmetrically.

(6) Elena granted that this justifies enforcing those rules as policy, but said a reason to apply a rule symmetrically isn't a reason to think both sides are equally justified in killing: we can keep the convention for good practical reasons while still holding that, morally, only the defender is in the right.

Neither budged. Could you go through the exchange move by move, say who has the stronger case at each step rather than just naming an overall winner, and note anything important that both of them seem to be missing?
