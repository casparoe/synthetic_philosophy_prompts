I'm a PhD student in philosophy of mind, currently drafting the dissertation chapter that surveys higher-order theories of phenomenal consciousness, and I keep running into the same sticking point. Almost every version of the higher-order thought (HOT) family locates phenomenal character in the content of a monitoring representation rather than in the first-order state that representation is supposed to be about. That commitment is what generates the familiar "misrepresentation" or "targetless HOT" worry: since the higher-order state can in principle misfire, occur with no first-order state to track, or misdescribe the state it does track, the theory seems forced to allow that a phenomenally "conscious" episode could be generated purely as an artifact of the higher-order representation, decoupled from whatever is actually happening downstream. My advisor thinks this worry cuts equally deep against every higher-order-style theory; I suspect the specific architecture each theory builds in (dispositional vs. actualist HOTs, perception vs. thought, "wide" intrinsic states, self-representation, etc.) actually makes them differentially exposed to it, and I'd like a second opinion before committing to that claim in the chapter.

Could you rank the following six theories by how exposed they are to this specific objection -- not by their overall plausibility, empirical support, or popularity, just by how directly their architecture commits them to the possibility of a phenomenally "conscious" episode generated by a misfiring or targetless higher-order representation: (1) Rosenthal's classic actualist HOT theory, on which an occurrent, assertoric, non-inferential HOT suffices for consciousness; (2) Carruthers' dispositionalist HOT theory, on which a first-order state need only be disposed to produce a HOT via a "mindreading" faculty; (3) Lycan's higher-order perception (inner sense) theory, which models monitoring on perception rather than thought; (4) Gennaro's Wide Intrinsicality View, on which the HOT is an unconscious intrinsic part of a single complex conscious state rather than a distinct meta-state; (5) Kriegel's self-representationalism, on which a state represents itself as well as its ordinary object; and (6) Brown and Lau's HOROR theory, which recasts the higher-order state as a representation of a representation rather than as a representation of the subject-as-being-in-a-state.
