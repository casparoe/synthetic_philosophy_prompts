This term, my seminar has moved from Leibniz's theory of truth directly into Hintikka's possible-worlds treatment of knowledge, and I want to work out how the two positions might bear on one another. On Hintikka's semantics, an agent knows that p just in case p holds at every world compatible with her information, which has the notorious consequence that she thereby knows every logical consequence of what she knows, including every logical truth — this is the "problem of logical omniscience." Leibniz, for his part, distinguished truths of reason, which resolve into identities through a finite analysis, from truths of fact, whose containment of predicate in subject can be exhibited only by an infinite analysis that finite minds cannot complete. He also held that the possible is simply whatever is free of contradiction, a possible world being a maximal set of compossible substances. Given these commitments, how do you think Leibniz would have assessed the problem of logical omniscience: would he treat our evident failure to know every logical consequence of our beliefs as showing that the possible-worlds model of knowledge is defective, or merely that it describes a reasoner unlike us? And would he have accepted Hintikka's own proposed repair, which admits "impossible worlds" at which contradictions can hold, or would his definition of possibility as freedom from contradiction commit him to rejecting that fix as incoherent on its face? Please defend your answer by appeal to Leibniz's actual doctrines rather than generic rationalist sympathies.
