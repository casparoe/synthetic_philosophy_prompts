I'm putting together next week's session for my early modern political philosophy seminar and I keep getting stuck trying to phrase the discussion question cleanly, so let me outsource the thinking to you for a sec.

We're pairing two texts that don't normally get put next to each other: Pascal's Pensées and Rousseau's Social Contract, on the relationship between force and justice. There's a fragment in the Pensées (I don't have the number memorized, its the "justice, force" one) where Pascal says justice and force really ought to coincide but in practice never do, and since we couldn't manage to make what's just strong, we settled for making what's strong count as just -- people obey laws not because they've checked that the laws are just but because the laws are established. He's got a nearby fragment making the darker follow-up point: ordinary people must never be allowed to see that this is how things actually work, because the whole arrangement depends on everyone believing the laws really are just rather than merely enforced. Then in Book I, chapter 3 of the Social Contract, "The Right of the Strongest," Rousseau opens from what looks like almost the same place -- force is a physical power, not a moral one, so yielding to a gun in your face is prudence, not obligation, and "might" can't by itself generate "right" -- except he refuses to land where Pascal lands, and spends basically the rest of the book trying to locate a genuinely legitimate basis for authority (the general will, etc) instead of just managing the appearance of one.

So here's the actual question: do Pascal and Rousseau really disagree here, or is Rousseau just declining to accept a conclusion that arguably follows from premises the two of them share? Walk through the actual structure of both arguments rather than their reputations. Then, factoring in each thinker's wider commitments -- Pascal's stuff on custom and the imagination, Rousseau's move to the general will -- tell me whose overall position you think ends up more coherent, and justify it.
