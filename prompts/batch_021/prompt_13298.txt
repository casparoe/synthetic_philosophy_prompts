I chair our university's academic integrity board this year (inherited the job when the usual chair went on sabbatical -- lucky me), and we've landed on a case that's turned into a weirder probability puzzle than I bargained for. Short version: in a 300-student intro course, a cluster of students gave the exact same, oddly specific wrong answer to a hard question, which looks a lot like a leaked photo of the exam circulated before test time. Room limits meant the exam ran in two sittings, morning and evening, each split across two rooms, so we've got four suspect groups -- Morning-1, Morning-2, Evening-1, Evening-2 -- and going in we had no actual reason to think any one of the four more likely than the others.

Then a proctor came forward, reluctant to name a specific student, and gave us exactly one piece of information: if the leak started in the evening sitting, she'd guess it's something like four times more likely to have come out of Room 2 than Room 1. She wouldn't venture anything about morning versus evening, or about the two morning rooms -- that's the entire tip. Now the board's split on what to do with it. Four of us want to update "locally": leave the morning/evening split right where it was, 50/50, and only sharpen the room breakdown within the evening group. But Priya, the stats postdoc we recruited onto the board for exactly this kind of thing, ran the numbers through some kind of formal updating procedure and insists that's actually incoherent -- that done correctly, the update has to move the overall morning/evening split too, by a non-trivial amount, and that this could genuinely change who we go after first. It nags at me that this must be a known type of case from some probability seminar I sat through in grad school, but I can't retreive the resolution from memory, and "it feels weird" doesn't strike me as a good enough reason to override Priya's math.

Here's the constraint we're actually under: before Friday's grade-submission deadline, we only have the investigative bandwidth -- swipe-log pulls, handwriting comparison, interviews -- to go hard at one group, or to spread that same total effort across two or three, and putting a formal hold on a group we get wrong is genuinely costly for the many innocent students in that group. Could you draft the memo I'll circulate to the board tonight? I need it to land on one specific allocation of the time we've got left rather than a menu of options, to actually take a side on whether Priya's global update or the "local" one is the one we should trust, and to give the losing view's strongest argument a fair hearing rather than just waving it off.
