In the program-equilibrium framework introduced by Tennenholtz (where players submit programs that can read one another's source code, rather than choosing actions directly), so-called FairBot-style agents secure mutual cooperation in the one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma: each cooperates with the other just when it can find a proof, within some fixed formal system, that the other cooperates with it. Löb's theorem is credited with guaranteeing that this pair of proof searches actually succeeds, rather than collapsing into the infinite regress you would expect from a naive program along the lines of "cooperate if and only if I can show that you cooperate with me." I can follow the formal derivation, but I would like a genuinely intuitive, gut-level explanation of what Löb's theorem is contributing here: why does recasting the cooperation condition in terms of provability, rather than the counterpart's actual behavior, let the reasoning terminate, and in what sense is the resulting cooperation non-circular rather than simply the same circularity wearing formal dress?
