I work as a software engineer, not as a mathematician or philosopher, but over the past few months I have been teaching myself mathematical logic in my spare time, largely to get a better handle on the theoretical foundations of proof assistants like Lean, which I have started experimenting with for a side project. Recently I worked through the Löwenheim–Skolem theorem and the accompanying "Skolem's paradox," and something about it has stuck with me in a way I cannot quite shake. As I understand the theorem, any consistent first-order theory with an infinite model also has a countable model, and since ZFC is a first-order theory over a countable language, this applies to ZFC as well. The trouble is that ZFC also proves Cantor's theorem, which says that the power set of the natural numbers is uncountable. So it looks like we can produce a model of ZFC that is, from the outside, only countably infinite in size, while that very same model contains an object that it internally certifies as uncountable. That strikes me as more than a curiosity — it looks like a genuine contradiction, where either Cantor's theorem does not actually establish what everyone claims it establishes, or the Löwenheim–Skolem theorem simply should not apply to a theory as strong as ZFC.

I raised this with a colleague who has a much stronger pure-math background than I do, and he brushed it off by saying that "countability just is not absolute," but when I pressed him, he could not really explain what that meant beyond restating the paradox in slightly different words, so I do not think he had actually resolved anything. My own reading is that the whole episode shows there is no model-independent fact about whether a given set is countable or uncountable — that Cantor's theorem is really a statement about what holds inside one particular model rather than a truth about infinity that survives across all of them equally. Given that, I am having trouble seeing why mathematicians keep treating a claim like "the real numbers are uncountable" as a plain, objective fact instead of something that is only ever true relative to whichever axioms and model happen to be in play at the time. Shouldn't Skolem's paradox make everyone considerably more careful about calling any set "uncountable" without qualification?
