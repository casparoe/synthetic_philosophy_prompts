As a software engineer who relies on large language models daily, I've become interested in an older debate that seems to bear on them unexpectedly: Thomas Reid versus David Hume on the epistemology of testimony. Hume, discussing miracle reports in the Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding, treats trust in testimony as reducible to ordinary induction: we believe witnesses because experience shows a reliable correlation between reports and truth, making testimonial justification a special case of cause-and-effect reasoning. Reid rejects this, arguing that two innate, non-inferential principles — what he calls, in the Essays on the Intellectual Powers of Man, veracity and credulity — make testimony a basic source of justified belief, on a par with perception and memory, operating before a child could have gathered the experience Hume's account requires.

Suppose Reid met an LLM: something producing fluent, assertion-shaped text without anything like the belief, sincerity, or communicative intent his theory presupposes. Would he say such output simply isn't testimony — demoting it to a mere natural sign, evidence read inductively the way smoke is read as evidence of fire — so his anti-reductionism quietly collapses into Hume's reductionism for exactly the sources we're least equipped to treat as fellow sincere minds? Or can a committed Reidian extend credulity to non-human testifiers without abandoning what makes the view anti-reductionist? I'd like your best reconstruction of how Reid's actual arguments bear on this, not just a general impression of where "common sense philosophy" would land.
