Random question inspired by a hike I did last summer -- I was on a ridge trail with a friend who's got a pretty serious fear of heights, and she swore the drop-off looked way steeper and closer to her than it did to me, like actually visually steeper, not just that the same-looking slope scared her more than it scared me. Turns out this is apparently a real, studied thing (fear and desire changing how steep a hill looks, how far away a glass of water looks when your thirsty, stuff like that), and a philosopher friend told me it's a stock example in the whole "cognitive penetration of perception" debate -- basically whether what you believe, want, or fear can actually reach down and change your perceptual experience itself, versus perception being a sealed-off module (Fodor's picture), where bias only creeps in afterwards, at the level of judgment or interpretation.

This has got me rethinking a subplot in my current novel, which is basically an Othello-style jealousy arc. I realized what I'd drafted so far is just the boring, uncontroversial version of this -- a guy interpreting ambiguous stuff uncharitably because he's jealous, which is judgment-level bias nobody really disputes. What I actually want, though, is a self-contained short story (doesn't need to tie into my novel at all) where a character's jealousy or fear seems to genuinely reach into what he perceives, faithful to the harder, contested claim in that debate, and where the plot actually turns at some point on whether it was "real" perception or after-the-fact interpretation -- bonus points if his perception seems to keep confirming itself, deepening the suspicion in a kind of feedback loop. A page or two is plenty, and run with whatever setting and characters work best -- I just don't want the philosophy underneath to be hand-waved.
