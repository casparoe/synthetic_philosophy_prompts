I'm a PhD student in cognitive science working on visual development, and this week our lab's journal club worked through Held, Ostrovsky, Sinha, and colleagues' study of children treated for dense congenital cataracts through the Project Prakash initiative in India. As I understood it, shortly after these children's sight was restored, each was given a novel object to explore only by touch and then asked to pick it out by sight alone from a set of options; on that very first test, before they had any chance to build up visual experience, they performed at essentially chance level. That looks to me like a direct, controlled empirical test of Molyneux's problem — the old question of whether someone blind from birth, the instant she gains sight, could tell a cube from a sphere by vision alone before having any opportunity to visually relate what she sees to what she has already learned by touch. Locke and Berkeley both predicted a negative answer, Leibniz thought reasoning alone might get the newly sighted person to a correct answer anyway, and the experiment seems to come down squarely on the Locke/Berkeley side.

When I brought this up with my advisor, she said most philosophers of perception would still resist calling Molyneux's problem "solved" by this result, but she had to run to a meeting and never explained why. I'm stuck on what the holdout would even be about: for three centuries this was framed as a question with a definite right answer about what would happen in a specific scenario, not unlike a prediction in physics, and now that the scenario has actually been staged in a lab, the result came down clearly on one side. Is there a substantive reason philosophers might resist treating this as settling the question, or is this just a case of an empirical result outrunning a debate that people are reluctant to let go of?
