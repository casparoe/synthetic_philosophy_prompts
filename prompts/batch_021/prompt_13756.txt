Never took a philosophy class in my life, but retirement's given me time to read, and lately I've been on a decision-theory kick. Something's bugging me that nobody seems to actually adress head on.

I spent my career as a population geneticist, and there's a classic bit of theory there: in a fluctuating environment, selection favors whichever genotype has the higher *geometric* mean fitness across generations, not the higher arithmetic mean -- a lineage can have a great arithmetic mean fitness and still go extinct with probability 1 if the variance is high enough, because what accumulates over generations is the product of fitnesses, not their sum. Its the whole logic behind bet-hedging traits, like some desert annuals only letting a fraction of their seeds germinate in any given year, so one bad year can't wipe out the whole lineage.

Now I keep running into the Kelly criterion and this "geometric rationality" / ergodicity-economics stuff (Ole Peters and that crowd), and it's obviously the same math transplanted into betting: maximize expected log wealth instead of expected wealth. Fine so far. But in the biology case, "geometric mean wins" is just a descriptive fact about what happens to survive, no different in kind from water flowing downhill. The decision theorists want that same math to spit out something normative -- that maximizing expected log wealth is what a rational person ought to do, even for a bet they'll only ever face once. Is that actually a legitimate move, or is "geometric rationality" just riding on the biology analogy to sneak an ought out of an is? And please don't just answer "log utility is concave, that's risk-aversion" -- that dodges the point, since the whole claim on offer is that this is supposed to be something more than a repackaged utility function.
