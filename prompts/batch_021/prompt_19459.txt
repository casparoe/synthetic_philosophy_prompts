So I'm a few years into a PhD in political philosophy, and for one of my dissertation chapters (on the scope of Nozicks side-constraints) I went back and reread the bit in Anarchy, State, and Utopia where he briefly takes on animals - I think it's the section literally called "Constraints and Animals," somewhere near the end of chapter 3. He basically floats the idea that ordinary morality runs on something like "utilitarianism for animals, Kantianism for people": animals get weighed in an aggregative utilitarian calculus (their suffering counts, but it can in principle be outweighed by enough benefit to others), whereas people get the full Kantian side-constraint treatment, where you can't just use them as a means no matter how good the payoff is. He's obviously not fully comfortable resting there though - he gestures at a "sliding scale" where moral status tracks something like psychological complexity, so more sophisticated animals might deserve stronger, more Kantian-ish protections, but he never really works out where the lines would actually fall.

I was arguing about this with a couple of people in my cohort last week and we couldn't agree on just how damaging that sliding-scale move actually is to the overall view. For the chapter I want a genuinely concrete illustration rather than just restating the problem in the abstract. Could you construct an actual example or thought experiment - involving specific beings, either real species or a stipulated hypothetical like a cognitively "uplifted" animal or a human with some particular condition - that brings out why the two-tier structure (plus the sliding-scale patch) can't avoid drawing an arbitrary, unprincipled line somewhere? Ideally the example should still cause trouble even if you're allowed to insert extra intermediate tiers between "full Kantian" and "pure utilitarian," so it's not the kind of thing you can just solve by adding more gradations. Would love something concrete enough that I could actually put in front of my committee, rather then just more handwaving.
