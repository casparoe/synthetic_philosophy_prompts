I'm a PhD student in philosophy working on distributive justice, and in my graduate seminar this term we spent a session on the problem of interpersonal comparisons of utility, moving from Robbins's classic objection that such comparisons rest on judgments no science can adjudicate, through Harsanyi's and Sen's later attempts to rehabilitate comparison for aggregative purposes. The point that's stuck with me, made almost in passing by our instructor, is that the real philosophical weight in these debates doesn't fall on whether interpersonal comparison is possible at all, but on which method of comparison we adopt once we've decided to make one — and that this prior choice can quietly settle the distributive question before any principle of justice has even been applied. I haven't managed to find an example that makes this vivid rather than merely asserted, and I'd like to work one out with your help, since it's going to form the opening move of a paper I'm drafting.

Could you construct a concrete allocation scenario involving two people (or two groups) competing for some scarce, divisible good? One of them, because of a history of deprivation, has adapted their preferences and expectations downward, so that they report relatively high satisfaction despite objectively worse circumstances; the other has not adapted in this way, so their self-reports track their circumstances more directly. I'd like the example built so that a utilitarian who compares the two by taking self-reported satisfaction at face value is committed to one distribution of the good, while a utilitarian who instead compares them via some alternative standard — a capability metric, an objective-list account of well-being, or a counterfactual "preferences absent adaptation" standard — is committed to the opposite distribution. Please make the rankings concrete enough (rough utility numbers are fine) that the reversal actually falls out of the two comparison procedures rather than being stipulated, and then say explicitly which step is doing the real ethical work: the aggregation rule itself, or the antecedent choice about how to render the two people's welfare commensurable.
