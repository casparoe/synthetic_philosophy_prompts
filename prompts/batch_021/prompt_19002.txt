I've spent a lot of time reading rationalist blogs and forums, and I've noticed that whenever Rawls's treatment of religion comes up in a comment thread, people tend to reach for slogans like "overlapping consensus" or "duty of civility" without engaging much with how specific his actual machinery can get. What convinced me otherwise was rereading the introduction to the paperback edition of Political Liberalism alongside "The Idea of Public Reason Revisited," where Rawls treats abortion as a genuine test case rather than staying at the level of slogans. He argues there that a reasonable balancing of the political values at stake — something like due respect for human life, the orderly reproduction of society over time, and women's equality as free and equal citizens — tells against any comprehensive doctrine that would flatly prohibit abortion in at least the earliest stages of pregnancy, and that insisting on such a prohibition to that extent makes a doctrine unreasonable rather than just one more entrant in the space of reasonable pluralism. That's a considerably stronger and more specific conclusion than the "neutrality" framing usually leads people to expect, and it's a little poignant given that Rawls reportedly started out as a theologically serious, devout Christian as an undergraduate before losing that faith during the war and, by his own later account, never fully recovering it.

I'd like you to bring that same level of specificity to two developments Rawls did not live to see. First, Dobbs v. Jackson Women's Health Organization (2022) and the wave of state bans on abortion from conception that followed it, several defended explicitly in religious terms invoking ensoulment or the sanctity of life. Second, the rise of a postliberal current in political theory — sometimes called integralism in its more explicitly Catholic form — associated with figures like Adrian Vermeule and Patrick Deneen, which argues that Rawlsian public reason is not neutral at all, but is itself a comprehensive liberal doctrine that illegitimately forces religious citizens to bracket their deepest commitments before they can participate in politics. Please work through the actual moving parts — the proviso governing when comprehensive reasons may enter public debate, the duty of civility, the distinction between reasonable and unreasonable comprehensive doctrines, and overlapping consensus — rather than defaulting to a generic "Rawls would just oppose theocracy" answer. I'm especially interested in whether you think the post-Dobbs legal landscape makes the integralist charge that his neutrality is covertly substantive harder or easier for a committed Rawlsian to rebut than it would have been in the 1990s.
