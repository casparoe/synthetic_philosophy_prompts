One of my colleagues down the hall, Priya, is getting married in three weeks, and because I am apparently "the funny one" in the department she's asked me -- a philosopher of religion who barely remembers her decision theory from grad school -- to give the toast at the reception. Priya's whole research program is on money-pump arguments and diachronic rationality; her dissertation was on whether an agent with cyclic (or even just incomplete) preferences can be pumped dry over time, and half her talks end with someone asking whether the fix is to be, in Edward McClennen's terms, a "resolute" chooser rather than a "sophisticated" or "naive" one. I'd like the toast to actually use that distinction to say something true about marriage -- basically, that vows only do their job if you're resolute about them (sticking to the plan even when a later self, faced with some marginally more appealing alternative, would want to renegotiate), and that treating the marriage as something to be re-optimized at every node, however "rational" that sounds locally, is exactly the dynamic-inconsistency trap that gets you pumped.

Here's my worry: half the room is going to be her cohort from grad school, who will notice immediately if I botch the resolute/sophisticated distinction or lean on the obvious Ulysses-tied-himself-to-the-mast joke that every decision theorist has heard at every wedding since Elster wrote about it. I looked into it a bit and I actually think that example is a bad fit anyway, since tying yourself to a mast is restricting your future options, which isn't quite what McClennen means by resolute choice -- he means you keep the option open and just don't take it, which if anything is the harder and more marriage-relevant version. The other half of the room is her extended family, who think a "money pump" has something to do with slot machines and will loose interest completely if this turns into a seminar. Can you write me a five-minute toast, maybe 600-700 words, that gets the theory genuinely right, includes one actual joke that only really lands if you know what a money pump is, and still ends with everyone -- philosophers and non-philosophers alike -- raising a glass and feeling something?
