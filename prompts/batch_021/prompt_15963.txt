So I'm a physics PhD student but I've been sitting in on a philosophy of science seminar this semester, and last week we spent basically the whole session on the hole argument in GR, which kind of broke my brain a little. As I understood the setup: because Einstein's field equations are generally covariant, if you take a solution and apply an active diffeomorphism that's the identity everywhere except inside some small "hole" region, you get a second solution that agrees with the first everywhere outside the hole (including on a Cauchy surface prior to it) but disagrees inside it. So you've got two solutions consistent with identical initial data that diverge for the hole region itself. That sounds like a real breakdown of determinism, but when I mentioned it to a couple people in my cohort they just said "relax, it's just a gauge transformation, like re-choosing coordinates, the two solutions are physically identical, nothing to see here" - which honestly made total sense to me, and I figured that was basically the end of the story, the same way nobody looses sleep over gauge freedom in E&M or Yang-Mills.

Then this week's reading was all Earman and Norton, and apparently a lot of philosophers think there's still a serious, live metaphysical dispute here between substantivalism and relationism that doesn't just dissolve once you point out the diffeomorphism/gauge structure? I don't really get why "it's just like re-choosing coordinates" doesn't immediately kill the whole argument - isn't that exactly the standard move physicists make with gauge symmetries all the time, and doesn't it prove the "indeterminism" was never physical to begin with, just a redundancy in the formalism? Would love someone to actually walk me through what I'm missing here, because right now it feels like philosophers are manufacturing a metaphysical crisis out of something any first-year GR course already resolves in passing.
