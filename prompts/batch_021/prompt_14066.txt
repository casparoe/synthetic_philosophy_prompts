I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums long enough that it's apparently rubbed off on my friend group, and a dispute I watched play out recently in a group chat is a good example. Ilana and Marcus co-designed a card game together back in their first year of grad school, self-published it, and it slowly built a modest cult following over about eight years. They never bothered writing up a formal ownership agreement, since it was just a fun side project, so when a mid-size publisher bought the IP outright for $120,000 a few months ago, they had to work out a split from scratch. Rather than just doing 50/50, they decided to treat it as a bargaining problem and asked a mutual friend with an economics background, Deborah, to run the numbers under two different formal bargaining solutions. Marcus needs the money for a specific goal (a house down payment) and treats each dollar as worth about the same as the last one, all the way up to the full $120k, so Deborah modeled him as having linear utility in money; Ilana is more financially comfortable and agreed her marginal dollar was worth noticeably less the more of the pie she already had, so Deborah modeled her utility as proportional to the square root of her dollar amount. Both had a disagreement point of $0 (what each would get if the deal simply fell through). Under those assumptions, the Nash Bargaining Solution gives Ilana $40,000 and Marcus $80,000, while the Kalai-Smorodinsky solution gives Ilana about $45,800 and Marcus about $74,200.

Once actual numbers were attached to actual names, the argument got heated fast, and neither of them backed down. (1) Marcus argued for the Nash split, since it's the standard solution in economics, uniquely pinned down by a small set of axioms that includes independence of irrelevant alternatives (IIA) -- the idea that other splits neither of them would ever actually pick shouldn't be able to move the one they do pick. (2) Ilana said that's true but beside the point: under Nash she'd get only about 58% of what she'd get in her own best-case scenario (the whole $120k), while Marcus would get about 67% of his own best case, and a genuinely fair solution shouldn't let one party concede proportionally more from their own ceiling than the other does -- which is exactly what Kalai-Smorodinsky guarantees by construction. (3) Marcus replied that "your own best-case scenario" is a hypothetical neither of them would ever actually accept, so it's precisely the kind of never-to-be-chosen alternative that shouldn't carry any normative weight, and the fact that Kalai-Smorodinsky's answer depends on it is a bug, not a feature. (4) Ilana said the deeper problem with Nash's solution is that it isn't monotonic -- your own payoff can fail to improve, or even get worse in absolute terms, when your own options improve -- which is essentially why Kalai and Smorodinsky proposed swapping out Nash's IIA axiom for a monotonicity axiom in their 1975 Econometrica paper. (5) Marcus said none of that has any bearing on their situation, since their pie and both utility curves are completely fixed and nobody's options are changing, so whatever the merits of that objection to Nash bargaining in general, it's irrelevant to picking a formula for one static, one-off split. (6) Ilana closed by pointing out that IIA is exactly the same kind of property as monotonicity in that respect -- both are claims about how a solution behaves across related bargaining problems, not facts about any single fixed pie -- so Marcus doesn't get to wave away her objection as "not about this case" while still resting his own argument on an axiom of the very same type.
