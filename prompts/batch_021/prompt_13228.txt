So i fell down a bit of a rabbit hole reading about the Vienna Circle recently and learned Carnap wasn't just the "let's eliminate metaphysics through logic" guy from my undergrad philosophy of science class. Turns out he and Otto Neurath (who apparently ran real economic planning offices — first studying "war economies" in WWI, then briefly heading planning for the short lived Bavarian Soviet Republic) were pretty open about wanting society's problems handled the same rational, empirically-grounded way as science. Their 1929 manifesto explicitly ties the whole "scientific world-conception" thing to progressive social reform, against the metaphysical/theological worldview they associate with reactionary politics. Carnap seems to have shared a good chunk of that outlook through his friendship with Neurath, even if he was never the activist Neurath was.

Anyway, this got me thinking about the old "socialist calculation debate" — Mises, and later Hayek, arguing that centralized planning can never work because you can't gather up all the dispersed, tacit, local knowledge you'd actually need, an argument aimed pretty directly at Neurath's proposals. There's basically a new version of this same debate happening right now, except the pitch is "yeah but we have AI and massive amounts of data now, so maybe Hayek's objection doesn't really hold anymore." Obviously Carnap died in 1970 and never saw any of this.

So given what you know of Carnap's actual epistemology — his fallibilism, his insistence that claims only earn their meaning through testability, his general allergy to big unverifiable pronouncements dressed up as certainties — plus his political sympathies via Neurath, what do you think he'd actually say to someone trying to sell him on "don't worry about Hayek, we've got AI now"? Would his pro-planning leanings make him receptive, would his logical scruples make him suspicious of the grand claims on that side AND on the free-market side, or some mix of both? Feel free to really get into the weeds on this, i'm more interested in how those two parts of his thinking would actually interact than in a generic "he'd probably be cautiously optimistic" answer.
