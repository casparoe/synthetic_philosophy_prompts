This got personally relevant fast so bear with the setup. My mom was in a bad car accident last year, and the settlement finally came through in two seperate pieces because of some quirk in how the driver's insurance and a totally different claim (against the tire manufacturer, over a defect) got handled -- don't ask me to explain the legal mechanics, I just saw the numbers on the page. For the main insurance settlement, she had to choose between (A) a guaranteed $600,000, or (B) letting it go to arbitration instead: 10% chance the arbitrator bumps it to $3,000,000, 89% chance it stays at $600,000, 1% chance a technicality voids the whole claim and she gets $0. She took the guaranteed $600k without much hesitation. Then, a couple months later, the seperate tire-defect claim came with its own choice: (C) join the main settlement pool, an 11% chance of $600,000 and an 89% chance of $0, or (D) join as one of a handful of "lead" claimants instead, only a 10% chance of anything, but if it hits it's $3,000,000, otherwise nothing. This time she went with D.

I brought this up at a family dinner and my uncle Dave (did an econ degree decades ago, never misses a chance to mention it) and my aunt Carol (a family therapist who's convinced Dave weaponizes math to win arguments) ended up arguing about it for like twenty minutes. Dave's opening move was that if you strip out whatever's identical between A and B, and separately whatever's identical between C and D, what's actually being decided between is the exact same underlying bet both times -- so taking the safe side of that bet in round one and the risky side of it in round two isn't tracking any real difference in the gamble, just how it happened to be dressed up, and that this is basically a textbook violation of what he kept calling "the independence axiom," half-remembered from some stats class (he had no idea this exact setup is apparently a famous, decades-old example with its own name -- neither did Carol). Carol's comeback was that nothing about mom's choices struck her as inconsistent at all: dropping from a sure thing down to 89% is a completely different kind of loss than dropping from an 11% shot down to a 10% shot, even though it's "the same" one point on paper, so why should anyone be forced to treat those as equivalent? Dave replied that if you let preferences bend that way, you can in principle chain a sequence of trades together and pump money out of the person for nothing, which is exactly why economists don't treat that axiom as optional. Carol shot back that the pump only works if mom would actually agree to reverse her answer when the same underlying bet gets relabeled back and forth in one sitting, and her two decisions were made months apart, under different procedures, over different pots of money entirely -- so there's no real pump available even in principle, and there are whole respectable decision theories built to accommodate exactly this pattern without falling apart that way. Dave's last word was: fine, maybe not a literal pump then, but Carol still owes an actual account of why the same one-point shift matters so much more in one spot than the other, and "certainty just feels different" isn't an account, it's a fancier-sounding label for the bias. Neither budged, and by the time dessert came out everyone else had checked out of the conversation.

I keep turning this over and can't decide who actually had the better of it.
