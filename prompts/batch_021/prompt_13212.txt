I'm teaching Hegel's Philosophy of Right in my graduate seminar this term, and we just spent two sessions on the material on sovereignty and war, right before he pivots into world history. My students got pretty worked up about the passage where he compares war to the wind that keeps the sea from stagnating (the "ethical health of nations" bit), plus his swipe at Kant's perpetual peace essay for assuming states can be bound by treaty the way individuals are bound by contract, when there's no judge above sovereign states to enforce anything. Somebody asked what I thought Hegel would make of the nuclear age and I didn't have a great answer on the spot, so now its been bugging me and I want to actually work it out.

Here's where I keep getting stuck. Hegel's case for war having positive ethical significance seems to depend on the same risk-your-life-for-recognition logic he uses for self-consciousness in the master-slave dialectic, just scaled up to states: citizens have to be willing to stake their private, particular existence, their property, their bodies, for the sake of the whole, and that willingness is what elevates ethical life above the system-of-needs bookkeeping of civil society. And the whole section caps off with that line he more or less lifts from Schiller, that world history is basically the only tribunal there is, which is how wars between states end up mattering: they're part of how that tribunal hands down its verdicts. Mutually assured destruction seems to break this in two directions at once: a war both sides know will be a wash for everybody can't function as a tribunal, since nobody survives to receive the verdict, and meanwhile so much of actual fighting now is done by drone and precision strike, where the attacking state's own citizens never risk a thing, which guts the recognition-through-risk mechanism from the opposite side.

So which is it: would Hegel look at deterrence theory and conclude it's just achieved, by other means, the perpetual peace he thought was both impossible and undesirable, a permanently calm sea with none of the storms he thought nations needed, or is there a way to run the argument where the sheer existential stakes of a standoff, even with nobody actually fighting, still count as keeping a state's ethical substance from going stale? I'd like an actual reconstruction of how the argument would have to go, not just "he died a century before the bomb, who's to say."
