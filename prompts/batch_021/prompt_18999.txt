so we've been doing hume in my philosophy elective and i can't stop thinking about how rough of a time he'd have with modern philosophy of probability, which obviously didnt exist yet in the 1700s. today theres this whole debate between frequentism (probability = long-run relative frequency over repeated trials), subjectivism/bayesianism (probability = your personal degree of belief, which has to be "coherent" or else a bookie can dutch-book you into a guaranteed loss), and propensity theory (probability = an actual objective tendency built into the setup itself, even for a single case -- basically Popper's answer to stuff like single quantum events that frequentism cant handle since there's no "long run" of one event). given that Hume's entire thing is that we never perceive necessary connection in nature, only constant conjunction, and that even our idea of causal necessity is just a habit of mind we project onto objects, which of those three is he actually stuck defending, and where does it start to hurt him? does frequentism's "limit as trials go to infinity" move sneak back in exactly the kind of unperceivable necessity binding events together that he spent his whole career denying? is propensity theory just necessary connection wearing a lab coat? and can subjectivism even work for him, seeing as Bayesians think there's a real rational requirement forcing your credences to be coherent, when Hume's other big move is that reason is the slave of the passions and doesnt get to boss around belief like that? he'd definately have a rough time picking one -- don't just tell me "he'd probably like the empiricist-sounding one," actually work through which view his own arguments trap him in and defend it.
