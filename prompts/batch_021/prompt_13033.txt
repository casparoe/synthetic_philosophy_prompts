I've been asked to give one of the toasts at the wedding of two friends from my department, Mara and Josh, who are both finishing dissertations on Sartre -- hers on bad faith and self-deception, his on the account of the Look and being-for-others in Being and Nothingness. I got to know them properly in a reading group we ran through Part Three of that book a couple of years ago, and it has since become a running joke between the three of us (though I don't think it's entirely a joke) that whoever toasts them had better not reach for "soulmates," "meant to be," or "everything happens for a reason," on pain of a visible wince from the head table. I'm in the same department but work on philosophy of mind, so I know these commitments well enough to take them seriously without being at all confident I can honor them in five minutes of after-dinner remarks.

The trouble goes deeper than dodging a few clichés, though. Sartre's own account of love in Being and Nothingness is not obviously wedding material: each lover wants to be loved by a freedom that remains free even as it is captured, which is exactly why he lines up love, masochism, and language as one set of strategies and indifference, desire, and sadism as another -- two failed attempts, on his account, to resolve a conflict built into being-for-others that neither one actually resolves. Mara is fond of pointing out that "hell is other people" gets quoted at weddings far more often than it gets understood. So a toast for these two needs to do more than gesture at warmth in the abstract: it should find whatever is genuinely affirmable about a lifelong commitment using Sartre's own vocabulary -- existence preceding essence, facticity, radical freedom -- something like a marriage as a project chosen and rechosen rather than a fate discovered, without quietly smuggling back in the essentialist comfort they'd both call bad faith, and without taking the "concrete relations with others" material so seriously that the toast turns into a seminar and loses their parents, college friends, and the half of the room that has never opened Being and Nothingness.

Could you write that toast? I'm picturing roughly five minutes, something a Sartre scholar would recognize as honest to the actual texts rather than a softened, textbook version of them, but that still works as a toast -- warm, a little funny, and fully legible to the guests who won't catch a single reference.
