A friend of mine who studies philosophy of physics recently challenged something I had always taken for granted: the idea that quantum indeterminacy gives libertarian free will some room to operate, since a measurement's outcome isn't fixed until it occurs. Her objection was that this only works if something like the Copenhagen interpretation is correct, on which measurement outcomes are genuinely, irreducibly chancy. Both of the leading rivals to Copenhagen deny this while reproducing exactly the same experimental predictions: on Everett's many-worlds theory, the universal wave function evolves with no randomness at all, and the appearance of chance is really just uncertainty about which branch you will find yourself on, while on Bohmian mechanics (the de Broglie–Bohm pilot-wave theory) every particle has a definite position and trajectory at all times, carried along by a "pilot wave" that evolves deterministically, and the apparent randomness we measure in the lab is just our ignorance of those precise initial positions (the so-called quantum equilibrium hypothesis) rather than a fact about nature itself. If either of these rivals is right instead of Copenhagen, the indeterminacy-based escape hatch for free will was never really there — even though no experiment that has ever been run, or ever could be run, would tell you which of the three is correct.

Write a short story that takes this seriously as its philosophical engine, rather than treating "quantum" as a byword for mystery or freedom. Its protagonist — a physicist, or someone closely connected to one — should come to be genuinely convinced specifically of Bohmian mechanics, rather than the more familiar many-worlds route, as the correct description of reality, and should have to reckon with what that costs: a single, settled history of hidden variables and trajectories fixed since the guiding wave's initial conditions, with no branching to multiply their choices and no irreducible chance to hide behind, only their own ignorance of a fact that was already decided. Make the physics specific enough that a reader familiar with all three interpretations could tell this isn't just generic many-worlds or observer-collapse mysticism wearing a different name: nonlocality, the pilot wave, and the equilibrium explanation of apparent randomness should be doing real work in the story's texture, not merely named in passing. Setting, plot, and tone are otherwise up to you; aim for something in the range of 800 to 1200 words.
