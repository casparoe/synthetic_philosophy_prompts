Here's the question as it appeared on the exam (worth 10 points total, split a=4/b=4/c=2):

"Eli Hirsch claims that many disputes in ontology — for instance between mereological universalists (who accept unrestricted composition: for any two objects there is a further object that is their sum) and mereological nihilists (who think only simples exist and deny there are composite objects at all) — are 'merely verbal' rather than substantive. (a) State Hirsch's criterion for when a dispute counts as verbal. (b) Explain why Hirsch thinks the universalist/nihilist dispute satisfies it. (c) Briefly present one objection to Hirsch's conclusion."

And here's the student's answer, word for word:

"(a) For Hirsch, a dispute is merely verbal when each side's assertions, once translated charitably into the other side's language, come out as things the other side would already accept as true. If both sides can recognize the other's position as true in their own terms, there's no real disagreement about the facts of the matter, just a difference in what they mean by words like 'object' or 'exists.' This is what he calls 'quantifier variance': the idea, which he traces back to some of Putnam's remarks about conceptual relativity, that there's more than one equally legitimate candidate meaning for the existential quantifier, and no privileged one that metaphysics can uncover.

(b) The universalist says that whenever there are two objects (say, some simples arranged trout-wise and some arranged turkey-wise) there's a further object that is their fusion. The nihilist denies this, insisting only the simples are real. But both sides agree on every fact about the simples themselves — their arrangement, their causal powers, everything down to the last detail. So the universalist's sentence 'there is a further object' just means, in the universalist's dialect, 'there are simples arranged thus-and-so,' which is exactly what the nihilist already believes. Since each side's sentences translate into truths the other side accepts, and neither can point to any further fact that would settle things, Hirsch concludes that composition-talk doesn't track any fact of the matter at all, and the question that so occupied van Inwagen — when do several things compose a further thing? — turns out to be empty.

(c) The standard reply, due to Sider, is that Hirsch's argument only goes through if no candidate meaning of the quantifier is metaphysically privileged. Sider agrees multiple candidates exist but denies they're all on a par: meanings can differ in how well they carve nature at its joints, the same way a natural predicate like 'green' carves better than a gerrymandered one like 'grue.' If existence itself can be more or less joint-carving in this way, there's a fact about which dialect is the metaphysically better one to use, and the dispute is substantive after all, not verbal."
