So I've been reading around for a seminar on philosophy of religion and stumbled back onto this odd little David Lewis paper, "Do We Believe in Penal Substitution?" -- yes, that David Lewis, modal realism guy, taking a weird detour into atonement theology near the end of his career. As I remember it, he takes seriously the standard objection that penal substitution (the idea that Christ was punished "in place of" sinners, thereby satisfying justice on their behalf) is basically a category mistake: you can pay off someone else's debt, but you can't serve someone else's prison sentence in any way that makes moral sense, because guilt and desert just aren't transferable the way a monetary obligation is. Lewis then pokes at how confident we should really be in that objection once you look closely at how we actually reason about blame, standing-in, and vicarious liability in ordinary, non-theological cases. He doesn't come down cleanly on either side, which is honestly what made him fun to teach with.

For my next class I want a dialogue to hand out, so could you write one for me between two philosophers, A and B, hashing this out from scratch? (They don't need to mention Lewis by name or know the paper -- I just want that kind of argument in the room.) A holds the "category mistake" view: punishment, unlike a fine, is essentially tied to the guilt of whoever bears it, so a substitute isn't really being punished at all, just made to suffer in a way that resembles punishment. B thinks this is too quick and pushes back with cases where substituted liability seems perfectly intelligible -- military discipline, legal fictions, a volunteer taking the fall for a friend -- and presses A on what principle is actually doing the work in ruling out penal substitution specifically, as opposed to those other cases. Neither should be a pushover: B has to really answer A's point about desert not transferring, and A has to actually engage with B's best cases rather then just repeating the objection louder. It doesn't need to be long, just long enough that they make some real progress, or at least end up clear on exactly where they disagree, instead of talking past each other.
