I'm reporting a long-form piece on the so-called metaontology wars, and before I try to explain the dispute to readers who have never taken a philosophy course, I want to test it against one concrete case: Eli Hirsch's "trout-turkey," the mereological fusion of the front half of a trout and the back half of a turkey. A compositional universalist is committed to saying this fusion exists as a single object; ordinary usage denies that any such "thing" exists at all. Hirsch takes the resulting dispute to be merely verbal: universalist and common-sense speakers are each speaking truly in a different but equally legitimate language, built around a different meaning of the quantifier, so that only a failure of interpretive charity makes the exchange look substantive. Ted Sider disagrees, arguing that candidate quantifier meanings are not all equally good at carving reality at its joints, so there may be a fact of the matter, expressible in what he calls "Ontologese," about which language actually matches the world's structure. Write a dialogue between a Hirsch-style quantifier variantist and a Sider-style realist, set at the bar during the reception of an ontology conference and focused specifically on the trout-turkey, in which each presses the other on a genuine weakness in their view and neither gets an easy last word.
