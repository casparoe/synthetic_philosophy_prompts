I have no formal training in philosophy, but over the past few months I have been working through G. E. Moore's *Principia Ethica* on my own, and I have written up some thoughts on the chapter where he argues that personal affection - friendship and love, broadly speaking - and the enjoyment of beauty are the two greatest goods a life can contain. I would like your comments on whether I have represented Moore's argument accurately, and on whether the difficulty I think I have found in it is a real problem or one he could easily answer. Here is what I have written:

Moore does not think either beauty or affection is valuable simply as a feeling, and he does not think an object is valuable merely by possessing admirable qualities. What makes them, in combination, the best things we know of is a specific kind of unity: an accurate awareness of something's real qualities, together with an emotion that actually fits those qualities - admiration for what is genuinely admirable, love for what is genuinely lovable. He calls the resulting whole an "organic unity," since its value far exceeds the value of the cognition and the emotion taken separately: a dispassionate, correct inventory of someone's virtues is worth little by itself, and an emotion directed at nothing in particular is worth nothing at all, but the two combined correctly produce one of the most valuable things in existence. He extends this line of thought with what he calls his method of isolation, using it to argue that a beautiful world no one will ever witness is still better than an equally unwitnessed ugly one - beauty retains some value even without an observer, though obviously far less than when it is rightly appreciated by someone.

My difficulty is this. If the value of the whole depends on the cognitive half being accurate, ordinary friendship should come out much poorer than Moore's account seems to allow, since real affection is shot through with a certain amount of flattering illusion: we regularly love people partly for qualities they do not fully possess, and overlook others that would embarrass us if we noticed them. Taken strictly, that counts as defective cognition, so the organic unity should be correspondingly degraded - in many ordinary cases, quite substantially. But this seems to prove too much. It implies that the imperfect friendships most people actually have are worth considerably less than we would naturally judge them to be, and Moore gives no indication of thinking his own friendships needed to clear some high bar of mutual accuracy before they counted for much. Either "correct cognition" is meant far more loosely than I am reading it, or Moore's account is more demanding, and more out of step with ordinary life, than it is usually given credit for.

Could you tell me first where I have gotten Moore's actual position wrong, if anywhere, and then separately give me your own assessment of whether the tension I have described is a genuine weakness in his argument, or one he could dispose of easily?
