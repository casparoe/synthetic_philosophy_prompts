I'm at work on a novel that recasts the Ulysses-and-the-Sirens episode as a modern story about a woman who arranges, well in advance, to have her own later convictions overruled: she suspects that an ordeal she is about to undergo will leave her far more convinced of something without making her any more correct about it. I've since read that Bas van Fraassen used this same myth to stress-test his own Reflection Principle — roughly, the requirement that one's present credence in a claim, conditional on knowing what credence one's later self will assign it, should equal that later credence (formally, P_t(A | P_t'(A) = r) = r for t < t'). Since I want the philosophical spine of the scene to actually hold up, could you explain in some detail why a case like Ulysses's counts as a genuine problem for Reflection rather than something the principle simply never covered, given that, as I understand it, van Fraassen restricts the principle to future opinions one currently expects to be reached rationally? I'm especially interested in whether that restriction actually rescues the principle, or whether it just relocates the difficulty onto the present self's capacity to judge, in advance, which of one's future opinions deserve trust.
