I spent my career in experimental physics, and only in retirement have I begun reading philosophy of science in earnest -- at the moment, working through Hans Reichenbach on causality. His principle of the common cause holds that any correlation between two events lacking a direct causal link must be explained by a common causal factor that renders the two statistically independent once conditioned upon. As I understand it, this principle was a direct ancestor of what John Bell later formalized as the condition of "local causality" -- precisely the condition his theorem shows to be incompatible with the observed quantum correlations. Reichenbach died in 1953, over a decade before Bell's 1964 paper and decades before the loophole-free experiments confirming the inequality violations, though he had already wrestled with quantum indeterminism in his own writing. Knowing what we do of his actual commitments on causality and probability, how do you think he would specifically have responded to Bell's theorem: as a refutation of the common cause principle itself, as grounds for rejecting one of Bell's other assumptions such as measurement independence, or as evidence that the principle was never meant to cover spacelike-separated correlations in the first place? I'd like your best reconstruction of his reasoning, not just a general impression of how he might have reacted.
