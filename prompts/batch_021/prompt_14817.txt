I help organize the guest-speaker segment of a graduate reading group on formal epistemology, and next month we're hosting a decision theorist for a recorded Q&A. She defends causal decision theory and has written specifically on the "Death in Damascus" puzzle -- the case where a near-infallible predictor fixes, before you choose, which of two cities you will die in, so that fleeing buys you nothing if the predictor has already anticipated the flight. Most of the group already knows Gibbard and Harper's original telling and the standard contrast with Newcomb's problem, so I'd like eight to ten questions that go past having her restate the setup.

I want at least one question pressing on why Death in Damascus is supposed to be distinctively hard for causal decision theory in particular -- harder than ordinary Newcomb cases -- given that, unlike Newcomb, there's no dominant act for the theory to fall back on; one question on the ratifiability requirement and whether she thinks the standard "randomize between the two cities" fix actually rescues ratifiability or just relocates the problem into the implausible demand that a rational agent carry a coin to flip; one question anticipating the evidentialist's likely comeback, that once causal decision theorists need a ratifiability patch to get a sensible answer here, they have quietly smuggled in the very evidential reasoning they officially reject; and one on whether the puzzle points to a real worry about rational self-prediction outside thought experiments involving literally infallible predictors, or whether she thinks it's a curiosity that dissolves once the predictor is merely very good rather than perfect. For each of these, please add a short follow-up tailored to the answer she's most likely to give -- for instance, if she defends the randomizing fix, a follow-up asking whether that amounts to conceding that causal decision theory, unsupplemented, is simply incomplete as a decision rule.

I'd also like one or two questions that bring in the more recent "functional decision theory" response to the puzzle (associated with the "Cheating Death in Damascus" line of argument), which claims to deliver a single determinate verdict where causal and evidential decision theory both struggle, asking whether she thinks that's a genuine advantage or just a rival theory helping itself to a different, equally contestable, notion of which act is "yours" to choose.
