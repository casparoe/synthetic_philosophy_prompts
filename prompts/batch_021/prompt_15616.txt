Q4. Karl Popper argued that a rational ethics/politics should aim to minimize suffering rather than maximize aggregate happiness.
(a) Explain Popper's reasoning for treating suffering and happiness asymmetrically. [4 pts]
(b) A famous objection says that a sufficiently strict version of this "negative utilitarian" view has a monstrous implication about painlessly ending all life. State the objection precisely, and say exactly which feature of the view it depends on. [6 pts]
(c) Give one way a suffering-focused ethicist could resist that objection without just giving up on suffering having priority over happiness. [5 pts]

Student's answer:

(a) Popper's reasoning was that suffering and happiness aren't symmetric in moral urgency: relieving concrete, identifiable suffering is always morally pressing in a way that adding more happiness to already-fine lives isn't. He was also worried, given his broader anti-utopian politics, that "maximize aggregate happiness" reasoning is exactly the kind of thing that lets you justify imposing a lot of present suffering on real people for the sake of some abstract future happiness. So he wanted "minimize suffering" to function as a more modest, harder-to-abuse political aim than "maximize happiness." This is basically the same asymmetry that motivates antinatalist writers like David Benatar, who argue that because pain is bad and the mere absence of pleasure isn't bad, it's always a harm to bring someone into existence — so Popper's negative utilitarianism and antinatalism are really two sides of the same coin.

(b) The objection is that if suffering is literally the only thing that counts morally, the best action available is to instantly and painlessly wipe out all sentient life, since a universe with zero beings has zero suffering and, on this view, there's no loss in there also being zero happiness. This "world-destruction" objection depends on the view being fully lexical — i.e., that literally any amount of suffering outweighs literally any amount of happiness, no matter how small the suffering or how large the happiness. If the view just gave suffering a lot of extra weight rather than infinite/lexical priority, the argument wouldn't go through, since enough happiness could in principle outweigh a bit of suffering.

(c) One move is to reject full lexicality and adopt a weaker, non-lexical suffering-focused view where suffering gets very strong priority but isn't literally incomparable to happiness — on a view like that, since real "destroy everyone" scenarios also destroy huge amounts of positive value and involve their own uncertainty, the math doesn't actually favor annihilation. So the suffering-focused ethicist gives up strict lexicality specifically to dodge this objection, which is why pretty much every serious suffering-focused view in the literature rejects lexicality.
