For a feature I'm reporting on the ethics of triage protocols used in disaster response, I've spent the past few weeks interviewing search-and-rescue coordinators, and one story has stayed with me. During a major flood, one coordinator's team had a single boat and about twenty minutes before rising water would cut off a stretch of river. Two rooftops radioed for help within moments of each other: one had a single occupant, the other had five. As far as she could tell, nothing distinguished the two situations except the number of people involved—both roofs were equally exposed, both groups equally likely to be swept away if the water kept rising, and there was no way to reach both in time or to split the trip between them. Her agency's protocol was explicit for cases like this: when one rescue can't cover everyone, it goes to wherever the greater number of people is at risk. She followed it. The five survived; the person on the other roof did not. At the debrief afterward, her colleagues justified the protocol simply by pointing out that saving five lives is better than saving one, and no one else in the room seemed troubled by that, but she told me the explanation never fully satisfied her, even though she couldn't say exactly what was wrong with it.

What strikes me about her account is that it isn't really a case about causing harm versus merely failing to prevent it: both roofs were already in danger before her team arrived, and heading toward either one would have counted as a rescue, not a harm. It's a cleaner test of a more basic question: is "go to wherever there are more people" really the self-evidently correct rule that emergency-management training treats it as, simply because five is more than one? Or is there a genuine gap in that reasoning—one that would mean her lingering doubt is onto something real, rather than just an emotional reaction to a tragic outcome? I'd like to understand, for the piece, whether the case for letting numbers decide is actually as simple as adding lives up the way you'd add any other quantity, or whether something significant gets lost when a decision like this is reduced to arithmetic.
