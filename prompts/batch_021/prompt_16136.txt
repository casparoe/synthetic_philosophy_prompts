The question, worth 10 of the exam's 50 points, reads: "Explain Galen Strawson's Basic Argument for the claim that no one is ever truly or ultimately morally responsible for what they do. In your answer, indicate whether the force of the argument depends on determinism being true, and briefly relate Strawson's conclusion to P. F. Strawson's account of moral responsibility in terms of reactive attitudes." Here is the student's answer, reproduced exactly as submitted:

"Strawson's Basic Argument is meant to show that nobody is ever truly or ultimately responsible for what they do, regardless of how the universe turns out to be at the most fundamental level. The core idea is that you act the way you do because of the way you are — your character, values, and motives — so if you were going to be truly responsible for what you do, you would have to be truly responsible for being the way you are, at least in the relevant mental respects. But to be responsible for being that way, you would have had to shape yourself into that kind of person on the basis of some earlier set of values or preferences you already held, and you would have to be responsible for those too, and so on backward. Since this regress cannot continue forever and cannot bottom out in something you simply chose from nowhere, you can never be the ultimate, self-caused source of your own character — you cannot be causa sui — and so you can never be truly responsible for anything you do. Because the argument turns on this regress rather than on any specific claim about physics, it is really just a restatement of classical hard determinism in different terms: if determinism is true, our actions trace back to a causal chain that predates us, so we cannot be their ultimate authors, and Strawson is simply cashing this out at the level of character formation instead of physical events. Strawson does not think this has to upend ordinary moral life, though. He points out that our practices of praising and blaming each other are grounded in natural reactive attitudes like resentment, gratitude, and indignation, which are not the kind of thing we could reason our way out of even if we became fully convinced that determinism was true. So although nobody is ultimately deserving of credit or blame in some cosmic bookkeeping sense, Strawson thinks it is these reactive attitudes that actually justify our everyday responsibility practices, which is why his view does not collapse into full-blown skepticism about praise and blame in ordinary life."
