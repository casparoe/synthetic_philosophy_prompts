I'm a software engineer, and a while back I went down a rabbit hole reading about "logical induction," the framework MIRI (the Machine Intelligence Research Institute) developed for assigning probabilities to mathematical statements that haven't yet been proven or refuted. As I understand it, the trick is to set up something like a prediction market: a countable population of computable "trader" algorithms trade shares tied to logical sentences (a share pays out 1 if the sentence eventually gets proved, 0 if it gets refuted), and as long as no trader from a suitably restricted, efficiently computable class can turn an unbounded profit off the market, the resulting sequence of prices ends up satisfying a lot of properties you'd want from rational credences: convergence to 0 or 1 on anything that actually gets proved or refuted, sensible behavior on statistical-looking patterns (like the parity of some far-off digit of pi), and non-trivial, evidence-sensitive probabilities on things that are still genuinely open, like the Collatz conjecture or the twin prime conjecture.

I described all this enthusiastically to a friend of mine who has a philosophy MA, and she wasn't having it. Her objection is that the whole framework is beside the point: on the classical Bayesian picture, rational credence has to respect "logical omniscience" — since mathematical statements are necessarily true or necessarily false, a genuinely rational agent should assign them probability 1 or 0, full stop, and anything in between is at best a report of the agent's own uncertainty about what it can currently prove, not a credence in the mathematical content itself. On her view, calling a logical inductor's 0.6 for some open conjecture a "credence" smuggles the language of rational belief onto what's really just a clever, self-consistent computational procedure. I pushed back that if no computable agent, including any human, can live up to logical omniscience, it seems strange to keep treating it as the standard of rationality rather than asking what a good, non-exploitable epistemic state looks like for agents like us. We argued about this for an entire evening without either of us budging, and I suspect we were each sharper at attacking the other's position than defending our own.

Could you write a dialogue that does both positions justice — one character defending "logical omniscience or bust" and the other defending "logical induction vindicates real logical uncertainty" — where they genuinely engage with each other's strongest objections rather than talking past one another? I'd like them to get concrete at some point, for instance by arguing about what odds it would actually be rational to trade at on a specific open conjecture right now, and I'd like the dialogue to end with them isolating a real crux: some specific consideration that would change one of their minds, or, failing that, an honest admission that they've been using "rational credence" to mean two different things all along.
