I'm drafting a scene for my novel in which two philosophy postdocs — once close collaborators, now estranged — run into each other while helping a mutual friend move apartments, surrounded by half-assembled bookshelves and packing boxes. Their old disagreement reignites: one has become a committed mereological universalist, holding that any objects whatsoever, however scattered or gerrymandered, compose some further object; the other has gone to the opposite extreme, mereological nihilism, insisting that strictly speaking there are only simples arranged chairwise, bookshelf-wise, and so on, and never any chairs or bookshelves as such. Please write their exchange, without reducing the dispute to a caricature: have both invoke the argument from vagueness against restricted answers to van Inwagen's Special Composition Question, such as his own organicism, and then contest whether that very argument really favors composition always occurring or never occurring. Both characters should be sharp, equally hard to refute, and genuinely responsive to what the other has just said rather than delivering parallel monologues; let the exchange end unresolved.
