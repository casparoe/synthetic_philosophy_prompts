Putting together some notes for a short essay on the Judy Benjamin problem (van Fraassen's soldier-lost-behind-enemy-lines puzzle about updating on conditional info) and I want someone to stress-test the argument before I write it up properly.

Setup, roughly: Judy's platoon gets dropped somewhere in a region thats half Red-held, half Blue-held, and each half is further split into two company sectors, so four equally likely locations to start (25% each). All that survives of a garbled radio message is a conditional: "if you're in Red territory, there's a 3/4 chance you're in the northern sector." Question: how should she update her full probabilities over the four locations?

My rough argument:

1. Plain conditionalization doesn't help — what she learned isn't a proposition in the algebra, it's a constraint on a conditional probability, so there's no event E to condition on.
2. Jeffrey conditionalization doesn't obviously apply either, since nobody handed her new flat probabilities for Red vs. Blue (like "Red is now 60%"), only something about the inside of Red.
3. The standard fix floating around in that literature is to pick whatever full distribution satisfies the new constraint while minimizing cross-entropy (KL divergence) against the prior — "change your beliefs as little as possible." Van Fraassen's whole point in raising the example is that this rule gives a weird answer here: I actually ran the numbers, and it drags P(Red) down from 1/2 to something like 0.47, even though the message never compares Red to Blue at all, only the inside of Red.
4. Stronger claim I want to make: this isn't a quirk specific to cross-entropy, its Bertrand's-paradox-shaped. "Minimal change" isn't well defined until you've already picked which quantities count as the natural variables to hold fixed, and cross-entropy minimization just picks one such parametrization and dresses it up as neutral. A different, equally natural "distance between distributions" would hold different things fixed and license a different update — so there's no such thing as *the* minimal-change update, only *a* minimal-change update relative to a prior choice that's doing real epistemic work.
5. The comeback I can't fully answer: there's an axiomatic literature (Shore and Johnson, I think, around 1980) arguing cross-entropy minimization is the *uniquely* consistent rule given some invariance/independence requirements, which if right would mean it's not an arbitrary parametrization after all. I vaguely remember there being pushback on those axioms too (Uffink, maybe?) but I'm fuzzy on it.

Can you check whether 1–3 are actually right, tell me whether the move in 4 is doing real work or is just a rhetorical flourish, and help me figure out whether 5 actually sinks the argument? Don't just be nice about it, I'd rather find the hole now.
