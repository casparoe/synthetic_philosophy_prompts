In my school's philosophy club we recently worked through the classic "money pump" argument against cyclic preferences: if I strictly prefer A to B, B to C, and C to A, then someone holding all three options can charge me a small fee at each step to trade me around the loop, leaving me back at my starting option but strictly poorer, even though every individual trade was one I was happy to make at the time. Everyone in the club found that part fairly intuitive. Afterward, though, I came across a discussion — in the context of arguments for why an advanced AI system would need to have "complete" preferences in order not to be exploitable — claiming that an analogous money pump can be built against an agent whose preferences are merely incomplete rather than cyclic: that is, an agent who, for some pairs of options, has no preference either way and does not regard the two as equally good either, because they are simply incomparable. Crucially, such an agent is never claimed to have an actual cycle of strict preferences anywhere among their options.

I have not been able to reconstruct this myself, since without a cycle I do not see where the exploitable loop is supposed to come from. Could you walk me through a concrete worked example — specific options, specific trades, and specific small fees at each step — showing exactly how an agent with no cyclic preferences at all could still be pumped for money purely because some of their preferences are incomplete? I would especially like you to point out which single move in your sequence is doing the real work: the one move that would not be available to someone who insists that finding two options incomparable is not the same as being willing to swap one for the other plus a fee. Finally, in a sentence or two, tell me what you think an example like this actually shows: that rational agents are required to have complete preferences after all, or merely that some other, independent rationality principle has to give instead.
