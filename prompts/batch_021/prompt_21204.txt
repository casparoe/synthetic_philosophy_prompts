"Brennan's Against Democracy rejects what he calls the 'folk theory of democracy' -- the idea that voting is valuable for its own sake regardless of whether voters actually know what they're doing. He sorts citizens into three rough types: hobbits, who pay little attention to politics and have few firm opinions; hooligans, who are highly engaged but closed-minded and tribal, like sports fans rooting for a team; and vulcans, who are engaged, informed, and weigh evidence dispassionately. Since he thinks vulcans are rare and most of us are hobbits or hooligans, he proposes things like restricted suffrage (you must pass a competence test to vote), plural voting (everyone keeps a vote but the more competent get extra ones), and an epistocratic veto, where a qualified council can strike down legislation passed by an otherwise democratic body.

Estlund's epistemic proceduralism, from Democratic Authority, is really the philosophical backbone for this kind of view. His whole point is that a decision procedure can't just be judged on fairness -- it also has to be judged by whether it reliably produces correct or just outcomes. Once you grant that some citizens are epistemically better positioned than others to identify correct outcomes, the natural next step is that their judgement should carry more institutional weight, which is why he calls it 'epistemic' proceduralism rather than plain old proceduralism. Estlund is explicit that this is what ultimately makes some carefully constrained form of epistocracy the most defensible view on offer, even though he wants to hedge it with constitutional safeguards that Brennan doesn't really bother with."
