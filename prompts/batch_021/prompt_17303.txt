As a journalist who spends a lot of time translating AI safety papers for a general audience, I've become preoccupied with one particular proposal that I can't quite get comfortable with: "AI safety via debate," the scheme first proposed by OpenAI researchers in 2018, in which two AI systems argue opposite sides of a question in front of a judge — originally conceived as a human, though the literature increasingly imagines a weaker AI standing in once the debaters get too capable for people to follow directly. The pitch is that judging an argument is easier than generating one from scratch, so debate should let us verify claims from systems whose reasoning has outstripped our own, which is exactly the promise packed into the phrase "scalable oversight." What keeps nagging at me, though, is the so-called obfuscated-argument problem: a dishonest debater doesn't need to be right, it only needs to construct a large enough thicket of individually plausible sub-claims that the one load-bearing lie becomes prohibitively expensive for the judge to locate, even when the honest opponent knows exactly where the rot is.

For a piece I'm drafting on how fiction can pressure-test the hidden assumptions behind formal safety schemes — the way Asimov's stories pressure-tested the Three Laws of Robotics — I'd like you to write a short story built around this specific vulnerability, rather than a generic "AI turns on us" plot. Perhaps set it around a small panel — human, or by that point perhaps delegated to an overseer model that is itself out of its depth — tasked with judging a debate between two far more capable systems over some irreversible, high-stakes decision, where the tension comes from the judge slowly losing confidence that the more persuasive debater is also the more honest one. I'd be curious to see how you handle the resolution: whether the flaw gets caught in time, and if so, by what means.
