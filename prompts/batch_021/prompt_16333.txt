I'm a school psychologist and I sit on my district's AAC (augmentative and alternative communication) review committee, which usually just rubber-stamps boring stuff like switch-access devices, but this month we've got a genuinely hard case. The parents of a nonverbal autistic third-grader want the district to keep funding a 1:1 aide trained in one of those letterboard methods in the same rough family as facilitated communication - I think it's Rapid Prompting Method or maybe Spelling to Communicate, I can never keep the acronyms straight - and, more importantly, to start treating what comes out through the board as the kid's real, authored voice for IEP goals and eventually class placement. Our staff speech pathologist is against it, citing the old blinded tests where it's the facilitator, not the student, who turns out to be driving the answers; the parents point out that their son "says" things through the board way beyond anything he's shown any other way, and ask what kind of person doubts a nonverbal kid's one shot at being heard. This case has hit me differently than it would have a year ago, because my best friend's son started a similar method this spring and is apparently spelling out whole paragraphs about missing his old house since the divorce, which has honestly been the best thing to happen to her in ages - so I definately don't have the stomach to just call it debunked and move on, even though I also can't shake the feeling that I don't actually know who's talking when that board lights up.

What keeps nagging at me is something from a philosophy of language class I took years ago, on Davidson's radical interpretation: you can't read a belief straight off someone's behavior, because you only figure out what they mean by simultaneously guessing at what they believe, and you only figure out what they believe by simultaneously guessing at what they mean - the two get solved together, using what he called the principle of charity, and there's no neutral, assumption-free spot to stand and check the result against the "real" facts afterward. That feels uncomfortably close to what's happening at that board: the more charitably you read the letters, the more coherent a person shows up, but that would be just as true if the coherence were coming from the facilitator's head instead of the kid's. Could you draft the memo I need to bring to Thursday's meeting? It should land on one clear recommendation - keep treating the output as authoritative, discontinue it and require only independently validated methods, or some conditional middle path - and whichever way it comes down, it actually has to engage the strongest version of the side it's rejecting, not just wave at it on the way to a conclusion.
