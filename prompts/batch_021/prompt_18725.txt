Explain (a) what leveling is and how the press specifically helps bring it about, and (b) why Kierkegaard thinks 'the public' names a genuinely new and troubling social category, rather than just being another word for a crowd or a community. And here's the answer, copied over pretty much verbatim (anonymized, obviously):

"Kierkegaard's Two Ages is nominally a review of a pretty forgettable novel, but its final section, 'The Present Age,' is really where he lays out his own diagnosis of contemporary Danish society. He uses the novel's two settings, the era of the French Revolution and his own 1840s Copenhagen, to set up a contrast between two 'ages.' The age of revolution is essentially an age of passion: people are willing to act decisively and put something on the line, even when the content of their commitment is wrong. The present age, by contrast, is reflective rather than passionate: everyone has an opinion about everything, but nobody actually commits to anything, because commitment would mean giving up the safety of endless deliberation. Kierkegaard isn't nostalgic for revolutionary violence exactly; what he wants back is the passion and willingness to act, not the specific politics of 1789.

The engine of the present age, for Kierkegaard, is what he calls leveling. Leveling is the process by which qualitative, essential differences between people (rank, vocation, individual excellence) get flattened into an abstract, numerical sameness. Nobody is allowed to really stick out, and the emotional fuel for this is envy: envy is what makes the present age suspicious of anyone who wants to stand for something in particular, so it would rather drag everyone down to the same level than let real differences show. Leveling doesn't do this on its own, though; it needs an instrument, and that instrument is the press. Newspapers create and sustain what Kierkegaard calls 'the public,' which he's careful to distinguish from an actual crowd or a genuine community: a crowd at least has to physically assemble somewhere, and a real community has some substantive commitment holding it together, but the public needs neither. It's a pure abstraction, 'everybody and nobody' at once, that can hold furious opinions about something without any single member of it ever being answerable for holding them. The press makes this possible, because it lets people have a stake in 'having a view' on everything from the safety of their own homes, without any of the risk that used to come from actually showing up and taking a stand in public. Writing under the pseudonym Johannes Climacus, Kierkegaard ties the whole essay together with his famous line that 'the crowd is untruth': for him truth is always a matter for the single individual, who has to relate to it in their own existence, and no aggregate of individuals, however large, can inherit that relation on anyone else's behalf.

It's hard not to read this essay in light of what happened to Kierkegaard himself not long afterward, when the satirical paper The Corsair spent months mocking his appearance and mannerisms. If anything, that whole episode is a live demonstration of exactly the dynamic 'The Present Age' describes: anonymous, collective mockery that no individual writer ever had to personally own."
