Me and my friend Priya got into a whole argument this weekend over whether "nudging" people is just manipulation wearing a nicer outfit. She kept insisting that if a tactic works by going around your rational deliberation instead of through it, it's manipulative no matter how good the intention behind it is. I pushed back that tons of totally normal persuasion -- most advertising, half of parenting, basically all store layout design -- also works below the level of conscious reasoning, and we don't call that manipulation just because its subtle. We never landed anywhere, and now I actually have to deal with a real version of this fight: I'm on my university's behavioral insights committee, which signs off on any "nudge"-style intervention a campus office wants to run on students.

There's a proposal sitting in my inbox right now from Dinning Services. They want to do two things at once: (1) make the plant-based entrée the default option on the online ordering app, so meat becomes the thing you have to actively click through to get, and (2) put a banner on that same ordering page claiming "73% of students on this meal plan went plant-based this week." I asked where that number came from and it turns out nobody knows -- it's a figure the office invented because it sounded "motivating," and there's no plan to ever disclose that. About half the committee wants to treat both moves the same way, as two flavors of the same choice-architecture manipulation; the other half thinks the default switch is completely fine (nobody's stopping you from getting the burger) while the fake statistic is where it actually crosses a line. I have to draft the committee's short decision memo before we meet Thursday -- approve, reject, or approve with changes -- and it needs to actually explain, in terms the Dining Services folks could respond to, whether and why these two tactics are ethically different, instead of just declaring "nudging good, lying bad" and calling it a day. Could you write that memo for me?
