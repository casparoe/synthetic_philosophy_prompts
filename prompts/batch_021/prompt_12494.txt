I spent about thirty-five years as an experimental physicist at a national lab, and since retiring I've fallen into a bit of a philosophy rabbit hole—my daughter got me a subscription to a philosophy magazine as a joke gift a while back and now I can't stop reading the stuff, plus I joined a little discussion group that meets at the library once a month. I brought the situation below to that group last week and they argued about it for a solid hour without agreeing on anything, so now I'm curious what you'd make of it.

A few years ago, four of us who used to work together on an accelerator experiment that got cancelled before it ever published any results decided, over drinks at a reunion dinner, that we owed it to posterity (and to our own vanity, probably) to write up an honest technical history of why the thing actually failed, since the official report glossed over all the interesting parts. We split the writeup into four sections, set a loose deadline, and have been checking in every couple of months since. Last week, one of the four—I'll call him Warren—emailed the rest of us to say he's dropping out. Not because he can't finish his part, just because he's lost interest and would rather spend his evenings on other things. He offered to send along his old notebooks to whoever wants to take over his section and seemed to think that settled the matter. When one of us pushed back a little, Warren said, reasonably enough on the face of it, that none of us ever sat him down and got him to promise, one on one, that he'd finish his part—we all just agreed together, as a foursome, over dinner—so as far as hes concerned he doesnt owe any single one of us anything beyond the notebooks.

The rest of us feel like he's done something wrong here that goes beyond just being flaky, but I honestly can't put my finger on what the wrong would even be, since Warren seems technically right that no individual promise ever got broken. Does deciding something together, as a group, create an obligation that's separate from four individual promises—one that Warren can't just cancel on his own by mailing out some notebooks? Or is he basically correct that once you strip away the individual promises there's nothing left to be obligated by? I'd love to hear how you'd untangle this one.
