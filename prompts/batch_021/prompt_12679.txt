"Franklin's project of moral perfection began when he decided that merely resolving to be good wasn't enough, since he kept slipping up without noticing -- habit could outsmart intention before he was even aware of it. So he drew up a list of thirteen virtues, each with a short precept (like 'Temperance: eat not to dullness, drink not to elevation'), and tracked his progress in a little book with a row for each virtue and a column for each day, marking a black spot every time he slipped. He worked through the virtues one at a time in a rotating weekly cycle, so that over the course of a year he'd give focused attention to all thirteen several times over, rather than trying to fix everything about himself at once.

The order of the virtues matters a lot to Franklin, and it's basically a ranking of moral seriousness: he starts with Temperance because he saw over-indulgence as the root of most of the other vices on the list, and he ends with Humility, which was really more of an afterthought, tacked onto the bottom of the list after a Quaker friend told him people found him arrogant -- it wasn't part of his original moral vision so much as a late fix for his reputation.

Franklin admits the project didn't fully succeed: he says he fell well short of the perfection he'd aimed for, especially with Order, which he admits he never really mastered. Still, he insists the attempt made him a better and happier man than he'd have been otherwise, and he points out that the biggest irony of all is that pride crept back in through the back door -- even being humble became something to feel a little vain about. This actually rhymes with the cod episode earlier in the book, where the teenage Franklin, having briefly sworn off meat on principle, talks himself back into eating fish after noticing smaller fish in the bigger fish's stomachs. He's already demonstrating, well before the virtue project even starts, that for him reason is mostly a tool for justifying whatever he already wants to do -- which is exactly the loop the moral perfection project can't escape: however sincerely he reasons his way into a virtue, he can always reason his way into feeling pretty good about himself for having it."
