At lunch last week, two people on my school's ethics bowl team got into a genuine argument about our friend Marcus, who has decided to enlist in the Marine Corps right after graduation instead of applying to college. Jae argued that Marcus cannot really make a rational choice here, because this is not just an ordinary case of not knowing what you're getting into: it is what philosophers call an epistemically transformative experience, meaning no amount of reading memoirs or talking to veterans can give Marcus the first-hand, what-it's-like knowledge he would need to genuinely compare military life against college life, so any expected-value comparison he makes is only informed on one side. Wren answered that this alone cannot be what makes the choice irrational, since it is true of nearly every major life decision — nobody has first-hand knowledge of what college is really like before enrolling either — and testimony plus imagination are treated as good enough everywhere else we make big choices. Jae replied that this case is worse, because it is also personally transformative: people who have actually served often say their whole outlook changed, so even a perfectly accurate preview would not fully solve the problem, since it is unclear whose preferences should decide whether it was worth it — the Marcus choosing now, or the Marcus that choice will produce. Wren countered that ordinary growing up reshapes a person's values in exactly that way too, and that if "your values will change later" were enough to make a decision irrational now, no long-term decision would ever count as rational, which seems like too strong a conclusion. Jae's response was that there is still a real difference between gradual change that grows out of who you already are and a deliberate leap into a sharply different identity, which is precisely why people describe choices like this one as something you can only jump into rather than calculate your way toward. Wren's last word was that "sharp" versus "gradual" is doing a lot of work Jae never actually justifies, so until he can draw that line in a principled way, he is just re-describing the puzzle rather than showing that Marcus's decision is any less rational than an ordinary one. Could you go through their exchange move by move and say who has the better of it at each point, rather than just declaring an overall winner? I would also like to know whether there is some standard move in this area of philosophy that neither of them thinks to make.
