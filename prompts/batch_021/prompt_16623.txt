I've spent the past few weeks reporting a piece on the strange afterlife of Kurt Gödel's ontological proof of God's existence — the modal argument he refined privately for decades and shared with only a few colleagues before his death, and which the logician Christoph Benzmüller and colleagues revisited about a decade ago by encoding it and checking its validity with automated theorem-proving software. Wanting to get past the "computer confirms Gödel was right" headlines and understand the actual argument, I tracked down the version that circulates among philosophers of religion, reconstructed from Dana Scott's notes on Gödel's own manuscript. Copied out, the core of it reads:

Definitions
D1. x is God-like if and only if x has as essential properties exactly those properties that are positive.
D2. A property φ is an essence of x if and only if x has φ, and for every property ψ, x has ψ necessarily if and only if φ entails ψ.
D3. x has necessary existence if and only if every essence of x is necessarily exemplified.

Axioms
A1. Either a property or its negation is positive, but not both.
A2. Any property entailed by a positive property is itself positive.
A3. The property of being God-like is positive.
A4. If a property is positive, it is necessarily positive.
A5. Necessary existence is a positive property.

From these five axioms, plus a modal logic at least as strong as S5, the standard derivation runs roughly as follows: positive properties are possibly exemplified; hence a God-like being is possible; hence, because being God-like would count as its own essence, and necessary existence is itself positive, a God-like being's existence is not merely possible but necessary.

I can follow that chain well enough on its own terms. But when I ran a draft of this section past a friend who works in modal logic, she waved it off and said the proof has a far bigger problem than the usual complaints about "positive" doing too much work: something called "modal collapse," apparently first pressed by Jordan Howard Sobel and taken up since by writers such as C. Anthony Anderson, according to which these same five axioms entail that every truth is a necessary truth, leaving no room for contingency anywhere in the world. I can't reconstruct that derivation from A1–A5 myself, and it is entirely absent from the popular coverage of the computer-verification episode, which treated the proof as a curiosity with no such consequence attached. Could you walk me through, in plain terms, which axiom or combination of axioms is actually responsible for forcing the collapse, why this counts as a distinct and more serious problem than the standard objections to the proof, and whether the fixes proposed since Sobel's critique genuinely block the collapse or simply relocate it elsewhere in the system?
