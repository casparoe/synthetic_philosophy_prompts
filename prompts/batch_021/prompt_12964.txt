I spent close to forty years as a field geologist before retiring, and I've been using some of my new-found free time to finally read philosophy properly rather than in odd stolen hours. A few former colleagues and I have formed an informal reading group, and we are currently working through the opening books of Plato's Laws. I confess I am stuck on a passage I did not expect: the Athenian Stranger spends a great deal of effort defending the rather startling claim that supervised communal drinking parties are actually good for a city, on the grounds that getting citizens mildly (or not so mildly) drunk, under the eye of a sober master of the feast, is a safe and convenient way of both testing and training their capacity for what Plato calls sōphrosynē, usually rendered as self-mastery or self-control. He draws an explicit parallel with testing a soldier's courage by deliberately exposing him to controlled fear.

What I cannot reconstruct for myself is the psychological story meant to make this non-paradoxical. Earlier in the same book, if I am following the text correctly, Plato pictures each of us as a kind of puppet of the gods, pulled about by opposing cords of pleasure and pain, and of fear and confidence, with calculation, or reason, serving as a further, "golden" cord we are meant to keep following regardless of how hard the others tug. Wine, on the Athenian's own account, works by loosening reason's grip precisely so that those other cords can pull harder. So how, on Plato's own terms, is deliberately weakening someone's self-command supposed to function as a genuine test of, let alone training in, that very self-command, rather than simply as licensed practice in vice? Naively, it seems a bit like proposing to train sobriety by getting people drunk.

Could you reconstruct the Athenian's argument as charitably as you can, including whatever response, explicit or implicit, he gives to something like this objection? I would also be curious whether you think this sits comfortably alongside the far less indulgent attitude toward drunkenness one finds elsewhere in Plato, for instance in the censorship of drunken gods and heroes from the guardians' education in the Republic, or whether the Laws represents a genuine change of mind on his part.
