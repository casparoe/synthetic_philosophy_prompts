I write infrastructure and pipeline tooling for a university genomics core facility, which means that even though I have no background in philosophy myself, I end up on the sidelines of a lot of arguments between the computational people and the wet-lab people on my team. The latest one started during our work book club's discussion of Siddhartha Mukherjee's The Gene, specifically the part about how Erwin Schrödinger, years before molecular biologists had even settled on DNA rather than protein as the actual carrier of heredity, predicted that it would have to work via some kind of stable "code-script" in the chromosomes. That set off a real argument between two of my coworkers, Ines (computational genomics) and Sam (developmental biology), over whether DNA is actually a code or just something people call a code because the metaphor is convenient.

(1) Ines said it's more than a convenient metaphor: the genetic code -- the mapping from three-letter DNA codons to amino acids -- was worked out experimentally in the 1960s, is discrete and largely arbitrary in its specific assignments, and gets decoded by ribosomes much the way a computer decodes bytes, so calling it a code is literally accurate. (2) Sam agreed that the codon table is a genuine code in that narrow sense, but said that's not the claim doing any work when people call DNA "the blueprint for a human being" -- that bigger claim is about the whole organism, and building one takes cytoplasmic gradients, methylation patterns, and environmental cues that are nowhere in the sequence; he pointed to honeybees, where nothing but a difference in larval diet pushes two genetically near-identical females down completely different developmental paths -- queen or worker -- by switching whole suites of genes on and off, which he took as proof that the environment supplies real informational content of its own, rather than just executing fixed instructions. (3) Ines replied that this proves less than Sam thinks: every program needs a runtime to do anything -- a compiled binary needs an OS and a processor -- and nobody concludes from that that the binary isn't "the code" for what the machine does, so a bee's code simply takes parameters, the way a function might read a config file. What actually makes genes special, she added, is that they're the one part of the system copied with high fidelity across generations and shaped by selection to produce reliable effects, which is why they count as the evolved information carriers even when other inputs matter for any single organism's development. (4) Sam's closing point was that the fidelity argument explains why natural selection acts mainly on genes -- a fact about evolution -- but not why genes specifically should count as carrying the instructions for development, which is a different question; and if high-fidelity transmission across generations were enough to earn something the label "code," a handful of unusually stable epigenetic marks that are also inherited in some lineages would qualify too, which he took as proof that Ines's criterion proves too much.

Neither of them budged, and as far as I can tell they both still think they clearly won. Could you go through the four numbered moves and say, for each one, whether it holds up, partially holds up, or misses something important -- rather than just picking an overall winner? I'd also like a closing paragraph on whether there's an actual fact of the matter about whether DNA "is" a code once the narrow codon-level point is granted, or whether the rest of the disagreement is more definitional than either of them realizes -- and, more generally, whether there's a point both of them are missing.
