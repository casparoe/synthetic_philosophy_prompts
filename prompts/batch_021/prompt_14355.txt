I'm on a small task force my grad student association set up after last spring's president election turned into a mess — two candidates with pretty similar platforms basically split the vote, which let a third candidate that most people actually ranked near the bottom end up winning outright. We got asked to come back with a recommendation on whether to keep plain plurality voting or switch to something else, and we meet again in two weeks to finalize what goes to the full board.

I brought this up with my friend Dana, who's a few years into an econ PhD and loves this stuff, fully expecting her to back me up on switching to ranked-choice voting. Instead we ended up arguing about it for like two hours. Her point was that because of the Gibbard-Satterthwaite theorem, basically any voting rule for three or more candidates that isn't a flat-out dictatorship and can still produce every candidate as a possible winner is manipulable by somebody voting insincerely — so ranked-choice, Borda count, approval voting, whatever, are all just "differently gameable" then plurality, not more fair in any principled sense. I said fine, but they can't ALL be equally bad in practice, and she agreed, then asked me what my non-question-begging way of ranking them was, if not resistance to manipulation, and I honestly didn't have a great answer. She also mentioned that even just picking one ballot at random to decide the whole election has its own strategy-proofness theorem attatched to it, and that it basically collapses into a randomized dictatorship rather than actually dodging the problem, which I'm still chewing on.

I still owe the task force an actual recommendation regardless of who "won" that argument, so could you draft the memo for me? I want it to name one specific method (or make a real case for sticking with plurality, if that's genuinely the better call), lay out a realistic timeline for rolling it out before fall nominations open, and actually engage with Dana's manipulability point instead of just shrugging and saying "well, no system is perfect."
