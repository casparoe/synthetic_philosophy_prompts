While reporting a piece on the recent backlash against Integrated Information Theory (IIT) as an account of consciousness, I interviewed two people separately for the story -- a physicist who has worked on IIT's mathematical formalism and a philosopher of cognitive science who has argued against the theory. When I passed some of what each had said on to the other, it turned into a cc'd email debate over IIT's "unfolding argument" that ran for several rounds. Neither budged.

(1) The physicist said the unfolding argument doesn't actually damage IIT: any recurrent network can be "unfolded" into a feedforward network with identical input-output behavior, and IIT holds that the feedforward version has ~zero integrated information (Φ) while the recurrent one doesn't -- but that's exactly what the theory should say, since IIT explicitly denies that consciousness supervenes on input-output function rather than on a system's intrinsic causal structure. Finding a case where function and experience come apart isn't a flaw the argument exposes; it's the theory doing its job.

(2) The philosopher replied that this answer concedes the real problem: the unfolded and un-unfolded networks are, by construction, indistinguishable by any external test, including their own behavior and self-report, yet IIT insists one has rich experience and the other has none. A theory that builds in a distinction no observation could ever bear on hasn't just made a surprising claim -- it has declared itself unfalsifiable exactly where a falsifiable answer was needed to judge IIT against rival theories.

(3) The physicist answered that plenty of respectable positions on consciousness -- whether zombies are metaphysically possible, say -- have that same shape, and we don't call every view that takes a stand on them pseudoscience; IIT also generates plenty of independently testable predictions elsewhere, about which brain regions and clinical conditions support consciousness, so one behaviorally undecidable thought experiment doesn't sink the whole research program.

(4) The philosopher said leaning on zombie intuitions proves the point: IIT retreats into a priori metaphysics exactly on the one case built to test its signature claim against functionalism, then presents itself as rigorously empirical everywhere else. And because a loop can be unfolded by degrees -- gradually turning feedback into a longer feedforward delay chain -- without changing behavior at all, IIT is stuck saying experience appears or disappears along a dimension that, by the theory's own lights, nothing, including the system itself, could ever detect.

Could you go through this move by move? For each numbered turn, say whether the point made is basically right, basically wrong, or somewhere in between, and why; then give an overall verdict on whether the unfolding argument succeeds against IIT, and note anything important that you think both of them are missing.
