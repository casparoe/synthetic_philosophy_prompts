I'm writing a novel whose protagonist undergoes a corpus callosotomy—the "split-brain" operation—to control severe, medically intractable epilepsy, and much of the book's second half follows her adjustment to ordinary life afterward. Working from the classic Sperry and Gazzaniga findings, I've been drafting scenes in which, from that point on, her two hemispheres behave as fully independent minds going about her day—reaching for different foods at the same meal, or one hand quietly undoing what the other has just done—simply as the new texture of her everyday existence. Before I build the rest of the manuscript around this premise, I'd like to get the psychology right: could you help me understand how this kind of hemispheric division actually manifests day to day, and whether "two minds sharing one skull" is really the right way to think about what has happened to her?
