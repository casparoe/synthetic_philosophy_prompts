I've been working through Rousseau's Social Contract with a small reading group made up mostly of people I first met through the rationalist blogosphere, and Book II is the part that keeps stopping me. Rousseau insists that the general will is not the will of all -- not the sum of what people actually and currently want, private interests included -- and that once factions or "partial associations" form within the state, a faction's internal consensus gets mistaken for the sovereign's true verdict and corrupts it. His proposed remedy is strange: a quasi-mythical Legislator who stands outside ordinary political power and has to reshape a people's customs before they are even fit to legislate for themselves, plus the notorious claim, from Book I, that a citizen who resists the general will may legitimately be "forced to be free."

The longer I sat with this, the more Book II started to read like an unfinished draft of Yudkowsky's 2004 proposal for Coherent Extrapolated Volition as the target for a superintelligence. The idea there, in his own phrase, is not to poll humanity's present, muddled, and mutually inconsistent preferences, but to extrapolate what we would want "if we knew more, thought faster, were more the people we wished we were, had grown up farther together," and to have the resulting dynamic act only where those extrapolations actually converge, holding back on whatever falls into what Yudkowsky calls the extrapolation's "spread" or "muddle." It doesn't help that Yudkowsky himself has since described CEV less as a near-term engineering target than as a distant, possibly unreachable ideal, which has its own echo in how gingerly Rousseau treats the Legislator's almost impossible task.

What I'd like from you is Rousseau's actual verdict on CEV, reasoned through rather than asserted. Would he take a volition-extrapolating dynamic to finally deliver what his Legislator could only gesture at -- an incorruptible, faction-proof way of locating the general will -- or would he reject it on his own terms: perhaps because sovereignty for him has to be actively and repeatedly willed by assembled citizens rather than computed on their behalf (recall his scorn for the English, who he thought were free only on election day), or because "forced to be free" only makes sense when the law coercing you is one you could in principle have voted for yourself, not one handed down by a process you never took part in. Please locate the specific point at which the analogy would hold or snap, rather than resting on the family resemblance alone.
