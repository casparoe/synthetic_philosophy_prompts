I've been reporting on human factors in aviation maintenance for a longer feature on how mechanics talk about certainty on the job, and something an interview subject told me a few weeks ago has stuck with me more than anything that's actually made it into the piece so far. She's a licensed airframe technician with almost twenty years of experience, and she described finding a faint hairline mark on an unpressurized cabin panel with no structural role, and signing it off in seconds: "I could see it wasn't a crack, I knew it was fine." A few days later she came across a mark on a wing spar fitting that she says was visually indistinguishable from the first, under the same light and the same magnification. This time she wouldn't sign it off on sight; she pulled the part and sent it for X-ray, even though she admitted that, shown photographs of the two marks side by side, she couldn't have told you which one was riskier. Her own gloss on this was that looking fine and actually knowing it's fine aren't the same thing once a wing is on the line.

What complicated things further was her supervisor's take on that same spar-fitting case, when I raised it with him separately. As far as the official sign-off paperwork goes, he said, nobody "knows" a fitting like that is sound just by eyeballing it, full stop; only the X-ray earns you the right to say you know. But out on the hangar floor, he added, mechanics talk about each other's eye for cracks constantly, and by that everyday standard, sure, she knew; she has one of the best eyes in the building. So the very same judgment, about the very same part, counted as knowledge by one standard and fell short of it by another, and it wasn't obvious to me that anyone involved thought they were contradicting themselves.

When I described all this to my editor, she called it ordinary risk-aversion dressed up as something fancier, and left it there. That doesn't quite satisfy me, because neither the shift between the cabin panel and the spar fitting, nor the shift between the floor-talk verdict and the paperwork verdict, looks like a case of someone simply getting more or less careful; each time, someone was making a claim about what was actually known, not just about how cautious to be. Is there a defensible way of holding that identical evidence can amount to "knowing" something under one set of circumstances and fall short of it under another, purely because of what's at stake or who's asking? And are the technician's own shift across cases and her supervisor's shift across conversations really the same underlying phenomenon, or two different things that happen to look alike?
