I'm a machine learning engineer with no formal training in philosophy, but I recently spent a few weekends reading about teleosemantics — the project, associated mainly with Ruth Millikan and in different forms with Dretske and Papineau, of explaining what makes a mental state "about" something by grounding its content in biological function: a state's content is fixed not by any current, intrinsic property of the system, but by the selection history of the "producer" and "consumer" mechanisms that make and use it. I got interested because the framework maps unnervingly well onto how we talk about trained models, and I wrote up some notes trying to work through a specific case. I'd like a critical read before I trust my own reasoning here.

1. On Millikan's view, a representation's content is fixed by what the systems that produce and consume it were historically selected to do. The standard illustration is the frog that snaps its tongue at small, dark, moving things: on her theory the internal state involved doesn't mean fly, or even small-dark-moving-thing, it means something like frog food, because what explains the persistence of frogs with that circuit is that the circuit correlated with nutrition, not with any particular visual profile.

2. Davidson's Swampman is the standard stress test: a molecule-for-molecule duplicate of Davidson, assembled purely by chance when lightning strikes a dead tree in a swamp, behaviorally indistinguishable from the original but possessing no selection or developmental history whatsoever. Etiological teleosemantics seems committed to saying Swampman's internal states mean nothing at all, and Millikan bites this bullet explicitly, treating the contrary intuition as simply mistaken — she compares it to pre-scientific intuitions about "water" before anyone knew it was H2O.

3. My first thought was that this generalizes directly to neural networks: train a network until some activation pattern reliably does what we'd pretheoretically call "detecting cats," then load just the resulting weights into a freshly instantiated copy of the same architecture that has never itself processed a training example. Same weights, same forward pass, same activations on the same inputs, but zero training history for this particular instantiation. Isn't the loaded copy just Swampman?

4. On reflection I think this is wrong, and the reason seems instructive. Millikan's technical criterion for when a token can inherit a function it didn't personally earn is "reproduction": B is a reproduction of A specifically when B resembles A because it was caused by A — this is her own explanation for why my heart has the proper function of pumping blood despite never itself being selected for anything (it's a copy, via genes, of ancestral hearts that were). Swampman fails this test because his resemblance to Davidson is explicitly not caused by Davidson — it's mere coincidence. But loading a state_dict is a paradigm case of resemblance-caused-by-copying: the new instance's weights are causally downstream of the trained weights via an actual copy operation. So by Millikan's own criterion, the loaded network looks like a legitimate reproduction, more like an identical twin or a photocopy than like Swampman, and should inherit content rather than lack it.

5. If that's right, the only genuine neural-network Swampman would be something far more contrived: a randomly initialized network that, through sheer coincidence and with no copying involved, ends up with exactly the trained weights. That case preserves the "no causal-copying relationship" feature that does the real work in the original thought experiment, but it's so far removed from anything that happens in practice that I doubt it tells us anything useful. Which leaves me stuck on whether my instinct that this mattered for ordinary, deployed model copies was confused from the start.

I'd appreciate comments on three things: whether I've represented Millikan's and Davidson's positions accurately, whether the move in point 4 is a legitimate application of her reproduction criterion or overextends it, and whether point 5 is really the end of the line here or whether there's a better candidate for what would make something a genuine neural-network Swampman.
