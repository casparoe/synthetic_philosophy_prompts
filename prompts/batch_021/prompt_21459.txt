I retired a few years back after a long career as an analytical chemist, and lately I've been filling the extra time by finally reading through the philosophy classics I always meant to get to. Right now that means Descartes' Meditations, in that old Haldane and Ross translation since that's what was sitting free online, and I've hit a wall in the Sixth Meditation, the part where he tries to nail down the "real distinction" between mind and body. Anyway, here's the bit that's got me stuck, copied out from the PDF my niece sent me, typos and all:

"...the fact that I am able clearly and distinctly to understand one thing apart from another is sufficient to make me certain that the one is different from the other, seeing that they may be at least separated by the omnipotence of God; and it does not signify by what power such separation is made, in order to compel me to judge them to be different. [...] because, on the one hand, I have a clear and distinct idea of myself in so far as I am only a thinking and unextended thing, and because, on the other hand, I have a distinct idea of body in so far as it is only an extended and unthinking thing, it is certain that this I -- that is to say, my soul, by which I am what I am -- is entirely and truly distinct from my body, and may exist without it."

What's got me stuck is the appeal to God's omnipotence. Descartes spent the Fourth and Fifth Meditations convincing himself that whatever he perceives clearly and distinctly just is true, full stop, so by the time he gets here I don't see why he needs God to "separate" mind and body at all -- why not just say "I conceive of one without the other, therefore they're seperate things," and skip the middle man? Is the omnipotence bit doing actual logical work in the argument, or is it just how a 17th century writer dresses up a claim about possibility? And honestly the bigger thing bugging me is the move underneath all that: going straight from "I can clearly conceive of X apart from Y" to "X and Y really can exist apart." In the lab I never got to conclude two substances were seperable just because I could imagine them apart on paper -- I needed to actually run the reaction. My niece, who's a couple years into a philosophy PhD, said something at Christmas dinner about this being basically the same move that shows up in modern arguments about "zombies," but then got pulled away before she could explain what she meant. Could you help me pick apart what Descartes is actually entitled to conclude here, and whether that jump from conceiving to really-being-possible is as shaky as it looks to me, or whether there's a good reason it isn't?
