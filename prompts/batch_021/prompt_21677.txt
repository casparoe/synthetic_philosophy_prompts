I'm a novelist myself (second book came out this year, did fine, nothing wild) and I've got a pretty specific speech-writing problem I'm hoping you can help me solve. My old MFA thesis advisor -- I'll just call her Deborah -- is retiring this spring after something like thirty-five years running the same fiction workshop, and I got asked to give the toast at her retirement dinner. The complication is that Deborah is a totally sincere, unembarrassed disciple of late Tolstoy, specifically the Tolstoy of "What Is Art?," not the big-novel Tolstoy. Every single year, day one of workshop, she'd read us the passage where he basically disowns his own most famous books -- decides Anna Karenina and War and Peace count as bad, "counterfeit" art, made for money and for the jaded tastes of one narrow class, rather than the real thing, which for him was some plain, sincere "infection" of feeling that could hit a duke and a peasant exactly the same way. Same shtick with his weird little essay trying to prove King Lear is actually just a badly built play. And she never let us off the hook either -- the running implication all semester was that most of what we were straining to write in that room, clever and structurally tricky as it was, was probably "bad art" by the identical logic, hers and ours alike, prizes and blurbs included.

So now I have to toast thirty-five years of her turning out a room full of, lets be honest, genuinely successful writers, in front of a crowd that's half those writers -- who will definately clock it if I get the Tolstoy wrong -- and half her actual family, who have never once heard the phrase "counterfeit art" and just want to hear that she was a wonderful teacher, full stop. I don't want to write something that praises her, in effect, for craft and cleverness in a way she'd have visibly winced at, and I also don't want five minutes of Russian aesthetic theory that leaves her mother staring at the tablecloth. The thing I keep circling back to is that Tolstoy, having supposedly disowned novel-writing as basically a con, went ahead and wrote Resurrection and Hadji Murad anyway, like he couldn't fully quit his own talent even by his own rules -- and I think there's both a decent joke and the right note of affection buried in that, if it's pointed gently at Deborah instead of at Tolstoy. Could you draft that toast for me? Five minutes, tops, one joke that actually works for both halves of the room, landing as affection and not as a lecture.
