I've spent more years than I'd like to admit reading rationalist blogs and forums -- Overcoming Bias, then LessWrong, then the various diaspora blogs that followed -- and decision-theoretic puzzles in the Newcomb family have always been my favorite rabbit hole. I co-host a small podcast that grew out of a local meetup's reading group, and next week we're recording with a decision theorist whose whole research program is built around defending a resolute-choice solution to Kavka's toxin puzzle. For anyone who hasn't run into it: an eccentric billionaire will pay you a million dollars tomorrow morning if, by midnight tonight, you form a genuine intention to drink a vial of toxin tomorrow afternoon -- a toxin that will make you extremely sick for a day but cause no lasting harm. You don't actually need to drink it to collect; the billionaire has an infallible way of reading your true intentions, and the payoff turns only on what you intend at midnight, never on what you do the next day. The trouble is that once midnight has passed and the money is either yours or not, you will have no remaining reason to drink the toxin, which makes it hard to see how you could sincerely intend, now, to do something you already know you'll have no reason to do later. Our guest thinks this worry is overstated. She argues that a properly rational agent not only can form the intention but has genuine reason to actually drink the toxin the next day, on broadly the same grounds this crowd usually invokes for paying the driver in Parfit's hitchhiker or one-boxing on Newcomb's problem: that rationality is properly assessed at the level of the policy or disposition an agent implements -- what people around here have taken to calling functional or updateless decision theory -- rather than at each local decision node in isolation.

I'd like at least one question each on: Bratman's "Simple View" objection that intending to do something requires believing you will do it, which seems to rule out ever forming the intention in the first place if you've reasoned the case through correctly; how her proposal relates to Edward McClennen's original resolute-choice framework, which was developed for dynamic choice problems under non-expected-utility preferences rather than for predictor-based cases like this one, and whether extending it here is a natural move or a substitution of one puzzle for another; what happens to her argument if the billionaire's lie-detector is merely very reliable rather than infallible; and where she draws the line between cases like this one, where being predictably the sort of agent who follows through is exactly what earns the reward, and blackmail-style cases, where the same kind of predictability seems to invite exploitation rather than pay off.
