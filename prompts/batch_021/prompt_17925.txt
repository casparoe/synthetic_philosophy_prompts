I'm a novelist, and for several years now I've belonged to a small manuscript-swap group that meets monthly in the back room of a bookshop; we're six at the moment, all at work on our own projects (I'm about two-thirds through a third novel that keeps refusing to end). At our last meeting, a real argument broke out between two of the members, Noor and Declan, over a third, Marisol, who wasn't there that night. Marisol has been revising the same novel for eleven years, through four substantially different versions; in the last eighteen months alone she has sent out something like a hundred and sixty queries, with exactly two full-manuscript requests and no offers. After a fresh round of workshop notes and a paid assessment from a well-regarded freelance editor, she has now decided that this draft is finally the one, and she and her husband have remortgaged their flat so that she can drop to part-time work for a year and pay that same editor for a full line edit and a publicist besides. Noor thought this was close to heroic; Declan thought it was close to reckless. I wasn't taking notes, but reconstructing a conversation after the fact is at least a skill I'm supposed to have, and this is roughly how the exchange went.

(1) Noor said Marisol's situation is exactly the kind of case William James described in "The Will to Believe": where an option is living, forced, and momentous, and cannot by its nature be settled by the intellect, we are entitled to let our hopes decide. No agent or algorithm can hand Marisol the true odds on this particular book; staying "undecided" for another decade is not a neutral holding pattern but a real choice with its own cost; and this may be her last serious run at the manuscript. (2) Declan answered with the shipowner from Clifford's "The Ethics of Belief," who also arrived, by quieting his doubts, at a sincere and comfortable conviction that his unseaworthy ship would make it home safely; his sincerity didn't excuse him once other people's lives were riding on that belief. Since Marisol's husband co-signed the remortgaging, Declan said, this is not a private wager on her own feelings, and it's exactly the kind of belief Clifford thought was wrong regardless of how it turns out. (3) Noor replied that Declan was running together two different charges: believing without investigating, and acting on a risk you have investigated honestly. Marisol, unlike the shipowner, has had the manuscript professionally read twice and knows her genre's submission statistics cold; she isn't denying the odds so much as choosing to act against them. James, Noor said, never made the right to believe conditional on a belief being costless to others, only on the intellect being unable to settle the underlying question, and here it plainly can't. (4) Declan said that was exactly the wrong lesson to take from James, who restricted his whole argument to options that are undecidable in principle, not merely undecided in Marisol's own head. The pattern for manuscripts with this many rejections at this stage, he said, is not remotely mysterious to agents; what Marisol has is a fairly clear signal that she is choosing to override, which puts her nearer to Clifford's territory than James's. (5) Noor's last word was that this left out James's own point that some beliefs are partly self-fulfilling — his examples of a climber whose confidence helps him complete a leap he would otherwise botch, or a person whose belief that she is liked helps bring the liking about. A novelist who submits, revises, and pitches herself differently depending on whether she expects to succeed, Noor said, is not simply defying a fixed base rate the way a roulette player defies the wheel; she is one of the inputs into whatever rate eventually gets recorded.

Neither of them gave any ground, and I've argued myself around in circles on it since. Could you go through the five moves in order and say, for each one, who has the better of it and why, rather than jumping straight to an overall winner? I'm particularly interested in whether either of them is misapplying James's or Clifford's own terms at any point, and in anything you think both of them are overlooking entirely.
