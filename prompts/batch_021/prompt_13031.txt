I'm doing a PhD in bioethics (it's a joint program so I get a lot of overlap with the philosophy department), and for a paper I've been going down a rabbit hole on relational/communitarian theories of personhood, specifically the Menkiti vs. Gyekye disagreement within African philosophy. Menkiti's classic line, from "Person and Community in African Traditional Thought," is that personhood isn't a metaphysical given but something you achieve progressively through participation in communal life and rites of passage -- so it comes in degrees, elders count as fuller persons than kids, and the individual is basically constituted by their web of relations rather than pre-existing it. Gyekye pushes back with what's usually called "moderate" communitarianism: he wants to keep the relational, Ubuntu-flavored insight that the self is genuinely shaped by community rather than just contingently related to it, but insists there's some minimal, non-derivative core of personhood (capacity for rational choice/evaluation, basically) that doesn't depend on communal recognition -- partly so you're not stuck saying weird things about individual rights against the community.

Here's the case that's been bugging me, that I can't find either of them addressing head on, and that I think makes for a genuinely hard test of the arguement rather than a strawman either way: what should each view say about someone with advanced dementia, who no longer recognizes family, can't participate in communal life anymore, and (on some readings) has lost most of the relational ties that supposedly constituted them as a person in the first place? The strong Menkiti-style answer sounds like it has to say personhood has diminished or partly lapsed, which feels awful, but there are actually interesting escape hatches available (e.g. maybe it's the community's continuing relation to the person, as remembered kin, that sustains their personhood even once they can't reciprocate) -- so I don't want this to be a free win for Gyekye either.

Could you write me a dialogue -- real back and forth, not two separate monologues -- between a Menkiti-style radical/strong communitarian and a Gyekye-style moderate communitarian arguing specifically about this dementia case? I want each side to actually respond to the other's strongest move rather than just restating their home position, and I want the strong communitarian in particular to come across as having genuine resources here rather than as the obvious villain of the piece.
