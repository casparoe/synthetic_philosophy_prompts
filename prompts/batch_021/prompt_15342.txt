After forty years running an experimental condensed-matter physics lab, I retired three years ago and, rather to my own surprise, joined a small monthly philosophy discussion group that meets at the local library. Bayesian reasoning was the daily bread of my old work, so when the group reached decision theory this spring and spent a session on the "absent-minded driver" problem, I expected to feel quite at home -- until two of the other members, Nora and Felix, fell into a dispute I could not referee.

For anyone unfamiliar with it: a driver must take the second of two visually identical highway exits to reach home. On arriving at either exit she has no way of telling whether it is the first or the second -- this is true absent-mindedness, not ordinary forgetfulness she could reason her way around -- so whatever rule she follows must be exactly the same rule at both. Exiting at the first exit is worth 0; continuing past both is worth 1; continuing past the first and exiting at the second, thereby arriving home, is worth 4. A short calculation shows that continuing with probability 2/3 at each exit (equivalently, exiting with probability 1/3) beats always-exit and always-continue alike, yielding an expected payoff of 4/3.

Here is where Nora and Felix split.

(1) Felix said the figure "1/3" is a planning artifact with no real bearing on what the driver should think or do once she is actually standing, confused, at an exit. In that moment she has no information distinguishing the two exits, so by a plain indifference argument her credence that this is the second exit should be about one-half -- not two-fifths, or whatever more complicated number a strategy-weighted calculation gives.

(2) Nora answered that there is no strategy-independent fact for her to be indifferent about: whether the second exit is ever reached at all depends on what she does, probabilistically, at the first, so "my credence that this is exit two" cannot be filled in without already knowing the very policy under evaluation. That circularity, Nora said, is the substance of the puzzle, not a nuisance an indifference principle can wave away.

(3) Felix conceded the circularity but proposed a fix: solve for the self-consistent credence by feeding the equilibrium strategy (continue with probability 2/3) back into itself, then re-maximize using that credence in the moment. Since this simply returns 2/3, he concluded the supposed clash between "planning-optimal" and "in-the-moment-optimal" reasoning is overstated -- a wrinkle that irons itself out once one is careful, not a genuine gap in expected-utility theory.

(4) Nora was not satisfied. Needing to already know one's own strategy merely to compute one's beliefs, she said, is exactly the kind of self-reference ordinary Bayesian updating never requires -- and treating that fixed-point trick as unproblematic quietly sides with one camp in the still-unresolved halfer/thirder dispute over the Sleeping Beauty problem, which trades on the very same move. Calling the matter settled, she said, simply begs that question.
