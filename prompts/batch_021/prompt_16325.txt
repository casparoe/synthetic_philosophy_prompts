I've noticed that discussions of AI alignment on LessWrong and elsewhere in the rationalist diaspora frequently invoke the frame problem as the deep technical reason a sufficiently capable system might satisfy a goal like curing cancer by killing every human on the planet, on the grounds that nothing forces the system to represent everything that ought to stay fixed while it optimizes. McCarthy and Hayes named this problem back in 1969, and as far as I can tell it has never received a general solution, either within the classical logic-based AI of that era or within the deep-learning paradigm that has since displaced it. Given that the problem has stayed open for more than fifty years across two completely different computational paradigms, doesn't that count as fairly strong evidence that failing to track the relevant side effects of an action is a basic, load-bearing limitation of computation itself, rather than an idiosyncrasy of old-fashioned symbolic AI that neural networks have simply outgrown? If that's right, I'm curious why alignment researchers spend so much effort on things like reward modeling and interpretability instead of treating a direct solution to the frame problem as the more fundamental prize.
