I'm a novelist working on a book about two former graduate-school flatmates, Ruth and Frank, who reunite for a week forty years on, not long after Ruth receives a diagnosis that will make this their last such visit. She finished a doctorate in philosophy while he drifted out of a mathematics program into finance, and the novel keeps circling back to an argument neither of them ever quite let drop across the intervening decades of scattered postcards and occasional phone calls: whether truth can outrun what is, even in principle, knowable.

Ruth has spent her career as an unfashionable but serious verificationist in something like Michael Dummett's mold. Frank likes to remind her, with the particular tenderness of a very old joke, of a result he half-absorbed in a logic elective decades ago and never quite forgot: that granting her favourite thesis -- every truth is knowable -- together with the merely honest admission that somebody, somewhere, fails to know some truth, yields the patently absurd conclusion that every truth is already known by someone. I understand this goes by the name Fitch's paradox of knowability, and I want to give it real weight across two or three scenes rather than have Frank simply spring the trick and win outright; a woman who has taught this material for thirty years would have something serious, not merely defensive, to say back to an old friend.

So, purely for my own preparation, and not for anything that would be quoted verbatim on the page: could you first give me a genuinely intuitive feel for why the derivation works -- not just the bare sequence of modal moves, but the underlying reason such a mild-sounding premise detonates into full omniscience -- and then talk me through the two or three most serious lines of response philosophers have actually tried (restricting the knowability principle to some narrower class of statements, tinkering with the background logic, or whatever else I'm missing), being candid about why none of them is generally reckoned a clean escape? I would like to distribute the disagreement believably between two tired, affectionate people on a porch, which means I need to understand its actual shape rather than hand either of them a Wikipedia summary.
