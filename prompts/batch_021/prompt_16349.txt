Over the past year or so I've been teaching myself philosophy of mind in whatever spare time I have, largely because of an argument with a friend that never really got resolved. We were watching body-cam footage of a police shooting, and when the officer said he "genuinely saw" a gun in the suspect's hand where there was actually just a phone, my friend insisted that settled the matter in the officer's favor: his eyes had simply deceived him, so there was nothing more to fault him for. That disagreement is what eventually led me to what philosophers call the cognitive penetration debate -- the question of whether what we believe, fear, or expect can change what we actually see, rather than just the judgment we form once we've already seen it. These days I run a small podcast out of my apartment where a couple of friends and I work through problems like this one, and next month's guest is a philosopher who has built her career defending the strong version of that claim: that perceptual experience itself, not merely the belief formed on top of it, can be reshaped by expectation, emotion, and even racial stereotype, with consequences for things like how much weight the law ought to give a defendant's sincere claim that a suspect "looked like" he was reaching for a weapon.

I don't want to spend the hour letting her simply restate that thesis, so I'd like help drafting eight to ten interview questions that actually push on it, each paired with a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give, the way a sharp interviewer presses rather than moving on to the next topic. I'd like at least one question built around the recent charge that nearly every classic demonstration of penetration is confounded -- including the well-known finding that hills look steeper to people who are tired, where, tellingly, the effect shows up in people's verbal and visual estimates but disappears once they're asked to act on the slope with an unseen hand, which sounds a lot like a shift in reported judgment rather than in seeing itself. I'd like one question on whether confining the claim to some "higher," more conceptual layer of perception -- since nearly everyone already grants that early, low-level vision is walled off from belief -- rescues the thesis only by making it true almost by definition. I'd like one question on whether the increasingly popular idea that the brain is fundamentally a prediction engine running on priors undercuts her whole framing, since if every percept already depends on expectation, "penetration" stops picking out anything distinctive. And I'd like a last question that presses the comparison she herself sometimes draws to expert perceptual learning -- a radiologist who has trained herself to see a tumor that a novice would miss -- and asks what, beyond simply approving of the outcome in one case and not the other, actually distinguishes that from the racially biased seeing she wants to call epistemically corrupt.
