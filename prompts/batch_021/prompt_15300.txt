I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and next week I'm interviewing a researcher for a long-form profile. She has spent much of her career pushing Amotz Zahavi's handicap principle well beyond its usual peacock-tail examples, arguing that the same logic explains cooperative and even seemingly altruistic behavior among social animals, and lately she has been extending it into human status displays as well. In every talk and prior interview of hers I could track down, she closes off follow-up questions with the same line: "an honest signal has to hurt." It's a good soundbite for a general audience, but I spent the weekend rereading Grafen's 1990 formal treatment of the handicap principle as an evolutionarily stable strategy, the taxonomy John Maynard Smith and David Harper later used to separate genuine handicaps from cost-free "index" signals and from conventionally maintained ones, and a critique — I believe it's usually credited to Thomas Getty — arguing that what Grafen's mathematics actually requires is a difference in marginal cost or benefit across quality levels, not high total cost as such. I don't want her coasting on the slogan for the whole piece, especially since this interview is meant to anchor a wider package we're running on costly signaling and trust.

Could you draft eight to ten interview questions, sequenced so the slogan gets progressively harder to lean on as the conversation goes? I need at least one question that makes her confront the gap between "costly, therefore honest" and the actual marginal-cost condition in the formal models; one that puts a genuinely cost-free honest signal in front of her and asks her to square it with the principle; one that asks her to defend extending the logic from ornaments to babbler-style "competitive altruism," where reputation or kin selection could be doing the real work under handicap-flavored language; and one, near the end, asking what finding — in animals or in people — would actually count as evidence against the handicap principle rather than simply being absorbed as one more confirming case. Please also work in a question that lets her extend the argument to human behavior, such as conspicuous consumption or status-driven giving, without just letting her assert the analogy for free. For each question, add a short line noting the deflection she's most likely to attempt and how the follow-up is meant to close it off.
