This summer I've been falling down a rabbit hole on bet-sizing and the Kelly criterion, partly because a few of us run a small pick'em pool where we wager on NFL games most weekends, and partly because I'm trying to get sharper with probability before an olympiad-style math competition this fall. Once I got past the standard "bet a fraction of your bankroll proportional to your edge" explanation, I found there's a real disagreement underneath it, not just a rounding-error dispute about the exact fraction to bet.

One side, which I've seen described as "geometric rationality," argues that maximizing the long-run growth rate of your bankroll (equivalently, maximizing the expected logarithm of your wealth) isn't just one reasonable strategy among others, but close to the objectively correct thing to do whenever you face repeated bets, because it is the quantity that actually determines what happens to a single bettor's wealth over time, rather than some average across many parallel bettors that no single person ever lives through. The other side says this is overclaiming: maximizing expected log wealth is simply what an agent with logarithmic utility should do, and nothing in the axioms of rational choice says every rational bettor has to have that utility function. On this view, a bettor who genuinely doesn't care about risk should rationally wager everything on every sufficiently favorable bet, full stop, even though repeating that strategy guarantees ruin with probability one eventually, because "probability one eventually" is a fact about the path wealth takes, not about the expectation such a bettor claims to actually care about.

I'd like to see this argued out properly rather than summarized, since every explanation I've found just states one side's conclusion. Could you write a dialogue between two characters, one who defends geometric rationality and Kelly betting as the objectively correct response to repeated wagers, and one who defends expected-value maximization as at least an equally rational choice for a bettor with the right preferences? Set it at the point where they're arguing concretely about how each of them would size bets on a coin known to be biased 60/40 in the bettor's favor, repeated fifty times in a row. I want real engagement rather than each side just restating its opening position: the expected-value defender should have an actual answer to the "you'll go broke with probability one" objection, and the geometric-rationality defender should have an actual answer to the charge that they are just smuggling in a preference for long-run outcomes over short-run ones while calling it objectivity. Please don't let either character come off as the obvious loser of the exchange.
