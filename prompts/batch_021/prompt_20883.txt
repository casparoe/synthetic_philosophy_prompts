As a philosophy professor, I regularly have to decide how much weight to give a colleague's assurance that a difficult technical result holds, in cases where checking the proof or data myself would take far more time than I actually have. Nyāya epistemology treats śabda (testimony) as an independent pramāṇa rather than reducing it, as the Buddhist epistemologists and the Vaiśeṣikas did, to a special case of inference: a cognition counts as knowledge through testimony only when it derives from the assertion of an āpta, a speaker who both possesses accurate knowledge of the matter and has no intention to mislead. Please describe the most important implications of this Nyāya account, and especially of the āpta requirement, for how I should calibrate my trust in expert testimony within my own field, and explain how the resulting advice differs from that of a broadly Humean or Frickerian reductionist, for whom testimonial justification is always parasitic on the hearer's own independent inductive evidence for testimony's general reliability. Please also address whether the externally oriented āpta requirement in fact pushes the Nyāya account closer to a reliabilist epistemology of testimony than to either side of that contemporary debate.
