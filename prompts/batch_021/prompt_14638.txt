So I got into a whole argument with my friend Priya last night over way too much wine about whether an AI could ever really trust itself, and it turned into this whole tangent about something she calls the "Löbian obstacle," which she picked up from an AI safety reading group she's in. Her claim: any agent that only acts on what it can formally prove runs into a wall from Löb's theorem the second it tries to trust a future or smarter version of itself to only take "safe" actions, because the reflection principle it would need -- something like "if I can prove X is safe, then X is safe" -- is exactly the self-referential setup that Löb's theorem turns into a machine for proving everything, safe or not. She said that's why the naive "tiling agent" idea (self-modify only after proving your successor is safe) doesn't actually work as stated, and that the fixes she'd heard of -- some hierarchy where each successor has to use a strictly weaker proof system than the one before it, plus a probabilistic version of reflection that supposedly dodges the issue entirely -- are patches, not real solutions. I said this all sounded like a cute fact about toy logician-robots with basically zero relevance to how any actual AI works, since none of them reason by proving theorems about themselves, they just do statistics on data.

Neither of us budged and its been bugging me all week.
