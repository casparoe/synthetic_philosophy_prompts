G. E. M. Anscombe's case for calling Truman a murderer over the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki, made in her 1956 pamphlet Mr. Truman's Degree, turns on describing the civilian deaths as an intended means to ending the war, rather than a merely foreseen side effect of destroying military targets — the redescription that the doctrine of double effect standardly uses to excuse civilian deaths from conventional strategic bombing. Historians still dispute Truman's actual purpose (shortening the war, averting an invasion's casualties, or signaling American power to the Soviet Union), which motivates the objection that Anscombe's verdict cannot be assessed until the dispute is settled, since intentions are individuated partly by the further ends they serve. Using a counterfactual test familiar from the double-effect literature — would the operation have achieved its aim had the bombs, by some miracle, killed no one? — please argue that the civilian deaths count as an intended means rather than a foreseen side effect under all three candidate purposes, so that the dispute over Truman's ultimate aim is immaterial to Anscombe's charge.
