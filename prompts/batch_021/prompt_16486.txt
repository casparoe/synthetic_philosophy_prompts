I cover tech and courts for a living, and for a story on pretrial risk-assessment tools I put two of my sources on a call together to compare notes -- which is how I ended up watching them get into an argument I found more interesting than anything either of them said on the record. Marcus used to build these risk scores (they predict how likely a defendant is to be rearrested before trial, and courts use them to help decide who gets released) and Dana is a public defender who deals with the fallout. The tool they were arguing about happens to be well "calibrated" across neighborhoods: if it rates a group of defendants 70% likely to be rearrested, about 70% of them actually are, and that holds up about equally whether your talking about the rich side of the county or the poor side. Marcus's opening move was that this pretty much settles it -- a calibrated prediction is close to the textbook definition of an unbiased one, so the complaints about the tool are baseless.

Dana's comeback was that calibration completely dodges the actual complaint: among defendants who were never rearrested, way more from the poor side of the county got wrongly tagged "high risk" than the equivalent people from the rich side -- something like three times as many, if I remember her numbers right. That's a real cost, falling very unevenly, and calibration is silent on it. Marcus said fine, then just recalibrate so both sides of the county get flagged high-risk at the same false-positive rate. Dana, who'd clearly done some homework, said that's not actually available to him for free: the two sides of the county have different underlying rearrest rates to begin with, and you cannot equalize false-positive rates across groups without wrecking calibration, so he'd just be trading one unfairness for a different one, not removing it. Marcus, sounding a little cornered, said fine, then drop neighborhood and race from the model's inputs entirely. Dana said the model would reconstruct that information anyway from things like prior-arrest counts and zip code, and that blinding it on purpose can make the gap worse instead of better, since you lose the one lever that would let you correct for it deliberately. It ended with Marcus declaring that the whole exchange proves "a fair algorithm" is a contradiction in terms and everyone should quit pretending otherwise and just optimize for raw accuracy, and Dana saying that's a dodge -- the impossibility just means he has to openly own which unfairness he's picking, not that the choice stops mattering.
