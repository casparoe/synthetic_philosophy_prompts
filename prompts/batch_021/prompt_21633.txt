And here's the student's answer, word for word:

"Clark and Chalmers use Otto, who has Alzheimer's and writes down everything he needs in a notebook, and Inga, who looks the same information up in biological memory, to argue that the notebook plays the same functional role for Otto that memory does for Inga. This is the parity principle: if a process in the world would count as cognitive were it done in the head, it should count as cognitive even when it's not done in the head. The claim isn't that the notebook is merely like memory, it's that it literally is part of Otto's belief-forming system, because it's coupled to him in the right way.

Really this is just an extension of the externalism that Putnam and Burge had already defended with Twin Earth: the idea that what's in the head doesn't fix the relevant facts (meaning, for Putnam; cognition, for Clark and Chalmers), so the mind can depend constitutively on the environment the same way meaning does. Clark and Chalmers add further conditions the resource has to meet: it has to be constantly available, retrieving it has to be easy, and information from it has to be accepted automatically, not treated with the suspicion we'd apply to a stranger's testimony.

A well-known objection is the coupling-constitution fallacy, due to Fodor: he argues that Otto's notebook only causally influences his beliefs, and mere causal coupling to a process isn't sufficient to make something constitutive of that process, any more than my pen is part of my handwriting just because I rely on it. I find this fairly persuasive, though I'm honestly not sure where it leaves something like a smartphone."
