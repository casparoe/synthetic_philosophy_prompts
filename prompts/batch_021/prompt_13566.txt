Question: (a) State Goodman's definition of the predicate "grue," and explain precisely why the emeralds we have examined so far confirm the hypothesis "all emeralds are grue" exactly as well as they confirm "all emeralds are green." (b) Goodman argues that "green" rather than "grue" is the projectible predicate because "green" is more entrenched, and he explicitly declines to ground this preference in the relative logical simplicity of the two predicates. Explain his notion of entrenchment, and explain why the simplicity move is not available to him. (c) Some commentators describe the new riddle as merely Hume's old problem of induction restated with a fresh example. Would Goodman himself have agreed with that description? Explain why or why not.

Student answer:
(a) Goodman defines an object as grue if it is examined before some future time t and found to be green, or if it is not examined before t and is blue. Since every emerald anyone has examined so far was examined before t and was green, every one of those emeralds is also grue. So the evidence we have confirms "all emeralds are green" and "all emeralds are grue" equally well, even though the two hypotheses disagree completely about emeralds we examine after t: the first predicts they will be green, the second that they will be blue.

(b) Goodman thinks "green" should be projected instead of "grue" because "green" is entrenched: it is built out of a simpler, non-disjunctive concept, whereas "grue" is a gerrymandered composite of "green," "blue," and a reference to a specific time, and disjunctive predicates of this kind are inherently less natural and therefore less simple. Since simpler hypotheses are more likely to be true, "green" wins out.

(c) The new riddle is really just Hume's problem of induction dressed up with a new example. Hume showed that no amount of past evidence can logically justify any conclusion about the future, and Goodman's grue case simply dramatizes that same gap between evidence and prediction by showing that induction cannot even tell us which regularities to extrapolate. So Goodman's ultimate conclusion, like Hume's, is that inductive inference has no rational foundation.
