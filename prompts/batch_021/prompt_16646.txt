I'm a consultant clinical psychologist leading the autism assessment pathway at my NHS trust, and until this spring "Pathological Demand Avoidance" was a term I mostly associated with continuing-education seminars I hadn't gotten around to attending. Then my sister's son came home from a private assessment with a PDA profile the family had first read about on a parents' forum, and within a couple of months the label had reorganised how everyone in the house talked about him, managed him, and — this is the part that has stayed with me — how he talked about himself. It made the question our trust's Neurodevelopmental Diagnostic Practice Group is about to vote on feel considerably less academic than it would have six months ago.

The group must decide whether the trust will start formally recognising PDA (some clinicians now prefer "extreme demand avoidance," partly to shed the word "pathological") as a named profile within autism assessment reports, or continue our current unwritten practice of noting demand-avoidant behaviour only in general descriptive terms, without the label, within a standard diagnosis. This is not merely a semantic choice: the wording of a report shapes Education, Health and Care Plan applications, school placement recommendations, and referral to the specific low-demand parenting and teaching approaches some clinicians swear by. One camp on the committee argues that declining to use a term families and, increasingly, autistic adults already use to describe themselves is a form of institutional bad faith. Another camp keeps citing Ian Hacking's account of looping effects — his nineteenth-century fugue patients, the 1980s surge in multiple personality diagnoses — to argue that a trust lending its authority to a contested category is precisely how a classification hardens into the behaviour it claims only to describe, especially once children old enough to read their own notes start doing so.

Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate before the group meets next month. It should recommend one concrete option — formally recognise the profile, decline to, or recognise it conditionally with a named review date — and it should take both Hacking's worry and the bad-faith objection seriously enough that a proponent of either could see their strongest argument reflected in it.
