For the last three years, I've written a small weekly newsletter recommending films to a few thousand subscribers. It started as a hobby during a stretch of unemployment, and I've kept at it because I love talking about movies, even though I never studied film or writing in any formal way. Since far more films come out every week than I could possibly watch myself, I've gotten into the habit of including a handful of new releases I haven't yet seen, basing the write-up on a few critics whose judgment I've come to trust over the years, along with the broader consensus on sites like Letterboxd. I'll write something like "the cinematography is stunning" or "the lead performance is quietly devastating," and until recently nobody seemed to mind.

Last month, though, a longtime subscriber pushed back in a way I haven't been able to shake off. She said she had no problem with my reporting things like the runtime, the director's earlier credits, or how the film performed at the box office secondhand, since those are simply facts I picked up from somewhere reliable. But she said it was different, maybe even a little dishonest, for me to assert that a film is beautifully shot or emotionally devastating before I had watched a single frame of it, no matter how much I trusted the critics from whom I was borrowing that judgment. In her view, you cannot really know that a film is moving or gorgeous secondhand the way you can know its runtime secondhand; you have to see it for yourself.

I keep turning this over. We take each other's word for all kinds of things every day without checking them ourselves, and nobody thinks twice about it. So why does it feel different when the claim being borrowed concerns how something looks, sounds, or moves you, rather than some plainer matter of fact? The same puzzle arises whenever I pass along a friend's opinion that a dessert is exquisite, or that a piece of music is beautifully arranged, without having tasted the dessert or heard the music myself. Is my subscriber onto something real about why judgments of beauty can't simply be taken on faith the way ordinary facts can, or is she just holding one part of my newsletter to a standard she wouldn't apply anywhere else? I'd like to understand whether there's an actual principle at stake here, rather than just deciding case by case what feels acceptable to write.
