My daughter, a professional violinist, is getting married next month, and has asked me -- her father, who has taught philosophy for thirty years and who put Schopenhauer in her hands in the first place -- to give the wedding toast. She is a rigorous and entirely sincere Schopenhauerian, and she has set two conditions on what I'm allowed to say about the string quartet playing at the reception. First, I can't describe the music as capturing her and her fiancé's particular love story, since she holds that music, unlike painting or poetry, doesn't represent the world of particular things at all -- for Schopenhauer it bypasses that world entirely and objectifies the will directly, in the abstract, independent of any particular occasion or narrative. Second, I can't use the music, or anything else, to imply that their bond is uniquely elevated, since she's just as committed to Schopenhauer's account of romantic passion as the will-to-live's ruse for producing well-matched offspring, dressed up to the lovers themselves as personal destiny. Most of the room has never heard of Schopenhauer, but three of her old philosophy-department friends will be sitting near the front and will notice immediately if I soften either point. Please write the five-minute toast: warm, funny in places, and faithful to both conditions.
