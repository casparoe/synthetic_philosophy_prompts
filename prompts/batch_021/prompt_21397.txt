At my company we build infrastructure for a couple of the labs training large models, and one of our research-adjacent engineers is really into the anthropic-reasoning side of AI risk debates. He got me reading Nick Bostrom's older work on observation selection effects, and I've been stuck for a week on the disagreement between the Self-Sampling Assumption (SSA) and the Self-Indication Assumption (SIA). As I understand it, SSA says you should reason as though you were a random sample from the observers who actually exist in your reference class, which is the premise that generates the Doomsday Argument's uncomfortable update toward humanity having a short future left. SIA is supposed to fix this: it has you additionally weight each hypothesis by how many observers it predicts to exist, so that being an "early" human stops being surprising once you condition on a hypothesis that implies trillions of future humans as well. The catch, which even Bostrom seems to treat as the more damning of the two problems, is that SIA generates the "presumptuous philosopher": if two cosmological theories are equally compatible with all available evidence but one implies a far larger universe (and hence far more observers) than the other, SIA lets a philosopher declare from an armchair that the high-population theory is overwhelmingly more likely, and tell the physicists they can call off their expensive experiment, since merely existing is already enormous evidence for it.

My colleague thinks this settles things in SSA's favor and that we should just live with the Doomsday Argument as the price of a sane epistemology. I'm not convinced, partly because SSA has its own well-known embarrassments (the reference-class problem, the "Adam and Eve" cases, and a tendency to make your credence about past events depend on choices you haven't made yet), and partly because I suspect "this conclusion sounds absurd" is doing more work in the standard verdict against SIA than it deserves to. Neither of us has any formal training in philosophy, so rather than trade half-formed intuitions again, I'd like a properly worked-out case to bring back to him. Please mount the strongest defense you can of SIA against the presumptuous philosopher objection -- that is, argue that a consistent SIA-theorist can accept the philosopher's conclusion about the physicists' two theories without having thereby abandoned good epistemic practice. I'd like the argument to actually engage the mechanics of that scenario, explaining why a population-weighted update feels illegitimate there in a way ordinary Bayesian updating on evidence doesn't, rather than simply asserting that SSA's costs are worse -- and to close by naming the strongest version of my colleague's position that survives it.
