I've been teaching myself decision theory in my spare time -- no formal background, just some behavioral economics books and a lot of late-night reading -- and I keep running into the Allais paradox in a form that the popular explanations don't really do justice to. As far as I can tell, the actual structure is this: most people prefer a certain $1 million to a gamble offering an 89% chance of $1 million, a 10% chance of $5 million, and a 1% chance of nothing. But if you take that 89% chance of $1 million, which is common to both options, and replace it with an 89% chance of nothing instead, most of the same people reverse course: now they prefer a 10% chance of $5 million (and a 90% chance of nothing) over an 11% chance of $1 million (and an 89% chance of nothing). The two situations differ only in what happens with the probability that's held fixed across them, and the independence axiom says that shared part shouldn't affect how you rank the rest -- yet for almost everyone, it clearly does.

What really got me interested is a story I came across about Maurice Allais first putting exactly this choice pattern to Leonard Savage in person -- I believe at a conference on rational decision-making in Paris in the early 1950s -- where Savage himself answered the two questions "inconsistently," and then, once this was pointed out to him, decided that his own preference had been the mistake rather than treating it as a strike against the axiom. That seems to me like the real fork in the road for this whole debate: does a stable, carefully considered preference against the axiom count as evidence against the axiom, or is the axiom allowed to override the preference, the way a completed proof overrides a mistaken bit of arithmetic?

I'd like you to write a dialogue between two present-day decision theorists who take opposite sides of exactly that question, constructed so that each one actually engages with the other's argument rather than just restating their own. One should argue the Savage side: that independence (which Savage's own framework treats via a version of the sure-thing principle) is a genuine requirement of rationality, that the pull toward the certain million -- and away from certainty once it's off the table -- is a bias to be corrected once clearly seen, and that someone who reliably violates independence displays a real structural inconsistency even though there's no literal sequence of trades to money-pump here, since this is a single choice among mutually exclusive lotteries rather than a repeated gamble played out over time. The other should argue the Allais side: that a stable, reflective preference against independence isn't automatically irrational, and that the axiom quietly assumes something substantive and contestable -- that an outcome's contribution to value has to be separable from the rest of the probability mixture surrounding it -- when a person might reasonably care about the shape of the whole gamble, for instance by placing special weight on eliminating risk altogether rather than merely reducing it. Please have the Savage character press the consistency worry hard and have the Allais character give it a direct answer instead of retreating to "but people do choose this way," and please have the Allais character push on what actually entitles the axiom to override considered preference in the first place, with the Savage character offering a substantive answer rather than just asserting that the axiom is self-evident. I'd like the disagreement to end unresolved, with neither side conceding, so it's clear exactly where the argument gets stuck rather than wrapping up neatly.
