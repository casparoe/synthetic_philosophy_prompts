Here's the question, and their answer:

the question: Using Hacking's distinction between interactive kinds and indifferent kinds, explain why the rise in adult ADHD diagnoses over the past twenty years isn't simply analogous to a rise in cancer diagnoses caused by better imaging technology. Then explain why, on Hacking's view, calling ADHD an "interactive kind" does not commit you to saying ADHD isn't a real disorder.

their answer: Hacking's looping effect is basically this: when doctors come up with a category to sort people into, the people in that category find out about the label and start changing how they act to fit it, which forces the category to get revised to match the new behaviour, and round and round it goes. That's different from a scanner finding a tumour that was there all along, because the tumour doesn't know its been classified as cancer and doesn't change what it does in response to being named — that's an "indifferent kind," where the label never feeds back into the thing being labelled. ADHD is an "interactive kind" instead: once someone is told they have it, that changes how they see themselves and how teachers, parents and bosses treat them, which changes what people go looking for, which changes what the DSM counts as ADHD next time round. So the diagnosis boom isn't just doctors getting better at spotting something that was sitting there the whole time, the way the scanner finds the tumour — it's a fundamentally circular process, and that's exactly why the numbers just keep going up and will keep going up for as long as the label exists.

That said, Hacking would still call ADHD a real disorder, because this whole looping idea is really just him giving a tidier, more analytic name to Foucault's point that power invents the categories that then produce the people who fit them — and "invented" was never supposed to mean "fake" for either of them. It just means built by people, at a particular moment, for particular reasons — the doctors aren't inventing the kid's restlessness, they're inventing the box that restlessness now gets sorted into. He makes the same move with multiple personality disorder, which he wrote a whole book about: the fact that diagnoses exploded in the 80s because of a loop between therapists, patients and daytime talk shows, then dropped off fast once the recovered-memory backlash hit in the 90s, doesn't mean anyone's distress was fake — it just means you can't ask "is MPD real" the same flat way you'd ask "is gold real," because gold isn't in a looping relationship with the chemists who classify it.
