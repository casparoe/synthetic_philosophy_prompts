I spent nearly forty years doing experimental physics, quoting confidence intervals and treating quantum-mechanical decay rates as objective facts about individual atoms, all without giving much thought to what "probability" actually means. Now that retirement has given me the leisure to read philosophy properly, Popper's propensity theory strikes me as obviously the right interpretation for a working physicist to hold, and clearly superior to its two main rivals. Frequentism can only define probability by appeal to an actual or hypothetical long run of repetitions, which never told me what it could mean for this particular atom, in this particular state, to have a seventy percent chance of decaying within the hour, rather than some hypothetical population of atoms; subjectivism avoids that difficulty but only by making the probability a fact about someone's degree of belief rather than about the atom itself, which seems worse. Propensity theory dissolves both worries at a stroke, by locating an objective probability in the single experimental setup itself, seemingly without any of the drawbacks that afflict its rivals. Given that it succeeds exactly where the alternatives struggle, why do philosophers of probability still treat it as merely one contested option among several, rather than as the settled answer for how to interpret quantum-mechanical chances?
