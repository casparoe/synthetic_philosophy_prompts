So a friend of mine who's way deeper into philosophy than I am was going on about something called Van Fraassen's Reflection Principle at a meetup last week (I think it's from some formal epistemology paper of his from the 80s?), and it's been stuck in my head ever since because it feels weirdly relevant to something that drives me up the wall at work. From what I understood -- and please correct me if I mangled this -- the basic idea is that your current degree of belief in some claim should equal whatever degree of belief you already know you're going to have in that exact same claim at some specific point in the future. So if you know for a fact that next Tuesday you'll be 80% confident in X, rationality supposedly requires that you already be 80% confident in X today, otherwise a sufficiently clever bookie could string together bets now and bets conditional on your future opinion in a way that guarantees you loose money no matter how things turn out.

This immediately reminded me of sprint planning. Every single cycle, on Monday I'll estimate we're something like 90% likely to ship a given feature by Friday, and then by Wednesday, once we've actually poked around in the code, that number has quietly collapsed to like 40%, and at this point I basically know in advance that Wednesday-me is always going to be way more pessimistic than Monday-me. Does the Reflection Principle mean Monday-me is being irrational for not just adopting Wednesday's number up front and skipping the charade entirely? That feels off to me though, because at least some of the reason Wednesday-me is gloomier is just that Wednesday-me is tired and jaded from actually wrestling with the code, not that Wednesday-me has learned some genuine new fact Monday-me lacked -- though other times it really is because we ran into some unexpected complexity. Could you describe the most important implications of the Reflection Principle for a situation like this -- i.e., under what conditions I'm actually rationally required to defer to my own predicted future opinion, versus when I'm allowed to just write it off as future-me being grumpy? I'd like to get past the one-line dictionary version of the principle if possible and understand what's actually going on underneath it.
