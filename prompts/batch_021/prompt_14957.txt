Aumann's agreement theorem is often summarized as showing that agents with common priors "cannot agree to disagree": if two Bayesian agents share a common prior, and their posterior probabilities for some proposition are common knowledge between them, those posteriors must be equal. Since two equally rational, equally well-informed people who talk through a disagreement—laying out their full reasoning and evidence for one another—are thereby making their views common knowledge, shouldn't the theorem guarantee that they converge on the same probability, with any remaining disagreement counting as proof that one of them is reasoning badly? Yet careful, mutually respectful thinkers routinely discuss questions at length and still end up with differing views. What explains this apparent gap between the theorem and everyday experience?
