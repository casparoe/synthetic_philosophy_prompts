I'm a philosophy PhD student working on formal epistemology (mostly Bayesian stuff), and I got derailed from my quals reading list last night arguing with a friend from the math department about Bertrand's paradox. Setup, in case it's useful: you inscribe an equilateral triangle in a circle and ask for the probability that a "randomly chosen" chord is longer than a side of the triangle. Pick the chord via two random endpoints on the circumference and you get 1/3; pick a random radius and a random point along it and you get 1/2; pick a random point in the disk to be the chord's midpoint and you get 1/4. Same question, three mutually exclusive answers, and there's no fact of the matter about which procedure counts as "the" uniform distribution over chords, since "pick a chord at random" just underdetermines the sampling procedure.

My friend's take, which I'm embarassingly close to being convinced by, is that this basically sinks the principle of indifference as any kind of rational constraint on credences. If "assign equal probability to symmetric-seeming outcomes" can be made to spit out three incompatible answers to literally the same question just by fiddling with the parametrization, then indifference reasoning was never tracking anything objective to begin with, and the only honest position left is pretty much full subjectivism - your priors just need to be internally coherent, and beyond that your "degree of ignorance" doesnt actually constrain credence at all. Is my friend basically right about this, or is there a decent reply available to people like Jaynes who still want symmetry/indifference considerations to do real normative work in pinning down priors? I feel like I'm missing something here but can't quite put my finger on what.
