Descartes held that the mind is a single, indivisible substance, and treated this indivisibility as key evidence for its real distinction from the body, which by contrast is always divisible into parts. In his physiological writings he pressed the point further, locating the seat of sensory unification in the pineal gland precisely because it is the one structure in the brain that is not paired, unlike the hemispheres themselves or the two eyes and ears that feed into them. Suppose he were confronted with the evidence from split-brain (commissurotomy) patients, in whom severing the corpus callosum can produce two apparently independent streams of perception, attention, and intention within a single skull. Would he have to concede that this refutes his indivisibility thesis, or does his framework contain the resources to accommodate it on its own terms — and what would he then have to say about the corpus callosum's role, now that the pineal gland can no longer be doing all the unifying work alone?
