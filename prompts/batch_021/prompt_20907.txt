I'm teaching a graduate seminar on philosophy of physics this term, and this week's session on Bell's theorem turned into a genuinely fun argument I haven't been able to settle to my own satisfaction, so I want to put it to you. Everyone in the room agrees on the basic timeline: Einstein died in 1955, Bell's paper came out in 1964, and the "loophole-free" experiments that finally closed the detection and locality loopholes together didnt happen until 2015 — so Einstein never actually had to look any of this in the eye. My TA's confident answer is that Einstein would've just become a Bohmian, since pilot-wave theory hands you back determinism and realism. A couple of students want him to retreat into superdeterminism instead, since that's the one loophole no experiment can ever close (it just denies that the experimenter's choice of settings is independent of the hidden variables). One student thinks he'd have simply denied the experiments proved anything conclusive. I'm not fully satisfied with any of these three and want your actual reasoning, not just a verdict.

Here's the wrinkle I think my TA is skating past: Bohm's theory isn't some later development Einstein missed out on — Bohm worked it out partly out of run-ins with Einstein at Princeton and published it in 1952, three years before Einstein died, and Einstein was reportedly lukewarm about it rather than relieved to finally have his local realist completion of QM. Which tracks, once you notice pilot-wave theory is realist and deterministic but explicitly *not* local, so it doesn't actually hand Einstein the thing he seems to have wanted most. So don't just default to "he'd go Bohmian" — I want you to treat locality, realism, and determinism as separable commitments (Bell's theorem is a joint constraint on locality and realism together, not on either alone) and work out which one was most load-bearing for Einstein and which he'd sacrifice first, using what he actually said about separability, "spooky action at a distance," and his objections to Bohr's operationalism as your evidence, rather than just the general impression of him as a guy who "didn't like quantum weirdness." And take the superdeterminism option seriously on its own terms too — is it really available to someone with Einstein's other commitments about what makes science possible at all, or is it a worse bullet to bite than nonlocality would have been? Feel free to tell me the three options on the table are a false trilemma if you think there's a better answer.
