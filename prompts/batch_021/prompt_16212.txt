I've been teaching the "machine ethics" week of my applied ethics seminar for a few years now, and last time around I swear at least three separate students turned in some version of the self-driving trolley problem or a Terminator-style uprising as their example. What I actually want them to sit with is the "responsibility gap" idea from people like Andreas Matthias and Robert Sparrow -- the worry that once a system is genuinely learning and adapting after its deployed, nobody involved (not the programmers, not the operator, not a commanding officer who signed off on using it) actually meets the ordinary conditions we use to pin responsibility on someone -- forseeability, control, the right kind of intention -- and the machine itself can't be blamed or punished in any way that does real retributive work either. So you can end up with a bad outcome, a perfectly traceable causal history, and still nobody who's the right kind of "answerable" for it.

Could you write me a short story (a page or two is fine) that actually dramatizes that specific structural gap, rather than the more familiar "whodunit, and who's really at fault" mystery? Pick whatever setting you like -- military, medical, industrial, elder care, whatever -- but build in some kind of formal proceeding (an inquiry, a court-martial, a coroner's inquest, an internal review board, something along those lines) where people go through the candidates for blame one by one and each one genuinely, structurally doesn't fit -- not because of a cover-up or a conveniently missing piece of evidence. And please don't let the machine turn out to be secretly sentient and nobly take the blame itself, and don't let some poor mid-level engineer get scapegoated just for the sake of narrative closure. I want the ending to leave that gap sitting there, open and a little uncomfortable, since that's really the whole point of the exercise.
