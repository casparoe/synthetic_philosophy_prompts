I'm a PhD student in political philosophy currently taking a graduate seminar on analytical Marxism. This week we paired the chapters on surplus value in Capital, Volume I with John Roemer's reformulation of exploitation in A General Theory of Exploitation and Class (1982) and his paper "Should Marxists Be Interested in Exploitation?", and in discussion a classmate asked a question I couldn't answer cleanly: what, if anything, survives of Marx's original charge against capitalism once exploitation gets redefined the way Roemer redefines it. I would like to think this through properly before turning it into a chapter of my dissertation, and comparing the two accounts side by side seems like the best way in.

Concretely, could you compare Marx's classical account of exploitation with Roemer's alternative? On Marx's account, capitalists exploit workers by appropriating surplus value, that is, labor time performed over and above what is needed to reproduce the value of labor-power, which the labor theory of value treats as the sole source of all value in a capitalist economy. On Roemer's account, by contrast, a class counts as exploited just when it would be better off withdrawing from the economy with its per-capita share of society's alienable productive assets while the complementary class would thereby be made worse off, a definition explicitly built to work without either the labor theory of value or the transformation problem it generates. I would like you to reconstruct as precisely as you can what Roemer's move preserves of Marx's original diagnosis of capitalism, and what it has to abandon, redescribe, or simply forfeit in order to buy its independence from value theory.

Beyond that reconstruction, I am especially interested in your own considered view on a worry that has been nagging at me since the seminar. Because Roemer's definition is stated entirely in terms of who owns what, rather than in terms of anything that happens to workers inside the labor process itself, does it sever the connection Marx drew between being exploited and being alienated, that is, between having one's surplus labor appropriated and having one's activity, and thereby oneself, estranged under capitalist relations of production? Or can that connection be rebuilt on Roemer's more individualist, asset-distribution-based foundations? I would rather you defend a specific answer than simply lay out considerations on both sides, though you are welcome to say if you think the question itself rests on a confusion.
