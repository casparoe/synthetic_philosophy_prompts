I'm a PhD student in philosophy finishing a dissertation on the metaphysics of causation, and I co-host a small graduate-run podcast where each month we invite someone to defend one specific position from the literature rather than survey a topic in general. Next month's guest has made her name with a particular resolution of the old dilemma about causation by omission: either you deny that any omission is ever an actual cause, which sits badly with how readily we hold people responsible for things they failed to do, or you admit that omissions can be causes, which seems to turn nearly everyone who didn't prevent some disaster into one of its causes. Her fix is to argue that the dispute conflates two distinct causal relations: omissions trivially satisfy a relation of counterfactual dependence, but never satisfy a relation of physical production, since no process carries anything from an absence to the effect it fails to prevent. Whenever a case is raised that seems to tell against her, she likes to say the questioner has "smuggled two questions into one sentence," ask which relation they actually meant, and treat the exchange as settled.

I want to put together eight to ten questions that don't let her reach for that move every time, while still tracking the real pressure points in the debate -- and nothing generic that just invites her to restate the dependence/production split, since I can already predict that answer verbatim. Please include at least one question asking whether the dependence relation, even once separated from production, still needs some further restriction to avoid certifying vast numbers of causally irrelevant non-preventers as causes; one built around a case where several people are equally well placed to have prevented the same harm, so that plain counterfactual dependence cannot distinguish among them even though we clearly want to blame one more than the others; and one asking whether the law's insistence that omission liability requires a pre-existing duty or special relationship counts as evidence for her distinction or an awkward fact she owes us an explanation of -- this last one matters to me personally, since I TA an undergraduate philosophy of law seminar and have never been able to tell students whether the metaphysics is meant to justify that legal doctrine or merely sit alongside it uneasily. I'd also like at least two questions that make the "smuggled two questions" deflection progressively harder to reach for, ending in one that asks what she would count as evidence that the dispute was never merely verbal at all -- something that would show that one of her two relations doesn't really track anything, or that ordinary causal talk is not actually ambiguous between them in the way her view requires.

For each question, add a short follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give, and keep the phrasing accessible enough for an educated non-specialist audience even where the underlying point is technical.
