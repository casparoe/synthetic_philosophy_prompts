We had a whole back-and-forth on my team last week that's still bugging me. We built a new lightweight "SLO health" metric for one of our services, computed straight off the raw request logs with a pretty simple formula. Nobody trusted a brand new metric right away, so to sanity check it I pulled up our old, battle-tested dashboard (a way more complicated metric the SRE team has relied on for years) and compared the two side by side for about six weeks. They tracked each other almost perfectly the whole time, every time the new metric spiked the old one spiked too, and whenever one looked healthy so did the other. So I went to the team and said this basically proves the new metric is solid since it agrees with the old trusted one practically every time, and we started paging people off it.

My tech lead wasn't buying it though. He pointed out that if you trace both formulas back far enough, they both bottom out in the exact same raw request logs, just sliced and weighted differently, so of course they move together, and if theres some silent bug upstream (say, duplicate log entries during retries) throwing off the raw data, both metrics would be wrong in exactly the same way and still agree with each other perfectly. He says that means my whole "validation" never actually proved anything. I don't totally buy that though, these are two different formulas, written by two different people, computed completely differently in the code, so if they keep agreeing that feels like real evidence to me, not nothing. I tried explaining this whole saga to my partner over dinner and she just kind of stared at me, so now I'm bringing it to you instead: is there some general principle I'm missing about what actually makes a "check" on a measurement genuinely independent versus just looking independent on the surface, and practically speaking, how would you go about validating a new metric like this the right way?
