In my theology elective, we've been reading Augustine's writings on lying -- excerpts from On Lying, Against Lying, and a chapter of the Enchiridion -- and one case from the reading turned into a running argument at lunch between two classmates of mine, Elena and Marcus. The case is Augustine's own: the bishop Firmus of Thagasta hid a fugitive from imperial officers and, when pressed, never said anything false -- he simply refused to say where the man was, holding out even under torture until the case reached the emperor himself, who was impressed enough to pardon the fugitive.

(1) Elena said this is basically Augustine's answer to every hard case: you may never assert a falsehood, but you may always refuse to answer, so the rule never actually leaves anyone stuck.

(2) Marcus said that only works if the refusal is ambiguous. When a pursuer will accept only "yes" or "no," a flat refusal tells them exactly what "yes" would, so the rule doesn't protect anyone -- it just keeps the speaker's hands technically clean while producing the same result as a lie. That's a loophole, not an ethic.

(3) Elena answered that Augustine's own ranking of lies backs her up more than Marcus thinks: he ranks a lie told to save someone's life as the seventh of eight kinds, less sinful than every kind except a lie told to protect someone's chastity. That's Augustine all but admitting this is the hardest case for his rule, which is exactly why he reaches for Firmus's technical solution instead of just calling it an ordinary grave lie.

(4) Marcus said the ranking cuts the other way: if Augustine has to strain that hard, the rule is already in tension with itself exactly where it matters most. He also pointed to the Enchiridion, where Augustine says the essence of lying is having "one thought locked in his heart, another ready on his tongue" -- a matter of the deceiver's intent, not the literal words used. A refusal chosen precisely because you know it will mislead just as well as a lie seems to involve that very intent, so by Augustine's own standard it shouldn't get a pass just because no false sentence was spoken.
