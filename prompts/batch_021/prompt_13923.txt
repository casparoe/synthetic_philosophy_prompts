I'm a science journalist working on an essay about whether the "features" and "circuits" that interpretability researchers extract from neural networks are genuinely there inside the model, or whether they are artifacts of whatever analytic method happens to be fashionable. Below are my working notes rather than a finished draft. I'd like comments on three things: whether I've represented the technical results accurately, whether the philosophical framing in the final note is doing real work or is just decorative, and whether the underlying argument is actually interesting rather than a repackaging of an old dispute.

Notes:

1. Anthropic's "superposition" hypothesis holds that networks routinely encode more features than they have neurons, by packing them into overlapping, near-orthogonal directions that are only decodable because activations are sparse. If this is right, most individual neurons are "polysemantic" — they fire for a jumble of unrelated things — and the real unit of representation is a direction in activation space that cuts across many neurons at once.

2. Sparse autoencoders are the current tool of choice for recovering these directions. But researchers keep running into "feature splitting": enlarging the autoencoder's dictionary causes broad features to resolve into clusters of narrower ones, with no obvious principled place to stop. That makes me doubt there is a uniquely correct feature ontology to discover at all — the grain size looks chosen by the analyst, not read off the network the way you'd read off an atomic weight.

3. Pulling the other way is the "universality" evidence from the circuits research that began at OpenAI and has continued at Anthropic: broadly similar low-level features (curve detectors, for instance) turn up across independently trained networks and even different architectures. That kind of convergence is usually treated as evidence that a category has been discovered rather than invented, the same move biologists make when unrelated lineages evolve the same solution (camera eyes, echolocation) and the trait gets called a genuine biological kind rather than a taxonomic convenience.

4. Against stability, though: 2021 work on BERT described an "interpretability illusion," in which a neuron that looks, on one dataset, like a clean detector for one concept turns out to be tracking something unrelated once it's probed against a different corpus. That looks less like discovering a fixed representation than like different probes calling up different regularities in noisy activity.

5. My working frame for the piece: this reproduces, empirically, an old split in philosophy of mind between full-blown representational realism and Dennett's more deflationary "real patterns" view, on which a pattern counts as real if it's predictively useful even without a unique, mind-independent boundary. Except now, instead of arguing from intuitions about belief and desire, researchers can actually run the experiment.

Is the argument in 1–4 sound as I've described it, and does the Dennett comparison in 5 actually illuminate what's at stake, or is it a stretch dressed up in philosophical vocabulary?
