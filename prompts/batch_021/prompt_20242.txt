A colleague of mine, Elias Marsh, died two and a half weeks ago after a short fight with pancreatic cancer, and I've somehow ended up on the hook to give the eulogy at the department memorial this Friday. That's seperate from the actual funeral, which was small and family-run; this one is a department thing, so it'll be maybe thirty people, roughly a third of them logicians and grad students who TA'd for him, and the rest his wife, his kids, and university staff who liked him fine but couldn't tell you what a large cardinal is.

Elias was a set theorist by training, but for almost twenty years he co-taught our grad seminar on Gödel, and he was, frankly, a little obsessed with him — not the incompleteness theorems, everyone and their dog knows those, but Gödel's actual private views on personal survival after death, which almost nobody outside logic circles has ever heard of. He'd go on for an hour about how Gödel thought a genuinely rationally-constructed world more or less had to include an afterlife, and about that argument — I think it's in Hao Wang's book, or maybe it's from the letters to Gödel's mother, I genuinely mix up which — that the human brain is wildly overbuilt for whatever survival and reproduction actually required of it, as if it were meant for something longer than one lifetime's use. Elias basically adopted that as his own considered position, not as some bit he did at parties, and he was militant about two things: he did not want a eulogy full of "he's in a better place" or "watching over us," which he thought was just false comfort he'd never have accepted while he was alive to object, and he did not want anyone folding this in with Gödel's ontological proof, which he always insisted was a totally separate and far more formal piece of business. He apparently said as much to his wife more than once, half as a joke and half not, and she's the one who told me directly when she asked if I'd do this. (A few of us in the department tried drafting something together over email first, and it turned into a three-day fight about whether even mentioning the ontological proof was a mistake, so now it's just fallen to me.)

So here's what I actually need: the eulogy itself, something like seven or eight minutes read aloud. It has to genuinely comfort the two-thirds of that room who've never heard any of this and are just grieving a guy they loved, not merely nod at the logicians in a way that sails over everyone else's heads. But it can't drift into saying anything Elias would've flagged as sloppy or dishonest, and it really cannot blur his afterlife argument together with the ontological proof — I've seen that exact conflation made in print before, and any logician in that room will clock it in about two seconds. If it can land on something warm at the end rather than just stopping once the philosophy part is finished, even better.
