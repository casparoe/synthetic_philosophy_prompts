I've spent the last few weeks of my graduate seminar on formal epistemology working through Richard Jeffrey's probability kinematics -- his generalization of conditionalization to cases where evidence itself is not received with certainty, motivated by his well-known example of inspecting a piece of cloth by poor candlelight and coming away more confident that it is green without ever becoming certain of it. The students found the basic update rule manageable enough, and the rigidity condition -- that your credence in any hypothesis conditional on the proposition in question, and conditional on its negation, must stay fixed across the update -- seemed intuitive once we worked through a few examples by hand. What has tripped several of them up, though, is something that surfaced when we tried updating on two different pieces of uncertain evidence in succession.

With ordinary conditionalization on propositions learned outright, it is a triviality that order does not matter: conditionalizing on A and then on B leaves you with exactly the same posterior as conditionalizing on B and then on A, since both operations just amount to conditionalizing on their conjunction. It came as a genuine surprise to my students that this commutativity fails for Jeffrey conditionalization in general -- shifting your credences first on uncertain evidence bearing on one proposition, and then on uncertain evidence bearing on another, can leave you with a different final distribution than performing the two shifts in the reverse order, even when both propositions and both bits of evidence seem, described loosely, to be "the same" either way. I can reproduce the algebra that generates this result well enough, but what I would like from you is a genuinely intuitive, gut-level explanation of what is going on underneath it: why does uncertain evidence resist being processed order-free in a way that evidence received with certainty does not; what does this reveal about what probability kinematics actually does to a distribution, as opposed to what strict conditionalization does; and is there some natural condition on the two pieces of evidence -- some sense in which they must be suitably independent or unentangled -- under which the order stops mattering? I would also value your own assessment of whether this order-sensitivity counts as a strike against treating Jeffrey's rule as a genuine norm of rational belief revision, or whether it is instead an unavoidable feature of updating on uncertain evidence that strict conditionalization only seemed to avoid because it never had to face a genuinely uncertain input.
