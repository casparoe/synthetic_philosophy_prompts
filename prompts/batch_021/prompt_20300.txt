I serve on my hospital's clinical ethics committee. A nonverbal autistic adult with a longstanding diagnosis of severe intellectual disability has begun spelling messages on a letterboard with a trained facilitator steadying the board, and through this method has reportedly refused an upcoming surgery that her parents -- her surrogate decision-makers -- want her to have. The committee is split: several members find her spelled answers, including apparent replies to questions the facilitator could not have known, too specific and consistent to be the facilitator's own invention, and want to honor her refusal; our consulting psychologist argues that this very appearance of fluency and insight is what unconscious facilitator influence would produce, and that nothing less than a blinded test of authorship should override her parents' consent. The surgery cannot be delayed indefinitely. Draft the committee's decision memo: state whether, and under what conditions or timeline, her spelled refusal should stand in place of her parents' consent, and engage seriously with the strongest argument against whatever you recommend.
