I'm working on a feature for my outlet about "nudge" policies—soda taxes, opt-out 401k enrollment, that kind of thing—and I interviewed an economist for background who said something that's been rattling around in my head ever since. His argument was that the whole behavioral-econ nudge crowd is quietly contradicting one of the oldest results in their own field: revealed preference theory. As he put it, economists proved decades ago that whatever a person actually chooses, when other options were on the table, is by definition what maximizes that person's utility. So if someone picks the soda over the salad, or opts out of saving for retirement, that choice has already revealed it's what's best for them, and any policy nudging them toward something else isn't correcting a bias, its just paternalism wearing a lab coat.

It sounded pretty airtight on the phone, and honestly it kind of settled an argument I'd been having with my editor about how to frame the whole piece. But the more I sit with it, the more it feels almost too convenient, like a magic trick where I can't spot the sleight of hand. I did poli sci in college, not econ, so I don't have great instincts for where this kind of argument usually breaks down. Is he basically right that revealed preference theory settles the paternalism debate against nudge-style policies, or is there something off in how he gets from "this is the technical definition of utility that economists use" to "therefore this choice was actually good for the person"? Would love to actually understand the mechanics here before it goes in the piece, rather than just present it as a he-said-she-said between him and the behavioral economists.
