I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and I'm working on a piece about how loosely science writers borrow the phrase 'possible worlds' when what they really mean is the multiverse of physics; before I lean on that analogy myself, I want to get the underlying philosophical dispute straight. Write me an essay on the controversy between counterpart theory and transworld identity as rival accounts of what it is for a particular individual to have modal properties -- the sense, for instance, in which Hubert Humphrey could have won the 1968 election. Take seriously both Kripke's objection that Lewis's counterpart theory changes the subject, since strictly speaking no other-worldly individual literally is Humphrey, and Lewis's own argument, from the problem of accidental intrinsics, that the seemingly free alternative of simply letting him be literally present in several worlds turns out to carry hidden costs. Weigh, too, how much damage Chisholm's paradox -- about individuating a single individual across a chain of worlds that gradually swaps properties between two people -- does to each side. Reach a real verdict; a qualified one is fine ('counterpart theory for the semantics, transworld identity for what ordinary de re talk seems to want', say), but argue for it rather than leaving the two views in a stalemate.
