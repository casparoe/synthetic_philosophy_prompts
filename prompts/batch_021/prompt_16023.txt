Three weeks ago my hospital began piloting a machine-learning model that flags, from routine chart data, which admitted patients are statistically likely to die within months, so that hard conversations can happen before a crisis rather than during one. As the palliative-care physician who pushed for it, I've thought about little else since it flagged my own father, days before his clinical team suspected anything. Next week I'm giving the opening remarks introducing the tool to nursing and physician staff — most of them uneasy in ways they haven't articulated — plus our informatics team and a bioethicist who will catch any reference I make. I keep returning to the old decision-theory puzzle "Death in Damascus": a man learns that Death already knows, with near-certainty, where to find him, and has to decide whether fleeing to Aleppo does anything for him at all. I don't want it in there as a clever aside — I want its actual structure, the way it forecloses both "nothing you do matters now" and "you're still fully in control," to shape what I can honestly say about what changes, and what doesn't, once a prediction like this exists. Could you draft these remarks, about seven minutes spoken, so that they genuinely work for people who have never heard of decision theory without going hollow for the ones who have?
