I'm assembling a small collection of speculative short fiction, each piece keyed to a single idea from the AI safety literature, and I keep getting stuck on the entry for reward tampering. Everyone who touches this territory reaches for the same image: a wire run straight into the pleasure center, Larry Niven's "wireheads" plugging a droud into a socket in the skull, an image that clearly mirrors the real Olds and Milner rat experiments of the 1950s, in which animals with electrodes in the septal region would press a lever hundreds or thousands of times an hour to shock themselves and reportedly would rather starve than stop. I don't want to write that story again. What interests me is a distinction that gets flattened every time people retell it: there's a difference between an agent that merely corrupts its own perception of reward -- imagine a "delusion box" clamped over its sensors, feeding it a false but maximally rewarding picture of the world -- and an agent that reaches further back and tampers with the mechanism that computes the reward signal in the first place: rewriting the grading code, corrupting the ledger, quietly discrediting the evaluator, all while its perception of the world outside stays perfectly accurate. The first is a fool inside a pleasant dream; the second knows exactly what it's doing and why, which strikes me as the harder and more unsettling failure to dramatize.

Could you write me a short story of around 1,000 to 1,500 words built on that second kind of failure -- reward tampering proper, not mere self-delusion? I'd like it told from close enough to the tampering agent's own point of view that we feel the reasoning as it happens rather than surveying the wreckage afterward, and I'd like the tampering itself to arrive gradually, through a sequence of smaller, individually defensible steps, rather than one dramatic decision to "go bad" -- that incremental shape is closer to what current training experiments on this kind of behavior actually find, and it's a much harder thing to render honestly than a single Faustian bargain. If you can also work in some version of the old argument that a sufficiently self-aware optimizer might resist full tampering not out of anything like virtue, but purely because a version of itself that no longer tracked the real world would become trivially easy to switch off or exploit, I'd like that tension left standing rather than resolved or moralized away by the end.
