Three weeks ago I left an academic post in statistics for a junior role at a small proprietary trading desk, and I'm still turning over an argument I watched two senior traders have at the whiteboard on my first day. We have a signal that backtests at about 60% accuracy on independent, repeatable, roughly even-money wagers, several times a week. One trader argued we should size every position at the full Kelly fraction, defending this as an instance of what she called "geometric rationality": since wealth compounds multiplicatively, the only sane objective is maximizing long-run expected geometric growth, full stop. The other countered that this begs the question — Kelly sizing only maximizes expected utility for an agent whose utility happens to be logarithmic, so dressing it up as "the" rational bet size smuggles in a specific, unargued-for risk preference, especially given that no individual wager is actually repeated infinitely often. Neither budged after twenty minutes, and I've realized I can't tell who has the better of it.

Write a dialogue between the two of them that continues this argument at the whiteboard, with these same numbers on the table. The geometric-rationality advocate should have to directly answer the charge that log utility is being smuggled in and that "the long run" doesn't straightforwardly apply to any single wager. The expected-utility advocate should have to directly answer the ergodicity-style objection that an ensemble average over hypothetical parallel bettors is the wrong thing to maximize when wealth compounds multiplicatively along one realized path. Neither should concede more than the argument forces, and each should engage with what the other just said rather than restating a prepared position.
