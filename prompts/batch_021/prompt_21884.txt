Write a dialogue between two philosophers debating Aristotle's sea-battle puzzle: if it is already true today that there will be a sea battle tomorrow, the battle seems unpreventable and hence necessary, and symmetrically if it is already false; either way, the future looks closed. The first philosopher blocks the fatalist's argument by denying that statements about undetermined future events are true or false at all until the matter is settled. The second grants that such statements are true or false today, just like any other statement, but argues that the fatalist's real mistake is a modal fallacy -- moving from the trivial necessity of the disjunction "true or false" to the substantive necessity of one of its disjuncts -- so accepting bivalence costs nothing. Have the second press the first on whether denying bivalence forces her to give up the law of excluded middle as well, and have the first press the second on whether calling the sea-battle statement true today doesn't already require some presently existing fact that settles how tomorrow will go. Neither should simply concede the point.
