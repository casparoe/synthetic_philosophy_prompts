I cover physics and philosophy of science for a general-interest outlet, and I'm in the final stretch of a long feature on why the grandfather paradox is still argued over by serious physicists and philosophers rather than being a solved exercise from an introductory logic class. Over the past few weeks I've worked back through David Lewis's old paper on the paradoxes of time travel, the Novikov–Thorne wormhole papers from the late 1980s, and David Deutsch's writing on quantum mechanics near closed timelike curves, among other things, and what strikes me is how differently these actually handle the contradiction once you get past the shared reassurance that time travel is "logically fine, actually." Most explainer journalism — including pieces I'm not entirely proud of having filed on deadline — flattens all of this into a single wave at "parallel universes," which I've increasingly come to suspect is doing a great deal of unexamined philosophical work.

For the piece, I want to be more precise about which of the standard fixes genuinely dissolve the contradiction and which ones merely relocate it or paper over it. Here are the seven I keep running into, described plainly rather than technically so we're starting from the same place: (1) the claim that history is simply self-consistent as a matter of physical law, so the traveler was always fated either to fail or to succeed in some way that doesn't actually prevent their own birth; (2) Lewis's argument that "can" is ambiguous between having the raw physical ability to do something and that act's being compossible with the already-fixed facts of the past, so the attempt is never impossible, merely reliably unsuccessful; (3) branching timelines, on which killing the grandfather spins off a new universe and leaves the traveler's own timeline of origin untouched; (4) the quantum-mechanical cousin of that idea, on which the "branching" happens locally, at the level of the loop itself, rather than as a whole new universe splitting off wholesale; (5) the flat denial that "changing the past" was ever a coherent notion in the first place, since the traveler was always part of one single, fixed history from the outset; (6) the "something always intervenes" family of explanations, where the gun jams or the traveler's nerve fails at the last second — which some dismiss as an ad hoc cheat and others defend as no stranger than the ordinary way a fixed past constrains what can happen in the present; and (7) proposals that add a second dimension of time so that the original past can be genuinely overwritten, at the cost of a considerably stranger metaphysics.
