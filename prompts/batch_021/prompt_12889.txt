I sit on the investment and grantmaking committee of a philanthropic foundation that has just received an unusually large bequest, unrestricted except for a broad mandate to "do the most good, weighing all persons and generations impartially." The committee has split along lines that mirror a real disagreement among longtermist thinkers about how such capital should be spent down. One faction favors something close to a patient-philanthropy policy: invest nearly all of it, spend only a token amount on operations and research each year, and let the endowment compound for decades or even centuries so that a far larger sum can eventually be deployed once better opportunities, or a clearer emergency, arise. The other faction regards this as too complacent. They argue that the present period looks unusually high-leverage for catastrophic and existential risk (transformative AI, engineered pandemics, and the like), that money left to compound for generations is exposed to expropriation and to value drift among trustees who no longer share our founding commitments, and that treating "we are probably not living at the hinge of history" as the safe default is itself a substantive and contestable bet.

I need to produce the committee's decision memo ahead of our meeting in three weeks: a recommended payout-rate policy, stated as an actual number or range, together with a fixed schedule for revisiting it, and the reasoning behind the recommendation. The memo should present the patient-philanthropy position as its strongest advocates would state it rather than as mere hoarding, and it should also confront the deeper objection that threatens both factions equally: that we are simply too "clueless" about the long-run consequences of anything we do for centuries-long expected-value reasoning to be action-guiding at all. Could you draft this memo?
