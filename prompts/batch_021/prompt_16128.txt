My mother is retiring this spring after thirty years leading our county's volunteer mountain rescue team, and I've been asked to give the toast at her retirement dinner. The story everyone still tells is from her second year on the job: a storm was going to close the only safe route down within the hour, her team could reach only one of two stranded groups in time, two climbers on one ridge and seven on another, and with nothing else to go on she had the team draw lots rather than simply send help to the seven. It split the volunteer network for years; she has never said much about it beyond that the numbers weren't the kind of thing that should settle it on their own. Reading on my own since then, I found out there's a real philosophical debate behind what she did: Taurek's paper "Should the Numbers Count?" argues against simply saving the greater number, while Parfit's direct reply, "Innumerate Ethics," argues the numbers should settle it after all. Half the room that night will be rescue volunteers who still argue about that call; the other half is family who just want to raise a glass to her. Write the five-minute toast: it has to actually take a side on whether she got that call right, using the real argument rather than a vague line about every life counting equally, and it still has to work as a toast, not a lecture.
