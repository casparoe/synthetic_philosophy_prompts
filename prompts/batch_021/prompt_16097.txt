Clark Glymour's problem of old evidence poses a sharp challenge to Bayesian confirmation theory. On the standard view, evidence E confirms a hypothesis H just in case P(H|E) > P(H); but if E is already known with certainty, so that P(E) = 1, then the probability calculus guarantees P(H|E) = P(H), so conditionalizing on E cannot raise an agent's credence in H at all. Yet historically, the discovery that Einstein's general relativity entailed the already-known anomalous precession of Mercury's perihelion was treated as strong evidence in the theory's favor. Daniel Garber's well-known response relaxes the assumption of logical omniscience: since the agent does not initially know that H entails E, the discovery of that entailment is itself new information, on which the agent can conditionalize to raise their credence in H without any change in the probability assigned to E.

Write a dialogue between two formal epistemologists who agree on all of this but disagree about what it shows. One should argue that Garber's maneuver successfully rescues Bayesian confirmation theory from Glymour's problem. The other should argue, in the spirit of critics such as John Earman, that it does not, because it requires assigning a prior probability to the proposition that H entails E, and no such assignment can be fixed non-arbitrarily by anything internal to Bayesian theory. The first should try to show that this is no worse than the ordinary problem of choosing priors that every Bayesian already lives with; the second should try to explain precisely why it is worse, in a way that undermines Garber's solution specifically rather than Bayesian confirmation theory in general. Neither should simply restate their opening position—each turn should respond to what the other has just said.
