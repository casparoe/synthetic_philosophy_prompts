I spent part of last month working through Frege's Begriffsschrift and "On Sense and Reference" for a side project on formal semantics -- I'm a backend engineer, and this was mostly an excuse to finally understand where some of the type-theory ideas I use at work actually came from. Somewhere in the resulting Wikipedia rabbit hole, I learned that Frege, right at the end of his life, kept a private diary -- dated 1924, the year before he died -- filled with virulently antisemitic and anti-democratic political opinions: contempt for parliamentary government, hostility toward Jews holding political rights, and a generally authoritarian streak. Apparently this was a genuine shock to Frege scholars when it came to light, Michael Dummett included, since Dummett had spent decades presenting Frege as essentially the founder of modern logic and the very model of a dispassionate, purely technical thinker.

What's been nagging at me since is a more pointed version of the old "can bad people produce good philosophy" question. Frege's entire philosophical program rests on a fierce rejection of psychologism: logic and mathematics, on his view, are not about how any particular mind happens to think, but about objective truths holding independently of us, inhabiting what he explicitly called a "third realm" -- his own term, from the essay "Der Gedanke," for the domain to which thoughts belong, distinct from both the physical world and anyone's private mental states. That is already a strikingly hierarchical, purity-obsessed picture of who, or what kind of mind, is even capable of reaching truth. I can't tell whether that's simply a coincidence -- one man holding two unrelated sets of views -- or whether there's a real, defensible line running from his logical objectivism and anti-psychologism to the exclusionary politics of the diary.

I'd like an essay that actually settles this for me rather than dutifully laying out "both sides" and stopping there. Make the strongest case that the diary is simply irrelevant to evaluating Frege's logic and philosophy of language -- genetic-fallacy-style arguments, the point that a valid proof doesn't care whether the person who found it was admirable or repugnant -- and then the strongest case that it isn't irrelevant: that there is some genuine, non-coincidental connection between his metaphysics and epistemology and his politics, or at least that the diary should change how charitably we read particular moves he makes elsewhere. Then commit to a verdict and defend it. Answers like "the diary is biographically fascinating but leaves the logic untouched" or "it genuinely should change how we read him" are both acceptable shapes of answer, but I want an actual position, not a shrug -- and I'd like you to close with a few sentences on what this specific case teaches us more generally about how much a thinker's character should be allowed to bear on the credibility of their ideas.
