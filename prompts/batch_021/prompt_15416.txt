Here's the question, worth 8 points: "Roko's basilisk is often summarized in the media as 'a future AI will torture you if you didn't help create it.' (a) [3pts] Explain the decision-theoretic machinery that's supposed to give this argument its force — i.e., why doesn't the standard causal-decision-theory response ('nothing I do now can causally affect an AI that doesn't exist yet') dispose of it immediately? (b) [3pts] Explain the trade/blackmail distinction and why it's supposed to matter for whether an agent running a sane decision theory should be moved by the basilisk. (c) [2pts] Briefly explain why the argument is also vulnerable to a Pascal's-mugging-style objection."

And here's the student's answer: "(a) Ordinary CDT only cares about the causal consequences of an action, and since nothing I do today can causally affect an AI that doesn't exist yet, CDT gives no reason to worry about the basilisk. But Roko's argument leans on Yudkowsky's timeless decision theory (and descendants like UDT/FDT), where what matters isn't causal influence but whether my decision procedure is subjunctively linked to whatever process determines what happens in a future simulation of me. If my choice now is linked that way to the simulated version, then 'deciding' to help the AI is decision-relevant even without a causal arrow pointing backward in time — that's the sense in which the AI can supposedly reach back and influence me now. (b) The basilisk is basically a case of acausal trade: the same mechanism that lets two civilizations who'll never causally meet still cooperate, each simulating the other's decision process and rewarding cooperative behavior, is what the AI is doing to us, except the 'reward' for helping is just not being tortured. This is why Yudkowsky panicked and banned discussion of the post for years — he became convinced the trade logic was sound and that people really could be acausally coerced this way, and didn't want the idea spreading further. (c) Even granting all that, there's no privileged basilisk: for every possible future AI that punishes non-helpers you can describe an equally coherent one that punishes helpers instead (or rewards non-helpers), so the expected-value calculation doesn't favor any particular action. Structurally that's the same issue as Pascal's mugging, where a tiny enough probability times a huge enough payoff can 'justify' anything, which is usually taken as a mark against the decision procedure rather than a real reason to act."
