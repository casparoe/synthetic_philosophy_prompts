A conversation with friends this past weekend left me stuck on a question I haven't been able to shake since. I've noticed that when I receive opening messages on dating apps, I almost automatically dismiss the generic ones ("hey," "what's up") or anything that looks copy-pasted to dozens of people, while I'll happily engage with someone whose message is far less polished but obviously took real time and attention — referencing something specific from my profile, asking a considered question, and so on. One friend, who works brutal night shifts as a medical resident and has almost no spare time, argued that this filter of mine is not just unfair but actually irrational: it has nothing to do with whether we'd get along, and it systematically rewards people who happen to have abundant free time and a way with words while penalizing people who might be just as interested in me but too busy or awkward to prove it at length. I don't think he's wrong that the correlation is far from perfect, but I can't quite convince myself that the effort itself is uninformative — it feels like it must be tracking something real about how much someone values talking to me specifically, as opposed to whichever of fifty people happens to respond first, even granting that it's a noisy and occasionally unfair proxy.

I've spent years lurking on LessWrong and similar forums, and usually that kind of background is enough to help me spot who's making the reasoning error in situations like this, but here I keep going back and forth. Is my friend right that I'm indulging a superficial heuristic with no real justification beyond "effort feels nice," or is there a solid underlying reason to treat someone's willingness to spend real time and effort as genuine evidence of interest?
