So I'm about two-thirds through a novel with a secondary character, Judith, who's a retired moral philosophy professor, dyed-in-the-wool Kantian, and also the person in the story everyone calls when theres a stray dog nobody else will take in. I had two friends over this weekend to read one of her scenes out loud and tell me honestly if she sounded like a real person, and instead we ended up in a forty-minute argument about whether "strict Kantian who's obsessed with animal welfare" is even a coherent character to write, which I still can't stop turning over.

It went like this. Owen said it isn't coherent, because Kant is on record saying we have no direct duties to animals at all -- cruelty to animals is only wrong, on his view, because it hardens us and makes us worse to other people, so Judith volunteering at the shelter "for the dogs' sake" is exactly the attitude Kant would tell her to drop. Fatima said that's not the same as Kant thinking animal suffering doesn't matter -- his actual point is narrower, about what it takes for a duty to be owed "to" someone (you have to be able to bind them back, which only rational beings can do), so he files cruelty under duties "regarding" animals rather than duties "to" them, and even says an old service animal is owed something like continued care, on the model of gratitude. Owen said that bookkeeping IS the problem: if the wrongness of kicking a dog fully cashes out as "it damages your own character," then in a freak case where it somehow left your character completely untouched, Kant's theory has nothing left to object with -- meaning the dog was never doing any real work, whatever he says elsewhere about gratitude. Fatima said plenty of ethical theories bite that kind of bullet under a rigged-up hypothetical without it sinking them, and that this exact worry is why Korsgaard later argued you can keep basically all of Kant's own machinery and still get animals counted directly: what makes rational beings matter, on Kant's own reasoning, is that they have ends of their own that they take to be good, and animals clear that bar too -- Kant just didn't draw the conclusion himself. Owen then brought up the bit from the lectures about the master who has his old dog shot once it can't work anymore, wronging only himself and not the dog, and said that's basically game over for Fatima's reading. Fatima said that particular line comes down to us through student transcripts of the lectures, not something Kant wrote for publication, and the version he did publish is more careful, so its a pretty shaky peg to hang the whole verdict on.

Nobody at the table would concede a single point, and I'd honestly like to get this right before I decide how self-aware to make Judith about the tension in her own worldview. Could you go through it move by move -- Owen's opening claim, Fatima's "to" vs "regarding" reply, Owen's bookkeeping comeback, Fatima's Korsgaard move, and the old-dog exchange at the end -- and say who had the better of it at each step, not just who wins overall? And if they're both flattening or oversimplifying what Kant actually thought anywhere along the way, I'd rather know that too.
