I'm a senior and chair my high school's student government Elections Board, which bars candidates from 'knowingly making false statements of fact.' Three days before the presidential runoff, a rival filed a complaint against the frontrunner over two things she said at a candidates' forum: that the outgoing treasurer 'quietly ran a $3,000 deficit in the student council's account that the administration is covering up' (false, and she admits she never checked the number a campaign friend gave her), and that she alone is the race's 'transparency candidate,' vowing to root out corruption in a student government she calls broken, though she names no wrongdoing beyond the deficit claim. The board is split: some members would sanction only the deficit claim, as the one specific, checkable statement, and write off the 'transparency candidate' branding as ordinary campaign rhetoric, however misleading; others think that branding is actually the more serious problem, precisely because it can't be reduced to one falsifiable sentence. I've been reading Hannah Arendt's essays 'Truth and Politics' and 'Lying in Politics' for my AP Research paper and think her distinctions bear directly on this case. Could you draft the short memo I'll bring to Thursday's meeting, before Friday's runoff is certified? It should give a specific disposition for each part of the complaint, propose a standard the board could reuse in later cases, and directly answer the strongest objection to whichever line you draw.
