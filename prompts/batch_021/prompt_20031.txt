I'm working on a magazine piece about why so many contemporary disputes — over vaccines, election results, climate data — seem to resist resolution by evidence, and I've been re-reading Wittgenstein's On Certainty for background. What keeps pulling me back in is his idea of "hinge propositions": things like the thought that the earth has existed for a long time, or that the hands one is looking at right now are not part of a dream — claims we don't seem to believe on the basis of evidence at all, but which have to stand fast, unquestioned, before any inquiry or doubt can get going in the first place.

Digging into the secondary literature, I've run into a disagreement that I can't quite resolve on my own and that seems to matter well beyond Wittgenstein scholarship. Crispin Wright wants to say that accepting the hinges is nonetheless rational, in a special non-evidential sense he calls "entitlement": we are warranted in trusting them because doing so is an unavoidable precondition for pursuing any cognitive project at all, not because we have positive grounds for thinking them true. Duncan Pritchard, in what he calls his "quasi-fideist" reading, laid out at length in his book Epistemic Angst, denies that this middle ground exists: for him, hinge commitments are simply arational — neither justified nor unjustified, outside the space of epistemic reasons altogether, closer to something we do than something we are entitled to believe. The two camps agree that the hinges are not evidentially supported; they disagree about whether there is any sense left in which holding them is rational, and that difference seems to carry real weight for whether we can say, in a principled epistemic way, that trusting one's senses is a "better" hinge to have than, say, a conspiracy theorist's trust in a preferred source.

Could you write me a short dialogue — perhaps ten to twelve exchanges — between two philosophers holding these two positions, imagined as continuing the argument in the hallway after a seminar on Wittgenstein's response to G.E. Moore's "here is a hand" proof? I would like a real argument rather than two monologues laid side by side: the entitlement theorist should have to answer the charge that "entitlement" is just a flattering name for groundlessness, and the quasi-fideist should have to answer the charge that treating hinges as beyond rational assessment leaves us unable to say what, epistemically, distinguishes a reasonable hinge from a reckless one. Neither one should get to have the last, unanswered word.
