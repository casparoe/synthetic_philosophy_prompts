I've been chewing on a chunk of Plato's Meno for a subplot in the novel I'm drafting right now — there's a retired classics teacher character who keeps circling back to this passage almost obsessively, and I want the argument to actually hold up rather than just gesturing vaguely at "ancient wisdom" the way lazier books do. I mentioned my confusion about it to a friend who did a philosophy MA a few years back and all she offered was "oh, that's the value problem," before her kid started screaming in the background and that was the end of that conversation. So here I am. I'm using the old Jowett translation below since its public domain and was already sitting around in a text file on my laptop — sorry if the Victorian phrasing feels a bit stiff. For context, Socrates and Meno have just been arguing about whether good action requires actual knowledge, or whether correct opinion can do the job just as well:

SOCRATES: I will explain. If a man knew the way to Larisa, or anywhere else, and went to the place and led others thither, would he not be a right and good guide?

MENO: Certainly.

SOCRATES: And a person who had a right opinion about the way, but had never been and did not know, might be a good guide also, might he not?

MENO: Certainly.

SOCRATES: And while he has true opinion about that which the other knows, he will be just as good a guide if he thinks the truth, as he who knows the truth?

MENO: Exactly.

SOCRATES: Then true opinion is as good a guide to correct action as knowledge; and that was the point which we omitted in our speculation about the nature of virtue, when we said that knowledge only is the guide of right action; whereas there is also right opinion.

MENO: True.

SOCRATES: Then right opinion is not less useful than knowledge?

MENO: The difference, Socrates, is only that he who has knowledge will always be right; but he who has right opinion will sometimes be right, and sometimes not.

SOCRATES: What do you mean? Can he be wrong who has right opinion, so long as he has right opinion?

MENO: I admit the cogency of your argument, and therefore, Socrates, I wonder that knowledge should be preferred to right opinion — or why they should ever differ.

SOCRATES: And shall I explain this wonder to you?

MENO: Do tell me.

SOCRATES: You would not wonder if you had ever observed the images of Daedalus; but perhaps you have not got them in your country?

MENO: What have they to do with the question?

SOCRATES: Because they require to be fastened in order to keep them, and if they are not fastened they will play truant and run away.

MENO: Well, what of that?

SOCRATES: I mean to say that they are not very valuable possessions if they are at liberty, for they will walk off like runaway slaves; but when fastened, they are of great value, for they are really beautiful works of art. Now this is an illustration of the nature of true opinions: while they abide with us they are beautiful and fruitful, but they run away out of the human soul, and do not remain long, and therefore they are not of much value until they are fastened by the tie of the cause; and this fastening of them, friend Meno, is recollection, as you and I have agreed to call it. But when they are bound, in the first place, they have the nature of knowledge; and, in the second place, they are abiding. And this is why knowledge is more honourable and excellent than true opinion, because fastened by a chain.

MENO: What you are saying, Socrates, seems to be very like the truth.

SOCRATES: I too speak rather in ignorance; I only conjecture. And yet that knowledge differs from true opinion is no matter of conjecture with me. There are not many things which I profess to know, but this is most certainly one of them.

Okay, so here's where I get stuck. Socrates and Meno agree, more than once and pretty emphatically, that someone with a merely correct opinion is "just as good a guide" as someone with actual knowledge — not almost as good, but literally not a "whit" worse, for the purpose of getting the action done right. Naturally Meno then asks why knowledge should be preferred at all if that's true, and Socrates answers with the runaway-statues bit: true opinions "run away" out of the soul while knowledge is tied down by "the tie of the cause" and so sticks around. Fine — except barely a breath later he's right back to saying true opinion "perfects action quite as well as knowledge," which sounds like exactly the claim his little statue parable was supposed to be overturning. So which is it? If stability genuinely makes no difference to how good a guide someone is in any single instance of acting (which he keeps conceding, both before and after the Daedalus bit), then what work is the tethering story actually doing? Is Socrates quietly switching from "value in this one act" to something like "value across a whole life of acting," and if so, is that switch actually licensed by anything he's said, or is he just dodging Meno's question while appearing to answer it? I don't want the "this is basically Gettier 2400 years early" one-liner that every intro guide gives this passage — I want to know whether the argument, read strictly line by line, actually holds together, or whether there's a real gap here that later philosophers had to go patch up.
