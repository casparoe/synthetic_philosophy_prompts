I am drafting a scene for a novel set at a family funeral in Kumasi, and I want the philosophical disagreement between two of my characters to be rigorous rather than merely decorative. Kofi, a retired philosophy lecturer, holds to Ifeanyi Menkiti's well-known thesis that personhood is not conferred at birth but achieved by degrees, through ethical conduct and progressive incorporation into community life; on this view, his own brother — human being though he remains — may, after thirty years of refusing every communal obligation, never have become a full person, and so is owed correspondingly diminished funeral rites. Abena, Kofi's niece and the dead man's daughter, was trained in Kwame Gyekye's moderate communitarian response to Menkiti: she grants that social life shapes character and moral personality, but insists that some non-negotiable core of the person — the natural capacities of reason and will — must be prior to and independent of communal recognition, since otherwise no individual could ever have had the standing to dissent from his community's judgment, as her father plainly did.

Write the dialogue between them as they stand over the body deciding what he is owed. Each should press the other on this particular case rather than retreat to slogans, and by the end neither should have had an easy time of it.
