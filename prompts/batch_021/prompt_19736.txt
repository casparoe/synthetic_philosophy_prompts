So I'm a backend dev with zero philosophy training, but I fell down a rabbit hole a while back reading rationalist / AI-safety blog stuff — started with Roko's basilisk, obviously, then kept going — and now I lurk in a Discord where people argue about this stuff constantly. Two of the regulars, Chris and Nadia, got into a long back-and-forth last week about whether "surrogate goals" actually work as a defense against acausal threats, and I've been chewing on it since because I honestly can't tell who came out ahead.

Quick context in case it's useful: the worry is that a sufficiently good predictor (a future AI, or just a hypothetical superintelligence used in these thought experiments) can threaten you "acausally" — it threatens to make something bad happen to you, or to a simulation of you, unless you comply now, and the threat works purely because you can predict it would follow through, not because it can causally reach you. The concern is that any decision procedure smart enough to make good acausal trades is, by that same logic, exploitable by acausal blackmail. "Surrogate goals" are a proposed fix: you publicly commit that whenever you're threatened, you'll react to a threat against some cheap decoy goal exactly as strongly as a threat against your real one, so a threatener who's only in it for leverage (not for hurting your actual values specifically) might as well threaten the decoy, since it gets them the same compliance either way, and your real goal stays safe. At least that's my layman's understanding of it.

Here's roughly how the argument went (reconstructing from memory, so its close but maybe not word for word):

1. Chris: adopting a surrogate goal protects what you actually care about, because a threatener who's indifferent between targets will rationally go for the cheap, fake one you've designated.
2. Nadia: that only works if the threatener doesn't realize it's fake. But these thought experiments always assume a threatener that can fully model you, so it'll see through the decoy and just threaten the real goal directly, explicitly ruling out "paying in decoy-currency" as valid compliance.
3. Chris: it doesn't matter that the threatener knows it's a decoy — what makes a threat worth making is whether it changes your behavior, and you've precommitted to behave identically either way, so writing "decoy payments don't count" into the threat costs the threatener nothing, but it doesn't get them any extra compliance either, since your response was already maxed out in both cases.
4. Nadia: except that assumes the threatener is economizing effort like a reasonable negotiator, when the whole reason acausal blackmail is scary in the first place is adversaries (or worst-case simulated ones, for safety analysis) that want to hurt your actual goal, full stop, not just extract generic compliance. And now that surrogate goals are a documented strategy anyone can read about, doesn't publishing the defense kind of hand threateners the workaround for free?
