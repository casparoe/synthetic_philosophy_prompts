For a research paper on the economics of the British Empire for my history class, I've been reading through parts of Adam Smith's *Wealth of Nations* that turned out to be much more critical of colonialism than I expected. I got stuck on this passage, from the chapter on colonies in Book IV:

"To found a great empire for the sole purpose of raising up a people of customers may at first sight appear a project fit only for a nation of shopkeepers. It is, however, a project altogether unfit for a nation of shopkeepers; but extremely fit for a nation whose government is influenced by shopkeepers. Such statesmen, and such statesmen only, are capable of fancying that they will find some advantage in employing the blood and treasure of their fellow-citizens to found and maintain such an empire. Say to a shopkeeper, "Buy me a good estate, and I shall always buy my clothes at your shop, even though I should pay somewhat dearer than what I can have them for at other shops"; and you will not find him very forward to embrace your proposal. But should any other person buy you such an estate, the shopkeeper would be much obliged to your benefactor if he would enjoin you to buy all your clothes at his shop."

Two things are tripping me up. First, Smith says the project is unfit for "a nation of shopkeepers" but fit for "a nation whose government is influenced by shopkeepers," as though those were obviously different things, but I don't see why they come apart — wouldn't a nation made up of shopkeepers just have a government full of shopkeepers too? Second, I can't work out the little story about the estate: who is supposed to be the "you" being addressed, who is the "benefactor," and why does the shopkeeper come out ahead in the second scenario but not the first? Could you walk through the analogy step by step and show exactly how it's meant to establish the distinction he's drawing?
