I'm nearing the end of a novel set in the closing months of a civil war in an unnamed country, and I've been stuck on a scene I want to get exactly right. My protagonist, Dalia, is a former field medic from the losing side, now awaiting a war crimes tribunal in a detention block alongside soldiers from both armies. One evening she talks for hours with Corso, a military ethicist retained to advise the tribunal on questions of combatant liability, who has built his career defending something close to the traditional view in just war theory associated most famously with Michael Walzer: that soldiers on opposing sides of a war are moral equals as combatants, each entitled to the same right to kill and to be killed, quite apart from which side's cause was actually just. Dalia, who has spent months turning over her own culpability, is no longer sure this is right, and finds herself arguing the more revisionist line associated with writers like Jeff McMahan — that this "equality" is at best a convenient legal fiction papering over a real moral asymmetry between those who fight for a just cause and those who don't.

Could you draft this scene as a dialogue between the two of them? I'd like Corso to make the strongest version of the traditionalist case — not merely an appeal to the law of armed conflict, but the deeper argument that combatant status itself, rather than the justice of one's cause, is what generates the right to kill, together with the claim that ordinary soldiers' poor epistemic position with respect to the justice of the war gives us reason to resist grounding their moral status in facts they are rarely equipped to judge. Dalia should press the revisionist case that liability to lethal force tracks individual moral responsibility for contributing to an unjustified threat, and she should have a real answer ready for the objection Corso is bound to raise: if most unjust combatants are excused because they couldn't reasonably have known better, what licenses anyone to call them liable to be killed at all? I would rather have genuine engagement than a lecture delivered in two voices, so each of them should treat the other as making their strongest case, press on the actual soft spots in the other's position, and concede small points where honesty requires it. It's fine, even preferable, if the conversation ends with Dalia no closer to a settled view than when she sat down, so long as a reader can see precisely why the question is difficult.
