I'm drafting a novel whose middle section turns on a single long dialogue: through a conceit I won't bother justifying here, David Hume finds himself auditing a contemporary graduate seminar in metaphysics, and the students decide to test him by asking whether his own picture of causation and law can survive two developments he never had to reckon with. Beta readers have told me my draft makes him too easy to deflate — a straw man in a wig — and I'd rather get the philosophy right before I write another line of dialogue, since the whole point of the scene is that he holds his own.

The first development is David Lewis's "Humean supervenience": the thesis that the world is at bottom just an arrangement of local qualities across spacetime, with laws recovered afterward as whatever regularities the best deductive system would deliver, and Lewis's later concession that this picture needs fundamental properties to be bare "quiddities" whose identity floats free of the causal role we use to pick them out. The second is an objection pressed by one faction in the room, committed dispositional essentialists, who argue that entangled quantum states resist being decomposed into local intrinsic properties plus spatiotemporal relations, and that this is better explained by properties having their causal profiles essentially than by anything in Hume's or Lewis's "loose and separate" mosaic of distinct existences.

Before I put words in Hume's mouth, I want a close argument, not a survey of the secondary literature, on three things. Would he recognize Lewis's apparatus of possible worlds, best systems, and quiddities as a legitimate descendant of his own denial of necessary connections, or as exactly the kind of reasoning he'd want to commit to the flames as sophistry and illusion? Does the entanglement case actually threaten anything Hume himself was committed to — his account of causal inference as custom rather than demonstration, his insistence that ideas answer to impressions — or only something Lewis bolted on afterward for reasons Hume needn't share? And if the dispositional essentialists corner him into admitting that something has to give, which commitment would he abandon first: his denial of necessary connections between distinct existences, or his own professed fallibilism about what experience might yet turn up? I'd like the strongest, most textually grounded answer you can construct, even where it complicates the version of Hume most people think they already know.
