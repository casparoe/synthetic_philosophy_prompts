And here is my classmate's answer:

"Aquinas's most sustained treatment of tyranny is in De Regimine Principum (On Kingship), where he coins the terms tyrannus quoad titulum and tyrannus quoad exercitium to distinguish two ways a ruler can be a tyrant: one who seizes power through violence or fraud and never had a rightful claim to rule, and one who came to power legitimately but governs only for his own advantage instead of the common good. For both kinds, Aquinas is remarkably cautious about letting private citizens take matters into their own hands: he worries that people who assassinate a supposed tyrant are usually driven by ambition rather than genuine justice, and that allowing this at all puts even good rulers at risk, since any disgruntled subject could decide his king counts as a tyrant. The proper remedy, in his view, has to come through whatever public authority originally had the power to install the ruler — the community itself, or some higher authority — and if no such recourse exists, subjects should turn to prayer and trust that God can convert or remove the tyrant himself. Aquinas repeats this same public/private logic in the Summa Theologiae's discussion of sedition, arguing that a tyrannical government is already a kind of disorder against the common good, so an uprising that restores the common good is not really sedition at all, unless the revolt ends up causing more harm than the tyranny did. Later thinkers pushed this reasoning in a more permissive direction than Aquinas himself was willing to go: John of Salisbury and, more famously, the Jesuit Juan de Mariana both argued that a manifest tyrant could be killed by any private person without waiting for public authority to act — a position controversial enough that Mariana's book was ordered burned after being associated with the assassination of Henry IV of France."
