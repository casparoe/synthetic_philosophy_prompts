Here is the student's answer in full:

Galen Strawson's Basic Argument tries to show that no one can be ultimately, or "truly," morally responsible for what they do, whether or not determinism is true. The argument can be reconstructed roughly as follows: (1) What you do, in any given situation, follows from the way you are — from your character, values, beliefs, and desires (what Strawson calls your "mental nature"). (2) To be truly, ultimately responsible for what you do, you would have to be truly responsible for being the way you are, at least in the mental respects that explain the action. (3) But you cannot be truly responsible for being the way you are. To be responsible for your mental nature, you would have had to choose to be that way on the basis of some principles of choice or values you already had; but then you would have to be responsible for having those principles too, which would require having chosen them on the basis of still earlier principles, and so on without end. Since a completed infinite regress of self-authorship is not something anyone can actually undergo, no one can be the ultimate cause of themselves (causa sui) in the way true responsibility requires. (4) Therefore, no one is ever ultimately morally responsible for anything they do.

What makes this a "basic" argument, rather than just another worry about determinism, is that step (3) is meant to hold independently of whether determinism is true. If determinism is true, your mental nature at any given moment is simply the product of a causal chain that long predates you and that you never had a hand in starting. But Strawson insists indeterminism does not help either: if some part of your character, or some particular choice, is genuinely undetermined, that undetermined element is not something you chose or controlled any more than a determined one was, so mere randomness cannot supply the missing self-authorship. Because the argument is built to defeat both the compatibilist's picture and the libertarian's, it belongs to the same family as van Inwagen's Consequence Argument, which likewise concludes that determinism rules out the sort of "could have done otherwise" that moral responsibility requires; this is also why Frankfurt-style cases, designed to show that responsibility does not actually require the ability to do otherwise, are often raised as a direct challenge to Strawson's conclusion.

A well-known response to the Basic Argument comes from Strawson's own father, P. F. Strawson, in "Freedom and Resentment." P. F. Strawson argued that our practices of holding one another responsible rest on reactive attitudes — resentment, gratitude, indignation, and so on — that are simply part of ordinary interpersonal life, and that these practices do not really depend on settling abstract metaphysical questions about determinism or ultimate self-origination, since we could not give up the reactive attitudes even if some argument convinced us that we should. Galen Strawson takes up this idea and essentially makes it his own way of living with the Basic Argument's conclusion: because we cannot help having the reactive attitudes no matter what the regress in (3) shows, our ordinary practice of holding people responsible is vindicated at the level of everyday life even though, at the metaphysical level, the Basic Argument remains sound. On balance, I think this is the right place to land, since giving up the reactive attitudes because of a regress argument seems neither practically possible nor obviously required, given that the argument only ever targets a very strong, desert-entailing notion of responsibility that our everyday practices may not actually need.
