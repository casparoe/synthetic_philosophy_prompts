I spent my career as an experimental physicist and have, since retiring, become an enthusiastic latecomer to philosophy -- enough so that I now run a small monthly discussion group, which has recently begun recording its sessions as a podcast. Next month's guest has published a book defending a version of the Lucas–Penrose argument: the claim that Gödel's first incompleteness theorem shows human mathematical understanding cannot be the output of any fixed, consistent formal system, and hence that minds are not Turing machines. Several members of the group know the Penrose literature well enough that, rather than have her simply restate the argument on air, I would like eight interview questions that press on specific soft spots rather than invite a summary: at least one on why we are entitled to assume our own mathematical faculties are consistent rather than merely usually reliable, one on whether her "knowably sound" version of the argument, as developed in Shadows of the Mind, escapes the charge that no one could ever be in a position to know that a given system is both sound and captures the whole of their mathematical competence, one on whether the argument is even coherent when applied to a single finite mind rather than an idealized, ever-growing mathematical community, and one on the standard rebuttal that a machine could match human progress by simply adjoining each new Gödel sentence as an axiom. For each of these four, please add a short follow-up question keyed to the answer she is most likely to give.
