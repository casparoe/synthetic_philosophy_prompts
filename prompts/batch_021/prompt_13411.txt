Last night a friend and I had a long argument about surrogate goals as a defense against acausal threats, and we never actually resolved it. My understanding of the proposal is that an agent who expects to be acausally threatened -- extorted by some other agent that can predict or simulate it well enough to make good on a threat without any causal contact -- can commit in advance to respond to threats against some cheap, otherwise unimportant 'surrogate' goal exactly as it would respond to threats against its true goal, on the theory that a threatener who cares only about extracting compliance should be willing to threaten the surrogate instead, since either target delivers identical leverage, while the agent's real values stay shielded if a threat is ever actually carried out. My friend's objection was that this gets the incentives backwards: a threatener who knows the surrogate was chosen precisely because the agent doesn't mind it being harmed has no reason to prefer it to the real goal, and every reason to doubt that harming it will extract the same compliance, so the very indifference the scheme relies on undermines it rather than helping it. I answered that credibility and common knowledge of the commitment should be enough to fix the threatener on the surrogate, but I couldn't spell out what actually pins them there rather than leaving them free to threaten whichever target they like, including the real one. Could you explain, in intuitive terms rather than through the formal decision theory, what is supposed to make the surrogate the goal that actually gets threatened, and whether my friend has identified a genuine gap in the idea or just a place where it still needs more structure?
