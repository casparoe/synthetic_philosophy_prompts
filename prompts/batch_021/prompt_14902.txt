My cousin and I inherited a small rental property together, and to avoid constant disputes we agreed on an unusual arrangement: each year, whichever of us currently holds the option may force a buyout, taking 60 percent of the property's value and leaving the other with 40 percent. If the option-holder instead lets the year pass, the option shifts to the other person for the following year, and we keep co-owning the property, which suits us both, since it has been steadily gaining value. The arrangement runs for five years, alternating who holds the option each year, after which it automatically lapses into an even split if neither of us has ever used it. My cousin recently argued that whoever holds the option in that fifth year will obviously use it rather than accept an even split; anticipating this, whoever holds the option in year four should use it instead, to avoid being left with the smaller share; and reasoning backward the same way, year by year, he concluded that I, holding the option first, ought to use it right now, in year one, even though the property has barely appreciated and we would both clearly be better off if we just kept waiting. I have no background in economics or philosophy, but something about that conclusion feels wrong to me, even though I cannot find a flaw in any individual step of the reasoning. Is my cousin right that I should use my option right now, or is there a flaw in his reasoning that I'm missing?
