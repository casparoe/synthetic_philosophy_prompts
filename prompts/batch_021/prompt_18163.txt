My daughter Maya's bat mitzvah is in three weeks and her portion is Vayikra, which is basically chapter after chapter of who brings which animal for which offering and how the priests are suppose to handle it. I got asked to give a few minutes of remarks before the rabbi's sermon, and while prepping I fell down a rabbit hole into Maimonides' explanation for sacrifices in the Guide for the Perplexed -- the idea that God didn't actually want animal sacrifice for its own sake, but everyone in the ancient world worshipped that way, and yanking it away all at once would've been psychologically impossible for people used to it, so instead He redirected the practice toward himself (one alter, one priestly line, a pile of restrictions) as a kind of weaning-off process on the way to something better, like prayer. I really like this because it turns the most boring-seeming parsha in the whole Torah into a claim about meeting people where they actually are and moving them forward gradually, which honestly feels like a nice thing to say about a 13 year old too. The thing is, I also vaguely remember -- maybe from a footnote, maybe from my rabbi in passing, possibly it was Ramban but don't quote me -- that this Rambam position was genuinely controversial among other big commentators, something like: it doesn't explain why Abel or Noah were already sacrificing animals generations before any idol worship existed for God to be weaning people off of, and it can come across as implying God settled for a consolation prize instead of what He actually wanted. My in-laws are a lot more traditionally observant than my side of the family and I really don't want to get up there and accidentally sound like I'm calling a chunk of the Torah a divine backup plan.

Could you actually write the remarks for me -- spoken out loud, so maybe a page, 4-5 minutes -- for a room that's maybe a third people who'd recognize "Rambam" and know instantly what I'm getting at, and two-thirds people who've never heard any of this and just want a nice moment about Maya? I want the Maimonides idea to actually be load-bearing in the speech and not just a fun fact I mention and move on from, and I don't want it to dodge the tension with the more traditional/mystical take by staying vague -- I'd rather it deal with that honestly and still land warm.
