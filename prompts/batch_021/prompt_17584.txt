So I got into this whole argument with my roommate about soldiers and whether they're "just doing their job" or actually doing something wrong if the war they're fighting in turns out to be unjustified. Like, obviously the generals and politicians deserve most of the blame if a war is unjust, but what about the random 19 year old conscript who's just following orders and isn't shooting at civilians or breaking any of the actual rules of combat? My roommate said something like "the rules of war treat soldiers on both sides as equally within their rights to shoot at each other, no matter who started it or why," and that this isn't just a legal technicality but an actual position philosophers defend. I went down a bit of a rabbit hole after that and apparently there's a whole debate about this - some people (I think the guys name is Walzer?) defend this "moral equality of combatants" idea, while other, more recent philosophers push back hard on it, arguing that if your side's cause is unjust then you personally don't have the moral right to kill enemy soldiers even while "just following the rules," kind of comparing it to the difference between attacking someone in self defense versus being the one who threw the first punch.

This has been bugging me because both sides seem to have a real point. It seems insane to expect every random soldier to correctly figure out whether their war is just before they're "allowed" to defend themselves out there, but it also seems insane to say that, like, a soldier invading a neighboring country for no good reason is in exactly the same moral position as a guy defending his own home and family. Could you write me an essay that actually gets into the weeds of this "moral equality of combatants" debate, lays out the strongest versions of the arguments on both sides (not just the surface level stuff), and then actually commits to an answer instead of just shrugging and going "well it's complicated" at the end? It's fine if the answer ends up being conditional on something (like "combatants are morally equal only when X, otherwise not") as long as you actually spell out what that something is and where you land, instead of just leaving it totally open ended.
