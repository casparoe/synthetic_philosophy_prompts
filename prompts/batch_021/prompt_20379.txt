I trained as a journalist before I ever wrote a novel, which is apparently why my university has asked me to give the joint commencement address for this year's graduating fiction-MFA and journalism cohorts — one ceremony, two professions that mostly define themselves against each other. The room will have parents who have never heard of Hannah Arendt sitting next to faculty who assign her every year, so whatever I build the speech around has to work at both altitudes. I keep returning to her argument, in "Truth and Politics" and "Lying in Politics," that the political liar and the storyteller draw on the very same imaginative capacity — the ability to hold a world other than the one that is actually the case — and that what separates them is not that capacity but what each does afterward to the shared factual world their audience still has to live in. Please write the address, about ten minutes long: it has to put that distinction to real use, saying something specific and true about each craft rather than just name-checking Arendt, and it can't leave the novelists thinking their trade is simply a nobler kind of lying, or the journalists thinking imagination is a luxury their trade can do without.
