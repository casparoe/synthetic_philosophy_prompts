Okay so a few weeks back my coworker Dave and I got into a whole thing over whether our fantasy football commissioner has been quietly fixing the schedule in his own favor (long story, he definately has). At some point Dave, who's way more into internet-rationalist stuff than me, pulls out this thing called Aumann's agreement theorem and says that since we both already know what the other one thinks, and neither of us thinks the other is stupid or lying, us continuing to disagree is basically impossible if we're both actually being rational - like one of us HAS to update. I went down a rabbit hole reading about it afterward (no formal background here, just wikipedia and a couple blog posts), and it turns out it's an actual proof, not just a saying - apparently if two people start from the same priors and their current opinions on something are "common knowledge" (meaning I know yours, you know mine, I know that you know that I know, and so on forever), those opinions are mathematically required to be identical. There's even a follow-up bit where you don't even have to explain your reasoning to each other - you can just keep trading your updated number back and forth and you'll eventually land on the same one.

This obviously did not settle the fantasy football thing, it just made Dave insufferable, since he took it as "you have to agree with me now." I felt like there was something off about using a theorem about idealized agents to settle two guys arguing about a made up sports league, but I couldn't totally put my finger on why. So instead of us hashing it out badly, I'd love to see two people who actually know this stuff inside out have the argument properly.

Could you write me a dialogue between two characters: one who genuinely believes that finding yourself in a lasting, informed disagreement with someone you consider rational and honest is basically a red flag that somebody has made a mistake, no different in kind from holding two contradictory beliefs at once - and another who thinks the whole thing is a neat result for a math seminar that completely falls apart the moment you try to apply it to real people, and who has actual specific reasons for saying so, not just "well, humans aren't perfect." I want real back and forth - so if one of them brings up the "you should update now if you can already predict you'll update later" type argument, the other needs an actual comeback and not just a shrug, and neither one should clearly be winning by the end.
