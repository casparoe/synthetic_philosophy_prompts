"As Lenman (2016) argues, consequentialism requires knowing the overall effects of an action in order to know whether it was right, but since effects ripple out indefinitely this is impossible even in principle, so agents are 'clueless.' He splits this into simple and complex cluelessness. Simple cluelessness is when the unforeseeable effects are symmetric - e.g. holding a door open for a stranger shifts the timing of everyone's day slightly, which eventually changes who has kids with whom, but there's no reason to think this nudges the future to be better or worse on net, so it washes out in expectation and can be set aside. Complex cluelessness is the more serious case: here we do have some evidence bearing on the unforeseeable effects, just not enough to be confident, and no principled way to weigh it against the effects we can see. E.g. donating to an anti-malaria charity plausibly also increases regional population growth, and it's unclear whether that's good or bad, so the sign of the total effect is murky. On whether expected value theory saves us here: I'd say yes - decision theory just tells us to assign our best subjective credence to each candidate long-run effect and multiply through, and being uncertain doesn't stop expected value from being perfectly computable, that's the entire point of using probabilities instead of certainties. So a careful enough consequentialist can always in principle grind out a number and act on it, which dissolves the objection."
