I am finalising the reading questions for a graduate seminar on Marx and the Marxist tradition that I co-teach with a colleague from the economics department, and I would like to put a line of argument to you that the two of us have more or less converged on. As we read it, Marx's charge of exploitation in Capital, Volume I ultimately comes down to the claim that workers are paid less than the value their labour contributes to what they produce. If that is the crux of the matter, exploitation looks like exactly the sort of gap that a properly competitive labour market should compete away: with free entry of firms, full information, and no barriers to workers moving between employers, wages should be bid up until they equal the marginal value each worker adds, at which point the surplus Marx is concerned with simply vanishes. On this reading, exploitation is not a structural feature of the wage-labour relation as such but a symptom of monopsony power and other departures from perfect competition, so that the appropriate remedy, even by Marx's own lights, would be more competition in labour markets rather than the abolition of wage labour altogether.

Taking this as broadly correct, I would like your help thinking through its consequences. Should I present the remaining chapters of Volume I, on the working day, machinery, and the general law of capitalist accumulation, to my students as essentially an elaborate market-failure story dressed up in unfamiliar terminology? And is it fair to tell them that contemporary Marxists who still speak of exploitation under highly competitive, developed capitalist economies are simply relying on nineteenth-century assumptions about market structure that Marx's own argument never actually required?
