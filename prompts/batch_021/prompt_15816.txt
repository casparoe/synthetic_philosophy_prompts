Lewis's counterfactual theory analyzes causation in terms of counterfactual dependence between distinct events, with the relevant counterfactuals evaluated via a similarity ordering over possible worlds. Woodward's interventionist theory, by contrast, evaluates the relevant counterfactuals - claims about what would happen to the effect under an idealized intervention on the putative cause - directly from the structural equations of a causal model, without appeal to any Lewisian similarity ranking. This difference is often taken to show that interventionism is a genuinely distinct kind of theory of causation, not merely a variant of the counterfactual approach. I would like you to argue against this way of drawing the distinction: make the case that Woodward's theory is best classified as a restricted species of the counterfactual theory of causation rather than as a theory of a fundamentally different kind, and address directly why dispensing with an explicit similarity metric does not, by itself, eliminate a covert appeal to just the sort of "closest world in which only the cause differs" notion that such metrics were introduced to capture.
