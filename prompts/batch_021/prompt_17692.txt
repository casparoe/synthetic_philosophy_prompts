Ok, this is a genuine rabbit hole, bear with me. I was reading about Alan Turing's Cambridge years and ran across the story (real, I think? someone correct me if I'm misremembering) that he actually sat in on Wittgenstein's 1939 lectures on the foundations of maths and got into a running argument with him in front of the class — Wittgenstein was weirdly chill about the idea that a hidden contradiction buried in a formal system is basically no big deal, and Turing kept pushing back that a bridge built on inconsistent maths could actually fall down. Turing died in '54, three years after Wittgenstein, and — this is the bit that actually got me — a full 28 years before Kripke's Wittgenstein on Rules and Private Language turned a handful of gnomic remarks in the Investigations into the actual rule-following paradox: the "quus" thing, where nothing about your past behavior fixes whether by "+" you've always meant plus or some deviant function that agrees with plus on everything you've actually computed and diverges after 57 (or whatever the number was).

Heres the part I can't stop chewing on: Kripke spends a good chunk of that book specifically demolishing the "dispositional" answer to the paradox, i.e. the idea that you mean plus rather than quus because you're disposed to answer "125" — on the grounds that dispositions are finite, can misfire, and can't ground a normative "should." Which is about as close as an epistemology book gets to taking a direct shot at the whole finite-state-machine picture of a mind that Turing basically invented.

So here's my actual ask: reconstruct what Turing's own reaction to Kripke's paradox would probably have been, using what we actually know about how he thought — the imitation game, his comfort treating minds as mechanisms, and his own demonstrated appetite (see: the bridge argument) for taking foundational disputes seriously as having real stakes rather than dismissing them as merely grammatical. Does the dispositionalist in him have a good comeback, or does the paradox land a real hit on the mechanist picture of mind he helped invent? Please don't give me the lazy "he was a behaviorist so he'd obviously be fine with meaning-skepticism" answer — actually work through the tension. If you want to close with a sentence or two on what this means for whether a modern LLM can be said to "follow" a rule at all (this comes up on LW/ACX constantly), that's a nice bonus, not the main ask.
