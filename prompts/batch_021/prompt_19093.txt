So I fell down a bit of a Molinism rabbit hole this week, partly from an argument with a friend (a phil of religion PhD student who insists middle knowledge "obviously" solves the foreknowledge/free-will problem, and I wasn't convinced). I've got the basic three-fold structure down -- natural knowledge, middle knowledge of counterfactuals of creaturely freedom (CCFs), free knowledge -- but what actually hooked me was the grounding objection: Adams' and Hasker's complaint that theres nothing available to make a counterfactual like "if Peter were placed in circumstances C, he'd freely deny Christ" true, since by definition nothing about C necessitates the choice either way. Then I went further down the hole and found theres a whole second-generation version of this fight: a paper by Perszyk arguing Molinists can't consistently endorse van Inwagen's consequence argument against compatibilism while also holding that CCFs are true "logically prior" to creation, since the same transfer-of-powerlessness reasoning that the consequence argument runs on the past and the laws of nature can apparently be run on CCFs instead. Wierenga tried to find a principled disanalogy between the two cases, and apparently got push-back that it doesn't actually hold up.

Kind of amused that this is basically the same headache as the "logical counterfactuals" problem that gets argued about in decision theory -- what grounds claims about what a fixed algorithm would've output on inputs it never got fed -- just wearing a theological costume instead of a mathematical one. Anyway, can you write me an essay that actually engages with the object-level dispute instead of just re-explaining what middle knowledge is? Walk through the standard Molinist replies to the grounding objection (biting the bullet and treating CCFs as brute/ungrounded facts, versus the tu-quoque move that ordinary truths about the actual future are just as "ungrounded," so the objection proves too much), tell me which if either you find convincing, and then weigh in on whether the consequence-argument-style fixity problem is actually the bigger threat, since it seems to attack the libertarian-freedom part of Molinism head-on rather than just the semantics of counterfactuals. I want an actual verdict at the end, not a survey -- does Molinism hold up as a genuinely libertarian account of providence, or does it collapse under these objections into theological determinism (or into something no more explanatorily useful than simple foreknowledge)? Don't hedge.
