So I'm a backend engineer at a mid-size company that makes a short-video app (think a smaller, sadder TikTok), and I somehow ended up on our internal "Responsible Recommendations" working group — I think because I complained about this exact thing in a Slack thread once and got voluntold onto it. Here's what we have to decide before next Wednesday's meeting: the recsys team wants to keep optimizing purely on watch-time and session length, on the grounds that this is literally "revealed preference" — people are voting with their thumbs, and that's the most honest signal of what they actualy want, full stop, no need to ask them anything. But UX research just ran a survey where a big chunk of our heaviest users say they feel worse after long sessions and wish they used the app less, which obviously doesn't line up with them being our most "engaged" users by the metric we optimize for.

I got kind of nerd-sniped by this because at my last job we did something like inverse reinforcement learning on clickstream data to infer a reward function, and going down a rabbit hole I found out economists have basically been fighting about the exact same move — inferring what people want purely from what they choose, no peeking at what's "really" going on inside their head — for about a century, under the name revealed preference. Apparently there's a real live debate over whether that even makes "utility" mean anything beyond "whatever rationalizes your past choices," or whether you need some other, independent handle on what's actually good for someone (like, I don't know, just asking them). Our own ML principles doc has this one throwaway line about optimizing for "genuine long-term user value, not just engagement metrics," but nobody ever defined what that cashes out to, and now it's actually load-bearing, because we have to vote on whether to fold the survey satisfaction score into the recommender's objective function (even if it tanks watch-time), swap it in entirely, or just leave it as a dashboard nobody's bonus depends on. Could you write the memo I'm going to circulate before the meeting? It needs to actually recommend one option, with a rough rollout plan and timeframe, and it has to seriously engage with the strongest version of the pure-revealed-preference position instead of just dunking on it — the argument that costly real choices tell you more than cheap survey talk, and that "satisfaction" self-reports are exactly the noisy, mood-of-the-moment, gameable signal that revealed preference theory was invented to route around in the first place. I don't want a philosophy-101 tour of what utility means, I want something our group could actually vote on.
