I'm nearing the climax of a near-future novel about a small, overstretched agency charged with certifying that frontier AI systems are safe to deploy, and I want the book's central technical conceit to read as a sober extrapolation of real research rather than as invented magic. The scene I'm building toward has my protagonist's team resolve a standoff with an uncooperative model by bypassing its answers altogether: they pull up its internal activations and read off what it actually "believes" about a disputed fact, rather than trusting whatever it chooses to say aloud. My understanding of where the science actually stands, pieced together from a friend in machine learning and some follow-up reading, is that the hard part of this is already behind us. I recall a widely discussed result in which researchers trained a simple classifier on a language model's hidden states in a completely unsupervised way, requiring only that a statement and its logical negation be assigned confident, consistent, and opposite truth probabilities, and found that the resulting direction in activation space tracked the truth better than the model's own stated answers did, even when the model had been prompted to lie. If that's right, then eliciting latent knowledge, which I gather is the standard term for this whole project in AI safety circles, is essentially solved in principle, and getting it to work on messy, high-stakes, real-world claims is now just a matter of scaling the technique up.

Given that, I've been leaning toward writing the last act so that the accuracy of this kind of readout is simply assumed, and putting the actual dramatic tension elsewhere, on whether the auditors are ethically entitled to interrogate a model's "mind" this way without anything resembling its consent. Before I commit to that structure, I'd like a sanity check from someone who actually knows the field: is it fair to treat the epistemic half of this problem as basically settled, so that the only interesting question left is the ethical one, or would I be skating past a technical difficulty that researchers still consider very much open?
