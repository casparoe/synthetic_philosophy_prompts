So my friend and I had this two-hour back-and-forth last night about Hume's essay on miracles (the one in the Enquiry, Section X) that neither of us won, and I can't stop thinking about it, so I want a fresh opinion. We'd both just re-read Part I for a phil of religion seminar and got stuck arguing about that footnote where Hume brings up the Indian prince who won't believe in frost. Here's the actual text, copied out so we're at least arguing about the same words:

Main text: "The Indian prince, who refused to believe the first relations concerning the effects of frost, reasoned justly; and it naturally required very strong testimony to engage his assent to facts, that arose from a state of nature, with which he was unacquainted, and which bore so little analogy to those events, of which he had had constant and uniform experience. Though they were not contrary to his experience, they were not conformable to it."

The footnote attached to that: "No Indian, it is evident, could have experience that water did not freeze in cold climates. This is placing nature in a situation quite unknown to him; and it is impossible for him to tell a priori what will result from it. It is making a new experiment, the consequence of which is always uncertain. One may sometimes conjecture from analogy what will follow; but still this is but conjecture. And it must be confessed, that, in the present case of freezing, the event follows contrary to the rules of analogy, and is such as a rational Indian would not look for. The operations of cold upon water are not gradual, according to the degrees of cold; but whenever it comes to the freezing point, the water passes in a moment, from the utmost liquidity to perfect hardness. Such an event, therefore, may be denominated extraordinary, and requires a pretty strong testimony, to render it credible to people in a warm climate: but still it is not miraculous, nor contrary to uniform experience of the course of nature in cases where all the circumstances are the same. The inhabitants of Sumatra have always seen water fluid in their own climate, and the freezing of their rivers ought to be deemed a prodigy: but they never saw water in Muscovy during the winter; and therefore they cannot reasonably be positive what would there be the consequence."

My friends take was that this footnote is Hume doing careful, principled work: the prince is being perfectly rational to doubt, and his doubt is totally different in kind from doubting a miracle report, because ice doesn't actually violate any law of nature, he's just never been in the relevant conditions before. My take was that Hume's quietly cheating here - the only reason WE get to call freezing "extraordinary but not miraculous" instead of the other way round is that we already know how it turns out, whereas from inside the prince's head, reasoning only from his own past experience the way Hume's whole method demands, ice and a resurrection look like exactly the same kind of report. And if there's no way to draw that line except by peeking at the answer in advance, then the big maxim a page later about no testimony ever being sufficient to establish a miracle isn't really the in-principle result it's supposed to be, it's just Hume being confident that nothing has met the bar so far, which is a much weaker claim. We went in circles on this for way too long and nobody budged.

Can you actually settle it, or at least clarify what's going on? Is there a non-circular way for Hume to draw the extraordinary/miraculous line using only the resources he's given himself here, and does the answer change how strong a claim the "proof against proof" stuff right after this passage is actually entitled to make?
