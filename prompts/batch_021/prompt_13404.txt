So I fell down a rabbit hole this week re-reading about the absent-minded driver problem (the Piccione and Rubinstein highway one) after seeing it namedropped in an old forum thread about updateless decision theory, and I want to see the actual disagreement fought out properly instead of getting the wikipedia-flavored summary again. Setup, in case I'm misremembering a number: a driver is on a highway with two consecutive, completely indistinguishable exits. Exit at the first one and the payoff is 0; sail past it and exit at the second one and the payoff is 4; miss both and end up further down the highway and the payoff is 1. Because the driver is "absent-minded" -- can't tell the two intersections apart, no memory of having already passed one -- whatever strategy he adopts has to be identical at both, and the standard answer is to continue with probability 2/3 at each one, since that's the number that maximizes the payoff computed in advance, before the drive even starts.

Here's the bit that's been bugging me. That 2/3 is the answer to a planning question, decided beforehand. But suppose you're the guy actually sitting at an intersection right now, with zero memory of whether this is your first time here or your second. It feels like there's a further question: what probability should you assign, right now, to "this is intersection one" versus "this is intersection two," and does continuing with probability 2/3 actually make sense once you plug that credence into ordinary expected-utility reasoning, or is 2/3 just a number some outside planner handed you that you have no way of personally endorsing. I've seen it argued both that this in-the-moment credence is perfectly well defined (derive it from something like expected visit frequencies) and has to match up with the planning answer or the theory's in trouble, and that chasing an in-the-moment credence here is basically a category error and the only thing decision theory owes you is the optimal policy, full stop -- which has always struck me as the more UDT-flavored take, but maybe thats just because that's the crowd I read.

Could you write me a dialogue between two decision theorists, equally sharp, who hold these two views and actually argue -- responding to each other's real points instead of just restating their own side louder? I'd like them to stay concrete, using the driver's actual numbers rather than floating in the abstract the whole time, and ideally to work out together whether there's some variant of the problem (different payoffs, a third intersection, whatever) where the two approaches would recommend genuinely different actions rather than just sounding different. Extra credit if one of them drags in the halfer/thirder fight over Sleeping Beauty, since honestly that's the comparison that got me thinking about this in the first place.
