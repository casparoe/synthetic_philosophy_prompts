Since retiring from a career in experimental physics, I have been working my way through the ethics literature, and I keep returning to Bernard Williams' and Thomas Nagel's paired 1976 papers on moral luck. I have drafted notes toward a short essay that tries to test their argument against an example from my own field, and I would value your comments before I develop it any further.

The standard illustration of what Nagel calls "resultant luck" is a pair of equally reckless drivers, one of whom kills a pedestrian and one of whom does not, with no further difference in what was under either driver's control. I want to substitute a case that I think is cleaner, because the community involved has an explicit, self-consciously articulated norm about what is supposed to be assessed.

In fields that rely on "blind" analysis — much of particle physics, parts of cosmology — the norm is to fix every analysis choice (cuts, corrections, statistical treatment) before looking at the result, precisely so that a researcher's hopes cannot contaminate those choices. Suppose Researcher A and Researcher B, working on comparable projects under comparable deadline pressure, each quietly glance at a provisional result before finalizing their analysis choices, and each makes a small, self-serving adjustment. Their conduct is identical in every respect that was under either one's control at the moment of decision. Now suppose the effect A was chasing turns out, over the following years, to be real and robust, while the effect B was chasing turns out to be a statistical artifact. A is remembered as an intuitive, if slightly informal, pioneer; B's episode becomes a byword for how deadline pressure erodes rigor, and it quietly ends B's career.

My claim is that this case is a sharper test of Williams' argument than the driving case, because the community here has explicitly built a "control principle" into its own methodology: one is supposed to be assessed on procedure, not on whether the underlying phenomenon cooperated. Yet assessment tracks outcome anyway. That looks to me closer to Williams' skepticism about whether a genuinely luck-free, control-based standard of assessment is achievable at all — as opposed to Nagel's point, which I take to be the narrower claim that our existing moral judgments happen, paradoxically, to be luck-infected.

I would be grateful for your comments on three things in particular. First, have I got the Williams/Nagel relationship right, or am I flattening a distinction I should keep sharper — is it fair to say Williams is making a claim about the incoherence of a certain picture of morality as such, where Nagel is more agnostic and simply wants to display the paradox? Second, is my physics case really an instance of resultant moral luck, or have I actually described a case of epistemic luck — luck regarding what other people can find out about the quality of someone's work — which is a distinct phenomenon that only superficially resembles moral luck? Third, does my closing inference actually go through, or have I only shown that a scientific community is inconsistent in applying its own stated norms, which is a considerably weaker and more mundane conclusion than the one I am reaching for?
