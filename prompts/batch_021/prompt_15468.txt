I teach one term a year in a low-residency MFA program. This fall, with our usual chair on leave, I've been asked to head the student appeals committee, and the case in front of us is more contentious than anything the program has handled before. A second-year fiction student has formally appealed a failing workshop grade. Her professor's written comments described the manuscript as 'overwrought' and 'purple' and said it refused to 'trust the reader,' but the student maintains these are not neutral craft judgments: her prose draws on Igbo oral tradition -- proverb, digression, direct address, ornament rather than restraint -- and she believes a workshop trained on Carver-and-Hemingway minimalism is simply mistaking a different tradition's conventions for personal excess. She has also identified two other students working in non-minimalist traditions who received unusually harsh marks from the same professor over the past three years, and argues this is a pattern rather than a coincidence.

The professor, whose own story collections are well regarded, considers the accusation itself corrosive, and two of my four fellow committee members agree with him: if any unfavorable aesthetic judgment can be recast as bias, he argues, reasoned literary evaluation collapses into a popularity contest, and the expertise a student enrolls in an MFA to acquire becomes impossible to exercise honestly. I don't think either side is simply right, and that is exactly the problem. I write fairly spare prose myself, which either makes me a reasonable judge of this dispute or precisely the wrong person for it, and I have no test that tells me which. Years of workshopping have persuaded me that trained literary judgment -- the sense that an image is doing too much work, that a scene hasn't earned its ending -- is real and not reducible to prejudice; but I also know that 'this just doesn't work for me' is precisely the phenomenology an unexamined bias would produce, since bias of that kind does not announce itself from the inside. A memo that simply ratifies the professor's authority, or one that treats any style mismatch as self-evident discrimination, would dodge the actual difficulty rather than resolve it -- and whichever way we rule will set an informal precedent for a program that is very likely to see this argument again.

Could you draft the committee's decision memo, due before we reconvene next Tuesday? I need a specific recommendation on the grade itself -- upheld, revised, or referred to a second reader -- and on whatever changes, if any, we should make to how the program handles aesthetic judgment going forward, with reasoning precise enough that either the student or the professor could see exactly why we ruled as we did, rather than a diplomatic gesture toward 'seeing both sides.' Please give the professor's worry its full due: that treating suspicion of bias as sufficient grounds to overturn a grade would make rigorous literary judgment impossible to defend at all -- not only in this case.
