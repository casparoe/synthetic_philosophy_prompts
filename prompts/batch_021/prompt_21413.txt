A close friend of mine, with whom I overlapped in graduate school more than forty years ago — she in economics, I in evolutionary biology — died ten days ago, and her family has asked me to give the eulogy at her memorial service next Saturday. She spent her career on the epistemic side of game theory: on what exactly "common knowledge of rationality" can and cannot deliver in a game like the centipede game, where backward induction seems to force players who are rational, and know each other to be rational, and know that they know it, and so on, to unravel all the way back to the first move and defect immediately, even though a few rounds of mutual cooperation would have left both of them far better off. I recognized a distant cousin of this puzzle from my own field — thirty years of applying evolutionary game theory to hawk-dove contests, without ever needing to ask what the players believed about each other's beliefs. It is only since retiring, and really only since starting to read philosophy in earnest for the first time in my seventies, that I have sat down properly with Aumann's argument that common knowledge of rationality forces the backward-induction outcome, and with the objection, associated with Binmore, Bicchieri, and Pettit and Sugden among others: that reaching almost any node of that game tree already shows the assumption has failed, which leaves it unclear what licenses assuming it still holds for whatever remains of the tree. That crack in the argument was close to the center of her life's work.

The difficulty is this: in every dealing I had with her across four decades, she was someone who kept cooperating long after any backward-induction reasoner would have defected, and she would have hated a eulogy that turned her into either a living refutation of her own field ("she proved rational people defect, but she personally was too good for that") or, worse, endorsed the lazy popular gloss of game theory she spent her working life complicating — the idea that rigor just means defecting on the last round. I would like the actual technical crack in the argument, the question of what players are entitled to believe about each other once a move has occurred that common knowledge of rationality said could never happen, to be doing real work in the eulogy — the thing that lets me say something true and specific about how she lived, rather than a credential I mention once and set aside. And it needs to be exact enough that the colleagues and former students in the room won't wince, without ever losing the neighbors and cousins who have no idea what a centipede game is.

Could you write this eulogy for me? I would like it to run to roughly eight minutes read aloud, warm rather than academic in register, and built so that the technical point and the personal one resolve together in the closing lines rather than sitting side by side.
