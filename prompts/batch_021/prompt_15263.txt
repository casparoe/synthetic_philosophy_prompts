And here is the student's answer, also verbatim:

"Yudkowsky's dust-specks scenario asks us to weigh one person being tortured for fifty years against an almost unimaginable number of people, 3^^^3 — so large that it has to be defined with Knuth's up-arrow notation rather than ordinary exponents — each receiving a single, barely-felt dust speck in the eye. The case for the thought experiment's conclusion is purely aggregative: a dust speck is a genuine harm, not nothing at all, and standard consequentialism sums harms across people without discounting them simply because they are spread thin. Since summing a fixed positive quantity over an ever-larger population produces an ever-larger total, there must be some population size at which the combined disutility of all those specks exceeds fifty years of concentrated suffering, and 3^^^3 is chosen precisely to sit safely past that crossover point. Taking the arithmetic seriously, the thought experiment concludes that a consistent aggregative consequentialist should prefer the dust specks to the torture, because dispersing the harm keeps total suffering lower than concentrating all of it in one victim. Yudkowsky frames the puzzle as a challenge to what he calls our 'scope insensitivity' — our inability to feel the moral difference between very large numbers — though I would say it is really just a restatement of the Repugnant Conclusion that Parfit made famous in Reasons and Persons, where total utilitarianism ranks a huge population with lives barely worth living above a small, flourishing one; both arguments trade on the idea that enough small quantities, multiplied by a sufficiently large number, must eventually outweigh one large quantity. The strongest objection denies that aggregative premise outright. Following the line of thought that, after Rawls, is usually called the separateness of persons, one could argue that a harm too small for any individual to register as significant does not become morally weightier simply because many separate individuals experience it, since there is no single subject who bears the 'sum' of those harms the way the torture victim bears the whole of his fifty years. On that view, disutility can be weighed and traded off within a single life, but cannot simply be added up across different lives as though humanity itself were the one sufferer of 3^^^3 dust specks."
