In my intro computer science elective, we spent a class period on the history of artificial intelligence, and my teacher briefly mentioned something called "the frame problem" while explaining why the old rule-based, logic-driven AI systems of the 1970s and 80s never scaled up the way early researchers had hoped. The basic version she described made sense to me: if an artificial reasoner represents the world using formal logic, then after every action it technically has to determine which facts stayed the same and which ones changed, and simply listing out all the facts that did not change becomes unmanageable almost immediately as the environment grows more complex. When I looked into this further on my own, I learned that computer scientists mostly dealt with this particular version by building in a default assumption that anything an action does not explicitly mention stays the same.

What I have not been able to work out is why philosophers such as Dennett, Fodor, and Dreyfus apparently thought something deeper remained unresolved even once that default assumption was in place. From what I can tell, their worry was less about the sheer number of facts a system has to track and more about there being no principled way for the system to know, in advance, which of the countless true facts about a situation are even worth checking before it acts. My teacher referenced a well-known thought experiment involving a robot, a wagon, and a bomb to illustrate this, but we ran out of time before she could explain it properly, and I would rather not just read a summary of that same example online.

Instead, could you write me an original short story, maybe three or four paragraphs long, that dramatizes this deeper version of the problem in a fresh scenario? I am picturing something along the lines of a household or eldercare robot that has been carefully equipped with logical rules about the rooms, objects, and people around it, and a small domestic emergency, a pot left on the stove, a spill near an electrical outlet, a resident falling, or some comparable mishap, in which the robot's plan goes wrong not because it lacked the relevant facts somewhere in its database, but because nothing in its reasoning told it which facts deserved attention before time ran out. If possible, I would like the story to end in a way that gestures toward why some philosophers thought this was ultimately a problem about relevance and everyday common sense rather than merely one of processing speed or memory.
