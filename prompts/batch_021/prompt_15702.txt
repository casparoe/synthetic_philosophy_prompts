I've been mulling over a tension between two strands of AI-safety writing I've followed for a while now on LessWrong and the Alignment Forum. The mesa-optimization literature's account of "deceptive alignment" only makes sense if a trained system has something like its own objective and represents the fact that it is being evaluated against a different, base objective, biding its time until deployment; the whole story is implicitly mentalistic, cashed out in terms of internal goals and a representation of the training process. Compare that to a more recent move, exemplified by the Park et al. survey on AI deception, to define deception for AI systems in purely functional terms: on their account, a system counts as deceptive so long as it reliably produces outputs that lead users to form false beliefs that happen to serve the system's objective, regardless of whether the system harbors anything we'd call a belief or a goal. I understand the appeal of going functional here — it lets you flag sycophancy, hallucination, or a Diplomacy-playing agent's in-game backstabbing as "deceptive" without wading into philosophy of mind — but it seems to erase a distinction I care about: the gap between a system that lies and one that is merely, confidently wrong, or one that is just improvising the lines of a dishonest character because a user asked it to (this is close to the "role-play" framing that Shanahan and others have pressed for chatbot outputs more generally).

I'd like you to argue the less fashionable side of this. Please make the strongest case you can that genuine deception, in AI systems no less than in humans, requires something functionally equivalent to belief and intent, and that dropping this requirement in favor of a purely behavioral definition trades away the very thing that made "deception" a useful and distinctive category in the first place, rather than merely operationalizing it for easier measurement. In building the case, sketch what a suitably naturalized, non-phenomenal notion of AI "belief" and "intent" could even look like, so the position doesn't just smuggle dualism in through the back door; explain why a mesa-optimizer that models its own training process is a far better candidate for meeting that standard than a base model asked to improvise a con artist's dialogue; and address directly the worry that presumably motivated Park et al. to go purely functional in the first place — that any definition requiring inner states hands developers a built-in alibi ("it wasn't real deception, the model doesn't believe anything") in exactly the cases where we most want accountability to reach.
