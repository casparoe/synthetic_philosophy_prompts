ok so i lurk in this discord for people into math/logic puzzles for fun (nobody there is an actual mathematician, me included) and two regulars had an argument about the axiom of choice that's been stuck in my head for days. want someone smarter than me to referee it properly, not just declare a winner.

Denny started it by saying the axiom of choice is basically obviously true: if you've got a pile of boxes and every box has something in it, of course you can pick one item from each, even if there's infinitely many boxes, that's just how picking works. Farah said not so fast, and brought up the classic shoes-vs-socks example (i think that's originally Russell's?): for shoes there's a built-in rule, always grab "the left one," so you don't need the axiom at all, but for socks there's no such rule since the two in a pair are identical, and that's precisely the situation the axiom is for.

Denny granted that but said he still doesn't see why it's shaky: each box is nonempty, so grabbing a member out of each one in an infinite pile shouldn't require some special axiom, it should just be automatic. Farah said the issue was never whether an element exists in each box on its own, it's whether you can fix infinitely many ruleless choices simultaneously, and that's exactly what lets the axiom prove the real numbers can be well-ordered, even though nobody's ever managed to actually describe such an ordering, we just know one exists.

Denny answered that "nobody can describe it" can't be the whole objection, since most individual real numbers can't be described either (no name, no formula, nothing), and nobody treats that as a reason those numbers don't exist. Farah agreed but said one indescribable number is nothing like a well-ordering of the entire real line, which has to hold together across an uncountable set all at once, and that clashes with stuff we're already confident about, like there being no smallest number in an open interval.

then Denny went for Banach-Tarski: cut a solid ball into finitely many pieces, don't stretch anything, just rotate and shift them, and reassemble into two solid balls each the same size as the original, he said that's an actual contradiction of how volume works, not just something unintuitive. Farah called that a red herring, since the pieces involved are non-measurable and never had a volume to begin with, so no quantity that's actually defined is doubling, it just shows you can't have a notion of volume that applies to every subset of space and also respects choice.

Denny's last move was that this should still definately make you suspicious of an axiom that manufactures sets this broken for free, without ever constructing one. Farah's was that dropping choice isn't free either: you lose "every vector space has a basis," you lose Tychonoff's theorem, and even ordinary analysis quietly leans on weaker choice principles, so its not choice versus nothing, it's choice versus a bunch of other stuff people also find obvious.

neither one budged. can you go through it move by move and say who's got the better of it at each step, not just who wins overall, and mention if there's something they're both missing?
