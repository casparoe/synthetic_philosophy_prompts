I'm working on a piece about whether a specific, largely forgotten wartime argument still holds up. In 1943, shortly before her death, Simone Weil wrote "On the Abolition of All Political Parties," arguing that a party should be defined not by the beliefs its members share but by its structure: it is a machine built to manufacture collective passion and impose that passion on members' private judgment, and because a party has no natural stopping point for its own growth, unlimited self-perpetuation becomes its only real purpose. This, she thought, is why every party, whatever its program, carries a totalitarian logic in embryo. Her remedy was blunt: no parties at all, with candidates and representatives answerable only to their own reasoned judgment.

Put the contemporary American primary system to her: in safely gerrymandered districts, the general election is a formality, and a small, highly motivated slice of partisans effectively chooses the officeholder in the primary. Would she read this as her fear fully realized, an even purer distillation of party passion than anything she witnessed in the Third Republic, or would she, reasoning from her own premises, judge it a different and lesser problem, given that primaries are at least nominally more participatory than the party apparatus she was attacking? Reconstruct her actual reasoning, and take seriously the strongest version of the standard defense of primaries: that they make parties more internally democratic, not less.
