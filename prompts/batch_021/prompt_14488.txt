I'm a novelist, and I've been asked to give this year's commencement address at the small liberal-arts college where I did my own undergraduate degree — the kind of place that still puts Dewey's The Public and Its Problems on the first-year civic-education syllabus and likes to call itself, half-jokingly, a "laboratory in democracy." I said yes before considering how hard this actually is to do well. My novels keep circling back to small, self-governing communities — town meetings, a housing co-op, a tenant association fighting condemnation — so the dean assumes, not unreasonably, that I have some feel for the material. But a feel for it in fiction isn't the same as standing in front of four hundred families who have never read a page of Dewey and making his actual argument land as something other than a "follow your passion, go do some good" speech with a footnote.

What I want to avoid is the soundbite version of Dewey — "learning by doing," democracy as "a way of life" — detached from the argument that earns it. What I'd like to structure the speech around instead is the quarrel with Lippmann: Dewey wrote The Public and Its Problems largely against the case Lippmann had just made in Public Opinion and The Phantom Public, that modern problems are too indirect and technical for ordinary citizens ever to grasp well enough to govern themselves, so that honesty requires handing decisions to insiders and experts rather than trusting a public that can't in principle be competent. Dewey's rejoinder wasn't to deny that the public of his day was inept — he thought it actually had become confused and eclipsed, unable to locate or organize itself under industrial conditions — but to insist the remedy was better conditions for communication and social inquiry, not rule by expertise in place of a public. I'd like that same picture of inquiry — an unsettled, indeterminate situation worked through by hypothesis and consequence toward what he called warranted assertibility rather than final certainty — carried over from his epistemology into the civic argument, so the graduates can hear the next uncertain years of their own lives as instances of the same experimental method, rather than as a separate "life advice" topic stapled onto a philosophy lesson. And I don't want the speech to pretend I'm more confident than I actually am that the conditions Dewey thought could restore the public — face-to-face community, uncorrupted communication — still hold in anything like their old form; I'd rather it be honest about that doubt than hand four hundred twenty-two-year-olds either empty uplift or a dressed-up version of Lippmann's pessimism.

Could you write me this address: about eight minutes' reading time, nothing that requires the audience to already know who Lippmann is, no slogans standing in for the argument, and something the graduates can actually walk out holding onto?
