For most of my forty-year career as an experimental physicist, I treated the spacetime manifold of general relativity as nothing more than a convenient mathematical stage on which the metric field does its work, and I never had occasion to ask what the points of that manifold actually are. Since retiring I have joined a small philosophy discussion group, and we recently spent two meetings on the Leibniz-Clarke correspondence, in particular Leibniz's argument that God could have had no sufficient reason to situate the entire material universe at one position in absolute space rather than at some other position obtained by shifting everything uniformly, given that the two arrangements would be indistinguishable in every relation among the bodies themselves. One of the group's members, a philosophy graduate student, mentioned in passing that the modern "hole argument" against manifold substantivalism — formulated by Earman and Norton, but descending from a puzzle that, I gather, troubled Einstein himself in the years before he settled on the final, generally covariant field equations — is best understood as a twentieth-century reincarnation of Leibniz's reasoning. I would like to test that claim properly rather than accept it on trust.

Could you compare the hole argument with Leibniz's shift argument in some depth, rather than simply presenting the two side by side? I am especially interested in which features of the classical argument survive intact in its modern incarnation — for instance, the reliance on empirically indiscernible alternatives to discredit a substantivalist view of space or spacetime. I am equally interested in which features are genuinely new to the twentieth-century version: the fact that the hole argument threatens determinism within the dynamical evolution of a single possible world, rather than merely multiplying indistinguishable possible worlds; and the fact that substantivalists have available to them, in the so-called "sophisticated substantivalist" response, a move that seems to have no straightforward analogue for Newton and Clarke against Leibniz's original shift argument. Please tell me, and defend, which of the two arguments you judge to be the harder one for a committed substantivalist to escape, and why.
