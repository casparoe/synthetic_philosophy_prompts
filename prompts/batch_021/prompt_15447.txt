I'm taking a political philosophy seminar this semester, and after a couple weeks on Hobbes we've moved on to Pascal's Pensées, which is honestly a way rougher read — I keep forgetting it's just a pile of notes he never got to organize before he died, so the arguments come at you in these dense little bursts with no throat-clearing. We got assigned the fragment on justice and force for this week (I think it's #298 in the old Brunschvicg numbering, but don't quote me on that) and I've read it like 5 times and still feel like im missing something. Here's the passage, typing it up from the Trotter translation since that's the one on our syllabus:

"Justice, force. — It is right that what is just should be followed; it is necessary that what is strongest should be followed. Justice without force is powerless; force without justice is tyrannical. Justice without force is contradicted, because there are always wicked men; force without justice is condemned. We must then combine justice and force, and for this end make what is just strong, or what is strong just.

Justice is subject to dispute; force is easily recognized and is not disputed. So we could not give force to justice, because force contradicted justice and said that justice was unjust, and said that it was force itself that was just. And thus, being unable to make what is just strong, we have made what is strong just."

Two things are tripping me up and I'd love your take. First, that last move feels like a real bait-and-switch — he opens sounding almost hopeful, like "ideally we'd unite justice and force," and ends on this much bleaker note that we've basically just defined the strong as the just, full stop. Is that supposed to be read as a flat cynical description of how legitimacy actually gets manufactured (very Nietzsche-before-Nietzsche), or is Pascal more resigned/sorrowful about it, or is this secretly doing setup work for some further theological point about grace and fallen reason that he makes elsewhere in the book? Second, I don't get the bit about force being "easily recognized and not disputed" while justice is "subject to dispute" — isn't literally most of human history just people disputing who's actually stronger (wars, power struggles, etc.)? What's he actually getting at with that asymmetry? Feel free to bring in whatever else you know about the rest of the Pensées (the stuff about custom and truth flipping depending which side of the Pyrenees you're on seems related) or how you'd stack this up against Hobbes, since that comparison is basically what our professor wants out of us.
