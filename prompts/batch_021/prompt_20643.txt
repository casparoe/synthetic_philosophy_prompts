I've been down a rabbit hole for a piece on the weirder edges of effective altruist philosophy, and I keep running into two different ideas that both get described using the language of "trade," and honestly I think I'm conflating them in my notes at this point. One is Toby Ord's concept of moral trade -- basically, take the logic of comparative advantage from economics and apply it to ethics, so someone who's horrified by, say, factory farming but totally indifferent to sweatshop labor, and someone who's the mirror image of that, can strike a deal (I'll go vegetarian if you only buy from ethical clothing brands) and each ends up doing better by their own lights, even though neither one actually updates their underlying moral views. The other is the "parliamentary model" for handling moral uncertainty inside a single person -- usually credited to Nick Bostrom, I think -- where your credences in competing moral theories get modeled as delegates in a mini-parliament who vote and logroll across decisions to settle on what you, personally, should do.

Both get pitched using this trade-and-bargaining vocabulary, but one happens between two seperate people with actually different values, and the other is a metaphor for something going on inside one uncertain mind. Could you write me a proper comparison of the two -- what's actually being "traded" in each case, and where the economic analogy holds up versus where it strains -- and then commit to an answer instead of hedging: which of the two strikes you as more defensible then the other as a normative idea once you push on it, and why?
