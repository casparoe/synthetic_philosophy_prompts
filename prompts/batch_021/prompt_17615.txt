I'm the clinical ethicist on our children's hospital's behavioral health practice committee, and we're stuck on something that keeps reminding me of an idea I picked up years ago in a philosophy of science class -- Ian Hacking's "looping effects," basically the idea that classifying a kind of person changes how people in that category understand and even live out their own experience. His go-to example back in the 90s was literally multiple personality disorder, and now, decades later, we're living out a version of it because of TikTok. We've had a real spike in teenage patients presenting with self-identified "systems" (multiple alters -- what the DSM now calls DID), most of whom picked up the whole vocabulary from a community of "system" content creators online months before any symptoms showed up. Our neurology team went through almost the identical fight two years back over a wave of tic-like presentations that also got traced to kids' TikTok feeds, and honestly we never landed on a real policy that time either, we just muddled through case by case, which is part of why I dont want that to happen again.

The committee is genuinely split down the middle. Half our psychiatry staff wants a policy of not mirroring back a patient's own terms ("alters," "fronting," "system") in session, and not opening a formal DID workup until every other explanation is exhausted, on the theory that adopting the kid's vocabulary basically hands them a script and helps produce the very thing we'd be diagnosing. The other half thinks that's condescending, clinically counterproductive, and unfair to the patients for whom this really is DID -- a rare but real, and historically under-diagnosed, condition -- and that refusing to use their own words just teaches them we can't be trusted with it. The only policy we've got on the books is a generic line about clinicians needing to "validate the patient's lived experience," which obviously wasn't written with a case in mind where the lived experience might be substantially shaped by what someone watched right before it started. I need to bring a short decision memo to the committee meeting in three weeks that recommends one concrete policy on clinical language and workup sequencing, not a "here are both sides" summary. Can you draft it? Please take the "don't hand them the script" worry seriously, as the strongest version of that objection rather than a strawman -- I'm honestly not sure the other half of the committee is wrong.
