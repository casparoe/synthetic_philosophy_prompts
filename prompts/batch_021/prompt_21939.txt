Classmate's answer: Van Inwagen's Special Composition Question (SCQ) asks: under what conditions do some objects, the xs, compose a further object, y? In other words, when do two or more things add up to a single thing? Common sense might say that composition happens when objects are stuck together, touching, or serving some joint function — for instance, the bricks of a house compose a house because they're cemented together and serve a common purpose. Van Inwagen calls this kind of answer a "moderate" answer, and he argues that all such answers fail because they make composition a matter of degree. If two bricks can be "a little bit stuck together," then whether the house exists at all becomes a vague matter. But van Inwagen argues that existence itself cannot be vague: there can't be a borderline case where it's unclear whether there are, say, 500 or 501 objects in a room, because number is a precise, discrete matter, not the kind of thing that admits of degrees the way baldness does. So any answer that makes composition vague has to be rejected.

Given this, van Inwagen's own answer is that composition occurs only when the activity of some simples constitutes a life: the xs compose something just in case they're caught up in a single life, the way the cells in my body are caught up in my life. This means most of the objects we ordinarily talk about — chairs, rocks, mountains — don't really exist as single things at all; there are only simples "arranged chair-wise" or "arranged rock-wise." In effect, then, van Inwagen ends up agreeing with the mereological nihilists that composition never really occurs, since the only "composite objects" he has left over, organisms, are just a limiting case he grudgingly allows for. This is quite different from mereological universalism, defended by philosophers such as Cian Dorr, on which composition is completely unrestricted: any two or more objects whatsoever, no matter how scattered or dissimilar — your left shoe and the Eiffel Tower, say — automatically compose some further object. Universalists accept this seemingly strange consequence because they think it's the only way to avoid drawing an arbitrary line anywhere else.
