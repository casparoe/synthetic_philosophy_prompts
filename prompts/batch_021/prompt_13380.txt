I've been thinking about Martin Shubik's dollar auction game, partly prompted by an ongoing disagreement with a friend from my local rationalist meetup about whether it really illustrates the same phenomenon as the "sunk cost fallacy," which is how it tends to get glossed in popular treatments. As I understand the game, its most interesting feature isn't that bidders are failing to ignore money already spent, but that the specific payment rule — both the highest and second-highest bidder must pay their final bid, while only the highest bidder receives the dollar — means that folding guarantees a loss while continuing to bid is a bet that the other side will fold first. That's a genuinely forward-looking calculation rather than a backward-looking one, which is why the escalation doesn't obviously require either party to be violating any elementary rule of rational choice, only to be uncertain enough about the other's resolve that neither wants to be the one who blinks. This is the sense in which I've seen people in the Scott Alexander/Yudkowsky orbit invoke the game when discussing "Moloch"-style traps: not a bias correctable by better individual reasoning, but an adversarial incentive structure in which unilateral de-escalation is punished, for both parties, more reliably than continued escalation is.

I'd like you to write a short story of roughly 1000–1500 words that dramatizes this specific mechanism. Rather than setting it at a literal auction, please relocate the dynamic to some other domain of escalating commitment of your choosing — a bidding war between two research groups racing to be first to a discovery, a standoff between rival commanders each escalating a border skirmish, a divorce mediation that spirals past the value of the estate being contested, or any other setting that renders the underlying payoff structure vivid. At least one character should, at some point, explicitly work out the game-theoretic shape of the trap they're in — in particular, the asymmetric-payment feature that distinguishes it from an ordinary auction, and the fact that further escalation functions as a bet on the other side's resolve rather than a simple refusal to cut losses — and the ending should follow from the logic of that situation rather than from an authorial decision to have someone learn a lesson about pride or stubbornness. I would rather read a story that gets the incentive structure right and lets the tragedy emerge from agents behaving rationally, under uncertainty, throughout, than one that resolves into a fable about irrationality.
