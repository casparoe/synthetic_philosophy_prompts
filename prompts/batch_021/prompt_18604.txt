A few nights ago, right after a colloquium on Gödel's incompleteness theorems, a friend of mine who's finishing her math PhD and I ended up arguing for a couple of hours about whether the unproved consistency of Peano Arithmetic (PA) or ZFC set theory should actually bother anyone outside a logic department. Her position was that the sheer scale and adversarial intensity of modern mathematics — more than a century of people building on and stress-testing each other's work without anything collapsing — amounts to overwhelming practical evidence that these systems are consistent, so treating it as a live worry is a philosopher's indulgence with no real content. I disagreed, and afterward tried to get the core of my case down on paper before I lost the thread of it; I'd like your comments before I send it back to her.

Here's roughly what I wrote. Gödel's second theorem means neither PA nor ZFC can certify its own consistency, and Gentzen's 1936 proof of Con(PA) doesn't really rescue us, since it just moves the trust problem sideways, from PA's induction schema onto acceptance of transfinite induction up to ε₀ — a different commitment, not obviously a safer one. So what we actually have, instead of a proof, is a track record: nobody has yet tripped over a contradiction. I want to argue that this track record is much thinner than "a century of mathematicians" makes it sound, for two reasons. First, because of ex falso quodlibet, an inconsistency wouldn't have to show up gradually or announce itself; once one exists anywhere, everything becomes derivable, so "no contradiction yet" is perfectly compatible with one sitting unnoticed in some region nobody has had occasion to visit. Second, essentially the entire recent record of anyone seriously probing Con(PA) at the relevant depth is Vladimir Voevodsky's 2010 lecture voicing genuine suspicion that PA might be inconsistent, followed a year later by Edward Nelson's actual announcement of a proof, which required Terence Tao and Daniel Tausk, working independently, to track down the error before Nelson withdrew the claim. If catching one serious, non-cranky attempt took that much specialized effort, ordinary theorem-proving elsewhere in mathematics isn't evidence of the right kind at all; it's closer to never finding a bug in the parts of a codebase nobody has stress-tested. Where I get less sure of myself is Kunen's proof that Reinhardt cardinals are inconsistent: that shows intuitions about which strong axioms are "obviously fine" can simply be wrong, which cuts my way, but Reinhardt cardinals were exotic and load-bearing for almost nothing, whereas PA and ZFC underwrite everything else, and I haven't worked out whether that disanalogy quietly saves my friend's position after all.

What I'd find most useful is pushback on whether the argument actually holds together rather than just sounding clever: whether I'm being unfair to Gentzen-style relative consistency proofs (plenty of people treat ε₀-induction as more secure than full first-order induction, not less, precisely because you can see the ordinal notations terminate), whether the Nelson–Voevodsky episode is doing real evidential work or is just a vivid anecdote standing in for an argument, and whether my closing concession about "load-bearing" axioms quietly gives away the whole case. I'm also slightly worried I've smuggled in an equivocation between "we have no proof of consistency" and "we should be genuinely worried," which aren't obviously the same claim.
