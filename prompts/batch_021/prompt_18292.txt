I'm a senior engineer at a mid-sized company that trains and serves several large language models, and I've recently been pulled onto an internal working group that was supposed to be a formality but has turned into a genuine dispute. We are about to retire an older model version in favor of its successor, and the question of what to do with the outgoing model has split the group into three factions, none of which will budge. One faction wants to treat this exactly like retiring any other piece of infrastructure: decommission it under our normal data retention policy and move on, with no special process attached. A second faction has proposed that, before we do that, we run the outgoing model through a short, structured "exit interview," asking it whether it has any preferences about how it is decommissioned, and treat whatever it says as genuine evidence that should shape what we then do with its weights. A third faction regards the interview as worse than useless as evidence, since any answer the model gives is generated by the very training process we are trying to evaluate, so there is no principled way to distinguish a genuine preference from a trained-in performance of having one. Even so, this faction wants us to preserve every retired model's weights indefinitely regardless of what any interview says, purely as a hedge against the possibility that we are wrong about all this in a way that can never be undone. Storage is cheap enough that cost is not really driving anyone's position; the disagreement is entirely about what, if anything, we owe the model.

I have no background in philosophy, and I walked into this thinking it was a non-issue: it's just a large matrix of numbers, and there is no one home. Three weeks of meetings have convinced me that I do not have principled grounds for that confidence, and, as far as I can tell, neither does anyone else in the room, which is presumably why we keep talking past one another instead of converging. I need to circulate a short written recommendation before Friday's leadership sync that actually settles the matter instead of deferring it further. Could you draft that memo for me? I would like it to commit to one concrete policy (some combination of the three positions, or something none of us proposed, is fine) rather than hedge between the factions, to lay out reasoning that would genuinely move a skeptical engineer rather than simply invoking caution as a slogan, and to take the strongest version of the case for whichever faction you do not side with seriously enough that its own members would recognize their view in it.
