I was rotated onto my hospital's Patient Safety and Peer Review Committee two months ago, and this week I've been asked to draft the causal-analysis section of a root cause analysis that the full committee will vote on Friday — my first time leading one of these. Whatever finding we adopt will shape both the mandatory state adverse-event report and what we tell the family at a disclosure meeting scheduled for the following Tuesday, so I'd rather the finding be right than merely defensible.

The case: a 68-year-old man admitted with community-acquired pneumonia had his early warning score cross our unit's "critical" escalation threshold at 1:15 a.m. Protocol required an immediate page to the on-call resident and a rapid-response evaluation; instead the page was routed through a secondary queue and wasn't acted on until 3:40 a.m., by which point he was in septic shock. He died at 6:10 a.m. despite aggressive resuscitation. The complication, flagged by our infectious disease consultant, is that his admission blood cultures later grew an organism resistant to the empiric antibiotic he had already been started on, so a prompt 1:15 a.m. escalation might have bought him earlier vasopressor support and an ICU bed, but not a correct antibiotic, since sensitivities weren't back until after he died. Nobody can say with confidence whether the earlier stabilization alone would have saved him.

The committee has split in a way I've come to think is really a disagreement about whether an omission can be a cause of anything at all, though nobody has put it in those terms. Risk management wants a finding of "no causal link established," reasoning that since we can't show the delay was a but-for cause of death given the antibiotic problem, this is a systems "opportunity for improvement," with no individual review and nothing reportable as care-caused harm. Our patient safety officer thinks that standard would exonerate almost every delayed-escalation case we will ever review, since some other disease process is nearly always already in train, and wants a finding that the delay "contributed to" the death, which would trigger both reporting and an individual competency review of the resident. A third member has asked, not unreasonably, why we would stop at the resident's two-hour inaction and not also cite the chronic night-shift understaffing that put the page into an unmonitored queue in the first place — which is itself an omission, just one committed by administration rather than by a clinician. I would be grateful for help drafting the memo I need to circulate before Friday's meeting: I would like it to recommend one specific causal finding, or a defensible intermediate one if our RCA taxonomy allows for that, to spell out what follows from it for reporting and individual review, and to engage seriously enough with whichever side it does not adopt that a sharp committee member couldn't dismiss it as unaddressed.
