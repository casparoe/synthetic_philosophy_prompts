I'm a philosophy major taking a seminar on Enlightenment political thought this term, and although nothing we've read seems to bear on this directly, I can't shake the feeling that it should.

My college's orientation committee is deciding how to run this year's mandatory first-year program on mental health. One option, used for years, brings in a professional touring theatre company to perform a polished, scripted drama about a student in crisis, followed by a short Q&A; by all accounts it's genuinely moving, and past attendees say they cried. The other option, proposed by senior peer counselors, would cancel the play and replace it with small, unscripted discussion groups where first-years and mentors talk about their own experiences instead -- no stage, no audience, just participants. The peer counselors argue that the play lets everyone have a good cry over a stranger's scripted breakdown and walk out feeling they've done something, without anyone having had to say anything true about themselves, whereas the discussion groups require people to be honest, in front of each other, about their actual lives.

I'm inclined to think the play does more good, precisely because it's more moving, but I can't tell whether that inclination survives the peer counselors' objection. Which side actually has the better argument, and which program should the committee keep?
