Robin Hanson's Great Filter has always struck me as an unusually good stress test for anthropic reasoning, because—unlike the standard presumptuous-philosopher setup—it isn't obvious in advance which of the two leading theories of self-location, the Self-Sampling Assumption or the Self-Indication Assumption, ends up sounding the more optimistic note about our prospects. Consider two rival hypotheses about where the "hard step" behind the Fermi paradox lies, stipulated to be equally plausible on ordinary scientific grounds, apart from any anthropic considerations. On the filter-behind hypothesis, reaching our current stage of development (intelligent, technological, pre-expansion, with no detected trace of any other civilization) is itself astronomically rare, but almost every civilization that reaches it goes on to expand across its galaxy. On the filter-ahead hypothesis, reaching our current stage is comparatively easy and common, but almost none of the civilizations that reach it survive long enough to expand. Write a dialogue between a philosopher who holds the Self-Sampling Assumption and one who holds the Self-Indication Assumption, in which each works out—live, and largely for the other's benefit—what their own assumption implies about the relative credence we should place in the filter-behind versus the filter-ahead hypothesis, given only the fact that we find ourselves at that pre-expansion stage. Each should start out with a rough guess about whether their own theory is going to turn out to be the gloomy one here, and the dialogue should show that guess surviving, breaking down, or getting complicated as the other presses for details. Both interlocutors should be equally sharp, argue in good faith, and engage with each other's actual reasoning rather than simply talking past one another.
