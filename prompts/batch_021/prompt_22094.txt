I don't have any formal training in philosophy or neuroscience, but for the past year or so I've been reading everything I can find on my own time about split-brain patients -- people who, usually to treat severe epilepsy, had the corpus callosum connecting their two brain hemispheres surgically cut. I'm endlessly fascinated by the classic Sperry/Gazzaniga-style experiments: show a picture only to a patient's left visual field, and their speaking half (almost always the left hemisphere) will insist it saw nothing, even while their left hand is simultaneously picking out the matching object from a set of hidden items on the table. This came up at a dinner recently after someone mentioned a documentary on the subject, and two friends of mine, Marcus and Ines, got into a back-and-forth I haven't been able to stop turning over since. I've reconstructed it as faithfully as I can, with each move numbered so it's easy to refer back to.

(1) Marcus opened by saying the experiments show there are two separate minds sharing one skull after the surgery -- unmasked by the operation, not created by it -- since each hemisphere can be shown something the other has no access to and can act on it (reach for it, point to it, reason about it) without the other side knowing anything happened. (2) Ines said that was too quick: outside the lab, these patients are single, coordinated people with one name, one face, and one continuous life story; they don't go around negotiating with themselves for control of the body, and the "two minds" impression only shows up because the tests are deliberately built to feed each hemisphere different information. Generalizing from a rigged setup to the whole person, she said, is the real mistake. (3) Marcus replied that the conflict isn't only a lab artifact -- patients have reported one hand undoing a button the other hand had just fastened, or shoving away something the other hand had just picked up, in everyday situations, not just under a testing apparatus. A genuinely unified agent, he said, shouldn't fight itself like that. (4) Ines answered that ordinary, non-split brains do things that look like self-conflict too: a hand swatting at a mosquito on reflex while you're consciously trying to hold still for a photo, or a dieter's hand reaching for the cookie their better judgment didn't want. We don't conclude there are two people from those cases, only one person with subsystems that aren't perfectly coordinated, and she wanted to know why the split-brain hand conflicts should be treated as different in kind rather than just a more dramatic version of the same thing. (5) Marcus said the difference is that in the mosquito or cookie case, it's one person weighing the same facts and getting pulled in two directions; in the split-brain case, each hand is being driven by information the other side literally doesn't have and structurally can't get. That isn't one person torn between two considerations, he said -- it's two separate streams of experience, each with access only one hemisphere possesses. (6) Ines's closing move was to point out that this commits Marcus to saying that before the surgery, when the callosum was still intact, there was clearly only one mind there -- so cutting a single band of fibers doesn't just disrupt something, it manufactures an entirely new person out of nothing. Maybe, she said, the real lesson isn't "one" or "two" at all, but that our ordinary way of counting subjects of experience was built for normal brains and simply has no determinate answer to give in this case, however hard either of them pushes it.
