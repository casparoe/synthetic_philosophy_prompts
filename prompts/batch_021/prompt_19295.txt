I got asked to join the patient and family advisory council at the teen mental health clinic where my niece has been getting treatment for about a year now (I guess I count as "lived experience" too, since I went through something pretty similar myself as a teenager, way before anyone used half these words with me). Next Tuesday the clinical team is bringing a policy question to the council for an actual vote, and they asked each of us to send in a short written opinion first so there's something on paper to argue about instead of just people talking over each other in the room. The question is: when a teenager's symptoms line up with the criteria for Borderline Personality Disorder, should the therapists just tell them straight out and put it in thier file, or stick to softer, vaguer language like "emotion regulation difficulties" and never actually say the diagnosis to a minor?

The clinic's lead psychologist is pushing hard for full disclosure. Her argument is that teens deserve to know the truth about themselves, and that if the clinic won't say the word, the kids are going to diagnose themselves off TikTok anyway, badly and with zero context, so at least this way an adult who actually knows them is the one explaining it. A couple of the other therapists are dead against it, their worry being that the second a 15 year old starts thinking of herself as "a borderline," it changes how she describes her own feelings and acts around staff and other patients, and that the label sticks to her file into her twenties even if it turns out not to fit anymore. I keep coming back to my niece, who overheard one offhand comment from her counselor, went home that night and read the entire DSM criteria list, and has basically talked about her moods in that exact language ever since. I honestly can't tell if that's her finally having words for something real, or the label sort of writing the rest of the story for her.

Can you write the memo I'm supposed to hand in? I want it to actually land on a recommendation, a real answer or a specific middle-ground policy, not a "here are both sides" shrug, and I need whoever reads it to see that I took the disclosure argument seriously instead of just being the emotional parent-type voice in the room, since that argument isn't obviously wrong just because it makes me uncomfortable.
