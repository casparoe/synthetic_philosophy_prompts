I'm deep into a novel set in Harlem in 1926, and while chasing period detail for it I went back to the primary text that everyone paraphrases but few people seem to actually sit with: W. E. B. Du Bois's address "Criteria of Negro Art," delivered that year at the NAACP's annual convention and printed in The Crisis that October. Every citation of it stops at the line that all Art is propaganda and ever must be, despite the wailing of the purists, and treats the essay as a simple manifesto for didactic writing. But reading the whole piece, Du Bois doesn't sound like someone who thinks beauty is beside the point; he keeps binding Beauty to Truth and to Right, as if the demand that Black artists serve the race's dignity were not a constraint on beauty but its very completion. That refusal to treat "propaganda" and "beauty" as competing masters is the part I can't get to sit still, and it wasn't merely a private position of his, either: that same year he ran a symposium in The Crisis, "The Negro in Art: How Shall He Be Portrayed?", putting the question of limits on Black representation to a wide range of Black and white writers -- which tells me the essay was one move in a live argument with people in his own circle who wanted more latitude than he was granting them. One of my own characters is a little-magazine editor caught in exactly this fight, and I'd rather understand the philosophy underneath it than keep gesturing vaguely at "art versus politics."

So: write me an essay that takes a real position on whether Du Bois's specific claim holds up. Reconstruct the strongest version of his argument, including how he would likely answer the charge that "propaganda" and "beauty" just name two different things he's illegitimately fused together -- don't let him off the hook by assuming he meant the slogan loosely. Engage with at least one serious objection from within the actual debate he was part of, not a generic worry about message fiction. Then commit to a verdict: is he right that art worthy of the name is inescapably propaganda, is he trading on an equivocation, or is there some more precise claim buried inside the slogan that survives even though the slogan itself doesn't? I'd rather have an essay willing to be wrong in an interesting way than one that hedges to the end.
