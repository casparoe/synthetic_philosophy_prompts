I've spent a lot of time over the years reading LessWrong and Overcoming Bias, and a handful of people I originally met through those circles still run a monthly reading-and-argument group, nominally devoted to philosophy of mind. At last month's meeting, one regular, Elena, told a story about driving home from the previous session: her attention wandered completely somewhere on the highway, and she "came to" several minutes later having apparently taken the correct exit, braked hard for a car that cut her off, and changed lanes twice, with no memory whatsoever of doing any of it. The anecdote derailed us from that week's assigned reading and instead kicked off an argument between two other regulars, Dana and Marcus, that never really got resolved. I'd like your help figuring out who actually had the better of it, move by move.

Dana's position was that Elena simply wasn't conscious during that stretch of driving. Her visual and motor systems were obviously processing the scene accurately enough to steer, brake, and change lanes, but none of that counts as experience, on Dana's view, unless there's also some further awareness of being in those perceptual states as they happen -- a kind of thought about the seeing and reacting, over and above the seeing and reacting itself. Since Elena couldn't recover any of it afterward, not even under prompting, Dana concluded that no such further awareness had been present, so nothing on that stretch of road was actually experienced, however sophisticated the underlying processing. Marcus pushed back hard: forgetting something is not the same as never having experienced it. Nobody remembers the taste of most meals they've eaten, he said, and we don't conclude those meals went untasted. Dana replied that the analogy fails, because ordinary forgetting is a failure of long-term storage, whereas Elena couldn't report anything even in the seconds right after "waking up" -- which suggests the relevant awareness was never there even fleetingly, not that it was there and then lost. Marcus's comeback was that this proves too much: hardly any of ordinary experience -- driving, walking, half-listening to a conversation -- involves explicitly registering "I am now seeing this" as it happens, and if that sort of self-directed awareness is the bar for consciousness, then most of adult waking life, and presumably all of animal life, turns out not to be conscious either, which he took to be a reductio. Dana's final move was to retreat slightly, suggesting that all that's really needed is that Elena's perceptual states were poised to produce that further awareness if attention had shifted toward them, whether or not it actually did -- though she admitted she wasn't sure whether this saves her original claim about Elena or just quietly hands Marcus the point.

Neither of them would concede, and I don't think either was simply confused -- they both seemed to be tracking something real. Could you go through the exchange move by move and say who has the better of it at each stage, rather than just declaring an overall winner? I'm especially interested in whether Dana's final retreat actually rescues her view or effectively concedes it, and, more generally, whether a little more conceptual care would dissolve this whole dispute or whether it's tracking a genuine, unresolved fault line in how philosophers of mind have tried to explain what makes a mental state a conscious one in the first place.
