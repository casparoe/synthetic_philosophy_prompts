I've been half-reporting, half-obsessing over the gerontocracy stuff in American politics lately -- Feinstein basically unable to function by the end, McConnell freezing up on camera twice, the whole meltdown over whether Biden was too old to run again, and now people seriously floating cognitive tests or hard age caps for the Senate and the Supreme Court. Mostly as background reading for a piece, I finally sat down with Cicero's De Senectute (in translation, my Latin's nonexistant), expecting some generic "wisdom comes with age" pep talk, but it's actually a much more specific argument than that. Cato the Elder's case is basically: running a state is a matter of judgement and counsel, not physical speed or strength, so supreme power properly belongs to old men -- he even points out that the Romans literally named their governing body after "old man" (senex), as if that settles it, and backs it up with guys who stayed sharp and politically active into their 80s and 90s. But there's a whole separate stretch where he concedes memory and body really do decline, and his fix is basically "keep exercising both and they won't decline much," which feels pretty thin next to an actual cognitive slip caught on video and replayed everywhere.

So here's what I keep chewing on: Cicero already had a take on old men ruling, but he never had to reckon with our specific wrinkles -- medicine keeping people nominally in power into their 90s, live footage catching every stumble in real time, the idea of a formal fitness test as a condition of holding office, mass elections instead of senatorial appointment doing the filtering. If you walked him through today's fight over age limits and fitness tests using his own arguments as the starting point, where do you think he'd actually land? Would a Feinstein-type case be exactly the exception his framework can't absorb, or does he have a real way of defending the old position anyway? I'd genuinely like a reconstructed argument here, not a "he'd probably say it's complicated."
