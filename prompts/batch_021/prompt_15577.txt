So I'm teaching a grad seminar on moral psychology this term, and we just spent two weeks working through the "moral worth" literature -- Kant's motive-of-duty stuff, then Arpaly's unprincipled virtue, then Markovits' coincident reasons thesis. Almost everyone in that literature leans on the Huckleberry Finn case at some point (Huck helps Jim escape even though his "conscience," which is really just the internalized racism of his society, tells him its theft and a sin), and its always presented the same way: Huck gets moral credit for being responsive to the reasons that actually make helping Jim right -- Jim's humanity, their friendship -- without needing any de dicto thought like "this is the right thing to do." Arpaly wants this to show moral worth doesn't require acting under any moral concept at all; Markovits wants it as her showcase case for the idea that motivating and normative reasons just need to coincide, guise-free.

Last night, re-reading the actual chapter for an unrelated class, I got stuck on something and scribbled out a little argument I want your take on before I inflict it on my students. The scene isn't just Huck quietly following his sympathy for Jim -- he actually writes the letter turning Jim in, feels relieved, says he's "washed clean of sin" for the first time in his life, and only after sitting with that does he reconsider, tear the letter up, and settle it with "alright, then, I'll go to hell." That looks less like zero moral cognition to me and more like Huck explicitly judging that he's choosing the damned option over the "right" one -- so there IS a de dicto moral thought floating around in there, its just attached to the wrong act (turning Jim in, not keeping him free). If that's a fair reading, it seems to mess with the tidy "pure de re responsiveness, no moral concepts required" story that both Arpaly and Markovits want to tell about this case.

Curious what you make of this: am I being unfair to either of them (maybe one already has the resources to absorb this and I'm just forgetting), does it actually bite harder against Markovits specifically (since her thesis needs the motivating reasons to be doing the justifying work, not just be lurking somewhere in Huck's head) versus Arpaly's stronger no-concepts-needed claim, and is this some version of an objection that's already out there under a name I'm forgetting? Also happy to hear "this is a stretch, here's why" -- I haven't run it by anyone else yet.
