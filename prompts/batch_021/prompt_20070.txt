My aunt Frankie died on Sunday, and because I'm the novelist in the family I've been asked to give the eulogy this Saturday -- everyone assumes that because I write books for a living (sad ones, mostly) I must be good at this, which is not actually how eulogies work. Here's the complication: Frankie was a moral philosopher for almost forty years, not famous outside her own little corner of the field, but she published, had a string of grad students who adored her, and her whole scholarly thing -- the hill she chose to die on, academically -- was arguing that supererogation basically doesn't exist. You know, "going above and beyond the call of duty." She thought the whole category was a comforting fiction, something like Kagan's "no options" line (Limits of Morality, I think? she quoted it at Thanksgiving so often we could all recite the gist) -- that if an act really is the best available thing to do, morality doesn't hand you a choice about it, it just requires it of you. And then, infuriatingly, she went and spent her actual life doing things that look, to the rest of us, exactly like the thing she said didn't exist: gave a kidney to a total stranger in her fifties, fostered something like nine kids over the decades, and was the aunt who'd already be on a plane to the disaster zone while everyone else was still doomscrolling. A local paper once profiled her as "a modern-day saint," and she made us all sit through her furious letter to the editor at the next family dinner, because apparently "saint" is a real technical term in this literature and she took it as an insult.

So here's my actual problem. Half the room Saturday is going to be old friends and family who want to hear some version of "she went above and beyond," and the other half is former students and colleagues who will visibly wince if I say that exact sentence, since it's basically the one she spent her career arguing was a category error. I can't get up there and deliver a philosophy lecture, and I definately can't imply that everyone who hasn't donated a kidney is a moral failure, which is uncomfortably close to what her actual position entails once you push on it. What I want is a eulogy, something like seven or eight minutes, that's honest to what she really thought -- that the philosophy people in the room recognize as fair to her actual views instead of the fortune-cookie version -- but that still comforts a room full of people who just loved her and could not care less about the literature. Could you draft it for me?
