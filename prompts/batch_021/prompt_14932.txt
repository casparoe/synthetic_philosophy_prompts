I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and next week I'm recording a long interview for a profile piece on the philosophical fault lines running through effective altruism and longtermism. My guest is a moral philosopher whose recent work pushes the "cluelessness" objection to consequentialism further than most people are comfortable with. She accepts Hilary Greaves's now-standard distinction between "simple" cluelessness -- unforeseeable effects we have no reason to think favor one act over its alternative, and so can treat as washing out in expectation -- and "complex" cluelessness, where the unforeseeable effects are backed by some evidence pointing each way with no principled method for weighing that evidence against the other. Her contribution, and the reason I'm interviewing her, is the claim that flagship longtermist interventions -- pandemic preparedness, AI-safety research, extinction-risk reduction generally -- are riddled with the complex kind, not the simple kind Greaves thought could be set aside, which would gut the "we can be reasonably confident this helps the far future" premise longtermism depends on. Whenever someone asks how she still manages to act at all given this, her stock answer is that cluelessness is a fact about our evidence rather than a license for nihilism -- a line she's given in at least three previous interviews I've listened to, which tells you what she denies without telling you what she'd have us do instead.

Please draft eight interview questions, each followed by a short follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give, that don't let her simply restate the simple/complex distinction. I want at least one question testing whether that distinction is principled or just relocates, rather than resolves, the very problem it was meant to dissolve; one that forces her "not a license for nihilism" line into an actual decision rule instead of a description of what she's ruling out; one on whether Christian Tarsney's persistent-difference strategy -- betting that some interventions lock a valuable state into place so firmly that only a rare, late-arriving disruption (what he calls an exogenous nullifying event) could undo it -- lets extinction-risk interventions specifically escape her critique even if nothing else does; one asking why any of this should count as a special problem for consequentialists specifically, rather than for any moral theory that needs facts about the world to apply its categories, given that earlier defenders of this same argument have simply exempted non-consequentialists from worrying about effects nobody could foresee; and a closing question about what kind of finding, empirical or philosophical, would make her retract the charge against longtermism in particular rather than against consequentialist ethics in general.
