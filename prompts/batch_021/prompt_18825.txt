Ok so I've been kind of obsessed lately with old Daoist stuff and I got stuck on this passage from the Zhuangzi (the "butterfly dream" guy, like 4th century BC China). Everywhere online people just summarize the butterfly dream as "he couldn't tell if he was a man dreaming he was a butterfly or a butterfly dreaming he was a man, so nothing is really real / its all relative," but when I actually read the fuller bit, in this old public-domain translation I found on one of those old-texts sites (not sure who translated it, just found it posted), it doesn't seem to add up to that. Heres what i copied down:

"How do I know that loving life is not a delusion? How do I know that in hating death I am not like a man who lost his way home when young, and no longer knows the way back? Those who dream of a merry drinking bout may wake up weeping; those who dream of weeping may in the morning go off to hunt. While we are dreaming we do not know we are dreaming, and in the middle of a dream we may even try to interpret a dream within it. Only on waking do we know it was a dream. And someday there will come a great awakening, and then we shall know that this whole life was a great dream. Yet fools think they are already awake, busily and cleverly sorting out who is ruler and who is subject. How dense! Confucius and you are both dreaming. And when I say you are dreaming, this too is a dream."

...(then a bunch of other stuff about arguments and shadows that I won't bother copying)...

"Once Zhuang Zhou dreamed he was a butterfly, fluttering about happily, quite pleased with himself, not aware that he was Zhou. Suddenly he woke and found he was solidly and unmistakably Zhou again. He did not know whether it was Zhou dreaming he was a butterfly, or the butterfly dreaming it was Zhou. Between Zhou and the butterfly there must be some distinction. This is called the transformation of things."

Two things are bugging me. First, if the whole point is that we can never really know whether we're dreaming or awake, why does he then say "when I say you are dreaming, this too is a dream" - doesn't that just cancel out his own claim, like a snake eating its own tail? And second, that last line about how there "must be some distinction" between Zhou and the butterfly seems to flat out contradict the "can't tell them apart, it's all the same" reading that everyone gives the story - so what's the butterfly bit actually saying if not that, and what's "the transformation of things" doing there instead? Am I missing something, or is the popular version of this story just kind of wrong?
