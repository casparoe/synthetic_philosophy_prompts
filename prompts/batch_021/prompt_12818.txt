Ok, this happened at work about a month ago and it's still rattling around in my head. I've been reading rationalist-ish blogs and forums for like a decade now, and I've got this itch that what happened is basically a real-life version of some old debate I've definitely seen referenced in that world, except I can't remember what it's called or track down the actual post, so bear with me. Quick background: I'm an ICU nurse, so multiple times a shift I calculate and double-check drug doses -- same formulary, same math, same lookup process every single time -- and normally, if you stopped me mid-shift and asked "do you know this dose is right," I'd say yes instantly, no hesitation, and any other nurse watching me would back that up without blinking.

A few weeks ago though I had a patient on a med where being even a little off on the dose, given another drug they were already on, could have killed them. Going into it, my evidence was, as far as I can tell, exactly as strong as on any ordinary night -- same chart, same lookup, same everything. But I suddenly didn't feel like I "knew" the dose the way I normally do, and I ended up checking it three seperate times before actually giving it. Here's the part that got me stuck, though: nothing went wrong, so a few days later it came up in the break room totally casually, and everyone, me included, was like "obviously you knew the dose, you run that exact calculation a thousand times a week." But then the same incident got brought up again, more formally, at a review where the whole point is to pick apart near misses -- and in that room, talking about that exact same night, a couple people said things like "you didn't really know it was right, that's the whole reason you checked three times, and thank god you did."

Same me, same original evidence, same actual event being described, both times, but totally different verdicts on whether I "knew" -- and it seemed to track the kind of conversation we were having way more than anything about the night itself. So now I've got two things tangled together in my head: first, whether actually being in a high-stakes situation can change whether you know something even while your evidence stays completely fixed, and second, whether "know" just quietly demands more depending on who's asking and why, even when everyone's describing one fixed, unchanging past situation. Are those actually two separate questions? And is there a real, defensible answer to either one, or does this whole thing just dissolve into "people use the word know loosely"?
