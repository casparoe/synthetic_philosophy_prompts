My colleague Diane is retiring after thirty-one years and I got roped into giving the toast at her dinner next month. Her whole career has basically been one long defense of Williams-style internalism about reasons -- she's the person half our field cites for the canonical reply to the McDowell line that there must be external reasons out there regardless of what anyone actually cares about. She's also genuinely famous in the department for interupting other people's toasts to point out when an "ought" isn't hooked to anything in the speaker's actual motivational set -- she heckled a colleague's retirement speech for exactly this two years ago. Which is a problem for me, because toasts run on exactly that move: "be more like her," "let her be an example to us all" -- a bare, desire-independent ought, which is precisely what she's spent thirty years arguing is either false or really something else in disguise. Half the room will be former grad students who'll clock this instantly; the other half will be her sister and a couple of college roommates who've never heard the phrase "sound deliberative route" in their lives. Can you write me the actual toast -- five minutes, genuinely moving, and it has to solve the problem rather than dodge it, so that whatever it ends up exhorting people to do is tied to something already sitting in their own S, not just handed down from nowhere.
