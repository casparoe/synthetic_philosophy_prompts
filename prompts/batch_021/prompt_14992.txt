Anyone who has spent time on LessWrong or the Alignment Forum will know Janus's "Simulators" essay, which pushes back against treating a large language model as a single agent with a stable set of beliefs and desires. On that view, the base model is better understood as a kind of generative "physics" capable of instantiating many different simulacra—characters or processes, each more or less agentic, none of them identical with "the model itself." This stands in some tension with Daniel Dennett's intentional stance, which licenses attributing beliefs and desires to a system whenever doing so makes its behavior more predictable, and which Dennett elsewhere defends through his "real patterns" argument for the reality of such attributions. Compare these two frameworks as accounts of what is going on when we say a language model "believes" something. Is the tension merely apparent—could a committed Dennettian simply relocate the intentional stance from the model to the simulacrum—or do the two views disagree about something more fundamental? Justify your answer with reference to a concrete phenomenon, such as in-context persona shifts or a model contradicting itself across different framings of the same question, that you think favors one framework over the other.
