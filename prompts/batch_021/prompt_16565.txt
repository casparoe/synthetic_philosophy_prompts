I am a retired neuroscientist who studied the neural basis of decision-making, and I have recently taken up philosophy, most recently L. A. Paul's work on transformative experience. I still serve on my former medical center's neuroethics subcommittee, which must decide whether to renew a deep brain stimulation trial for treatment-resistant depression. Long-term follow-up shows several participants describing themselves as having become different people after years of stimulation - changed in temperament, ambition, and even values they had long taken to be fixed - in ways neither they nor their families anticipated when consent was obtained. Half the subcommittee wants to pause enrollment until a fundamentally different consent process is designed, since no disclosure could have conveyed what it is like to become a different kind of person; the other half worries this argument, taken seriously, would block almost any life-altering choice, medical or otherwise. Please draft the short memo I will bring to next month's meeting: recommend a concrete course of action with a time frame, and take the transformative character of the intervention seriously without letting the objection prove too much.
