Ok so last night I got into it with my friend Priya for like an hour over something that started as her econ paper and spiraled into a full-blown argument about time itself, basically. She's writing about the social discount rate for climate policy and brought up the whole Stern vs Nordhaus thing -- how Stern used a discount rate close to zero because he thinks any "pure" time preference (discounting someone's wellbeing just because they're further in the future, as opposed to discounting for risk or economic growth or whatever else) is basically indefensible, like discriminating against people for the decade they happen to be born in, and Nordhaus thought that was hopelessly naive and totally out of step with how any real economy actually prices the future. Priya's firmly in the Stern camp, zero pure time preference full stop, anything else is just bias wearing a suit. I pushed back that it's not that simple: if you actually set pure time preference to zero in a Ramsey-style growth model, summing welfare over an infinite future with no discounting at all can fail to even converge to a number you can compare across different policies, so at some point the math itself seems to demand some kind of discounting whether or not its "ethical." She said economists have workarounds for exactly that (something about an "overtaking" criterion that ranks paths without needing the sum to converge), and that needing a technical patch to keep the math well behaved doesn't make it okay to discount actual people's welfare by their birth date. Fine, I said, but then what about existential risk -- surely you can discount the far future somewhat just because theres a real chance we never even get there -- and she said that's smuggling risk in through the back door to rescue what is, underneath it, just impatience dressed up in equations. We went around like this for ages and neither of us actually budged, we just got louder.

I'd love to see this fought out properly by two people who actually know the material cold, instead of two amateurs repeating their priors louder like we did. Could you write me a dialogue between two economic advisors stuck in a room the night before they have to sign off on the discount rate for a government climate white paper -- one of them thinks any positive rate of pure time preference is bias dressed up as math and has to go, the other thinks the zero-discounting position sounds nice until it runs into the actual math and practice of long-horizon policy. I want real back and forth: each of them should have to engage the other's strongest point (the convergence / "overtaking criterion" issue, the risk-versus-pure-time-preference distinction, and maybe also the difference between discounting wellbeing itself versus discounting consumption just because future people will probably be richer) rather than talk past each other, and neither one should be the obvious strawman.
