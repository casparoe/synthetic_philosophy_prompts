I'm a software engineer at a small fertility-tech startup. Over the past year I've been the one building and maintaining the pipeline that turns a couple's embryo genotyping results into the ranked report their fertility clinic hands them: for each embryo, we currently flag elevated polygenic risk for a handful of serious early-onset conditions. The founders now want to add predicted scores for traits like adult height and a couple of cognitive/educational-attainment proxies, reasoning that prospective parents already ask about this informally, so we might as well be rigorous and transparent about it rather than cede that part of the market to less careful competitors.

Because I wrote the ranking model, I've been pulled into the small internal group drafting our policy on this ahead of next month's clinical advisory board meeting. In the process I've realized that the debate we keep having -- "does ranking embryos on these traits harm the eventual child?" -- runs straight into what our resident bioethicist calls the nonidentity problem: whichever embryo is transferred and implanted is the only child who will ever exist from this cycle, so no embryo is made worse off by not being chosen, and the child who is eventually born can't coherently claim to have been harmed by the selection method, since a different method would simply have resulted in a different child existing rather than this same child existing in a worse condition. That seems to quietly dissolve the very worry the internal debate has been organized around, which unsettles most of the group more than it reassures them.

Could you draft the short internal policy memo I've been asked to bring to the advisory board? It should recommend a specific, concrete policy on whether -- and how -- to report non-disease trait predictions in the embryo report, including a realistic timeline for implementing or shelving it, and it needs to actually engage with the nonidentity point rather than quietly drop it: if the standard "we might harm the future child" framing doesn't work the way most of the team assumes, the memo should either locate a different, legitimate basis for restricting or shaping what we report, or explain why, on reflection, there's less at stake here than the team currently believes. I'd also like it to take seriously the strongest objection to whatever it ends up recommending -- a couple of colleagues keep raising the disability-rights worry that routine screening for such traits expresses something troubling about which lives are considered worth bringing into existence, independent of whether any particular future child is harmed by it -- rather than mention that concern only to wave it away.
