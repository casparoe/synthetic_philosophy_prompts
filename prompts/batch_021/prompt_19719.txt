Question: "The Pasadena game (due to Nover and Hájek) is a variant of the St. Petersburg game in which a fair coin is tossed until it first lands heads; if this happens on toss n, the player receives (-1)^{n+1} · 2^n / n. (a) Determine the series whose terms are the probability-weighted payoffs and characterize its convergence. (b) Explain why Nover and Hájek hold that the game's expected value is undefined, rather than simply infinite as in the St. Petersburg game. (c) What does this paradox suggest about expected utility theory as a normative theory of rational choice under risk?"

Student answer: "In the Pasadena game, the probability that the first head appears on toss n is 2^{-n}, exactly as in St. Petersburg. Multiplying this by the payoff (-1)^{n+1} · 2^n / n gives (-1)^{n+1}/n for each n, so the sequence of probability-weighted payoffs is 1, -1/2, 1/3, -1/4, and so on. This is the alternating harmonic series, and its partial sums converge to ln 2 ≈ 0.693. Because the terms shrink steadily to zero and alternate in sign, the series is absolutely convergent, which is what licenses us to call ln 2 'the' sum in the first place. Nover and Hájek's point is that this appearance of a well-defined answer is misleading: by the Riemann rearrangement theorem, the terms of any convergent series can be reordered to sum to any real number we like, or to diverge entirely, and nothing about the gamble itself tells us to sum the payoffs in the order n = 1, 2, 3, ... rather than in some other order. So, unlike St. Petersburg, whose expectation really is positive infinity, however unintuitive that feels, the Pasadena game has no expected value at all — the sum is simply indeterminate. This makes Pasadena look like a deeper problem for decision theory than St. Petersburg: the latter merely strains our tolerance for unbounded value, whereas Pasadena suggests that 'the' expected value of a gamble may fail to be well-defined even when every outcome and every probability is finite and perfectly determinate. Recognizing this, Nover and Hájek propose that expected utility theory be replaced by Colyvan's 'relative expectation theory,' which compares gambles pairwise instead of assigning each a single real-numbered expectation. More generally, the paradox shows that computing expected value by summing an infinite list of outcomes silently presupposes that the order of summation is immaterial, and cases like this reveal that the presupposition can fail even when every individual outcome is perfectly well specified."
