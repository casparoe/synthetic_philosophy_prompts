After a forty-year career as an experimental physicist, most of it spent working in statistical mechanics, I have always trusted a careful calculation over a bare intuition, and retirement has finally given me the leisure to test that habit against philosophy rather than particle counts. My daughter, who is midway through a philosophy doctorate, recently introduced me to the "torture versus dust specks" problem that circulates in rationalist and effective-altruism circles: is it worse for one person to be tortured for fifty years, or for some almost incomprehensibly large number of people—3^^^3, in the standard notation—each to receive a single, momentary speck of dust in the eye? To me the arithmetic seems inescapable: if a dust speck carries even an infinitesimally small but strictly positive amount of disutility, then multiplying that quantity across enough people must eventually swamp any finite amount of disutility, torture included, just as an arbitrarily small pressure, integrated over a sufficiently large area, yields an arbitrarily large total force. Refusing to pick the dust specks therefore strikes me as exactly the kind of "scope insensitivity" that behavioral economists have documented in willingness-to-pay studies, where respondents offer roughly the same sum to protect wildlife whether the number of animals at risk is in the thousands or in the hundreds of thousands.

Given how hard this seems to dispute on its own terms, I was surprised when my daughter told me that professional philosophers still treat the matter as genuinely open, rather than as a solved special case of expected-value reasoning, much as no serious physicist still debates whether energy is conserved in an isolated system. Is there a rigorous flaw in the aggregative argument that I am simply not seeing, comparable to some hidden, unstated boundary condition in a physics problem, or is this an area where the discipline is reluctant to accept an unwelcome conclusion that follows validly from premises it already accepts elsewhere? I would value a careful account of exactly where the disagreement actually lies, and whether my impulse to treat the matter as settled by arithmetic alone is itself the more interesting mistake.
