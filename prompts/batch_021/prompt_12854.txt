Spent my whole career in the lab assuming death was the one biological concept that didn't need philosophers messing with it - heart stops for good, done. Now that I've got time to read outside my field, turns out we actually run two pretty different tests for it: circulatory death (heart and lungs quit for good) and whole-brain death (or just brainstem death, if your in the UK), and the second one gets declared even while the heart's still pumping away on a vent. Fine, we need workable criteria - but then I read that some whole-brain-dead bodies keep doing recognizably organism-ish things for a long time afterward: healing wounds, fighting off infections, even carrying a pregnancy for months in a few documented cases. That sounds exactly like the kind of self-integration that loosing the brain was supposed to destroy. So is there really one coherent concept of "death" that the circulatory test and the whole-brain test are both aiming at, just measured two different ways, or have we got two unrelated, practically-motivated stopping points that happen to share a word? Skip the pamphlet answer - give me the actual conceptual argument underneath, including the strongest reply available on behalf of whole-brain death.
