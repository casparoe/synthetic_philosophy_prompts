I'm several drafts into a novel whose central figure is a literary translator who has built her reputation on rendering other writers' grief, rage, and longing into flawless English cadences while maintaining, quite sincerely, that she does not experience these states herself in anything like the ordinary sense — a condition her therapist has floated, not altogether satisfyingly, as a mild form of alexithymia. I keep returning to Meursault's flatness at his mother's funeral in Camus's *The Stranger* as one literary precedent for a character like this, but I would rather ground her condition in something more conceptually exact than authorial fiat, so I have been reading around in the philosophy of emotion literature: Solomon's and Nussbaum's insistence that an emotion is, at bottom, an evaluative judgment; James's older claim that it is essentially the feeling of a bodily change; and Prinz's attempt to split the difference with a perceptual theory that treats emotions as embodied appraisals.

What I would like from you is a single, carefully worked example of an emotional phenomenon that poses a genuine conceptual problem for at least one of these three ways of classifying emotions — ideally not the well-worn case of the phobic who is afraid of something she has already persuaded herself is harmless, since that is the example every introductory treatment reaches for first. Please lay out the case itself, explain why it counts as a real difficulty for the theory in question rather than merely a disagreement about how to use words, and then show how a sophisticated defender of that theory would try to absorb the counterexample, together with your own judgment about whether the accommodation costs the theory something substantive or lets it off cheaply. If you have a view on which of the three categories, if any, my translator's alleged emotional absence would actually fall under, I would be glad to hear it, though that's very much the secondary question here.
