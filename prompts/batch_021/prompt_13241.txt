The question, as assigned to her:

"Expressivists hold that a sentence like 'Cruelty is wrong' functions to express the speaker's attitude (for instance, disapproval) rather than to state a fact that is true or false. Peter Geach argued that this view runs into a serious difficulty once we consider an argument of the following form:

(1) If cruelty is wrong, then getting your little brother to be cruel is wrong.
(2) Cruelty is wrong.
(3) Therefore, getting your little brother to be cruel is wrong.

(a) Explain, in your own words, what this difficulty (the 'Frege–Geach problem') is, and why the argument above is what generates it.
(b) Briefly describe one strategy an expressivist might use in response to the problem."

Her draft answer:

"(a) Expressivists think that when I say 'cruelty is wrong,' I'm not describing the world; I'm venting a feeling of disapproval, something like 'Boo, cruelty!' Geach's point is that in premise (1), 'cruelty is wrong' sits inside the 'if' clause, where I'm not actually asserting it or expressing any disapproval — uttering the conditional doesn't boo cruelty. But for the argument to work as modus ponens, 'cruelty is wrong' has to mean exactly the same thing in (1) as it does in (2), or the argument would be trading on an ambiguity, a bit like an equivocation. Since on the expressivist story the only meaning a moral sentence has is whichever attitude it expresses, and it isn't expressing any attitude in (1), the expressivist hasn't yet explained how (1)–(3) can be modus ponens the way it would be for an ordinary factual claim. So, strictly speaking, the expressivist has to bite the bullet and admit that this bit of everyday reasoning isn't actually valid in the way we ordinarily assume it is.

(b) One response, going back to Ayer's original emotivism, is to say that moral sentences express not only first-order attitudes like disapproving of cruelty, but also higher-order attitudes about combinations of attitudes — so uttering the conditional in (1) expresses something like disapproval of the combination 'disapproving of cruelty, but not disapproving of getting your little brother to be cruel.' Holding (1) and (2) without accepting (3) would then count as holding an inconsistent set of attitudes, which gives the expressivist something to play the role that logical inconsistency plays for factual claims, even without any shared proposition running through the argument."
