This term I am taking a seminar on formal epistemology and rational choice, and for the last two weeks we have been working through the "Death in Damascus" puzzle that Gibbard and Harper folded into their 1978 paper on counterfactuals and expected utility, as a decision-theoretic descendant of the old fable about the Baghdad servant who flees to Samarra that Somerset Maugham made famous. In the version we were assigned, Death is a perfect predictor of where the man will be, so wherever he decides to go is exactly where Death was always going to turn up, which is what makes fleeing from Damascus to Aleppo look exactly as futile, or exactly as safe, as staying put, depending on how the expected utilities are set up.

What I have not been able to work out to my own satisfaction is why the case is usually treated as so much worse for evidential decision theory than for the causal kind. As far as I can tell, an evidentialist can never settle on a stable recommendation, since conditioning on the very fact that you have decided to flee to Aleppo makes Aleppo look like the deadly city, and conditioning on staying makes Damascus look deadly, so the theory seems to chase its own tail; a causal decision theorist, by contrast, is supposed to be able to rescue things with the notion of a "ratifiable" choice, or, failing that, by falling back on a fifty-fifty mixed strategy as the one option that does not undercut itself. But I also recall the professor mentioning, in passing, a further complication from the later literature (I want to say a paper by Andy Egan, though I would not swear to the attribution) in which adding a third option to the story turns Death in Damascus back into a counterexample to causal decision theory as well, so that neither camp gets to leave feeling entirely vindicated.

For a short presentation I am giving next week, I would like to bring this alive as a scene rather than as a table of payoffs. Could you write a dialogue, set in the marketplace in Damascus just after the man's encounter with Death, between a committed causal decision theorist and a committed evidential decision theorist, each trying to talk the man, and each other, into a particular course of action? I would like neither side to be a pushover: the evidentialist should have some real answer to the "chasing its own tail" objection rather than simply conceding it, and the causal decision theorist should have to reckon with the ratifiability wrinkle and the worry that the case can be turned against causal decision theory too once a third option is on the table. Ideally the two characters genuinely engage with each other's specific moves rather than merely restating their own position, and the man is still standing in the marketplace undecided at the end, since that is roughly where our seminar discussion left matters as well.
