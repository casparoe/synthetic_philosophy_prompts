I'm a software engineer, and for the last few months I've been trying to get a real handle on the "do LLMs actually understand anything, or are they just stochastic parrots" debate, mostly because it keeps coming up in arguments with coworkers who all seem very sure of themselves in opposite directions. That reading eventually took me to Searle's Chinese Room argument, and from there to a line in the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry on it noting that Searle's argument has an important antecedent: something called "Leibniz's Mill," from section 17 of Leibniz's Monadology, written in 1714. When I mentioned this at lunch, a coworker who'd minored in philosophy said, more or less, "everyone rediscovers the mill argument eventually," and pointed me to the primary text instead of letting me get away with a summary. Having now sat with it for a while, I find I can't quite pin down what's actually being argued -- and since I have zero formal philosophy training, I can't tell whether I'm missing something everyone else would find obvious. Here is the passage, in Robert Latta's 1898 translation, which seems to be the one most commonly quoted:

"Moreover, it must be confessed that perception and that which depends upon it are inexplicable on mechanical grounds, that is to say, by means of figures and motions. And supposing there were a machine, so constructed as to think, feel, and have perception, it might be conceived as increased in size, while keeping the same proportions, so that one might go into it as into a mill. That being so, we should, on examining its interior, find only parts which work one upon another, and never anything by which to explain a perception. Thus it is in a simple substance, and not in a compound or in a machine, that perception must be sought for. Further, nothing but this (namely, perceptions and their changes) can be found in a simple substance."

What I'd like help with is the actual structure of the inference, not just the gist. First, is Leibniz reporting something like a failure of imagination -- he looks inside the mill, can't picture how pushing parts would add up to a perception, and concludes it doesn't -- or is there a tighter, more principled claim buried in "parts which work one upon another" about why relations between parts could never, even in principle, constitute a perception rather than merely accompany one? Second, why does the enlarging-the-mill device matter: would the argument lose anything if Leibniz had instead shrunk us down to wander around inside an ordinary brain, or a modern GPU cluster? Third, his positive proposal -- that perception must belong to simple, partless substances -- is a pretty specific and unfashionable metaphysical commitment, so I'm trying to work out whether the negative half of the argument (mechanism alone can't explain perception) really stands on its own once you discard monads, or whether it quietly depends on machinery almost nobody signs up for anymore. If you can walk me through those three questions, I'd also be curious, at the end, whether you think the mill argument, if it survives at all, is just a more evocative dress rehearsal for the Chinese Room, or whether it's making a genuinely different claim -- one that would bite an LLM, which is after all just an enormous number of "parts working one upon another," in a way the Chinese Room doesn't quite capture.
