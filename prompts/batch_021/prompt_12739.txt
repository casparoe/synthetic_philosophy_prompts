I cover science and ideas for a general-interest outlet, and I keep encountering confident but incompatible claims about the Church-Turing thesis: that it is a proven theorem settling the limits of computation for good, that it is a mere convention with no empirical content, and that it is a falsifiable empirical claim some sufficiently exotic machine could overturn. Logicians and philosophers of computation genuinely disagree about which of these is right, partly because "effectively calculable procedure" was never given an independent mathematical definition against which Turing's and Church's formal proposals could be checked. There is a further complication: even if the classical thesis is secure, the "physical Church-Turing thesis" — that every physical process is Turing-computable — is a distinct and more clearly empirical claim, and it is this second thesis that is really in play when people invoke proposals like David Deutsch's, that a universal quantum computer could efficiently simulate any physical system whatsoever. Please lay out the most important implications of this unsettled status for how I should responsibly evaluate two kinds of claims that recur in science coverage: (1) that some physical or biological system — an analog device, a quantum computer, the human brain — computes something no Turing machine could, and (2) that a quantum algorithm's speedup on a problem like factoring shows classical computation has been "transcended" in some deeper sense. Be concrete about what would, and would not, count as evidence for each.
