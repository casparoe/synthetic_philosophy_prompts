I'm a PhD student in media studies working on theories of "the public" — Dewey, Tarde, Habermas, and their nineteenth-century antecedents — which is how I ended up guest-teaching the unit on Kierkegaard in the journalism school's required media-ethics capstone. That capstone spends its final two weeks on Kierkegaard's diagnosis of "the present age" in Two Ages: A Literary Review, and this year's graduating cohort — half in earnest, half as an in-joke from section — voted to make "what Kierkegaard would say to us" the theme of their small departmental convocation, then asked me to give the address. The room will be split evenly between graduates and their families, who know Kierkegaard only from that one unit, and journalism and philosophy faculty who assigned the reading and will notice if I soften it. Please write the address, about five minutes long. It has to take seriously Kierkegaard's actual charge that the press manufactures "the public" — an anonymous, leveling abstraction that lets people hold opinions and pass judgment without ever becoming a single individual answerable for them — and it can't resolve that charge with a reversal he would have rejected as false comfort, but it still has to send this particular group into newsrooms and editorial jobs with something to stand on, not a scolding.
