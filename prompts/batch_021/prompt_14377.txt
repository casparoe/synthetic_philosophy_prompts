Ludwig Boltzmann spent years defending his statistical version of the second law of thermodynamics against two objections: Josef Loschmidt's point that the underlying laws of mechanics are time-reversible, so no purely mechanical argument can favor entropy increase over decrease, and Ernst Zermelo's point that Poincaré recurrence guarantees any isolated system will eventually return arbitrarily close to its starting state, so entropy cannot keep rising forever. Boltzmann's answer, worked out most fully in his mid-1890s replies to Zermelo, was to treat entropy increase as merely overwhelmingly probable rather than strictly necessary. Pressed on why we should find ourselves in a universe so far from equilibrium in the first place, he went further and floated the idea that the cosmos as a whole spends nearly all of its existence in featureless equilibrium, with our entire observable region of low entropy being a rare, spontaneous statistical fluctuation — the only kind of place where a memory-forming observer could ever exist to notice its own surroundings.

I recently learned that this idea has a direct descendant in contemporary cosmology, one that happens to carry Boltzmann's own name. Physicists who take seriously a universe that settles forever into an eternal, de Sitter-like vacuum, as in many eternal inflation scenarios, point out that such a vacuum has a nonzero horizon temperature and will therefore go on fluctuating indefinitely — and that a fluctuation cheap enough to spontaneously assemble one conscious brain, complete with false memories of an orderly cosmic history, is vastly more probable than one elaborate enough to assemble a whole galaxy or civilization. Taken literally, this implies that a "typical" observer in such a cosmology should expect to be exactly this kind of short-lived, deluded "Boltzmann brain" — a conclusion most cosmologists treat as a serious problem for the model, not as a possibility to accept.

Suppose Boltzmann himself were confronted with this argument, as the natural extension of the very fluctuation hypothesis he had already floated against Zermelo. Would he recognize it as a bullet he was, in effect, already committed to biting, or does something in his actual position — his sense of what counts as a well-posed probability claim, the timescales he was and wasn't willing to reason about, his running disputes with contemporaries over the reality of atoms and probabilities — give him a principled way to resist the modern conclusion that his nineteenth-century critics never had available? Defend a specific verdict, anchoring it in what Boltzmann actually argued in the recurrence debate rather than in generic assumptions about "what a nineteenth-century physicist would say," and say whether you think the "measure problem" this raises for eternal inflation today is substantially the same difficulty Boltzmann was already wrestling with, or a genuinely new one.
