I am a philosophy PhD student working on formal decision theory and longtermist ethics. For a side project, I have been collecting short fiction that dramatizes a genuine technical idea rather than a well-worn trope, in the spirit of how Greg Egan's fiction follows out the actual consequences of a thesis about consciousness. The idea I would like you to try is evidential cooperation in large worlds (ECL), sometimes still called multiverse-wide superrationality: the claim that in a sufficiently large or infinite universe, agents who can never causally interact with one another—too distant, too separated in time, perhaps residing in different branches of a multiverse—can still have genuine reason to act cooperatively toward one another's very different values. The reasoning is evidential rather than causal: choosing to give weight to values unlike your own does not influence anyone else, but it is evidence that relevantly similar reasoners elsewhere are, by the same logic, simultaneously choosing to give weight to values like yours, so that your own compliance is the best evidence available to you that theirs occurs too. Please write a short story, around 800 words, in which a character or civilization reasons this way about some high-stakes decision, such that the resolution turns specifically on this correlation-rather-than-causation structure and on the genuine difficulty of knowing how correlated your reasoning really is with agents you will never observe, rather than on a more generic sentiment about cooperating with strangers.
