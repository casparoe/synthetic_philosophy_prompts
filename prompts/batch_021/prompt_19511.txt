I've been putting together my animal minds seminar for next week, and I keep running into the same issue with my students: they want a tidy yes/no on which animals are conscious, and I keep telling them the field has mostly given up on that framing. Ever since that New York Declaration on animal consciousness thing a couple years back said something like there being a "realistic possibility" of conscious experience across a huge swathe of taxa, and Birch's precaution book made the case for acting under uncertainty instead of waiting around for certainty, the interesting question isn't really binary anymore, its about how the evidence actually stacks up differently case by case. My students keep flattening this into "so is the bee conscious or not," and I want something more textured than that for next week's handout.

So here's what I'd like from you, partly as a favor and partly as a sanity check on my own intuitions before I write the thing: rank the following seven taxa by how strong you think the *current* evidence is for genuine valenced/phenomenal experience specifically, not mere nociception, not general behavioral complexity, but something that actually looks like there's something it's like to be that creature. The taxa are: octopus, bumblebee, jumping spider (the genus Portia is the usual poster child here), hermit crab, cleaner wrasse, the sea hare Aplysia, and C. elegans. For each one, give a short justification, one sentence is fine, that names the actual kind of evidence doing the work, things like motivational trade-offs, nociceptive wind-up plus analgesic preference, play absent any obvious reward, mirror self-recognition, associative versus merely non-associative learning, whatever's relevant, rather than just gesturing at neuron count or phylum membership.
