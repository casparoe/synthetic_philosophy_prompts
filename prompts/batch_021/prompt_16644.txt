I am a PhD student in computer science and currently serve on the five-member panel that awards our department's competitive summer research fellowship. Funding requires a proposal to be judged both (a) technically feasible within one summer and (b) genuinely original beyond the applicant's published work; failing either condition is grounds for rejection. Panelists rated every proposal yes or no on (a) and (b) separately, and then, independently, gave an overall yes-or-no recommendation. On this year's most contested proposal, a majority said yes on feasibility, and a different, only partly overlapping majority said yes on originality, so tallied criterion by criterion, the proposal clearly passes. Yet tallying our overall recommendations directly, a majority came out against funding it — even though each panelist's overall vote was itself perfectly consistent with that panelist's own (a) and (b) judgments. Some of us want to just go with whichever tally favors the applicant; others want to fix a standing rule for next year so this never happens again. Is one of these two tallies simply the correct way to read the panel's collective decision and the other a mistake, or is something more fundamental going on that a standing rule cannot cleanly fix?
