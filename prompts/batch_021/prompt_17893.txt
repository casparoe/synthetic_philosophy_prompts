I'm a PhD student in political theory, and this term our "ethics of war" reading group has been slowly working through the moral equality of combatants debate -- Walzer's version in Just and Unjust Wars against McMahan's revisionist attack in Killing in War. After last week's session, two people in the group, Nate and Farah, kept arguing about it well past the point where anyone else cared, and it turned into a proper argument at the pub. I want a neutral third party to go through it because by my second beer I'd lost track of who was winning which specific point (as opposed to who was just talking louder). Nate opened with the traditional line, basically Walzer's: whatever you think about whether a state was right to go to war in the first place, the rules about how individual soldiers may fight are supposed to apply the same way regardless of which side you're on -- sometimes called the independence thesis. His reasons were that ordinary soldiers aren't in a position to correctly judge the justice of their state's cause, so their permission to fight can't sensibly hinge on getting that judgment right, and that a war convention granting combatants symmetric rights is what makes surrender, POW protections, and eventually peace possible, since a side told it's murdering people either way has no reason to ever stop. Farah answered that this is precisely the independence thesis McMahan spends a good chunk of Killing in War attacking, and that it mixes up two seperate questions: what the laws of war should say for practical reasons (where symmetry might well be wise policy) and what's morally true about who's actually liable to be killed by whom. On McMahan's liability account that tracks moral responsibility for posing an unjustified threat, full stop -- you can think legal symmetry is the best policy while still denying it reflects the moral reality, and refusing to keep those apart is exactly how the traditional view dodges the question of whether unjust wars really license their own soldiers to kill.

Nate's comeback was two-pronged: first, that "your killing was wrong, but you're excused because you couldn't have known better" is a verdict that's never guided a single soldier, general, or war-crimes tribunal, so a theory of a practical domain that cashes out in nothing practical isn't doing its job; second, that the whole "unjustified threat" framework is borrowed from individual self-defense cases -- one attacker, one defender, a fairly clear fact about who's threatening whom -- and doesn't obviously survive being transplanted onto a mass, bureaucratic activity where a given supply clerk or conscripted rifleman is a causally negligible, totally replaceable part of the machine. Farah's reply was that excused-but-wrong isn't idle, since it still settles other things people care about -- whether a third party may intervene against the unjust side, or how compensation and historical judgment should go afterward -- being let off the hook for blame and having your act count as permitted are just different questions. And causal necessity was never the test for liability anyway: a lookout in a robbery, or one rifleman in a firing squad, is on the hook for their share of a jointly unjustified act even though nothing changes if you swap them out for someone else.

Nates last move granted that the excuse/justification distinction is coherent, but pushed further: maybe individual liability is the wrong unit of analysis altogether for something that exists only because of chains of command and conventions between states, and equal combatant status is better defended not as "both sides are equally in the moral right" but as a rule of a mutually beneficial practice both sides have reason to accept before anyone knows who's actually just -- like how the rules of a game grant permissions that don't track antecedent desert. Farah started to say something about conventions still needing moral grounding from somewhere, Nate said sure, but so does self-defense, and that's when we got dragged into another round instead of resolving anything. Could you go through this move by move and adjudicate it -- not just an overall winner, but who's got the better of each specific exchange, and whether either of them is quietly leaning on a premise that doesn't actually hold up?
