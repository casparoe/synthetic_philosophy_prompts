The question (worth 20 points) was:

"Dialetheism and paraconsistent logic are closely associated but are not the same thesis. (a) Define each precisely. (b) Explain the relationship between them, including whether either position depends on the other. (c) Using the Liar sentence, illustrate how a dialetheist's preferred logic must treat the inference from a contradiction to an arbitrary conclusion."

Here is the submitted answer:

"Dialetheism is the view that some contradictions are true: there are sentences A such that A and its negation ¬A are both true, rather than exactly one of the two being true. The paradigm case is the Liar sentence, 'This sentence is false,' which the dialetheist takes to be both true and false rather than neither, since assigning it a single classical truth value generates paradox in either direction.

Paraconsistent logic is a logic whose consequence relation is not explosive: unlike classical logic, where a contradiction entails every sentence whatsoever (ex contradictione quodlibet), a paraconsistent logic allows {A, ¬A} to be accepted without every B being derivable from them. Graham Priest developed paraconsistent logic specifically to give dialetheism a formal home, since without such a logic a dialetheist who accepted even one contradiction would be forced into trivialism, the absurd view that every sentence is true. His system LP (the Logic of Paradox) is a three-valued logic on which a sentence can be true, false, or both, with 'both' counted as a designated value alongside 'true.' On this semantics, if A is the Liar sentence itself, one can construct a valuation on which A and ¬A both take the value 'both' while an unrelated sentence B takes the value 'false'; so the inference from {A, ¬A} to B comes out invalid, exactly as the dialetheist needs in order to avoid trivialism.

Since dialetheism cannot be stated non-trivially without a paraconsistent logic to contain it, the two positions really stand or fall together: to adopt a paraconsistent logic is already to grant the dialetheist's central claim that a contradiction can be accepted without catastrophe, which is only a hair's breadth from granting that some contradictions are true."
