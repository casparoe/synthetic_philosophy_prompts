I spent my working life as a research chemist, and since retiring I've finally been working through the philosophy I never had time for; decision theory is my current preoccupation. At the small discussion group I attend, we recently spent a session on the "Death in Damascus" case, usually traced back to Gibbard and Harper: an agent in Damascus learns that Death -- an unfailingly accurate predictor -- has already fixed on a city, Damascus or Aleppo, in which to keep an appointment, the choice having been made by predicting what the agent will do; the agent must then decide whether to stay or flee to Aleppo, with no antecedent reason to think Death favored either city over the other. Two people in the group, Owen and Claire, kept arguing about it afterward over coffee, and since neither of them is a professional philosopher, I couldn't tell who was scoring real points and who was just being stubborn.

(1) Owen: "Once the predictor is infallible, the puzzle has no real content: you end up wherever Death is, so 'stay or flee' isn't a genuine choice, and all the talk of instability just mistakes a dramatic premise for a paradox."

(2) Claire: "That misses the actual issue, which is about deliberation, not fate. On evidential decision theory, whichever act you currently favor becomes evidence that Death predicted that very act, so it now looks like the worse option, which flips your favorite -- and then condemns the new one too. That flip-flopping, not the death itself, is why the case gets used to argue causal decision theory is the better theory: it holds Death's location fixed and independent of your own deliberation, so it never chases its own tail. And evidential decision theory has a reply of its own: Richard Jeffrey's requirement that a rational act be 'ratifiable' -- that it stay optimal once you factor in having chosen it -- singles out a fifty-fifty coin flip as the one policy that doesn't undermine itself."

(3) Owen: "Two problems with that. First, look at what causal decision theory actually delivers under a symmetric prior: flat indifference between the two acts. Calling that 'stable' rather than 'empty' is just relabeling a tie as an achievement. Second, the moment evidential decision theory is allowed to rank policies or coin flips instead of the two acts actually on offer in Damascus, it has quietly borrowed the causal theorist's trick of holding something fixed independently of what your own choice tells you -- that's a patch, not a vindication."

(4) Claire: "There's a real difference, though, between 'nothing makes one act better than the other' and 'every act undermines itself the moment you pick it': the first is a coherent, if modest, verdict, the second is a genuine defect no theory should have. And the coin flip isn't borrowed from anywhere -- tossing a coin is an act available to the agent from the very start, on a par with staying or fleeing, so evidential decision theory earns its stable answer rather than smuggling it in."

Could you go through this move by move -- (1) through (4) -- and say who has the better of it at each stage, rather than just declaring an overall winner? I'd also be curious whether you think Owen and Claire are, between them, missing something important about what this case was originally meant to establish.
