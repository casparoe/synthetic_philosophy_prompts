I cover emerging technology and the philosophical questions tangled up in it, and for the past month I have been reporting on how researchers in AI safety think about bargaining between sufficiently advanced, mutually transparent agents. One idea keeps resurfacing in my notes: the "commitment races problem," the worry that in games shaped like Chicken, whoever manages to lock in a credible commitment first forces the other side to adapt to it — which pushes agents to commit as early as possible, sometimes before they have reasoned carefully enough to know what they are committing to.

Next week I am recording a long-form interview with a decision theorist who works on this problem and is known for arguing that the standard proposed remedies — transparency, conditional or "safe" commitment devices, simply deliberating longer before acting — do not make the race disappear so much as relocate it to an earlier or higher level. When pressed on the darker implications of his own view, he tends to retreat to the line that "an unwise commitment still beats no commitment against an exploiter," which is true as far as it goes but lets him avoid the harder question of how an agent inside the race is supposed to know that its own commitment isn't the unwise one.

Could you draft eight to ten interview questions that go beyond having him restate the problem for a general audience? I would like at least one question on why the structure of Chicken-like games makes early commitment rational in the first place, and whether that logic really transfers to messier, less symmetric real-world bargaining; one on what "early" even means once the race is run in something closer to logical time than calendar time, and whether that notion is doing more work than it can bear; one on the counterintuitive claim that additional computation or more careful deliberation before committing could be a liability rather than an asset; and one that presses his "safe" or conditional commitment proposals to see whether they truly dissolve the race or merely displace it onto a prior race over who adopts the safety protocol first. For each of these, please add a follow-up question keyed to the answer he is most likely to give, including at least one spot where you turn his own "unwise commitment beats no commitment" line back against him.
