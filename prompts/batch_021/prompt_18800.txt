I have spent more than a decade reading LessWrong, Astral Codex Ten, and similar corners of the internet, which is presumably why my instinct in any hard decision is to reach for an expected-value calculation before anything else. That instinct is currently failing me, and I would like help figuring out whether it is failing for a good reason. Eighteen months ago I was injured in a car accident, and I am now deciding whether to accept a settlement or go to trial. My attorney puts the trial option at roughly a 60 percent chance of a $200,000 verdict and a 40 percent chance of leaving empty-handed, against a certain $85,000 on the table right now; the expected value of trial, about $120,000, is comfortably higher than the settlement. Nor do I think I can dissolve my hesitation via the usual move of diminishing marginal utility: I have a stable income and enough savings that neither sum would change my life in a way that should bend my utility curve much between $85,000 and $200,000. And yet the certain $85,000 keeps exerting a pull that feels stronger than "shape of my utility function" can explain.

What makes me suspect something more specific is going on is that the pull is not a generic dislike of uncertainty. If my attorney told me the odds were instead 90 percent for the award and 10 percent for nothing, I do not think I would feel anywhere near as much hesitation, even holding the expected-value comparison roughly fixed; it really does seem to be that 40 percent chance of the floor outcome, specifically, that bothers me, out of proportion to what its probability alone should justify. I recognize this as the kind of pattern that shows up under "probability weighting" in the behavioral-economics reading I've done, always as an example of a bias to be corrected rather than anything defensible. My attorney takes the same line: he says that since I would obviously take the higher-expected-value option if this exact bet came up a thousand times, I should take it this once, and that anything else just lets a cognitive quirk cost me thirty-five thousand dollars in expectation.

Before I defer to him, I would like a second opinion. Is there a serious, principled sense in which what I am feeling could be tracking something rational, rather than a bias to be trained away? And if there is, does it actually bear on what I should do in this genuine one-off decision, or is it just a story I could tell myself after the fact to feel better about taking the safe option?
