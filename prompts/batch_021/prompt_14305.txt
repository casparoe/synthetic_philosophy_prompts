I was recently invited onto the conservation advisory panel of a small trust that runs a seabird sanctuary on an offshore island, where I've spent the past two breeding seasons as a volunteer monitor watching invasive mice take a large share of the eggs and chicks each year. The sanctuary's staff have proposed an aerial operation with brodifacoum, the anticoagulant rodenticide behind most successful island eradications, timed for the coming winter, since anything short of complete removal risks the survivors rebounding once competition drops away. One panelist, a wildlife veterinarian, opposes this: brodifacoum kills over several days through internal hemorrhage, non-target scavengers risk secondary poisoning, and she argues that the mice's capacity to suffer doesn't stop mattering morally just because we've classified them as the pest rather than the protected species. She wants to postpone a season for a fertility-control or gene-drive method still in development that would be far less painful to the mice individually, even though it might never achieve full eradication and could leave the colony exposed to the same predation indefinitely if it falters partway. Please draft the panel's recommendation memo to the board -- proceed this winter, delay for the alternative, or some hybrid -- with a concrete decision timeline, engaging the welfare objection to the poison on its own terms rather than letting the urgency of protecting the colony automatically override it.
