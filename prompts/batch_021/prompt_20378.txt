My Lincoln-Douglas debate topic this month is on civil disobedience, and my research keeps running into a question the sample cases never address head-on: whether ordinary citizens have any general obligation to obey the law in the first place. Write a dialogue set in a holding cell just after a climate activist — a committed Rawlsian — has been arrested for nonviolently blockading a highway and has chosen to stay and accept the charges rather than flee. On her view, citizens of a "nearly just" society are bound by a natural duty of justice to support and comply with their institutions, which is why she thinks civil disobedience is permissible only within strict limits: public, nonviolent, and undertaken with a willingness to accept legal punishment. The friend who comes to bail her out is a philosophical anarchist in the tradition of A. John Simmons: he thinks none of the usual grounds — consent, gratitude, fair play, or natural duty — actually establish that ordinary citizens owe obedience to their own state, and can't see why she stuck around to be arrested at all. Have him press her on whether a natural duty owed impartially to just institutions wherever they exist can really explain an obligation to this particular government rather than to any other reasonably just one (Simmons's "particularity" objection), and have her press back on whether his own position, followed through, leaves him with no principled reason to prefer civil disobedience over simple sabotage. I want genuine back-and-forth — each of them should engage the other's strongest point rather than just restate their own, and neither should come off as the obvious loser.
