I cover science and ideas for a living, and lately I've gone down a rabbit hole that started as a totally different story -- about how the IPCC and various AI-risk forecasters hedge their probability estimates. Turns out there's a whole corner of philosophy about "imprecise probabilities": instead of pinning your credence in something to one number (like "I'm 70% confident"), you represent it as a range, or a whole set of probability functions, on the grounds that forcing a single number onto genuine ignorance is basically fake precision. The classic hook is Ellsberg's urn: 90 balls, 30 known red, 60 in an unknown mix of black and yellow. Offered a bet on red vs. black, most people take red; offered "red-or-yellow" vs. "black-or-yellow," most people switch and take black-or-yellow. That pair of preferences can't be rationalized by any single probability assigned to the three colors plus ordinary expected-utility reasoning -- it's basically a proof that people treat known risk and open-ended Knightian uncertainty as different animals, not just two flavors of "50-50."

But then I read a couple of papers that go after imprecise credences pretty hard once you bring decisions and time into it. One argument (Adam Elga's, I believe) says that if you're licensed to act on an imprecise credence whenever some probability function in your "set" would justify the act, you can be walked through a sequence of bets that each look individually fine and yet, taken together, are guaranteed to loose no matter which way the world turns out -- basically a dressed-up money pump. There's also a phenomenon people call "dilation," where updating your imprecise probabilities on some new, seemingly unrelated evidence makes your credence about a completely different matter LESS precise than before, not more -- which seems to violate the basic idea that learning stuff should sharpen your beliefs, not blur them. Imprecise-probability defenders bite these bullets anyway and say the alternative -- fake sharp numbers dressed up as rigor -- is worse.

I brought this up with a couple of philosopher friends over drinks and it turned into a whole argument that went nowhere, so now I want a referee. Write me an actual essay, not a "here are both sides" shrug: lay out the strongest case for genuinely imprecise rational credence, lay out the strongest version of the dynamic-consistency/dilation objections against it, and then tell me who wins and why. You're allowed a hedged-sounding verdict as long as its a real verdict with a rule attached -- something like "imprecise credences are the right model of evidence, but you need an extra tie-breaking norm the moment you actually have to act" would count, so would "no, credences have to be sharp, full stop." Just don't leave me at "reasonable people disagree."
