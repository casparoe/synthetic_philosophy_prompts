I'm a PhD student in philosophy specializing in mathematical logic and the foundations of mathematics, and a disagreement that surfaced in our foundations reading group after we worked through Gentzen's consistency proof for Peano Arithmetic has been nagging at me ever since. One faction in the group treats the consistency of PA, and even of ZFC, as close enough to settled that dwelling on it seems like an affectation; another faction, including one of the more senior people in the room, thinks that confidence is far less earned than it feels, and that we have mainly just gotten used to not asking the question. Rather than rehash the argument badly myself at next week's meeting, I'd like to see both positions made as strong as they can be.

Could you write a dialogue between two characters of equal technical sophistication: one who holds that we are entitled to near-certainty that our basic axioms are consistent, and one who thinks the live probability of a hidden inconsistency deserves to be taken far more seriously than working mathematicians usually take it? Neither should be allowed to simply assert their conclusion. The confident character should have to spell out exactly what Gödel's second incompleteness theorem does and does not rule out, and should address whether Gentzen's proof, which works by transfinite induction up to ε₀, actually buys any assurance that isn't itself hostage to accepting some further unproven system. The doubting character should go beyond a vague appeal to "we could be wrong": they should draw on at least two strands of the historical record -- Frege's Grundgesetze, Kunen's proof that Reinhardt's embeddings of V into V are outright inconsistent with choice rather than merely very strong, and Edward Nelson's own later-retracted attempt to show arithmetic inconsistent -- to argue that the risk cannot be safely confined to some remote corner of the large-cardinal hierarchy that ordinary mathematics never visits. I want real back-and-forth, where each responds to the other's specific move rather than restating their own position, and I'd like the dialogue to end at whatever point, if any, the two can agree on what is actually at stake -- for mathematics as it's practiced day to day -- if such a proof were ever found.
