For a research paper in my economics elective, I've been reading about early debates over what actually gives money its value, and I keep running into a striking disagreement between John Locke and George Berkeley. Locke, writing during the English recoinage controversy of the 1690s, argues that a coin's value is inseparable from its silver content, so that clipping or debasing the currency doesn't just relabel money but genuinely destroys value. Berkeley, by contrast, poses a series of skeptical queries in The Querist suggesting that money functions more like a ticket or counter for circulating credit and "industry" among a people, so that its worth rests on collective trust and convention rather than on any metal backing. Could you compare these two positions in depth, laying out the differing assumptions each makes about value, convention, and trust? Given how fiat currencies and digital payment systems work today, whose view do you think anticipates the modern monetary system more accurately, and why?
