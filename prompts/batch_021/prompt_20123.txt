Over the winter holidays, my sister and I sat down to divide our late father's estate, and the negotiation has raised a puzzle that feels like it should have a clean answer, even though neither of us can find one. The estate consists mainly of two things that cannot sensibly be split in half — his hardware store and a small lake cabin — along with a modest amount of cash. If we simply fail to agree, the will's fallback instruction is to sell everything and divide the proceeds evenly, which by our accountant's estimate would leave each of us with about $550,000. Neither of us wants that outcome: I would much rather keep the cabin and use some of the remaining cash to repair its roof and dock, which, counting sentiment alongside money, is clearly the best outcome I could hope for.

My sister, who has run two small businesses and has concrete plans for the store, stands to do considerably better if things go her way: by her own account, keeping the store together with enough capital to expand it is worth substantially more to her than my best case is worth to me, sentiment aside. A mediator we consulted proposed that, rather than dividing the dollars evenly, we find the single largest percentage such that if I received that percentage of the distance from the fallback sale to my own best outcome, and my sister received that same percentage of the distance from the fallback to her best outcome, the two resulting claims together would still not exceed what the estate actually contains.

My sister is unconvinced. She sees no obvious reason why matching percentages, rather than matching dollar amounts or splitting things some other way entirely, should count as fair, and she half-suspects the mediator has simply dressed up an arbitrary compromise in official-sounding language. I find I cannot produce a decisive reply to her, which bothers me more than I would like to admit, since I teach practical philosophy for a living and this feels like exactly the kind of question I ought to be able to answer. Over dinner I put the puzzle to two colleagues, one of whom thought the mediator's rule was self-evidently the fair one and the other of whom thought it was plainly indefensible, so I am no closer to a settled view. I would welcome a more considered verdict: is there a genuine, principled basis for preferring the mediator's percentage-matching rule to the alternatives my sister has in mind, or is her suspicion justified? And, taking your answer seriously, what would you actually advise the two of us to do?
