This semester I am taking a seminar on twentieth-century French political thought, and last week's session was devoted entirely to Simone Weil's late essay on political parties -- the "Note sur la suppression générale des partis politiques," usually translated as "On the Abolition of All Political Parties" -- which she wrote in London in 1943, in the final months of her life, while drafting memoranda for the Free French on what institutions a liberated France should adopt. What struck several of us in the seminar is how little her argument depends on a party's size or degree of formal organization: she insists that even a very small grouping already exhibits the same pathology in miniature, since on her account a party is defined by three features -- it is a machine for manufacturing collective passion, a machine for exerting collective pressure on the private judgment of its own members, and an organism whose real, if usually unstated, purpose is its own unlimited growth, quite apart from whatever good or harm it happens to do. On this basis she does not propose reforming the party system but abolishing it outright, on the grounds that party loyalty structurally displaces a person's attention to truth and justice with something that fits the charge she makes elsewhere against collective loyalties in general: that they function as a kind of idolatry.

We discussed the essay only against the backdrop she actually had in mind -- the fragmented, discredited parliamentary parties of the collapsing Third Republic -- so I have been wondering how she would size up a landscape she never lived to see. Political engagement today is organized at least as much through algorithmically curated online tribes as through formal party membership, and several prominent populist movements explicitly market themselves as "beyond party" or "neither left nor right" while still generating, at a speed and scale no 1940s party apparatus could approach, exactly the sort of tribal, emotionally charged pressure on individual judgment that worried her. Arguing strictly from her own three-part criterion rather than from surface analogy, would Weil most plausibly treat a leaderless, algorithm-driven political movement as an even purer specimen of the disease she diagnosed -- a party in her structural sense, minus the card-carrying membership -- or would the absence of formal candidate lists, party discipline, and direct institutional power-seeking make it, on her premises, a genuinely different problem? I would also be curious whether her logic gives her any grounds to endorse contemporary proposals for sortition-based citizens' assemblies as a way of reaching collective decisions without party mediation, or whether she would suspect that any standing deliberative body, however its members are chosen, eventually reproduces the same three features. I would like your best-argued reconstruction of her likely position, developed from her actual premises rather than simply asserted.
