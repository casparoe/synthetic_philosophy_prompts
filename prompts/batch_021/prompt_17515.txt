One of my friends, who's finishing up a philosophy PhD, sent me a podcast episode on Plato's Meno after we got into a dinner argument about whether it actually matters if you "really" know something, as long as you can act on it correctly. The episode covered a puzzle that epistemologists apparently still debate under the heading of "the value problem": why is knowledge supposed to be worth more than mere true belief? Plato's own example contrasts a man who knows the road to Larissa with one who has only heard a rumor about which road to take, since both would get you there just as reliably. What stuck with me is that the host said this creates a real headache for simple reliabilist theories of knowledge: if all that ultimately matters, practically speaking, is ending up with a true belief, then the reliability of the process that produced it seems to add nothing further once that true belief is already in hand. I believe it was Zagzebski (though I might be misremembering) who illustrated this "swamping problem" with an analogy about a coffee machine -- once you're holding a genuinely good cup of coffee, you don't care how reliable the machine that made it is in general.

This got me thinking about structurally similar cases from my own work as a software engineer: a flaky test that happens to pass on a given run, a machine-learning classifier that outputs the correct label without anything you'd want to call "understanding" of the input, a cached value that's technically stale but coincidentally still matches the current state of the world, or a junior developer who fixes a bug by copy-pasting a Stack Overflow answer that happens to apply to the case at hand. Some of these feel like obvious cases of true belief without knowledge, yet the swamping argument above seems to suggest that nothing beyond the correct output should matter once you already have it.

Could you construct a new example along these lines -- not the road to Larissa, and not the coffee machine -- that (a) makes vivid why we still want to insist that knowledge is worth more than mere true belief, and (b) specifically resists being "swamped away" in the manner described above, i.e., a case where some further ingredient still seems valuable even once the correct belief or output is already in hand? I'd also be curious whether, on your example, a virtue-epistemology-style response (which locates knowledge's extra value in the reliability of the knower's cognitive character rather than in the token process that produced this particular belief) or a safety- or anti-luck-based response fares better, and why.
