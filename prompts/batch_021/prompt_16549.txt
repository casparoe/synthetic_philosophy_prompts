So a few of us were at the bar after an AI interpretability workshop a few weeks back, a couple beers in, and I ended up half-refereeing an argument between two friends that's been rattling around in my head since. Renata does mechanistic interpretability at a lab and has clearly been on a Dennett kick lately; Owen is finishing a philosophy PhD on theories of mental content and was having none of it. The spark was a talk earlier that day about probing a model's internal activations for a "truth direction" — some linear-ish signal that predicts whether the model will get a factual question right, even on topics that weren't in the probe's training set.

Renata's opening move was that this is basically just what belief is, in Dennett's sense: you adopt the intentional stance, treat the system as an agent that believes true things and wants to say them, and if that assumption is what actually lets you predict its outputs better than staring at the weights ever could, there's no further fact about "real" belief left over to demand. The truth-direction finding, she said, is just evidence the stance is earning it's keep. Owen shot back that she was only using half of Dennett — the intentional stance is explicitly holistic, he brought up Quine and Davidson here, you interpret a whole belief-desire system at once under an assumption of overall rationality, and a lone probe lighting up on true statements doesn't show anything is embedded in the web of inference and contradiction-checking that makes an attribution a belief attribution rather than just a correlation. Renata's comeback was that this is now an empirical bet, not a conceptual objection: models increasingly do chain inferences, flag their own contradictions, and update related answers when corrected, and Dennett himself was happy to grant a thermostat a maximally thin "belief," so coherence obviously comes in degrees and Owen was just asking for more data, not identifying a principled line. Owen's last word was that even granting the empirical point, belief-talk isn't free-floating even on Dennett's own scheme — a thermostat earns even its minimal belief because there's a design story explaining why its state tracks temperature at all, and the design story for a language model is "predict the next token of internet text," not truth, so a direction that predicts true/false judgments might just be tracking what confident, widely-agreed-on text looks like, and calling that a belief about the world smuggles in more than the actual design story supports.

Neither of them budged, and honestly both sounded like they knew their stuff, which is exactly why I cant tell who's right. Could you go through the exchange move by move and say who's got the better of it at each point, rather than just handing out an overall winner? And if there's a consideration neither of them raised that actually matters for whether a language model can have beliefs in anything like Dennett's sense, I'd genuinely like to hear it — I'm noodling on a piece around this and want to get the philosophy right, not just quote two smart people talking past each other.
