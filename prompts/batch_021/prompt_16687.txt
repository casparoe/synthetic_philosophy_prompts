So I've been chewing on this on-and-off for months and want a genuinely rigorous take, not just a survey of "some people think X, some people think Y." There's a whole subfield in ethics on "moral testimony" — basically, is it epistemically fine to just believe a moral claim purely because someone told you it's true, the same way you'd believe your doctor about a diagnosis or a physicist about some quantum result you can't verify yourself? Most philosophers who've written on this lean "pessimist." Alison Hills has this argument (from her original piece on this, I forget the exact title) that even if you defer to a friend and end up with a true, justified belief that eating meat is wrong, and even act on it, you're still missing "moral understanding" — basically the ability to see why it's wrong and trace the reasons yourself — and that without that, your action doesn't have full moral worth even though the belief itself checks out. Sarah McGrath pushes back pretty hard on this and points out that we obviously rely on pure moral deference all the time (kids learning from parents, deferring to someone with way more relevant life experience than you), and that a lot of the pessimist arguments, if applied consistantly, would also condemn totally uncontroversial cases of ordinary empirical deference.

Anyway, I keep re-litigating this with a friend from my old LessWrong meetup, because the instinctive rationalist take seems to be pretty optimistic about deference in general as long as the source is reliable and well-calibrated — think of all the Aumann's agreement theorem-flavored arguments about why you shouldn't just agree to disagree with an honest, competent peer, or the stuff in *Inadequate Equilibria* about when "modesty" toward other people's judgment is actually warranted vs. just a cop-out. So it seems weird that so much of the ethics literature treats moral testimony as uniquely suspect compared to other kinds. What I actually want from you: make the strongest case you can that moral testimony pessimism is mistaken, i.e., that pure deference to a trustworthy moral testifier, even with zero independent grasp of the underlying reasons, can transmit real moral knowledge just as well as ordinary empirical testimony does. Engage directly with Hills's "moral understanding" argument and at least one other standard pessimist argument (say, the autonomy-based one, or the claim that a moral belief only "counts" if it's held for the right kind of reasons), rather than just asserting deference is fine — and be honest about which parts of the pessimist case still survive even if you're right that the no-knowledge-transfer claim doesn't hold up.
