In my AP Microeconomics class, we've been debating 'nudge' policies, such as automatically enrolling workers in retirement plans or taxing sugary drinks. Out of curiosity, I read further into the theory of consumer choice and learned that, under revealed preference theory, economists infer what someone truly prefers directly from what they actually choose, without needing to consult their psychology at all. On this view, a person's 'utility' is by definition whatever their choices maximize. That seems to settle the nudge debate immediately: if utility just is whatever a person's free choices maximize, then any policy designed to steer someone away from their own choices must, by definition, leave them worse off. So how can economists who accept revealed preference theory also defend nudges as a way of improving people's welfare, rather than rejecting one of these two ideas?
