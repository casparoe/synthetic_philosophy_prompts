I've never had any formal education in philosophy, but for the last couple of years I've been slowly working through classic texts on my own, mostly secondhand paperbacks bought cheaply and read with a notebook beside me. Recently I reached Aristotle's short treatise "On Interpretation," and chapter 9 — the one everybody who writes about "future contingents" seems to mention before hurrying on to talk about Newcomb's problem or quantum mechanics — brought me to a complete stop. Once I sat with the actual argument instead of the two-sentence summaries you find online, I realised I didn't really understand it at all.

The chapter opens by building what looks like an airtight argument for fatalism: if every statement about the future, like every statement about the past, has to be already either true or false, then whatever is going to happen was already "going to happen" at any earlier time you care to pick, so nothing could have gone any other way, so nothing is really left to chance or to deliberation, and everything happens of necessity. Aristotle clearly thinks that conclusion is intolerable, and this is where he brings in the famous example, which in the translation I have reads: "a sea-fight must either take place to-morrow or not, but it is not necessary that it should take place to-morrow, nor that it should not take place, yet it is necessary that it either should or should not take place to-morrow." A little further on he says that of the two opposed statements "one or the other must be true, and the other false — not, however, this one or that one, but whichever it may chance to be," and he explicitly contrasts this with statements about the present or the past, where whichever of the pair is true is already fixed and settled.

What I can't work out, and what none of the summaries I've found actually explain, is why that middle move is supposed to rescue anything. Saying "it's necessary that one or the other will happen, but not necessary that this one will happen, and not necessary that the other one will happen" feels to me like it might just be repeating the original puzzle in more careful language rather than solving it — after all, whatever is going to happen with the sea-fight is going to happen one particular way, so isn't the outcome already settled regardless of what we're willing to call "necessary"? A friend of mine who studied classics tells me that this exact question has divided scholars for a long time: some think Aristotle is quietly denying that statements about particular future contingents are true or false yet at all, while others think he lets them be true or false but denies that this fixes anything necessarily, and that it's genuinely disputed which of those readings, if either, actually stops the fatalist argument from going through. Could you walk me through the logic of the passage itself, lay out as plainly as you can what is really at stake between those two readings, and then tell me which one (if either) you think actually blocks the fatalist conclusion, rather than just quietly moving it somewhere we can no longer see it?
