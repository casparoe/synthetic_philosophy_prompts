Had a long argument with my friend (he's doing a CS PhD) last night about "program equilibrium" - that game theory idea where instead of just picking a strategy, each player submits an actual program that gets to inspect the other players program before acting, which apparently lets you get real cooperation in stuff like the prisoners dilemma as a genuine equilibrium, not just through repeated play or an outside enforcer. We couldn't agree on whether thats a real insight into rational cooperation or basically cheating - smuggling in the interesting part (mutual transparency) as an assumption instead of deriving it. Anyway it got me thinking about von Neumann, since he's basically responsible for both halves of the groundwork here: he co-founded the game theory all this sits on top of, and separately, the whole idea of a program being data that another program can read and act on is literally named after him (von Neumann architecture) - he even designed a theoretical self-reproducing machine whose whole trick is reading a description of itself off a tape and using it both as instructions and as something to copy. So what's your honest best guess at how he'd have reacted to program equilibrium? Would the automata side of him find it natural, or would the game theorist in him say it just redefines the original problem instead of solving it? Try to actually reconstruct his reasoning rather than just hedging and saying we can't know.
