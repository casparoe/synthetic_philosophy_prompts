A friend and I got into a long argument over dinner last night that I still haven't been able to shake, and I'd like a neutral opinion on who actually had the better of it. I have a fairly unpleasant dental surgery scheduled for next Tuesday (both lower wisdom teeth, local anaesthetic only), and for the past week I've been dreading it far more than seems reasonable — it's the first thing I think about most mornings, and I've caught myself mentally rehearsing it just to "get it over with" in advance. My friend pointed out that I went through an almost identical procedure about two years ago: same surgeon's difficulty rating, same recovery time, and by my own account just as painful at the time. Yet I almost never think about that one, and when I do, I feel nothing like dread — if anything, a faint sense of accomplishment. She argued that this difference has no rational basis: a given amount of pain doesn't get smaller or larger depending on which side of "now" it falls on, so treating it that way is exactly as arbitrary as being more upset about stubbing your toe a mile from your house than ten miles from it. In her view, the only intellectually consistent options were to dread the memory of the old surgery just as much as the coming one, or to stop dreading the coming one as much as I now dread it, and since the first is psychologically impossible for anyone, the second is what a rational person ought to aim for.

I pushed back at the time, but everything I offered in response — "it hasn't happened yet," "something could still go wrong," "the old one is over and done with" — struck me, on reflection, as just restating the very asymmetry she was asking me to justify, rather than actually justifying it. So I'd like to know: was she right that this is simply an arbitrary bias with no real defense, or is there a genuine, principled reason to treat future suffering differently from past suffering of the same magnitude?
