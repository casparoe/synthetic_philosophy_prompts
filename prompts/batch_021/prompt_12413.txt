I am a junior double-majoring in philosophy and economics, and I sit on the editorial board of my university's undergraduate research journal, where four of us referee each submission independently before meeting to decide whether to accept, revise, or reject it. This term's seminar on social epistemology has made me notice something about how those meetings run: our editor-in-chief has everyone announce a verdict aloud in the same fixed order every time, most senior members first, before any discussion begins, reasoning that this helps newer members calibrate their judgment. Over the last two issues, I have noticed that once the first two readers agree, the other two almost always match them, even on papers where, as I later learned privately, one of the last two had flagged serious problems that the first two never raised. Half the board reads this pattern as proof that the senior readers simply judge better; the other half, myself included, worries that we are no longer combining four independent reads into one verdict, just amplifying whoever spoke first. We are rewriting our procedures before the next submission cycle, and each member owes a short recommendation memo. Please draft mine: it should propose a specific procedure for eliciting and sequencing verdicts that the editor-in-chief could actually act on, and it should take the case for open sequential discussion seriously rather than simply asserting that the practice produces herding.
