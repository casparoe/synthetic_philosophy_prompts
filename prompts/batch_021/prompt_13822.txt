I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and next week I'm interviewing a logician who has spent her career on the Löbian obstacle to self-trusting agents -- the consequence of Löb's theorem that a system reasoning by explicit proof can never fully vouch for its own future conclusions, or even for an exact copy of itself, without risking inconsistency. Whenever I raise cases that don't obviously involve explicit proof -- a person trusting their own future judgment, a neural network with no built-in theorem prover -- she deflects with the same line: 'if the trust is real, the proof is in there; we just haven't found the encoding yet.' Draft seven interview questions that make that line progressively harder to sustain, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer you expect her to give. Cover why Löb's theorem strengthens Gödel's second incompleteness theorem rather than merely restating it, whether letting an agent trust only a strictly weaker copy of itself -- the move used in the tiling-agents literature -- counts as a genuine fix or just a finite postponement, and whether trading proof for the probabilistic self-trust used in reflective oracles or logical induction dissolves the obstacle or merely relocates it. End with one question asking her to name a reasoner, human or machine, that the obstacle doesn't bind.
