Ok so I'm working on a longer feature about this trend in physics-adjacent philosophy where people argue relations are more fundamental than the things they relate — the whole "structure all the way down" picture that gets invoked for stuff like quantum entanglement or spacetime geometry. I ran it by an old-school metaphysician friend of mine and she said the whole program is quietly dodging a problem that's over a hundred years old and was never really put to rest: Bradley's regress.

Her quick version: if you say "a is related by R to b," something has to actually bind R to a and b into one fact, instead of a, R and b just sitting there as three separate things nobody's connected. But whatever does that binding is itself a further relation, which then needs its own binding to a, R and b, and so on forever. Bradley thought the regress was genuine and used it as one more brick in his case that relations — and honestly most of what common sense believes in — are mere "appearance" rather than fully real. Russell tried to block the regress in the Principles of Mathematics by distinguishing a relation that's actually doing its relating job in a given proposition from that same relation just sitting there named as an abstract object, but my friend thinks this move basically just re-asserts that relations unify their terms without explaining how, which was Bradley's whole complaint to begin with. Then there's the more modern response, something like Armstrong's states-of-affairs move, where you just say the unified fact is metaphysically basic and stop the regress by fiat — which is either the obviously correct move or a bit of a cheat depending who you ask.

What I don't want is another one of those "some philosophers think X, others think Y" summaries, I've already got three of those from various encylopedia entries. Can you write me an essay that actually stakes out a position: lay out why the regress looked so devastating to Bradley, give the strongest form of at least one serious attempt to stop it, and then tell me — and defend — whether the regress is genuinely vicious or ultimately benign, and what that verdict implies for whether a proposition (or a fact) can ever really have a single "unity" rather than just being a list of parts sitting next to each other. Write it for a smart reader who's never heard of Bradley in their life, but don't flatten the actual argument to get there.
