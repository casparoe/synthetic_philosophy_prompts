I'm a novelist drafting a book about a country rewriting its constitution after a civil war, and my research into real "eternity clauses" turned a dinner party into an argument two of my guests still haven't dropped. The spark was Germany's Basic Law (Grundgesetz), Article 79(3), which bars any amendment -- even one passed unanimously by parliament, the states, and referendum -- from touching human dignity or the democratic and federal order, a rule written precisely because Weimar's constitution had been legally amended into a dictatorship by a two-thirds vote in 1933.

1. Owen called this the cleanest possible Ulysses contract: having sailed toward the Sirens once, the nation lashes its future self to the mast so that no majority can ever vote democracy away again.
2. Mireille said the analogy flatters the framers: Odysseus bound only himself, briefly, knowing exactly what he faced; a constitution binds the unborn, against dangers nobody can fully specify, forever -- Jefferson's "the earth belongs to the living," in Homeric dress.
3. Owen answered that every constitution already does this -- supermajority rules, judicial review, bicameralism all bind the present for a future it can't consult -- so her objection would condemn constitutionalism as such, not just this clause.
4. Mireille replied that raising the price of change and abolishing the exit are different acts: a two-thirds rule still leaves a door future majorities can eventually open, while an eternity clause tells them in advance that on this question they will never be a majority that counts.
5. Owen pointed to Article 146, which lets the German people adopt an entirely new constitution outright: the eternity clause only closes the amendment route, not the constituent one, and a people re-founding itself is a different kind of act from a parliament re-legislating.
6. Mireille said that concedes rather than answers the point: if the constituent route is realistically available, the clause adds nothing beyond ordinary entrenchment; if it isn't, today's Germans are exactly the dead hand's hostages Jefferson warned about.

Neither would concede. Could you go through the exchange move by move rather than just naming an overall winner: say who has the better of each specific point and where each is trading on something shaky, note what larger consideration about precommitment both of them seem to be missing, and close with one sentence giving your own verdict on whether a clause like this is, on balance, a legitimate Ulysses contract or an illegitimate imposition on the living?
