So this happened in my philosophy of mind seminar last week and I'm still chewing on it. We'd just finished going through the classic Moore's paradox material -- the "it's raining, but I don't believe it" sentence, why it's absurd to assert even though it could easily be true, and the extra wrinkle that the weirdness is specifically a first-person, present-tense thing (there's nothing odd about "he believes it's raining and it isn't" or "I believed it was raining but it wasn't"). I mentioned in passing that Wittgenstein was apparently so taken with this discovery of Moore's that he called it Moore's single best contribution to philosophy, which the class found funny given how throwaway the original example sounds. Anyway, discussion was basically over when one student, Owen, brought up a real case: a friend of his, right after a bad breakup, kept saying stuff like "I know he's never coming back, but I don't believe it" -- totally sincerely, more than once, over several weeks. Owen and another student, Lena, ended up arguing about it in the hallway for a good twenty minutes after everyone else left, and I just kind of stood there taking notes like a creep instead of going home.

Owen's position: this is an actual case of holding a Moore-paradoxical belief, not just being tempted into a weird assertion. His friend genuinely believes, on some level, that the guy's gone -- she's acting on it, deleted his number, told her family it's over -- while also genuinely disbelieving it, since she's still checking his socials and telling Owen she "just knows" they'll get back together. So belief, in this case, seems to be split in exactly the way the classic puzzle says a rational believer can't be split. Lena's comeback: "believe" isn't doing the same job on both sides of that sentence. The first half reports an evidential judgment; the second half expresses something more like a refusal to emotionally accept the judgment, not a flat denial of the very same proposition -- so there's no single belief that's simultaneously present and absent, and the paradox just evaporates once you disambiguate. Owen shot back that this move is too convenient: if "believe" gets redefined every time it produces an inconvenient case, the word stops doing any real work, and anyway ordinary speech treats this as a real belief conflict -- we say things like "deep down, she still believes he's coming back," not "deep down, she has some other mental state entirely." Lena's last word was that the "deep down" phrasing actually proves her point, not his: we only reach for that kind of qualifier because we already sense a difference in kind between the two states (something more reflective and endorsed versus something more automatic and gut-level), and if the two states were just ordinary garden-variety beliefs clashing, "deep down" wouldn't be doing any work either.

Could you go through their exchange move by move and say who's got the better of it at each point, not just an overall winner? And seperately -- do you think Owen's friend is a genuine case of contradictory belief, or is Lena basically right that it dissolves once you disambiguate "believe"? I'd also be curious whether you think there's something both of them are missing, maybe about what the original puzzle (for Moore, and then for Wittgenstein) was actually a puzzle about in the first place, or about how all this connects to self-deception more generally.
