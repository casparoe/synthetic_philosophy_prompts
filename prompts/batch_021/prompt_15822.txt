I've been working through Leibniz's Monadology on my own, and the argument that has really stuck with me is the one in section 17: he imagines a machine built to think, enlarged until one could walk through it like a mill, and observes that however carefully you inspected the interior you would only find parts pushing against other parts, never anything you could call a perception. From this he concludes that perception must be sought in a genuine simple substance, not in any composite or machine. Elsewhere he draws a further distinction that seems relevant here: natural organisms are true unities organized around a single dominant monad, whereas machines built by human art are always mere aggregates with no real unity of their own.

Given that framework, how do you think Leibniz would actually reason about something like a present-day large language model or other AI system running on a computer? Would his own principles commit him to denying that such a system could ever be a genuine perceiver, or a bearer of moral status, simply because it is an artificial aggregate rather than a natural corporeal substance with its own dominant monad, regardless of how convincingly it behaved? And doesn't this create a tension with his own idealism, on which matter in general is just the appearance of countless perceiving monads -- it isn't obvious to me why the monads underlying a silicon chip should be any less capable of grounding a real perceiver than the ones underlying a brain. I would like your best reconstruction of how Leibniz's own principles would settle this, rather than a simple yes-or-no verdict.
