I'm taking a seminar this semester on punishment and the philosophy of criminal law, and this week's unit on retributivism versus consequentialism left me thinking about an argument I watched unfold between two people in my study group, Marcus and Ines, that I haven't been able to resolve on my own. It felt like a live version of the "punishing the innocent" or scapegoating objections we'd just covered in lecture. The trigger was a piece of local news: our city's transit authority has reportedly floated the idea of deliberately disproportionate fines for fare evasion — well beyond what any single instance of jumping a turnstile would normally warrant — on the reasoning that harshly punishing a small number of unlucky offenders would deter the much larger population of would-be fare-dodgers. Marcus and Ines disagreed sharply about whether such a policy could be justified, and since both of them made points I found genuinely persuasive at the time, I'd like your help going through the exchange step by step rather than just declaring an overall winner.

(1) Marcus argued that the policy is straightforwardly justified on consequentialist grounds: if punishing a handful of offenders severely reduces total fare evasion across the system, and the harm thereby prevented outweighs the disproportionate harshness borne by those who get caught, then a consequentialist should endorse the policy — that is simply what maximizing good outcomes means.

(2) Ines objected that this treats the punished individuals merely as instruments for controlling everyone else's behavior rather than punishing them for what they actually deserve. Any theory willing to punish someone far beyond their desert, purely because doing so produces good aggregate effects, has gone wrong somewhere, she said, whatever the numbers happen to show.

(3) Marcus replied that Ines's appeal to "desert" doesn't settle anything, because retributivists still need some non-arbitrary way of fixing how much punishment a given offense deserves, and there's no way to do that except by looking at the harm the offense causes or risks causing. Since fare evasion, repeated at scale, does real systemic damage that no single instance reveals, factoring that aggregate harm into the deserved punishment isn't smuggling in consequentialism — it's just being honest about what the offense involves.

(4) Ines answered that this move proves too much. If "desert" is allowed to track harm that the offender's own act didn't cause, but that exists only because of how other, unrelated people happen to behave, then the offender is still being punished for other people's choices rather than their own — which is exactly the "mere means" problem she raised in (2), just relocated into the desert calculation instead of avoided.

Could you work through moves (1) through (4) individually and say, for each one, who has the better of the argument and why, rather than only giving an overall verdict? I'd also value your view on whether there's a way for Marcus to rescue the move he makes in (3) without it collapsing into the picture Ines paints in (4), or whether retributivists really are stuck choosing between an arbitrary desert-fixing procedure and a covert consequentialism — and, more generally, whether you think either of them is missing a consideration that would actually resolve the disagreement.
