I write about science and ideas, and I'm currently reporting on how research into animal sentience gets translated into policy — prompted by the UK's 2022 decision to extend legal protection to octopuses and crabs on the strength of a government-commissioned review of the scientific evidence for their sentience. Digging into the academic debate behind that review, I keep running into two rival methodologies for deciding which animals are conscious. What I've come to think of as the "theory-heavy" camp will only attribute consciousness to a species whose nervous system can be shown to meet the specific conditions laid down by an independently motivated theory of consciousness — the global broadcasting architecture that Global Workspace Theory requires, say, or the level of integrated information that Integrated Information Theory specifies — and treats unresolved disagreement between such theories as grounds for suspending judgment on animals like insects or octopuses. The "marker-based" camp instead looks for a cluster of theory-neutral behavioral and physiological signs — flexible, motivation-sensitive learning; nociception paired with self-protective and analgesia-seeking behavior; trade-offs between competing drives — and extends the benefit of the doubt, on precautionary grounds, to any animal that shows enough of them. Compare the two approaches on their own terms: what is each designed to guard against, and where does each risk going wrong? Then tell me which one should actually govern welfare policy, and justify your answer.
