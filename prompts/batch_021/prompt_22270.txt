I'm a software engineer, not a philosopher, but after one too many hand-wavy references to "the Turing test" at work, I went back and read Turing's actual 1950 paper. Near the end he takes up what he labels the Argument from Extra-Sensory Perception, admits it is "quite a strong one," describes the statistical evidence for telepathy as "overwhelming," and concludes that a properly rigorous imitation game would need to be run in a "telepathy-proof room" so that a telepathic interrogator or contestant couldn't unfairly sense who was human.

Write me a short story built around that specific proposal rather than the usual "can the AI fool the judge" setup. Set it in a near future where telepathy has been confirmed and telepathy-proof rooms are standard equipment for any serious Turing test. Center the story on one test session in which a machine handles the ordinary text exchange flawlessly, but the judge — a trained telepathic receiver, testing through the shielding out of habit — reports afterward that she sensed nobody on the other side. Let that disagreement sit unresolved for the people who have to write up the result: I don't want a clean verdict on whether her impression is tracking something the transcript can't, or is simply the cruder of the two instruments.
