A few months ago I picked up a secondhand copy of Kripke's short book on Wittgenstein and rule-following, mainly because a friend had used the word "Kripkenstein" at dinner and I didn't want to admit I had no idea what she meant. I've since read it a couple of times, and I think I have the central argument straight: for any sign I've used in the past, say "+", there are infinitely many functions besides addition — "quus"-like functions that agree with addition on every calculation I've actually performed but diverge from it on numbers large enough that I've never tried them — and nothing about my past mental states, my dispositions, or some inner grasp of a rule seems able to settle which of these functions I really meant. Rather than leaving this as an open wound, Kripke has Wittgenstein respond with what he calls a "skeptical solution": stop looking for facts that make meaning-claims true, and instead explain our practice of making them by pointing to the role such claims play inside a community of speakers who check and correct one another. He also uses this to argue that a person permanently alone — Robinson Crusoe on his island forever, say — could never really be said to follow a rule correctly or incorrectly, because there is no one there to do the checking.

The part I keep getting stuck on is this: why doesn't exactly the same skeptical argument apply to the community as a whole? A community's shared dispositions to answer "+" problems are, in the end, just as finite as any one person's dispositions are, and there will always be some computation, however large, that no member of the community, alone or in combination, has ever actually carried out. So what fact makes it true even of the whole community that they are disposed to answer according to plus and not according to some deviant quus-like rule that simply has not come up yet? If shared communal agreement is itself just a big, finite, purely descriptive fact about a group's behavior, it seems to inherit exactly the defects — finiteness, silence about untested cases, an inability to say what anyone ought to answer rather than merely would answer — that were supposed to disqualify individual dispositions as a basis for meaning in the first place.

So I'd like you to argue for the position that Kripke's skeptical solution does not actually solve the rule-following paradox but merely relocates it, unsolved, from the individual to the community — and that this in turn undermines his route from the paradox to the private-language argument, since the solitary-individual-versus-community distinction he relies on cannot be doing the philosophical work he needs it to do if communities are exposed to the identical skeptical difficulty. Please build the strongest, most rigorous case you can rather than re-explaining the original paradox and solution, which I feel I already have a decent handle on; what I actually want is a real answer to whether this "relocation" objection lands, or whether there is some principled reason communal facts escape a problem that individual facts do not.
