I'm a backend software engineer with no formal training in philosophy or machine learning theory, but a colleague recently pointed me to Paul Christiano, Ajeya Cotra, and Mark Xu's 2021 report "Eliciting Latent Knowledge," and I've since spent a surprising number of evenings on it and on the discussion it spawned. What hooked me is that underneath the ML framing, the report is really wrestling with an old epistemological puzzle in new clothes: how could you ever train a system to sincerely report what it believes, rather than report whatever a human overseer would conclude from the same evidence, when the two would give the exact same answer on every case you're able to check directly? The report's structure is to propose a training strategy meant to force the system's "reporter" component into being an honest translator of its internal state, and then to construct a counterexample showing that a mere "human simulator" would pass that same strategy equally well. I've now worked through most of these strategy/counterexample pairs, and I'd like a second opinion on how they really stack up against each other, since I suspect I'm over-weighting whichever one I read most recently.

Below, in my own paraphrase (deliberately stripped of the report's own labels, so the ranking is about substance and not name recognition), are seven such proposals:

(a) Replace unaided human labels with the judgments of an amplified team of humans (e.g., via debate or recursive reward modeling), so the overseer is harder to fool.
(b) Penalize a reporter whose answers to many different, logically related questions turn out to be mutually inconsistent over time.
(c) Regularize toward whichever reporter is the lowest-complexity function of the predictor's internal state, on the theory that honest translation should be simpler than simulating an entire human from scratch.
(d) Penalize reporters that take longer to compute an answer, on the theory that reading off an existing belief should be cheap compared to simulating a human.
(e) Instead of training a reporter at all, have a human search over candidate explanations such that, when the human reasons using the explanation, their own predictions on held-out cases match the predictor's — and treat whichever explanation achieves this as an implicit translation of the predictor's ontology.
(f) Train an adversary whose sole job is to find inputs on which two independently trained reporters disagree, and penalize both when it succeeds.
(g) Skip training a separate reporter altogether, and instead use interpretability techniques to locate the predictor's "belief" about the relevant fact directly in its weights or activations.

Judging each proposal purely by how much genuine progress it makes on distinguishing an honest translator from a human simulator specifically — not by how practical or cheap it would be to implement today — please sort (a) through (g) into a tier list, given as a JSON block of the following form, where S means "comes closest to actually resolving the difficulty" and D means "barely touches the core difficulty at all":

```json
{
  "S": ["a", "..."],

  "C": ["..."],
  "D": ["..."]
}
```

Every tier must be non-empty, and every letter (a)–(g) must appear in exactly one tier. Once you've given the JSON, add two or three sentences per item explaining the placement, focused specifically on whether its associated counterexample fully defeats the proposal or only partially limits it.
