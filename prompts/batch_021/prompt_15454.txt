so we're in the middle of a unit on Hobbes in my social contract theory seminar, and last week we spent basically the whole session on that bit in Leviathan (chapter 15) where he brings up "the Foole" -- the guy who argues its perfectly rational to keep your covenants when it benefits you, but just as rational to break them the second breaking one is more convienent, especially once the other party has already held up their end and has no way to retaliate. Hobbes' response is basically that nobody would ever admit a person with that policy into a cooperative scheme in the first place, so a purely self-interested agent should actually want to be the type of person who keeps covenants, even in cases where keeping one isn't to their advantage anymore in the moment it counts.

Then completely separately, in my decision theory seminar (cross-listed w/ econ) we did Parfit's hitchhiker this week and it's basically the same structure in a totally different costume: you're dying of thirst in the desert, a driver says he'll only take you into town if he's convinced you'll pay him once you arrive, but the second you're actually standing in town safe and sound, paying gets you nothing and he's got no way to force you. Our TA kept calling the idea that you should still want to be the kind of agent who pays "rational irrationality," and we spent like half the seminar arguing over whether causal decision theory (says don't pay, since forking over the cash now can't retroactively change whether you got the ride) or something more like resolute choice / constrained maximization (you should've committed to paying, full stop, and stuck to it) is actually the rational policy here.

Nobody in either class made the connection even though it seems pretty obvious once you see it, so here's my question: if you dropped Hobbes into that decision theory seminar and walked him through Parfit's hitchhiker, Newcomb's problem, and the whole CDT-vs-resolute-choice fight, which side do you think he'd land on, and why? And does his argument that a covenant-breaker could never really be trusted into a cooperative society even survive the transplant, given how much it leans on reputation and repeated interaction, when the hitchhiker case is basically built to be a one-off with a driver you'll never see again -- or is there a way his reasoning still bites even without any future encounters to worry about?
