Random tangent from a wikipedia rabbit hole last night: did you know Leibniz basically invented binary arithmetic and built one of the first mechanical calculators? The guy clearly took the idea of mechanizing thought pretty serious, centuries before computers existed. But he's also got this famous 'mill' argument where he says imagine blowing up a thinking machine to the size of a mill so you could walk around inside it — all you'd find is parts pushing on other parts, never anything you could point to as a perception or a feeling. People cite this as basically an early version of the philosophical zombie / conceivability argument thing, i.e. you can imagine (or build) something that does everything a mind does with nothing actually going on inside.

So now i'm stuck wondering what Leibniz would actually conclude if you showed him a modern LLM. His whole monad theory says literally everything, even a rock, is built out of tiny substances that have SOME dim, confused perception — so does that commit him to saying an LLM has a little bit of inner experience too, just nothing like self-aware human consciousness? Or does the fact that a computer (like a rock, or a mill) is just an "aggregate" of monads rather than one true unified monad mean he'd actually side with the zombie camp and say there's nothing it's like to be an LLM at all? Genuinely curious how those two peices of his own system would have to shake out against each other.
