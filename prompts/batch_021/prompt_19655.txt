I have just finished a novel whose central figure, a career prosecutor, spends the second half of the book quietly persuaded by something very like Galen Strawson's Basic Argument against moral responsibility -- a conviction she cannot admit at work and cannot shake at home. To mark the book's publication, my publisher has arranged a public conversation between me and a philosopher who has spent much of her career defending and refining that very argument. I would rather not spend our forty minutes on stage watching her re-derive the regress for an audience that has already read a summary of it in the programme notes; I want questions that work the argument where it is actually under pressure in the literature, the way a sharp colleague would in a seminar rather than the way a generalist interviewer would on a books-and-ideas radio hour.

Could you draft eight to ten questions for that conversation? At least one should press on the argument's debt to Nietzsche's remark that the causa sui is a kind of self-contradiction, asking her to say what turns the Basic Argument into a genuine regress rather than a restatement of that dismissal. At least one should test the feature that supposedly distinguishes her position from ordinary hard determinism -- that the argument is meant to hold whether the universe turns out to be deterministic or not -- and should push on whether libertarians who locate real indeterminism at the moment of character-forming choice, in Robert Kane's manner, are offering a genuine alternative or merely relocating the same problem one step back. At least one should go after her own apparent willingness, elsewhere, to keep an ordinary, practice-based sense of responsibility even while denying the "true" or desert-entailing kind that would make sense of ultimate reward and punishment, and should ask what is left of the argument's sting if everyday praise, blame, and punishment survive its conclusion untouched. And at least one should ask her to place her position against P.F. Strawson's "Freedom and Resentment," since that essay's naturalism about the reactive attitudes is often read as an attempt to dissolve exactly the question she has built a career on reopening. For each of these four, add a short follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give, so the conversation doesn't stop just where she would like it to.
