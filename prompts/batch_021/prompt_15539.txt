I'm a software engineer, and as a side project I run a small interview podcast where I talk to academics about ideas that reward close, formal thinking; the audience is mostly other engineers who, like me, never studied philosophy formally but enjoy watching an argument get taken apart carefully. Next week's guest is a scholar of classical Chinese philosophy who has built her career on Gongsun Long's "White Horse Dialogue," the best-known argument to survive from the School of Names, and who defends a mereological reading of the thesis "a white horse is not a horse": roughly, that classical Chinese general terms work more like mass nouns picking out quantities of stuff (as "water" does in English) than like count nouns picking out classes of individuals, so that "horse" and "white horse" denote different, if overlapping, stuff rather than a class and one of its subclasses -- a view sometimes called the "mass-noun hypothesis" for classical Chinese. I got interested in this through an unrelated argument I keep having with a junior engineer on my team about whether a ReadOnlyList should really be allowed to inherit from List, until a friend doing a PhD in Chinese philosophy told me, half-joking, that Gongsun Long was already fighting about "is-a" relationships twenty-three centuries ago. I've since watched the one public talk of hers that exists online, and whenever someone in the audience raises the obvious objection that the whole argument just equivocates on "is not," she has a practiced answer along the lines of "that's only a fallacy if you assume classical Chinese nouns already work the way you're assuming they do, and that's exactly what's in dispute" -- and I don't want to give her an hour in which that line goes unchallenged.

Could you draft eight to ten interview questions that go beyond having her restate the thesis, arranged so that her stock answer above gets progressively harder to fall back on? I want at least one question on the dialogue's own argument that a yellow or black horse satisfies someone "seeking a horse" but not someone "seeking a white horse," and what that is actually supposed to prove about the two terms; one that presses directly on whether the negation in "a white horse is not a horse" is quietly sliding between "is not identical to" and "is not a kind of," since that is the equivocation charge in its sharpest form; one that borrows the linguists' distinction between intersective, subsective, and privative modifiers (a red car is a car, a skilled surgeon is a surgeon, but a fake gun is not a gun) to ask whether her reading commits her to treating an ordinary color adjective as working like "fake" rather than like "red," and whether that is a cost she is willing to pay; one on how she would answer the old charge, leveled against the School of Names by rival thinkers of the period, that this sort of argument can defeat an opponent's mouth without ever convincing their mind; and one that takes my own ReadOnlyList example at face value and asks her to say exactly where the analogy to subtyping in software helps and where it becomes a category error. For each of these, add a short follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give, and end the list with one question asking what specific textual or historical evidence would be enough to make her abandon her own reading for a rival one.
