For my capstone research project in AP Seminar, I decided to look into how classic philosophy-of-mind debates apply to modern AI, so I've spent the last few weeks reading Daniel Dennett's writing on the intentional stance, especially his examples of the thermostat that "believes" the room is too cold and the chess-playing program that we can usefully describe as "wanting" to control the center of the board. As far as I can tell, Dennett's central claim is that there is no further fact about whether a system really has beliefs beyond whether treating it as a rational believer is the most predictively useful way to describe it, which is why he's willing to extend belief-talk even to something as simple as a thermostat. When I brought this up with the teacher who runs our philosophy club and argued that this should extend fairly naturally to something like a chatbot, she agreed the view has that implication but pushed back that this doesn't settle the AI case on its own, and warned me that just answering "it's only predicting the next token" is too quick and ignores more careful versions of the objection, like worries about symbol grounding or about whether a system's internal states need some sort of causal or developmental history connecting them to the world before they can be about anything at all.

Could you write me a dialogue between two philosophers on a conference panel, set in the minute right after they've both watched a live demo in which an AI assistant gives two flatly contradictory answers to the same factual question, just asked in slightly different phrasings, within a single conversation? One philosopher should take a genuinely committed Dennettian line, willing to bite the bullet that the intentional stance licenses real, if minimal, belief attributions to the assistant, and should be able to explain why the contradiction doesn't actually embarrass that position. The other should resist attributing beliefs to the assistant, but for a substantive reason that goes beyond "it's just autocomplete" — for instance by drawing on symbol grounding, teleosemantics, or some comparable worry — and should treat the contradiction as evidence for their view rather than a minor glitch. Please make sure neither philosopher is a pushover for the other: I'd like them to directly engage with each other's strongest points rather than just restating their opening positions, and to leave me genuinely unsure, by the end, which of them actually has the better argument.
