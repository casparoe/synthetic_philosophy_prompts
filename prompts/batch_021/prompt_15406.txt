Last night a friend and I got into a long, unresolved argument about whether David Lewis's reformed conditional analysis of dispositions actually survives Alexander Bird's antidote case, or whether masking shows that no amount of patching can rescue a conditional analysis of dispositions. Neither of us gave ground, and it left me wanting a second, more careful opinion on a piece of grading I still owe from that same unit.

The exam question was: "Explain how C. B. Martin's (1994) fink cases are supposed to refute the Simple Conditional Analysis of dispositions, how David Lewis's (1997) reformed conditional analysis is meant to block the fink cases, and whether Alexander Bird's (1998) antidote case creates a further problem for Lewis's repaired analysis. Illustrate with examples throughout."

Here is one student's answer:

"The Simple Conditional Analysis (SCA) says that having a disposition just consists in a conditional being true: the glass is fragile iff it would break if struck. Martin's fink cases show this is wrong in both directions. A wire that is actually dead can be wired up to an 'electro-fink' that makes it live the instant a conductor touches it, so 'if touched, it would conduct' comes out true even though the wire lacks the disposition to conduct at the relevant time; since the conditional holds without the disposition, this shows the conditional isn't necessary for having the disposition. Symmetrically, a live wire can be fitted with a fink that kills the current on contact, so the disposition to conduct is present but the conditional is false; this shows the conditional isn't sufficient for the disposition either. Either way, having the disposition and the truth of the SCA's conditional come apart.

Lewis's reformed conditional analysis is meant to handle both Martin's finks and Bird's antidote case. It repairs the SCA by relativizing the conditional to an intrinsic causal basis: x is disposed to give response r to stimulus s iff there is some intrinsic property B of x such that x would give r if x underwent s and retained B throughout the process. This blocks the fink, because what the fink actually does is change the wire's intrinsic basis (dead vs. live) exactly when the stimulus arrives, so the counterfactual in which B is held fixed no longer matches what the fink brings about.

The antidote case doesn't cause the same trouble for the repaired analysis, because the antidote leaves the poison's chemical basis completely untouched: B is retained, s occurs, and B and s are still what would jointly cause death, so the reformed biconditional comes out true. Lewis's analysis therefore correctly counts the poison as still disposed to kill even when the antidote masks the effect."
