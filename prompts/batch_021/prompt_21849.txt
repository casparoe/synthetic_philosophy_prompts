I'm a software engineer, and I've been asked to give the best man speech at my college roommate's wedding next month. He's finishing a philosophy PhD on collective intentionality, and from years of hearing about his work, I understand him to be firmly on Michael Bratman's side of that debate: for him, shared intention is nothing more than two people's individual plans meshing reliably through overlapping sub-plans, mutual responsiveness, and common knowledge of the whole arrangement -- no extra ingredient required. He's argued, more than once, against Margaret Gilbert's rival picture, on which a promise like a wedding vow creates a "joint commitment" and a genuine plural subject: a standing obligation between two people that neither can unilaterally walk back just because a feeling changed. About a third of the guests are his philosophy department and will notice immediately if my speech quietly borrows Gilbertian language he'd reject -- two souls merging into one, a bond existing over and above the couple themselves, vows that bind regardless of what either person comes to want. The other two-thirds have never heard of either philosopher and just want a good wedding speech. Could you write me five minutes that stays honest to what he actually believes, still works as a real toast for the friends-and-family half of the room, and never turns into a lecture?
