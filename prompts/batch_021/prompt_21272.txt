My grandmother, Eleanor, died twelve days ago at eighty-four, and I have been asked to deliver the eulogy at her memorial service next Saturday. She taught comparative philosophy for over three decades before retiring, but everyone in the family understood that the real center of her intellectual life was the Zhuangzi, especially the Inner Chapters, which she reread every year the way other people reread a favorite novel. She had very little patience for people getting it wrong: she could not stand hearing Zhuangzi flattened into generic "go with the flow" self-help, and she would visibly bristle whenever someone conflated his view of death with Buddhist rebirth, with the ancestor-watches-over-you piety of religious Daoism, or even with Epicurus's "death is nothing to us," which she always maintained is a superficially similar but actually quite different argument. In the note she left with instructions for her own service, she was direct about this: no talk of her looking down on us, no comforting vagueness about her going somewhere, nothing that would quietly reintroduce the picture of death she had spent her career arguing the text does not actually offer.

The service is being held in the divinity school chapel where she used to guest lecture, so the room will be split roughly down the middle: former colleagues and the graduate students she advised on one side, who will recognize a mangled allusion to the basin-drumming scene or the deathbed scene from a mile away, and neighbors, in-laws, and family on the other, most of whom have never read a word of the text and simply want to hear that someone loved her and is allowed to be sad that she is gone. I am currently taking the seminar on early Chinese philosophy that she more or less willed me into, and my classmates and I have spent the last two sessions arguing about how to even translate hua (化) -- transformation, change, process -- in a way that does not sound either too clinical for a room of mourners or too breezy, as if she has simply changed trains. I do not trust myself to hold both halves of that room and get the philosophy right at the same time, so I am hoping you can write the eulogy for me.

I would like it to run about six or seven minutes aloud and to be built around the actual texture of her thinking rather than a vague gesture at "Eastern acceptance of impermanence" -- ideally drawing on something like the scene of Zhuangzi drumming on a basin after his wife's death and what he tells Huizi about it, or his own deathbed refusal of an elaborate burial because he would rather be eaten by crows and kites above ground than by mole crickets and ants below. It needs to comfort people who did not know any of this while still being something her former colleagues could sit through without cringing -- and, ideally, without ever claiming, even by implication, that she is somewhere watching it happen.
