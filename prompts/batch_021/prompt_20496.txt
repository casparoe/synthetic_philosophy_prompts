I don't have any formal training in philosophy, but over the last several months I've been working through David Benatar's asymmetry argument on my own, after a documentary on the antinatalist movement got me curious enough to track down "Better Never to Have Been." I brought it up at my monthly book club -- we mostly read novels, but one member had suggested a philosophy title for a change -- and it turned into a genuinely heated argument between two of the other members that I've been turning over in my head ever since. Renata opened by laying out the four-part asymmetry pretty much as Benatar states it: the presence of pain is bad, the presence of pleasure is good, the absence of pain is good even when nobody is around to enjoy that good, and the absence of pleasure is bad only if someone actually exists to be deprived of it. Once you grant that, she said, bringing anyone into existence is a net harm no matter how happy the resulting life turns out to be, because you are guaranteeing some pain -- which is bad -- in exchange for pleasure whose absence would never have been bad for anyone anyway. Denny pushed back immediately: he said the whole thing looks rigged, because the very fact that "there's nobody around to be deprived" is supposed to stop the absence of pleasure from being bad, and by that same logic there's nobody around to benefit either, which should stop the absence of pain from counting as good -- yet Benatar wants to keep the pain side of the ledger charged while zeroing out the pleasure side, and Denny couldn't see what earned that difference in treatment.

Renata's response was that the asymmetry isn't just asserted -- Benatar defends it by pointing to cases where we already reason this way. We think it's wrong to deliberately conceive a child we know will suffer greatly, she said, but nobody thinks there's a matching duty to conceive a child purely so a happy life can exist; and we're able to feel glad, for a person's own sake, that a miserable life was never brought into being, while nobody feels sad, for any particular person's sake, about a happy life that never occurred, because there's no one there to be sad for. Denny granted that these intuitions are real but said they can be explained more cheaply, by ordinary facts about the burdens and risks parents actually take on, without buying into a deep metaphysical asymmetry between pleasure and pain. He also pointed out that Benatar's own view implies that even someone with a spectacularly good life was still harmed by being born, which contradicts what nearly everyone says about their own life -- and a theory that can wave away that near-universal testimony as "Pollyanna" bias whenever it's inconvenient, he said, starts to look unfalsifiable rather than well-supported.

Neither of them budged, and the rest of the group just sat there unsure what to think. Could you go through Renata and Denny's exchange move by move and say who has the better of the argument at each stage, rather than just declaring an overall winner? I'd also be curious whether some revised version of the asymmetry could survive Denny's parity objection, or whether the whole argument ultimately stands or falls on accepting that initial asymmetric evaluation as a brute starting point.
