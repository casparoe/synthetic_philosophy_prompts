I'm a PhD student working on theories of intention and dynamic choice, and I keep returning to Kavka's toxin puzzle as a test case for the dispute between what, following McClennen, I'll call the "sophisticated" and "resolute" choosers. The sophisticated chooser holds that, because drinking the toxin will serve no purpose once the money is already secured, a rational and self-aware agent can foresee at midnight that they will not go through with it at noon — and since genuinely intending to do something requires being able to expect that you will actually do it, they conclude that the intention, and so the money, is simply unavailable to a rational person. The resolute chooser denies this: they hold that an agent who assesses their options plan-by-plan, rather than re-litigating each step in isolation once it arrives, can truly intend at midnight to drink the toxin at noon, and will in fact drink it, without thereby doing anything irrational.

Please write a dialogue between these two figures set in the minutes before midnight, each trying to talk the other out of their position. The sophisticated chooser should press the resolute chooser on what, concretely, is supposed to move them to drink the toxin once the money is already in the bank and the local balance of reasons points the other way; the resolute chooser should press the sophisticated chooser on why rationality should be keyed to that local, moment-by-moment balance at all, rather than to the plan as a whole, given how self-defeating the sophisticated view turns out to be in a case exactly like this one. Neither should simply restate their opening position — each should come away visibly unsettled, if not converted, by the other's strongest objection.
