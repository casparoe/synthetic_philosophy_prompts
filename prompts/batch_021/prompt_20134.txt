I'm a philosophy PhD student taking a graduate seminar on Buddhist philosophy of logic, and this week we're working through Chapter Eighteen of Nāgārjuna's Mūlamadhyamakakārikā (the "Examination of Self") in Jay Garfield's translation, alongside Garfield and Priest's well-known paper "Nāgārjuna and the Limits of Thought," which argues that Nāgārjuna should be read as a dialetheist. Verse 18.8 gives what's probably the most quoted tetralemma in the whole text: "Everything is real and is not real, both real and not real, neither real nor not real," which Nāgārjuna then glosses as nothing less than the teaching of the Buddha. What's been bothering me since our last meeting is that this particular use of the catuṣkoṭi runs in the opposite direction from most of its other appearances in the text. A few chapters later, in the examination of the Tathāgata, Nāgārjuna puts the very same four options to the opposite use: "'Empty' should not be asserted, 'nonempty' should not be asserted, neither both nor neither should be asserted" — there he's rejecting all four horns as things one shouldn't even assert, rather than affirming all four as simultaneously true of everything.

Half the seminar is happy to just accept the Garfield and Priest line and treat both passages as two faces of the same paraconsistent coin: Nāgārjuna genuinely asserting true contradictions at the limits of thought. The other half, myself included, keeps reaching for something more like Richard Robinson's or Jan Westerhoff's more deflationary readings, on which the apparent contradiction dissolves once you're careful about what "real" is being predicated of, or about the difference between prasajya and paryudāsa negation — though I'll admit I only half-understand that grammatical distinction and can't yet tell whether it's doing real philosophical work here or just relocating the problem.

What I'd find genuinely useful is a close, disjunct-by-disjunct reading of 18.8: for each of the four conjuncts ("real," "not real," "both," "neither"), what would it have to be predicated of — conventional objects, their svabhāva, something else entirely — for all four to come out true together without collapsing into outright contradiction? And does whatever move rescues 18.8 in this way also make sense of why the very same four-cornered structure runs in the negating direction in the Tathāgata passage, so that the two count as expressions of one consistent method rather than two incompatible ones Nāgārjuna switches between as convenient? I'm less interested in a tidy summary of "what emptiness means" than in whether the contradiction can actually be defused at the level of these two specific passages, and, if it can, whether something philosophically important about Madhyamaka — the sense in which this is supposed to be a therapeutic method rather than a doctrine — ends up getting lost in the defusing.
