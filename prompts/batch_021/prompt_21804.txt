Ok so this happened at my writing group last week and I can't stop chewing on it. We were workshopping Owen's novel, where the male lead's daughter dies in a car crash two-thirds of the way through -- and honestly its the best material in the book, everything after just hits different once you know what's coming. Presenting it, Owen argued the book is put together tightly enough that swapping in a smaller tragedy (the kid badly hurt, say, instead of killed) would unravel everything downstream -- the wife's arc, the ending, even a joke in chapter two reads different in hindsight -- so the death isn't gratuitous, it's load-bearing, and given everything else already in the book, this is the best possible version of it. Cass wasn't buying it: to her the death felt narratively convenient, a smaller tragedy would've bought almost as much emotional weight without being so cruel to the character, and anyway "best possible version" talk is just a best-of-all-possible-worlds move and a total cop-out, since Owen can never actually rewrite the book the other way and compare, so "necessary for the best version" really just means "I couldn't find a way to cut it." Owen said unfalsifiable or not, it's still true that in a story this interconnected, small changes aren't small, so demanding he "just try removing it and see" misunderstands how tightly-plotted fiction actually works. Cass's comeback: fine, but then he doesn't get to also claim the death is justified BY its payoff, since on his own logic literally nothing he could've written would ever come out as anything other than "necessary" -- he's rigged the test so he always passes it.

I don't just want to know who won overall, I want to know who's got the better of it at each step, and whether either of them is missing something the other one should've pushed on. End with a final verdict on its own line, in exactly this format: \answer{Owen} or \answer{Cass}, whichever one is more right on balance.
