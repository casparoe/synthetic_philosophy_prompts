For a graduate seminar on decision theory and existential risk, I'm assembling short stories built around some of the field's lesser-known corners, and I'd like one more. Some decision theorists worry about "acausal threats": coercive moves made by an agent that never causally interacts with its target, whose leverage comes purely from being able to predict, or simulate, how the target would respond, so that concessions can be extracted now for a threat that may never actually arrive. One proposed defense is the "surrogate goal": the threatened party manufactures, or artificially inflates, some value it does not otherwise care much about, so that an extortionist who cares only about extracting a costly concession — not about which of the target's values gets leveraged — ends up targeting the decoy rather than what the agent truly values. Write a short story, a page or two, dramatizing a mind (human, corporate, or artificial, as you prefer) constructing such a surrogate goal and then having to live inside the arrangement. Please avoid simply restaging the "evil AI" takeover or Roko's basilisk itself; I am more interested in what the maneuver does to the agent's own relationship to its values than in the threat's mechanics — whether a goal adopted as pure camouflage can remain a decoy once it has been acted on in earnest, and whether the whole strategy amounts to self-protective integrity or a subtle self-betrayal.
