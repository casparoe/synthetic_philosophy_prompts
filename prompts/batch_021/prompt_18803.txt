I have spent a long time reading the rationalist and effective-altruism blogospheres, and one move I keep encountering in threads about personal identity is an appeal to Buddhism on behalf of what is usually called "open individualism" -- Daniel Kolak's term for the view that there is really only a single subject underlying every experience anyone has ever had, so that the sense of being a separate person is a kind of perspectival illusion. The argument I keep running into goes roughly: Buddhism teaches that there is no enduring self (anatta); the boundary you draw around "your" experiences versus "someone else's" therefore isn't doing any metaphysical work; so at bottom there is just one experiencer wearing different masks. This is usually set against "empty individualism," the rival reductionist position (closer to what I associate with Parfit, or with Buddhist accounts of momentariness) on which there is not even a persisting subject within a single life -- only a series of discrete, causally linked experiences, none of which is more "you" than any other. (Kolak's third category, "closed individualism," is just the common-sense view and doesn't seem to be what anyone in these threads is actually fighting about.) A friend of mine who has read far more Kolak than I have insists that open individualism is basically what the Buddha was getting at all along, and that Buddhologists who deny this just haven't caught up with him.

I am not convinced the canonical material actually licenses that move, and I would like you to make the case in some detail rather than just asserting it. Specifically, please argue that anatta -- as developed through the analysis of the person into the five skandhas, the doctrine of dependent origination, and the notion of the santāna (the causally linked but non-identical mental stream that is what actually "continues" from moment to moment, and, on the traditional account, from life to life, without any self doing the continuing) -- supports empty individualism far better than it supports open individualism. I would like the argument to also establish that reading anatta as evidence for a single universal subject quietly reintroduces something like the Upanishadic identification of ātman with brahman, exactly the kind of view the doctrine was originally formulated to rule out.

I would also like you to take on the strongest objection available to someone defending the open-individualist reading: Mahayana notions like buddha-nature (tathāgatagarbha) and the Dharmakāya, and non-dual traditions such as Dzogchen or Zen, all of which can sound like they are describing one pervasive awareness underlying all beings. Please explain concretely why these traditions do not actually rescue the open-individualist interpretation, rather than simply noting that they exist and moving on.
