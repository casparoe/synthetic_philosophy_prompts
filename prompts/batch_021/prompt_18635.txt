I don't have any background in philosophy, I just like chewing on this stuff late at night, and lately I've been stuck on something that came up after a conversation with my roommate about whether dying young is actually bad for the person it happens to (not for the people left behind, just for them). I get the basic Epicurus move -- you can't be harmed by something you never experience, and since you're not around anymore once you're dead, and death hasn't happened yet while you're alive, there's never a moment where "you" and "your death" actually overlap, so how can it be bad for you? Makes sense on its face. But then the usual comeback is the deprivation idea, something like: death is bad not because it feels bad but because it robs you of all the good years you would've otherwise had, kind of like how loosing a winning lottery ticket you never even knew you had can still be bad for you even though you never "experience" the loss.

Ok but here's where my brain keeps looping. If the badness of death is this deprivation thing, WHEN does it actually happen to the person? It can't be while they're alive, because they haven't lost the years yet at that point. And it can't be after they're dead, because by then there's no "them" around anymore to be the one who's worse off -- isn't that the whole thing Epicurus was pointing at in the first place? It kind of feels like this harm is just floating out there with no actual time slot, which seems like a really weird property for something that's suppose to be a real, concrete bad thing happening to a real person and not just an abstract idea on paper. Is this an actual recognized problem in this debate, and if so, how do the people who still want to say death is bad for the person try to wriggle out of it? I'd love an explanation of the main ways of handling this "when" question and whether any of them genuinely dissolve the weirdness or if they're all just kind of dodging it with clever wording.
