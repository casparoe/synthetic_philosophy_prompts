For the past year or so I've been teaching myself probability and decision theory as a hobby, and one argument I keep coming back to is the Dutch book argument: the idea that if a person's degrees of belief don't fit together the way the probability axioms require - say, if their confidence that something will happen and their confidence that it won't fail to add up to complete certainty - a sufficiently clever opponent can offer them a series of bets, each one looking fine on its own, that together are guaranteed to cost them money no matter what happens. What unsettles me is that this isn't about anyone getting a fact wrong; every individual belief could be reasonable, and yet the whole set can still be exploited just because the pieces don't hang together. I'd like you to write me a short story built around this: a character whose beliefs are incoherent in exactly this way, and someone who notices it and constructs a real Dutch book against them over the course of the story. I'd like the bets to genuinely add up as a Dutch book, so that the trap would actually hold up if a reader worked through the numbers, and I'd rather the ending dwell on what this reveals about the character than settle into a simple moral about the dangers of gambling.
