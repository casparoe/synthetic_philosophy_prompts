I'm taking a research seminar this semester on nineteenth-century American constitutional and political thought, and this week we've been working through the famous rupture between Frederick Douglass and the Garrisonian abolitionists over how the Constitution should be read. For the assigned reading we went through Douglass's 1860 Glasgow address, "The Constitution of the United States: Is It Pro-Slavery or Anti-Slavery?", alongside selections from Garrison and Wendell Phillips, and my professor made it pretty clear she thinks Douglass's post-1851 position — that the document, read on its own terms and apart from the framers' private intentions, is at bottom an anti-slavery text — represents real interpretive progress over what Garrison and his allies branded a "covenant with death, and an agreement with hell." Almost everyone in the seminar has fallen in line with her reading, and I'd like to show up to section with a serious defense of the minority view instead of just going along with the consensus.

Could you help me by writing the strongest argument you can that the Garrisonian reading is actually more defensible, textually and historically, than the position Douglass develops in the Glasgow speech? I want the argument to engage the actual interpretive principles at stake rather than just re-narrating history everyone already knows: for instance, why weighing the concrete compromises written into the text itself — the three-fifths clause, the fugitive slave clause, and the clause barring Congress from banning the slave trade before 1808 — together with the known intentions and practice of the men who wrote and ratified the document, might be a sounder method of constitutional interpretation than Douglass's insistence that a law's meaning is fixed by its words alone and is indifferent to anyone's intentions, private or public. If it strengthens the case, feel free to argue that the Constitution's studious avoidance of the words "slave" and "slavery" is weaker evidence for Douglass's reading than he assumes, since euphemism is just as compatible with shame as with principle. I don't want a balanced survey of both sides — I can get that from the syllabus. I want one sustained, honest case for the side my professor is least sympathetic to.
