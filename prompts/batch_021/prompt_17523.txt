Me and my flatmate got into a proper argument over dinner last night about whether absences can really be causes -- she thinks "nothing" just can't do causal work, full stop, and I think that's obviously too strong once you look at ordinary cases, like a missed inspection causing a fire. We must have gone back and forth for a good hour, and this morning I realised the argument is annoyingly relevant to something I actually have to deal with this week. I sit on the incident review board at the biotech company I work for, and we're closing out the report on a small fire in one of our cold-storage rooms two months back. The mechanical story is uncontroversial: a compressor overheated because a filter had clogged. What's not settled is whether the report should list "failure to perform the scheduled monthly filter check" as a cause of the fire. Half the board wants it in, since if the check had happened the buildup would almost certainly have been caught in time. Our facilities director is blocking that, and her objection is sharper than "omissions can't be causes" -- she points out that the fire also counterfactually depended on the manufacturer never fitting an auto-shutoff, on the county not requiring one, and on her own not randomly double-checking that week even though it wasn't her job, and asks why, if we're promoting the missed filter check to "a cause," we don't have to put all of those on the list too. Nobody on the board has a principled answer for her; they just feel like the technician's omission is obviously the relevant one.

Could you write the memo I'm going to circulate before the board meets again on Friday? I need an actual recommendation on whether "failure to perform the scheduled filter check" belongs in the causes section of the report, or whether missed duties should go in a seperate, explicitly non-causal category (contributing conditions, say) while the causal narrative sticks to the physical chain of events -- and the reasoning has to be something the facilities director could actually respond to, not just an assertion that some omissions "obviously" matter more than others. It would help to draw on how the going accounts of causation actually handle this: the counterfactual-dependence line that Lewis-style theories are committed to (and the overgeneration problem it runs into), the various proposals for picking out the "right" omission by appeal to norms, roles, or what would normally have happened, and the more skeptical position that no omission is ever strictly a cause and that citing one is really giving an explanation, not identifying a cause. Don't give me a wishy-washy "there are considerations on both sides" memo -- pick a position and defend it against the strongest version of the objection.
