I'm currently taking a graduate seminar on epistemology, and this term we've spent several weeks on the puzzles surrounding misleading higher-order evidence. A stock example that keeps coming up: a competent reasoner is told—truly or falsely—that she has just been given a drug that impairs people's arithmetic while also inflating their confidence in their own calculations, and must then decide what to make of a long-division problem she has just worked through correctly. Sophie Horowitz's paper "Epistemic Akrasia" has become something of a touchstone in our discussions of these cases: she argues that the so-called Enkratic Principle—roughly, that rationality forbids believing P while also believing that one's evidence doesn't support believing P—can never be rationally violated, because any akratic combination of attitudes is, on her diagnosis, guaranteed to be somehow defective no matter how the world turns out, in a way that resembles a dominance argument. A few of my classmates, siding instead with what's sometimes called the "level-splitting" view associated with Adam Elga's unpublished but widely-cited work on the topic, think this proves too much, since it seems to rule out by fiat the kind of self-doubt that looks entirely appropriate whenever one has strong independent reason to distrust one's own reasoning in the moment.

For my term paper I've been assigned to argue the level-splitting side, and while the position feels intuitively right to me, I keep producing responses that amount to little more than insisting that higher-order evidence can be misleading, which doesn't actually engage Horowitz's argument. Could you construct the strongest available case for the claim that it can be rational to retain a first-order belief that P while simultaneously and rationally believing that one's evidence doesn't support P? I'd like the argument to directly confront the dominance-style reasoning behind the Enkratic Principle rather than sidestepping it, and ideally to say something about why, in cases like the drug example above, remaining akratic might be better calibrated to the reasoner's total evidential situation than revising either the first-order or the higher-order attitude to restore coherence.
