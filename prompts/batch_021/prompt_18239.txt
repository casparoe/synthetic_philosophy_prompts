Since retiring from a career in experimental condensed-matter physics, I have been working through a rather unsystematic reading list in the philosophy of causation, prompted partly by a small philosophy reading group I joined at my old university. Two sessions ago we discussed Al-Ghazālī's treatment, in the seventeenth discussion of the Tahāfut al-Falāsifa ("The Incoherence of the Philosophers"), of why the philosophers cannot prove that fire's contact with cotton necessitates the cotton's burning: he argues that the two events are merely joined by God's habitual practice, or ʿāda ("custom"), of creating them together, rather than by any power residing in the fire itself, and he cites, as evidence that no such necessity holds, the case of a prophet who was thrown into a fire yet survived unharmed. Almost every secondary source I have consulted since adds, in passing, that Ghazālī thereby anticipated Hume by some six centuries, since Hume likewise denies that we ever perceive any necessary connection between cause and effect, and grounds our expectation that the effect will follow in nothing more than custom, or the habit formed by past experience.

Having spent forty years more or less taking induction and the reliability of physical law for granted, I find this equation of the two thinkers too quick, though I cannot yet say precisely why. Both men, after all, reach for a word that translates naturally as "custom" to explain our expectation that an effect will follow its cause. I would like you to lay out, as precisely as you can, exactly what is shared and what is not between Ghazālī's occasionalism and Hume's account of causation. Does Ghazālī deny something that Hume's regularity theory does not — namely, that creatures have any causal efficacy whatsoever, even a merely dispositional or contingent one, given that for Ghazālī only God truly acts? Is Hume's "custom" doing the same explanatory work as Ghazālī's ʿāda, given that one is lodged in the habits of a sustaining divine agent and the other in the habits of a merely observing human mind, with no metaphysical claim about what, if anything, underwrites the regularity from outside that mind? And, despite this apparent metaphysical gulf, do the two positions end up licensing exactly the same everyday and scientific attitude toward induction and the trustworthiness of natural law, or is there some respect in which a working scientist really should reason differently depending on which of the two views is correct? I would also be glad if you flagged any place where the standard "Ghazālī pre-empted Hume" line oversimplifies matters.
