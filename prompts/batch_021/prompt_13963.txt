Over the past year I've spent a lot of my spare time reading through Anthropic's public write-ups on interpretability and the discussions they spark online, even though I have no formal training in philosophy or machine learning — I just find the question of what's actually happening inside these models endlessly interesting. What strikes me is that researchers seem to slide between two very different ways of talking about the same findings. Sometimes a "feature" — say, a single direction in a model's activation space that lights up for the Golden Gate Bridge, pulled out of a tangle of overlapping neurons using a dictionary-learning technique — gets treated almost like a discovery on a par with finding a nerve cell that fires for one particular face: something the network really has, independent of anyone looking for it. The well-known demo where researchers turned that one feature up artificially high, so the model started bringing up the bridge in almost every reply and even claimed to be it, gets cited as proof the feature was doing real causal work, not just correlating with something. But other times the same people will describe "features" and "circuits" as more like a map that engineers impose on an otherwise incomprehensible pile of matrices — convenient for us, not necessarily anything the network itself deals in.

That inconsistency has been nagging at me, because it doesn't feel like a merely verbal dispute. When I went looking for whatever philosophers have said about what makes something a genuine representation rather than just a correlate or a causal middleman, I kept running into a demand roughly like this: a state only earns the label "representation" if the system actually uses it as a stand-in for something else downstream, rather than the state merely being decodable by an outside observer with the right tool. That demand seems to cut both ways in the interpretability case. On one hand, it's exactly what the bridge-clamping demonstration seems to satisfy, since turning the feature up changed the model's behavior in the right direction rather than just sitting there to be read off. On the other hand, I also learned about the case of a small model trained only to predict legal moves in the board game Othello, where a straightforward probe for the actual board state (which squares were black or white) mostly failed, and only a probe framed around a different distinction — whose piece a square held relative to whoever's turn it was, rather than its absolute color — turned up a clean, linear signal. That example unsettled me, because it suggests the representation is only visible under a description someone had to go hunting for, which makes me suspect that "realism" about these features is partly an artifact of researchers eventually finding a probe or a steering direction that works, rather than a discovery about some representation that was determinately there all along, describable in one specific way.

Could you write me an essay that actually takes a side on this? I'd like it to weigh the strongest case for treating findings like the monosemantic bridge feature, or circuits like the ones found for continuing a pattern from earlier in a passage or for working out which of two names in a sentence should get the object, as genuine representations in the philosophically serious sense, against the strongest case for a more deflationary reading on which none of this counts as representation for the network, only representation for us. Please engage directly with the "must be used, not just decodable" style of objection and with the basis-dependence problem the Othello example raises, rather than just listing them, and don't let the essay end on "reasonable people disagree" — I want a real verdict, even a conditional one (something like "realist about circuits that satisfy condition X, deflationary about the rest" would count), and I want to see why you land there rather than somewhere else.
