I was reporting a piece on how classical texts get pressed into service in contemporary debates about aging when I sat in on a small classics symposium. After the formal panel ended, two of the speakers -- a classicist and a philosopher -- kept arguing about Cicero's *De Senectute* over drinks, and I ended up just listening.

(1) The classicist argued that Cato's answer to the complaint that old age strips away pleasure is a substantive philosophical claim, not just a consolation prize: freed from the "tyranny" of bodily appetite, reason finally has room to operate unobstructed, so old age is in some sense better suited to distinctively human goods than youth is.

(2) The philosopher objected that Cato undercuts this himself a few pages later, when he praises the pleasures of farming, modest dinner parties, and conversation among friends -- ordinary hedonic goods, not purely rational ones. So Cato isn't subordinating pleasure to reason at all; he's just trading one set of pleasures for another.

(3) The classicist replied that there's no real tension: Cato can hold that reason should govern which pleasures are worth having, and how, without denying that well-chosen pleasure is still good. The target was appetite as master, not enjoyment as such.

(4) The philosopher shifted ground, saying none of this touches the deeper problem: Cato is a wealthy, healthy, still-influential former consul with an estate to run, so his reassurances amount to "old age is fine if you were already lucky and virtuous" -- cold comfort for the far more ordinary old age marked by poverty, illness, or irrelevance.

(5) The classicist answered that Cicero heads this off in advance: Cato insists that the querulousness, suspicion, and greed people blame on old age are faults of character that were there all along, not products of age itself, so a bad old age reflects a badly spent life rather than bad luck. On this view, the "privileged outlier" charge just restates Cato's own thesis rather than refuting it.

Neither of them budged, and I couldn't tell who had the better of it. Could you go through the exchange move by move and say who is right at each step -- not just who comes out ahead overall -- and flag anything you think both of them are missing?
