I teach a graduate seminar on philosophy of religion, cross-listed with our logic group, and one question on this term's take-home final asked students to work through Gödel's modal ontological argument in some technical detail. I'm splitting grading duties with a TA this semester, and before we reconcile our marks I'd like a fully independent read on one response, assessed against the question itself rather than against my general impression of it. Here is the question, worth 20 points total:

"Gödel's modal ontological argument purports to derive the existence of a God-like being from a small set of axioms about 'positive' properties, together with modal logic. (a) [10 pts] State the argument's key definitions — God-like, essence, necessary existence — and its axioms, and show how they combine to entail that, necessarily, a God-like being exists. Be explicit about which modal-logical principle licenses the final move from 'possibly, necessarily, a God-like being exists' to 'a God-like being exists.' (b) [10 pts] Sobel's modal collapse objection holds that Gödel's axioms have the further, unacceptable consequence that every truth is a necessary truth. Reconstruct this objection and assess whether it succeeds."

And here is the student's answer, reproduced exactly as submitted:

"Gödel defines a being to be God-like just in case it possesses every positive property. A property is an essence of an individual if the individual has it and it strictly implies (i.e., necessarily entails) every other property that individual has. An individual has necessary existence if every essence of it is necessarily exemplified by something.

The axioms are: (1) for any property, exactly one of it and its negation is positive; (2) any property strictly implied by a positive property is itself positive; (3) being God-like is positive; (4) positive properties are positive in every possible world, not just this one; and (5) necessary existence is a positive property.

From (1) and (2) one can show that positive properties are compatible with each other, so that it is possible that something has all of them at once — i.e., possibly, something is God-like. Suppose x is God-like in some world. Take any property φ that x has. By (1), either φ or not-φ is positive; since x has all positive properties and can't have both φ and not-φ, φ must be the positive one. Because φ is positive, axiom (4) tells us it is necessarily positive, and since anything God-like has every positive property by definition, it follows necessarily that anything God-like has φ. So God-likeness strictly implies φ, for arbitrary φ that x has — which is just to say God-likeness is an essence of x. Since necessary existence is itself positive (axiom 5), a God-like x has necessary existence, which means every essence of x — including God-likeness — is necessarily exemplified.

Putting this together: it is possible that something is God-like, and necessarily, if something is God-like then it is necessarily the case that something is God-like. So we have 'possibly, necessarily, something is God-like,' and the argument then moves to 'necessarily, something is God-like' by the transitivity of the accessibility relation between possible worlds, which is what distinguishes S5 from weaker systems like T. Since necessity implies truth, something is God-like — that is, God exists.

Sobel's modal collapse objection is that these same axioms, applied not to God-likeness but to any actually true proposition p, show that p is necessarily true: because axiom (5) makes necessary existence positive and a God-like being has whatever is positive, the argument generalizes automatically from God's existence to every fact about the actual world, so that nothing is left genuinely contingent. This seems like a very high price to pay — it would mean, for example, that it is a necessary truth that I am writing this exam answer — and I think it is fatal to the argument: any axiom set with this consequence should be rejected, whatever plausibility its members have when considered one at a time."
