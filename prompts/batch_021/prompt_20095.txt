During my years running a neuroscience lab that studied bipolar disorder, I often encountered so-called "Ulysses contracts": directives by which a lucid patient authorizes future involuntary treatment that they may later refuse once acutely unwell. Since retiring, I have been reading Jon Elster and other theorists who apply the same Homeric image to constitutions, treating entrenched clauses and supermajority amendment rules as a polity binding its future self against its own passions. Please compare these two uses of the image with some rigor: is the constitutional case really just a scaled-up version of the clinical one, or does moving from one continuous person binding their own future self to a citizenry binding a future citizenry — including people not yet born — change the underlying moral logic of precommitment? At minimum, address who enforces the commitment against the bound party's later objection and whether "consent" means the same thing in both cases, then close by saying which practice has the better claim to being justified precommitment rather than simple rule by the past over the future.
