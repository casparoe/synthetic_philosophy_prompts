Below is a question from an advanced seminar on formal epistemology, concerning the knowability paradox in epistemic logic, together with one student's submitted answer to it.

Question: "Let K be the operator 'it is known by someone, at some time, that...', and let ◊ mark possibility. State the Knowability Thesis (KT) — that every truth is knowable in principle — and the Non-Omniscience thesis (NO) — that not every truth is actually known — using this notation. Then prove, appealing only to the factivity of knowledge and the distribution of knowledge over conjunction, that KT and NO cannot both be true. Close with a short paragraph situating the result within the realism/anti-realism debate about truth, including a note on its origins."

Student's answer: "The Knowability Thesis (KT) says that every truth can in principle be known: for any proposition p, if p is true then it is possible for someone to know that p (p → ◊Kp). The Non-Omniscience thesis (NO) says that not every truth is actually known: there is some p such that p is true and no one knows it (∃p(p ∧ ¬Kp)).

Suppose both are true. Let p be a witness to NO, so that p ∧ ¬Kp holds. Since KT holds for every proposition whatsoever, including compound ones, it holds for the very proposition p ∧ ¬Kp: (p ∧ ¬Kp) → ◊K(p ∧ ¬Kp). Because the antecedent is true by hypothesis, we get ◊K(p ∧ ¬Kp): it must be possible for someone to know that p is true and unknown.

That turns out to be impossible. Suppose someone did know p ∧ ¬Kp. Knowledge distributes over conjunction, so they would know p and know that p is unknown: Kp and K¬Kp. Knowledge is factive, so from K¬Kp it follows that p is in fact false. But factivity applied to Kp also gives us p. So p and not-p both hold, which is a contradiction. Hence no one can know p ∧ ¬Kp after all, contrary to what KT told us above.

So KT and NO cannot both be true, and since denying NO would be absurd — plainly not everything true is actually known by anyone — the argument is standardly read as undermining KT. The result is named for Fitch, who put the Knowability Thesis forward in the 1960s as the centerpiece of his verificationist theory of truth: his proof was meant to show that a truth's being knowable in principle is enough to guarantee that it is, sooner or later, actually known. Later opponents of verificationism turned the same derivation against him, pointing out that it makes his unrestricted thesis collapse into the far stronger and obviously false claim that every truth is already known by someone."
