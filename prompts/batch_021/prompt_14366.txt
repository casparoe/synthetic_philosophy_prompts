A colleague told me about a disagreement on a three-person graduate admissions committee on which she sits. Committee policy admits an applicant if and only if both (i) the quantitative background is adequate and (ii) the fit with a potential advisor is adequate. For one applicant, the judgments were: Alvarez — background adequate, fit adequate, personal verdict admit; Bennett — background adequate, fit inadequate, personal verdict reject; Chen — background inadequate, fit adequate, personal verdict reject. A majority found the background adequate (Alvarez and Bennett), and a majority found the fit adequate (Alvarez and Chen), so Alvarez, who chaired the meeting, declared the applicant admitted on the strength of these two majorities — even though a majority of the committee, Bennett and Chen, had each personally concluded that the applicant should be rejected. Bennett later raised this with Alvarez. (1) Bennett said the committee's ruling does not track what any majority of its members actually believes should happen, since two of the three, by their own all-things-considered judgment, think the applicant should not be admitted; a procedure that overrules the majority's actual verdict has gone wrong somewhere. (2) Alvarez replied that voting directly on the bottom line would let each member's private, unstated considerations, or an idiosyncratic personal weighting of the two criteria, decide the outcome; the committee fixed the two criteria in advance precisely so that the result would track reasoned majorities on those criteria rather than unanalyzed overall impressions. (3) Bennett answered that this missed her situation: her judgment to reject was not grounded in some third, unstated factor but followed directly from her assessment that the fit criterion fails, one of the two agreed conditions; she had simply weighed the evidence for it differently than the majority did, and a procedure that consults only her yes-or-no answer on each criterion in isolation discards exactly the judgment she was appointed to exercise. (4) Alvarez responded that if direct votes on the conclusion governed instead, the committee's stated reason for its decision would no longer correspond to any shared position on the criteria at all, since the majority for the conclusion and the majorities for the criteria can diverge, exactly as they did here; the committee would then be unable to justify its verdict, to the applicant or to itself, in terms of the standards it claims to apply.
