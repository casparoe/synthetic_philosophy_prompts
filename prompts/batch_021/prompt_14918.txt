Over the last month I have been sitting in on a graduate seminar on the philosophy of vagueness, largely because it collided head-on with my day job of rewriting the review policy for the trust-and-safety classifier that decides whether a post counts as "harassment." When I ran the model over a hand-edited sequence of ninety near-identical comments, each just a shade more hostile than the one before it, its verdict flipped from "not harassment" to "harassment" at one specific comment, with nothing in the system able to explain why that comment crossed the line and its neighbor did not. My professor's reaction was to hand me Williamson's Vagueness and Keefe's Theories of Vagueness and tell me I had simply rediscovered the sorites paradox, and that epistemicists and supervaluationists disagree precisely about what to say about cases like mine. Four weeks in, I can reconstruct each position well enough on its own, but in class I have mostly seen them talk past each other rather than really engage.

Could you write me a dialogue between a committed epistemicist and a committed supervaluationist who are conducting a forced-march sorites together, calling out "tall" or "not tall" for each person in a line of a hundred, ordered from clearly short to clearly tall? I would like the epistemicist to defend the claim that there is a real, sharp, permanently unknowable cutoff somewhere in that line, and to explain why our ignorance of it differs in kind from ordinary ignorance, such as not knowing the number of grains in a heap — presumably via something like a margin-for-error story about inexact knowledge. I would like the supervaluationist to defend truth as supertruth over admissible specifications instead, and to give a real answer, not a hand-wave, to the standard charge that this "definitely tall" and borderline-case apparatus just relocates the sharp-boundary problem rather than dissolving it, i.e., the problem of higher-order vagueness. Above all, I want the two of them actually pressing each other on exactly these weak points and responding to what the other has just said, rather than each restating their own view from the top — neither should come off as the strawman here. If one of them happens to say something that bears on my classifier problem, all the better, but that is a bonus, not the point of the exercise.
