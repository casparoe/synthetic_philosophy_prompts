I spent thirty-six years directing a materials-science laboratory before retiring three years ago, and I have since been using much of my new-found time to finally read philosophy properly, mostly on my own and through a small discussion group at the local university. A minor puzzle left over from my working life has resurfaced in a form I cannot quite reason my way out of, and I would value your perspective on it.

Looking back on the laboratory years, the periods I remember most fondly were rarely the ones that mattered most by any external measure: not the grant renewed on schedule, nor the paper that finally satisfied a hostile referee, but the odd afternoon spent running down some anomalous reading purely because it was strange, with no publication or promotion riding on it. At the time I filed this away as simple stress relief. Since retiring, the same pattern has reappeared in a form I find harder to dismiss. I built myself a rigorous, chronological philosophy curriculum, logged my progress in a spreadsheet, and set weekly quotas much as I once set them for a postdoc's experiments, on the assumption that this discipline was itself what would make the undertaking worthwhile. Yet the hours I actually look forward to turn out to be quite different ones: unplanned evenings at the piano with my granddaughter, sight-reading whatever score happens to be lying around and producing nothing that could go into any spreadsheet. By the standard I applied throughout my career, that would count as idling and the curriculum as the serious use of a life; in practice, my experience of the two is almost the reverse.

This bothers me more than it probably should, since it seems to threaten an assumption I had never before had occasion to examine: that a stretch of a life becomes worthwhile in proportion to the sustained, directed effort invested in reaching something beyond the present moment. My wife tells me I am overthinking a simple case of having worked too hard for too long and needing to relax, but that diagnosis does not fit what I actually experience: the piano evenings do not feel like relaxation so much as a different, more complete way of being occupied. I would be grateful for help thinking through why these unplanned, ostensibly purposeless hours feel more worthwhile to me than the disciplined ones, and for your honest opinion on whether I ought to restructure my remaining years around that observation, rather than continuing to treat it as a pleasant but inessential quirk of temperament.
