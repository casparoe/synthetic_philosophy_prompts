I'm a PhD student in formal epistemology working through I. J. Good's 1967 theorem that a Bayesian agent who will act optimally on whatever they learn can never expect, in expected-utility terms, to do worse by consulting a cost-free, informative signal before acting than by acting without it. The result is often read as vindicating Carnap's total-evidence requirement, and it gets invoked in rationalist and Bayesian decision-theory writing as a near-general license for curiosity: since free information can only help in expectation, an ideal reasoner should always want to look before leaping.

I suspect that slogan claims more than the theorem actually delivers. Three considerations cut against it: (i) strategic settings, where what a player is known or believed to know can be exploited by others, so that being better-informed can leave that player worse off even granting optimal use of the information -- the way public disclosure can unravel voluntary insurance and other risk-sharing arrangements is one instance; (ii) Good's own distinction between idealized rationality and rationality that also prices in the cost of deliberation, since processing evidence, even when the evidence itself is free, is not costless; and (iii) cases -- a terminal diagnosis, a spoiler, a friend's unflattering opinion of you -- of the sort bioethicists discuss under the heading of a "right not to know," where the disvalue seems to attach to the knowing itself rather than to any action the knowledge could improve.

Write an essay that first states Good's theorem precisely enough to make its assumptions explicit, then assesses whether considerations like these genuinely restrict its scope or can instead be absorbed into the theorem's own framework. Reach and defend a specific, general verdict on when, if ever, a rational agent is justified in preferring not to know something or in declining freely available evidence, rather than simply cataloguing considerations on both sides.
