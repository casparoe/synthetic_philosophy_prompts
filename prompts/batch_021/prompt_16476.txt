I found this quite surprising, since the only Bentham I had encountered before was through the felicific calculus and the panopticon in our ethics unit, and I had never really considered how that same pleasure-and-pain framework could be turned toward something as socially explosive as sodomy laws in his own era.

Could you walk me through the actual mechanics of his reasoning, rather than just the historical trivia surrounding it? Specifically, I would like to understand how Bentham leans on his distinction between the principle of utility and what he calls the "principle of sympathy and antipathy" to argue that laws criminalizing these acts track unreasoned prejudice rather than any calculable harm, and how he goes about dismantling the standard objections of his day — that such acts are "unnatural," that they offend religious teaching, or that they threaten the state by reducing population — all while staying strictly within his own hedonistic accounting of pleasures and pains. I would also be curious to hear your thoughts on the irony that a thinker who spent his entire career publicly agitating for legal reform apparently judged this particular argument too dangerous to publish in his own lifetime.
