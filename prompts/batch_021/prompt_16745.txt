I've spent the past few weeks reporting on the growing use of psychiatric advance directives — legal instruments that let someone bind their future, possibly incompetent self, much the way Ulysses had his sailors lash him to the mast before the ship passed the Sirens. Political theorists — most systematically Jon Elster, in *Ulysses and the Sirens* and its later sequel, *Ulysses Unbound* — extend the same image to constitutional design: entrenched clauses, supermajority requirements for amendment, independent central banks, all cast as a polity binding its future self against its own worst impulses. It's an intuitive analogy, and I've leaned on it myself in a couple of articles without examining it too closely. But the more I sit with it, the shakier it looks, and I don't think the doubt is only mine: Elster, who did more than anyone to popularize the comparison, devotes real attention to cataloguing where it breaks down. When Ulysses is tied to the mast, the person being restrained and the person doing the restraining are, barring the temporary derangement induced by the Sirens' song, one and the same individual across a short stretch of time. When a constitution binds "future generations," though, as Jefferson wrote to Madison in 1789, reasoning from mortality tables that a constitution or law should lapse after about nineteen years — the span in which half the adults who had agreed to it would already be dead — the people ratifying the constraint and the people living under it decades later are not remotely the same agents: most of the latter were never born, let alone consulted.

For a piece I'm drafting, I'd like your help pressure-testing this analogy properly. Compare the individual-level Ulysses contract with constitutional precommitment devices: where does the comparison genuinely hold, and where does it come apart once you take seriously that the constitutional "future self" is a changing population rather than a single continuing person with a momentary lapse in judgment? Is there a principled way to repair the analogy — for instance, by treating a political community as an agent that persists through its members rather than through any one continuous consciousness, or by distinguishing entrenchment that merely raises the cost of change from entrenchment that forecloses it outright — or is the Ulysses framing, however rhetorically convenient, simply being asked to do more work than it can bear once exported from personal self-control to constitutional design across generations? Don't just list similarities and differences; reach and defend an actual verdict.
