So I've been reading about the discursive dilemma for a social choice seminar, and honestly I feel like everyone is overcomplicating what seems like a pretty easy fix. The classic toy example is a three-judge panel where liability legally requires two separate things both being true (say, that the defendant had a duty of care AND that the duty was breached). If the judges vote on each condition separately and then apply the rule, you can get "liable" as the outcome even though, if you look at each judge's overall view of the whole case, a majority of them don't actually think the defendant should be liable. Everyone treats this as some deep unsolved puzzle in judgment aggregation.

But isn't the fix just... don't vote issue-by-issue? Have each judge cast one up-or-down vote on the bottom-line verdict itself, then go with whatever the majority says on THAT. That seems to make the mismatch mathematically impossible, since your not stitching together two different majorities that never actually agreed on anything as a bloc in the first place. My professor keeps insisting that legal theorists still argue about premise-based vs conclusion-based procedures for good reason, and that switching to conclusion-based voting doesn't make the underlying problem go away, just relocates it somewhere else. I don't get what's even left to argue about once the actual inconsistency is gone though. Is she just being precious about a problem that's basically solved, or is there something real that survives even after you fix the voting rule like this?
