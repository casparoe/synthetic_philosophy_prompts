I'm researching background for a novel whose narrator slowly becomes convinced that everyone close to her lacks any inner life at all -- that they are, in every observable respect, perfect imitations of conscious people. While sitting in on philosophy-of-mind talks to get this right, I watched two researchers who've sparred with each other since graduate school -- Devi, a philosopher, and Callum, a cognitive scientist -- fall into a genuine argument at the reception afterward, one I'd like to adapt faithfully into a scene rather than rig into a strawman fight for one side to win. It went roughly like this.

(1) Devi opened with the standard case: we can coherently conceive of a being physically and functionally identical to a conscious person, down to the last synapse, yet with no subjective experience at all -- what philosophers call a zombie. Nothing in that scenario harbors a hidden contradiction, she said, so it describes a genuine possibility -- and if such a being is possible, consciousness is something over and above the physical facts, so physicalism is false. (2) Callum objected that conceivability is a bad guide to possibility here: before modern chemistry, people could conceive of water turning out not to be H2O, yet that turned out to be impossible once we learned what water actually is, since the identity, though necessary, was only knowable a posteriori. Pain and its physical basis could be exactly this kind of case, he said, so the conceivability of a zombie shows only our ignorance of a hidden identity, not a real gap in nature. (3) Devi replied that the water case doesn't transfer: our concept of water fixes onto its referent indirectly, through some rough description like 'the clear stuff that fills the lakes,' which is exactly why chemistry could tell us something new about what water is. Our concept of pain isn't like that, she said -- it picks out the felt quality directly, with nothing playing the role 'clear stuff in lakes' plays for water, so there is no further fact for any future science to discover about what pain really is beneath how it feels. (4) Callum answered that this simply restates what's in dispute: several physicalists argue that phenomenal concepts could be recognitional concepts that latch onto physical states without conceptually revealing that this is what they are doing -- which would produce exactly the appearance of a direct, nothing-further-to-discover concept that Devi was describing, with no actual gap in the world behind it. So, he said, Devi hadn't shown any disanalogy with the water case; she had only redescribed her conclusion as though it were a premise.

Neither budged, and I don't want to load the scene in either character's favor. Could you go through the exchange move by move and say who has the better of it at each stage, including whether Callum's closing charge -- that Devi is begging the question -- actually lands? I'd also like to know what each of them is quietly taking for granted that a sharper version of either character should have to defend.
