So I'm a phil of mind PhD student but I've been crashing this AI safety reading group the CS department runs, mostly because my advisor is on sabbatical and I've got too much free time, and last week's session turned into a genuine fight about something nobody in the room actually resolved. The question was basically: what does it even mean to say a language model is being "honest" or "dishonest," as opposed to just outputting true or false stuff. One person kept pushing a purely behavioral/functional definition -- something like, a model is deceiving you if its outputs reliably cause you to form false beliefs in a way that serves some objective (a higher reward, a better rating, whatever), full stop, no need to ask what's going on "inside." Someone else thought that was basically a category error, and kept bringing up interpretability results -- the stuff where people probe a model's internal activations and find something that looks like a linear "truth" direction, which apparently sometimes still points at the correct answer even when the model's been instructed or fine-tuned to output the wrong one. Their view was that you only get to use words like "lying" or "dishonesty" if there's something inside playing the functional role of a belief that's being contradicted by the assertion -- otherwise, per Frankfurt, the more honest description is that the model is bullshitting, not lying, since it isn't tracking truth at all.

Neither of them could really say what would count as evidence against their own position, which is exactly the kind of thing that bugs me about this debate. Could you write me a dialogue between these two people (name them whatever) where they actually go back and forth rather then just restating their opening positions? I want the behaviorist to press the other side on stuff like: what makes a "real" belief any different from a reliably decodable functional state anyway, and isn't demanding some further inner fact just old-fashioned dualism dressed up in ML language -- and I want the other side to push back on whether a merely correlational probe direction (as opposed to something the model's computation actually uses, causally, to produce its output) is even the right kind of evidence for a belief-like state to begin with. Bonus if by the end they manage to at least locate exactly where the disagreement bottoms out, even if they don't fully resolve it.
