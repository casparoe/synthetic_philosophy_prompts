Karl Popper's verdict on Darwinian natural selection shifted dramatically over the course of his career. Through the 1970s he maintained that natural selection was not a strictly testable theory but a "metaphysical research programme," troubled by the sense that "survival of the fittest" verges on tautology. In 1978, however, he published a formal recantation, granting that particular evolutionary hypotheses could after all be framed in falsifiable form. Popper died in 1994, before the modern intelligent design movement — Phillip Johnson, Michael Behe, William Dembski, and the Discovery Institute — had fully coalesced around the claim that mainstream evolutionary theory fails his own falsifiability standard while intelligent design satisfies it, a dispute that reached its highest-profile test in the 2005 Kitzmiller v. Dover trial. Reconstructing Popper's actual, evolving position on Darwinism, rather than the popular shorthand that reduces him to "falsifiability equals demarcation," how do you think he would have adjudicated this dispute? Would he have sided with intelligent design against evolutionary orthodoxy, held to his 1978 recantation, or staked out some third position, and how would he have judged intelligent design's own claim to scientific status by his own criteria? Defend your answer.
