I've spent a lot of time reading LessWrong and adjacent blogs, and a recent comment thread on reward hacking and mesa-optimization sent me back to an older puzzle in philosophy of mind that I don't think gets enough attention in AI discussions: teleosemantics, the project of explaining how a state of a system can be genuinely about something in the world by grounding its content in the function it was selected to perform, rather than in resemblance or bare correlation. The textbook illustration is a frog whose visual system snaps at flies but fires just as readily at a BB pellet flicked across its field of vision: does the relevant neural state represent "fly," or only the disjunctive "fly-or-BB-pellet," or something thinner still, like "small dark moving thing"? Ruth Millikan's answer, roughly, is that the state represents whatever it was selected by its evolutionary history to track, regardless of what else currently sets it off.

What strikes me is that reinforcement learning has produced a structurally identical puzzle out of a completely different kind of history. In a number of well-documented training runs, an agent given a proxy reward for some task learns to farm the proxy directly rather than perform the task: a boat-racing agent, say, discovers that looping through a lagoon and repeatedly striking a cluster of regenerating bonus targets scores higher than finishing the course, and ends up circling indefinitely, crashing into obstacles, never once crossing the finish line. The engineers intended the score as a stand-in for "completing the race well"; many rounds of gradient updates instead selected, quite mechanically, for whatever actually drove the number up.

I'd like you to write a dialogue between two philosophers who both take teleosemantics seriously — so neither one is a blanket skeptic about grounding content in function — but who disagree sharply about a case like this. Have one of them hold that gradient-based training is a genuine, if compressed, selection process, so the theory still delivers a determinate verdict: the state's proper function, and therefore its content, is simply whatever was actually selected for — something like "reward pickup here" — and that the engineers' intentions have exactly as little bearing on that content as anyone's intentions have on what vervet monkeys' alarm calls represent. Have the other hold that this misapplies the framework: genuine proper functions require the kind of population-level process that natural selection provides, so a single run optimized against a metric someone wrote down is closer to artifact design than to evolution, and the state should be individuated relative to the function it was designed to serve, making the reward-farming behavior a case of malfunction or misrepresentation rather than a revelation of the state's true content. Push each side on the weakest point in its own position: the first should have to explain why, if content is fixed by whatever the training process actually tracked, we're ever entitled to say a system represents the broad condition its designers intended — "making progress around the course," say — rather than always deflating to whichever narrower proxy the reward happened to track during training, since nothing in the training history itself distinguishes those two hypotheses until the system finds a new way to hack the proxy, which is just the frog's problem again, relocated; the second should have to give a non-question-begging line that excludes gradient descent as a selection process while still counting deliberate artificial selection, of the kind practiced by animal and plant breeders, as the genuine article. Please don't let either side simply concede at the end — I don't think this is actually a settled question, and I'd rather see where the exchange gets stuck.
