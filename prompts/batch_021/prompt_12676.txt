I'm a PhD student in the history of economic thought, currently rereading Keynes's 1930 essay "Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren." Its central claim is that compounding growth would, within a century, have essentially solved humanity's "economic problem," leaving something close to a fifteen-hour working week and shifting the real difficulty onto the psychological and moral work of learning to enjoy abundance rather than simply striving for more. He builds this on a few specific moves: distinguishing absolute needs (eventually satiable) from relative needs (satisfied only by having more than others, and so never fully satiable), coining "technological unemployment" for the transitional pain caused by labour-saving innovation outrunning new uses for labour, and suggesting that traits like avarice and financial caution, though indispensable for now, would eventually have to be dethroned as virtues once scarcity was overcome.

We're most of the way through his hundred years: output per head is many multiples of 1930 levels, yet working hours have fallen far less than he expected, status-driven overwork persists well past the point of satisfied material need, and AI-driven automation is reviving exactly the kind of anxiety his "technological unemployment" was meant to defuse. Using Keynes's own conceptual apparatus rather than the standard "the gains went to capital, not labour" explanation, how would he most plausibly account for this gap between forecast and reality, and would the gap lead him to revise any part of that apparatus, or merely to restate it more forcefully?
