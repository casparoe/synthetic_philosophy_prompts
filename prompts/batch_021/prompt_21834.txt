Over dinner last week, a friend and I spent almost three hours arguing about imprecise probabilities, and neither of us budged. She holds the orthodox line that rational credence has to be given by a single, sharp probability function, on pain of not counting as a genuine attitude toward the truth at all; I found myself defending the imprecise camp, on the grounds that when evidence is scant, ambiguous, or symmetric, representing your credal state by a whole set of probability functions -- sometimes called a 'representor' -- is more honest than pretending to a precision the evidence doesn't support. As it happens, I co-host a small podcast on formal epistemology, and next month's guest is a philosopher who has spent her career defending imprecise credences against exactly the kind of sharp-probability orthodoxy my friend was pressing. I'd like the interview to pick up roughly where the dinner argument left off, rather than retreading the motivating examples everyone reaches for first, like life on other planets or the bias of some unknown coin.

Could you draft eight to ten interview questions for her? Since I ended up on her side of the argument, I don't want this to read as a friendly softball for a view I'm sympathetic to, so please make the questions press exactly where my friend pressed me and I didn't have a good answer. I'd like at least one question on the dilation problem: cases where conditioning an imprecise credal state on new evidence makes it less precise rather than more, which seems backward for a theory that's supposed to track the evidence. I'd like one built around Elga's argument that any way of stringing together a sequence of decisions under imprecise credences either collapses back into something functionally sharp or leaves the agent open to a guaranteed loss across the sequence. I'd also like a question on the dilemma facing decision rules for acting on imprecise credences: a rule like Γ-maximin (maximize your minimum expected utility across the whole set) tends to hand back a single determinate act anyway, which raises the question of what the imprecision at the level of belief was buying you in the first place, while a more permissive rule like E-admissibility (any act that maximizes expected utility relative to at least one distribution in the set) can leave you no closer to knowing what to do. And I'd like a last question on where the boundary of the representor itself is supposed to come from, and whether drawing that boundary is just as arbitrary as picking the single number the view was invented to avoid. For each question, please add a short follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give, especially if it amounts to some version of 'that's exactly what imprecision is for.'
