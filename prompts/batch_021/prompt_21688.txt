I sit on my philosophy department's graduate admissions committee, and for the past two years I've also taught a graduate seminar on social epistemology that covers the classic economic literature on information cascades and rational herding. The two collided rather vividly at last week's meeting, where I watched what looked like a textbook case of the phenomenon unfold in real time, and afterward two colleagues of mine, Marcus and Helena, got into a disagreement about it that I found myself unable to referee.

Our procedure is to have each of the committee's nine members state, once everyone has read the file, whether they support admitting a given applicant, going around the table in a fixed order. For one borderline candidate, the first three speakers all said yes, and so did the remaining six, including two people whom Helena happened to know privately had serious reservations. Afterward, she and Marcus argued roughly as follows. (1) Helena said this was simple bandwagon voting, and that we should scrap the format for simultaneous, private ballots. (2) Marcus objected that "bandwagon" begs the question: since everyone had read the same file, a run of "yes" votes is itself evidence about its contents, so a rational Bayesian who privately leaned "no" but then hears three independent colleagues say "yes" has genuine reason to update — exactly the mechanism behind Bikhchandani-Hirshleifer-Welch-style informational cascades, not a sign that anyone behaved irrationally. (3) Helena granted that the first deferral or two might be rational in that sense, but said the deeper problem survives the concession: once a cascade starts, no later vote carries any further information, so our apparent nine-person consensus was really only ever a three-person judgment wearing a nine-person costume, which defeats the entire point of having nine people independently read the file. (4) Marcus conceded this, and agreed that something like Helena's fix — or perhaps just having each speaker state their private reasons rather than a bare verdict — was warranted, but insisted this was a mechanism-design correction for a cascade problem, not evidence that anyone at the table had been irrational, as her original complaint implied. (5) Helena replied that his concession was too neat: some of what she saw looked less like Bayesian updating on the informational content of "yes" votes than like colleagues not wanting to be the lone dissenter in front of senior people who'd remember it later — a reputational motive of the kind Scharfstein and Stein describe in fund managers who herd to protect their records, which has nothing to do with extracting information from others' choices, and which a fix aimed at cascades, such as requiring stated reasons, might not touch, and could even make worse.

Could you go through Marcus and Helena's exchange move by move and say who has the better of the argument at each of the five numbered points, and why? I'd also like to know whether a substantive disagreement is still standing between them by the end, or whether one of them has quietly absorbed the other's point without acknowledging it, and whether there's anything relevant that both of them are overlooking. I'd welcome your own recommendation, at the end, on whether we should actually change the committee's procedure.
