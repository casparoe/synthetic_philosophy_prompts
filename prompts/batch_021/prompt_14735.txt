I don't have any formal training in philosophy, but I've spent a good part of the last year working through Derek Parfit's Reasons and Persons on my own, largely because a friend kept insisting it was one of those books that permanently changes how you think about yourself. Two ideas from that reading have stuck with me, and lately they've started to bump into each other in a way I haven't seen discussed anywhere. The first is Parfit's argument that personal identity over time isn't the deep, all-or-nothing fact we normally assume, but reduces to overlapping chains of physical and psychological continuity that can hold to a greater or lesser degree - what people often call his "reductionist" view. The second is the "hitchhiker" scenario that gets discussed a lot in decision theory circles, usually under Parfit's name: you're dying of thirst in the desert, and a driver will only agree to take you into town if she can somehow tell in advance that you'll actually pay her once you arrive safely, even though paying up at that point does nothing further for you causally - by then the ride has already been secured.

Almost everything I've read about the hitchhiker case treats it purely as a puzzle about rationality: is it rational to be the kind of agent who pays even once paying no longer helps you, and does that show something is wrong with judging rationality act by act rather than by whole dispositions or policies (this is basically the move that newer approaches like functional decision theory make explicit, by evaluating the output of a decision procedure rather than a single isolated choice)? What nobody seems to ask is how this sits alongside Parfit's own view of identity. If the "you" standing safely in town is only weakly connected, in Parfit's own terms, to the "you" who struck the bargain out in the desert, it's not obvious that the ordinary self-interested reasons that are supposed to make paying worthwhile actually transfer cleanly from one to the other - the question of whether town-you is rationally required to sacrifice for desert-you seems to assume more unity between them than the reductionist view really allows.

What I'd like is the strongest argument you can build for the claim that Parfit's reductionism about personal identity actually undermines, rather than supports, the standard verdict that a rational agent should pay the driver. Please engage seriously with whatever you think is really doing the work in the usual "you should pay" verdict - dispositional rationality, resolute choice, functional decision theory, or whatever you judge the strongest version of that case to be - before showing concretely where the reductionist picture of the self cuts against it; I don't want an argument that just gestures at identity being "fuzzy" and stops there. I'd also like you to deal with the obvious complication that Parfit himself thought his reductionism should make people more willing to make sacrifices across time and toward others, not less, since it narrows the gap between self-interested and other-regarding reasons generally - does that end up rescuing the "pay the driver" conclusion anyway, just on different, less purely self-interested grounds, or does the argument you built survive that complication?
