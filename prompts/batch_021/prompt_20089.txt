A friend from my local rationalist meetup and I spent most of a recent evening arguing about which of the two classic axiomatic bargaining solutions is more defensible—Nash's or Kalai and Smorodinsky's—and neither of us managed to convince the other. I first ran into this rivalry through old LessWrong and Overcoming Bias threads about which solution concept should govern bargaining between agents with differing values (the kind of question that comes up in discussions of cooperative AI, or of splitting gains from trade more generally), and it has bothered me ever since that I've only ever seen the two solutions set side by side in a table of axioms, never actually argued out by someone who holds one of the positions.

I understand the basic setup well enough. Both solutions apply to a convex feasible set of utility pairs together with a disagreement point, and both satisfy Pareto efficiency, symmetry, and invariance to positive affine transformations of the players' utilities. They diverge on the fourth axiom: Nash's solution, which maximizes the product of each player's utility gain over the disagreement point, is the unique one that additionally satisfies Independence of Irrelevant Alternatives (IIA); Kalai and Smorodinsky's solution is the unique one that instead satisfies what they called Individual Monotonicity (roughly, that if the feasible set expands in a way that only raises the best-case outcome available to one player, that player shouldn't end up worse off). Since the two solutions actually disagree on plenty of bargaining problems, each must fail the other's signature axiom somewhere—and I'd like to see that tension played out concretely rather than just asserted.

Could you write me a dialogue between a committed Nash-solution proponent and a committed Kalai-Smorodinsky proponent working through this disagreement together? I'd like each side, at some point, to construct a concrete numerical bargaining scenario (the numbers can be as simple as you like) in which the other's solution produces an outcome they find objectionable: the KS proponent presenting a case where Nash's solution violates individual monotonicity, and the Nash proponent presenting a case where the KS solution's reliance on the "ideal point" makes it sensitive to alternatives that were never actually available for choosing, thereby violating IIA. Each side should engage directly with the other's specific example—whether by biting the bullet, disputing the example, or arguing that the intuition behind the other's axiom is less compelling than it first appears—rather than delivering parallel monologues, and both should come across as equally sharp; I don't want either axiom strawmanned. There's no need to engineer a resolution at the end—I'd rather see the real structure of the disagreement laid bare than watch a winner get crowned.
