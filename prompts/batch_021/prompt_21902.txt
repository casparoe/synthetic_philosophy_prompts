I've been mulling over the centipede game and what's sometimes called the backward induction paradox, after a disagreement with a colleague about why two engineers "negotiating" over scope during a long feature-flag rollout kept extending trust to each other well past the point where a purely self-interested, forward-looking calculation would seem to recommend stopping. I don't have any formal training in game theory or philosophy, so I wrote up my own understanding of the puzzle, along with what I think might be a resolution, and I'd like a sanity check before I bring it to my team's informal "paper club."

Here's my summary of the puzzle. In the centipede game, standard backward induction reasoning says that at the very last decision node, whoever's turn it is strictly prefers to end the game and take their share rather than pass; anticipating this, the second-to-last mover should also end things there rather than pass into a node where they know they'll be exploited, and so on, all the way back to the first move. The conclusion is that fully rational players should end the game immediately, even though almost any amount of sustained mutual cooperation would leave both of them richer. What I find genuinely puzzling isn't just that this prediction fails in practice, since people keep cooperating for a while, both in lab experiments and in ordinary life. It's that I recall reading that Aumann proved something close to the opposite of what I'd have naively guessed: that common knowledge of rationality among the players doesn't merely permit the backward-induction outcome, it actually forces it. Apparently this result was (and still is) controversial precisely because the argument seems to require reasoning about how players would behave at nodes that, under the very outcome being predicted, are never actually reached, which raises the question of what "common knowledge of rationality" could even mean at a node whose occurrence would itself already be evidence that the theorem's premises had failed.

My own tentative resolution treats this the way I'd treat a violated precondition in a function's contract. The backward-induction argument at any given node implicitly assumes that everything upstream of that node went "according to spec," i.e., according to the equilibrium strategies. Once a player passes where the equilibrium said they should take, they've violated that precondition, and nothing downstream is still governed by the guarantees that made backward induction compelling in the first place, much like how a function's documented postconditions no longer bind once it's been called with invalid inputs. On this view, continuing to cooperate after a single deviation isn't "irrational" in some free-floating sense; it's a reasonable update given that the common-knowledge assumptions have already been shown false. I suspect this is either a well-known move dressed up in software-engineering vocabulary, or it's quietly dodging something that people who actually work on epistemic game theory would consider decisive.

Could you help me pressure-test this? I'd particularly value comments on: (1) whether I've represented Aumann's result and the objections to it accurately or garbled them somewhere (I believe the pushback came from people like Binmore and/or Reny, though I honestly am not confident I have the attributions right); (2) whether my "precondition violation" analogy actually tracks any recognized treatment of off-path beliefs in extensive-form games, or whether it just papers over the hardest part of the problem; and (3) whether there's a cleaner, more standard resolution of the paradox that I should go read instead of reinventing a worse version of it myself.
