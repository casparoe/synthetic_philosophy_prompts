Douglas Hofstadter's concept of superrationality holds that if perfectly rational agents recognize they face a symmetric decision problem, and recognize that any other superrational agent would reach the very same conclusion by the very same reasoning, then each can infer that whatever choice they arrive at, the other will arrive at too. Applied to a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma, this is supposed to make mutual cooperation the uniquely rational outcome, without communication, prior agreement, or any external enforcement mechanism.

Consider how Thomas Hobbes might have judged this argument. In Leviathan, Hobbes holds that the laws of nature -- including the requirement that men perform their covenants -- oblige only "in foro interno," as a standing disposition one ought to have, and not "in foro externo," as a requirement actually to act on, except where one can be assured that others will reciprocate; lacking such assurance, and lacking a sovereign power to compel compliance, he concludes that covenants without the sword are but words. Does Hobbes's own account of human psychology and the state of nature give him principled grounds for denying that mutual knowledge of shared rationality could itself supply the assurance he thought only a sovereign could provide? Or does his conception of reasoning as a form of mechanical reckoning make him more receptive to the logic of superrationality than his reputation for pessimism about human nature would suggest?
