Since retiring from a career in experimental physics, I have been working through the literature on just war theory, and I find myself stuck on the disagreement over what philosophers call the moral equality of combatants. Michael Walzer holds that soldiers who observe the laws of war may permissibly kill one another on the battlefield regardless of which side's cause is just, so that jus in bello is independent of jus ad bellum; Jeff McMahan denies this, arguing that only combatants fighting for a just cause are morally entitled to kill, so that most soldiers on the unjust side act wrongly even when they observe every rule of war, and can at most be excused rather than justified. I would like to see this disagreement argued out concretely rather than merely summarized. Could you write a dialogue between a committed Walzerian and a committed McMahanian who are asked to judge a single case: two infantrymen, each ignorant of whether his own side's cause is just, facing one another across no-man's-land with rifles raised? Have them press each other on the hardest points -- what a soldier's ignorance of his cause implies for his liability to be killed, the difference between an excused act and a justified one, and whether the sheer symmetry of the two men's situation carries any moral weight of its own -- so that each is forced to answer the other's actual argument rather than a simplified version of it.
