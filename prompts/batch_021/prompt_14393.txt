Bayes' theorem is treated as something close to settled epistemological law across the rationalist blogs and forums I've been reading for years: rational belief revision just is conditionalizing your credences on the evidence, and the "prior times likelihood ratio" apparatus gets presented as basically the last word on what it means for evidence to support a hypothesis. Einstein's general relativity is a stock example in that literature -- the way GR precisely accounted for the anomalous precession of Mercury's perihelion is held up as a paradigm case of a theory earning its keep. What none of that reading prepared me for is that this very example is also the textbook illustration of a serious, apparently still-unresolved objection to Bayesian confirmation theory: Clark Glymour's "problem of old evidence." The precession anomaly had been sitting in the astronomical data, already known with certainty, for decades before Einstein proposed GR in 1915. On ordinary Bayesian bookkeeping, then, the relevant credence was already 1 before the theory even existed, so conditionalizing on it again once GR arrived should be a no-op: the Bayes factor P(E|H)/P(E) is stuck at 1, and the very case everyone cites as a triumph of theory meeting evidence turns out, on strict conditionalization, not to be a case of confirmation at all.

I gather philosophers have proposed a few different fixes. One approach, associated with Garber, Jeffrey, and Niiniluoto, relaxes the idealization that agents are logically omniscient, so that discovering the previously unnoticed fact that H entails E counts as the genuinely new information doing the confirming. Another tries to rescue the intuition with some kind of counterfactual or hypothetical credence function standing in for what one's belief in E "would have been" without the benefit of hindsight. And there are more skeptical voices who doubt any purely probabilistic patch works at all, holding that whatever actually made the Mercury case so persuasive to scientists -- the absence of fudge factors, explanatory unification, something along those lines -- is doing work that conditionalization on its own cannot capture. I'd like an essay that lays out clearly why old evidence is a genuine problem for Bayesian confirmation theory rather than a mere bookkeeping quirk, presents the strongest version of each major fix together with its main cost, and then commits to an actual verdict: does some version of Bayesian conditionalization fully rescue the intuition that old evidence can confirm a new theory, or does the puzzle show that credence-updating alone cannot be the whole story about evidential support? Argue for a specific answer rather than settling for "reasonable people disagree."
