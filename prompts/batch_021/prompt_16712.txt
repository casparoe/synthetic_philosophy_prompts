I've just joined my university's committee that reviews historical honorary degrees, and our first task is reconsidering one awarded decades ago to a former head of government for a wartime bombing campaign that killed many civilians; that assignment sent me back to Anscombe's 1956 pamphlet opposing Oxford's own honorary degree for Truman. I'm now lining up a guest for my department's speaker series -- a contemporary just-war theorist who defends Anscombe's charge that Hiroshima and Nagasaki amounted to straightforward murder -- and I'd like eight or nine interview questions that go beyond restating that the bombings intentionally killed innocents and were therefore murder. I want at least one question on whether the doctrine of double effect can really exclude Truman's own preferred redescription of the civilian deaths as foreseen rather than chosen, one pressing the consequentialist rejoinder that refusing ever to kill the innocent, even to avert a costlier invasion, just privileges the absolutist's own clean hands over the people who die anyway, one on whether her argument, if sound, equally condemns the conventional firebombing of Japanese and German cities, and one on how Anscombe's later objection to nuclear deterrence as a conditional intention to murder should bear on today's deterrence postures. For each, add a follow-up that anticipates the answer she's most likely to give.
