As someone who teaches philosophy for a living, I was a little embarrassed that I couldn't give a better answer to a challenge my nephew put to me over the winter holidays. He'd just finished an introductory statistics course and was insufferably pleased with himself about it. Over dinner, he defended the theory that a particular pair of socks is "lucky" for his football team, since on the three occasions he'd worn them to watch a match, the team won. When I dismissed this as an obvious case of mistaking coincidence for cause, he shot back that I reason in exactly the same way myself: whenever I see smoke rising from a distant hillside, I unhesitatingly infer that there's a fire, even though I haven't personally verified every instance of smoke that has ever occurred. My inference, he pointed out, also just generalizes from a limited run of past correlations to a case I haven't checked — so what, if anything, makes it any more entitled to confidence than his?

I offered the usual gestures toward sample size, the availability of confounding explanations, and the presence of a plausible mechanism, but these felt, even as I said them, more like a list of after-the-fact excuses than a principled answer, and he wasn't slow to notice this either. I'd like a sharper treatment of the underlying issue than I managed to produce at the table: what, exactly, separates an observed regularity that's genuinely solid enough to license confident inference about new, unchecked cases from one that only looks that way, given that neither kind of regularity has been — or ever could be — checked against literally every instance of it?
