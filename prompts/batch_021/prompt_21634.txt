Okay so this came up in my philosophy of math reading group this week and I haven't been able to let it go. We were working through Skolem's paradox - the result where Lowenheim and Skolem showed that any first-order theory with an infinite model (which includes Zermelo's axioms for set theory) has to have a countable model too, even though those same axioms prove that uncountable sets exist. Skolem's own resolution back in 1922 was basically that "countable" isn't an absolute property of a set, just something relative to which model you happen to be looking from - the countable model is simply missing the bijection that would reveal its "uncountable" set to actually be countable, even though such a bijection exists outside that model. Zermelo hated this reading and argued it just showed first-order logic was too weak to properly capture set theory, rather than accepting that something as basic as cardinality could be relative like that.

What's been nagging me since is that Cantor died in 1918, missing all of this by about four years, but he had really specific, strong metaphysical commitments about the transfinite numbers - not just that theyre legitimate mathematical objects, but that the whole hierarchy of alephs is a determinate, objective fact. There's also his correspondence with Cardinal Franzelin where he distinguishes the "Transfinitum," the transfinite numbers, which he thought were fully graspable mathematically, from the "Absolute," which he thought was inherently beyond any mathematical determination and basically reserved for God. Given all that, what do you think Cantor himself would actually have said if Skolem had shown him this result? Would he have sided with Zermelo and treated it as a defect of first-order axiomatization rather than a problem for the transfinite numbers themselves, or would the fact that "uncountable" turns out to be model-relative have genuinely threatened his realism in a way he couldn't just explain away? Or is there some way he could've folded it into the Transfinitum/Absolute distinction, like treating the fact that no formal theory pins down the "real" cardinalities as itself evidence that the true universe of sets outruns formalization, the way the Absolute outruns the transfinite? I don't want the generic "well he was a Platonist so he'd have just rejected it" answer - can you actually reconstruct the strongest version of the argument he'd have made, using his actual views rather than a caricature of them?
