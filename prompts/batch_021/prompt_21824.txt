I'm a linguistics PhD student writing a dissertation chapter on Quine's philosophy of language, and I keep getting stuck on a distinction he insists on but never, to my mind, fully earns. In "On the Reasons for Indeterminacy of Translation" and in Word and Object, Quine treats the indeterminacy of translation as more radical than the ordinary underdetermination of theory by evidence: underdetermination allegedly leaves a fact of the matter that the evidence simply fails to reveal, whereas indeterminacy is supposed to hold even once the totality of speakers' dispositions to behavior is fixed. Compare the two theses directly for me, using the gavagai case to anchor indeterminacy and the Duhem-Quine problem to anchor underdetermination, and address whether Quine's own proxy-function argument in "Ontological Relativity" threatens to erase this very distinction by showing that reference and background theory are just as susceptible to a "no-fact-of-the-matter" treatment as translation manuals are. In the end, does indeterminacy survive as the more drastic thesis, or does it collapse into a special case of underdetermination? Justify your answer.
