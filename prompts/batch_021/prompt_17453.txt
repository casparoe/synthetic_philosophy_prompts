I'm a novelist currently at work on a book in which the protagonist, well into her seventies, discovers that the husband she spent four decades loving was, in ways she never suspected, not at all the man she had believed him to be — while her own experience of that love, and of the walks, gardens, and evenings that made up its texture, felt every bit as rich and complete as if none of it had been false. I want the novel's late reveal to carry genuine philosophical weight rather than function as a cheap twist, so a friend who teaches moral philosophy pointed me toward G. E. Moore, on the theory that his chapter "The Ideal" in Principia Ethica — where he tries to pin down exactly what is good about beauty and about personal affection — contains an argument that anticipates this situation almost exactly, decades before Nozick's experience machine became the standard reference point for these questions. Having now read the relevant section several times, I can feel the shape of an argument in it, but Moore's dense, clause-stacked Edwardian prose keeps slipping out of my grasp whenever I try to restate its logical steps to myself. The passage, from §119, runs as follows:

"If, now, we put this question, I cannot avoid thinking that it should receive an affirmative answer. We can put it clearly by the method of isolation, and the sole decision must rest with our reflective judgment upon it, as thus clearly put. We can guard against the bias produced by a consideration of value as a means by supposing the case of an illusion as complete and permanent as illusions in this world never can be. We can imagine the case of a single person, enjoying throughout eternity the contemplation of scenery as beautiful, and intercourse with persons as admirable, as can be imagined; while yet the whole of the objects of his cognition are absolutely unreal. I think we should definitely pronounce the existence of a universe, which consisted solely of such a person, to be greatly inferior in value to one in which the objects, in the existence of which he believes, did really exist just as he believes them to do; and that it would be thus inferior not only because it would lack the goods which consist in the existence of the objects in question, but also merely because his belief would be false. That it would be inferior for this reason alone follows if we admit, what also appears to me certain, that the case of a person, merely imagining, without believing, the beautiful objects in question, would, although these objects really existed, be yet inferior to that of the person who also believed in their existence. For here all the additional good, which consists in the existence of the objects, is present, and yet there still seems to be a great difference in value between this case and that in which their existence is believed. But I think that my conclusion may perhaps be exhibited in a more convincing light by the following considerations. (1) It does not seem to me that the small degree of value which we may allow to the existence of beautiful inanimate objects is nearly equal in amount to the difference which I feel that there is between the appreciation (accompanied by belief) of such objects, when they really exist, and the purely imaginative appreciation of them when they do not exist. This inequality is more difficult to verify where the object is an admirable person, since a great value must be allowed to his existence. But yet I think it is not paradoxical to maintain that the superiority of reciprocal affection, where both objects are worthy and both exist, over an unreciprocated affection, where both are worthy but one does not exist, does not lie solely in the fact that, in the former case, we have two good things instead of one, but also in the fact that each is such as the other believes him to be."

What I would like help with is reconstructing this argument step by step, in plain contemporary prose. Specifically: how many logically distinct reasons is Moore actually offering for why the illusion-filled universe counts as the worse one, and what is each reason doing that the others cannot do on their own? I can see that "the beloved object doesn't really exist" is one consideration, but he seems to think mere true belief contributes something further, over and above both that and any practical advantage of not being deceived — and I can't quite see how the aside about "merely imagining, without believing" is supposed to isolate that further thing. I would also value your view on why he needs the case of "reciprocal affection" at all: what would be missing from his argument if he had rested it entirely on the case of beauty? And, finally, do you think this passage is doing essentially the same work as Nozick's experience machine argument, or does Moore's specific apparatus of organic unities and "the method of isolation" commit him to something subtly different?
