At my school there's a low stone wall along the main walkway that everyone calls "senior wall," and by long-standing custom, only seniors are allowed to sit there before the first bell. There's no mention of it in the student handbook, no teacher has ever enforced it as far as I know, and nobody currently at school actually decided it: this year's seniors inherited the privilege from last year's, who inherited it from the class before that, going back further than anyone I've asked can remember. I'm a junior, so I've now watched one senior class graduate and the next settle onto the wall without a single conversation about it that I ever heard. It's also clearly not the only way this could have gone — my cousin, who goes to a school across town, says their equivalent is a set of couches in the library, and access there has nothing to do with grade at all; it's controlled by whichever club happened to claim it years ago and has kept it since. So nothing about seniors, specifically, makes this the "natural" outcome; it just settled that way here, and keeps re-settling the same way every year.

This turned into a real argument at lunch after my friend Mara, a sophomore, sat on the wall last week without knowing the custom and was told — politely but firmly, by seniors she'd never met — to move. She's still annoyed, and her position is that since nobody voted on this, no adult enforces it, and it isn't written down anywhere, it has no real authority over her; at most it describes something some seniors prefer, which she's free to ignore. A couple of my other friends think she's missing something, because almost everyone, including most sophomores, treats sitting on the wall as a minor act of disrespect rather than just an odd or unexpected choice — closer to how people treat cutting in a line than to how they'd treat wearing an unusual color. To complicate things further, a few student council members recently floated making the privilege "official" by writing it into school policy, and that idea seems to bother people, including several seniors, more than the unwritten version ever did. I brought the whole thing up in AP Government, half-expecting our unit on laws versus social norms to settle it, but it didn't, and I've been stuck on it since.

So I'd like your take on two things. First, is Mara right that something nobody wrote down, voted on, or officially enforces simply can't obligate her — or is there a real sense in which a custom like this can be binding even though no one is in charge of it? Second, is "senior wall" doing basically the same thing as a norm everybody agrees really matters, like not cutting in line, just at smaller stakes — or does the reaction to the student council's proposal suggest that writing a norm down and putting someone officially in charge of it turns it into a genuinely different kind of thing?
