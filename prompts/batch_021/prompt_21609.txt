I'm about two-thirds of the way through a novel whose central thread turns on a woman named Miriam, who learns that her late mother left a sealed letter with the family lawyer -- one that, per the terms of the will, names Miriam's real father, a fact her mother concealed for four decades. The book's engine is Miriam's monthslong refusal to open it, and whatever else that refusal is, I want it to read on the page as a considered, even defensible choice, not as cowardice dressed up in careful prose.

The complication is a friend from my old graduate program, now a working decision theorist, who has been beta-reading chapters and keeps pushing back on exactly this point. He tells me that I. J. Good's theorem on the value of information settles the matter outright: because the expected utility of costlessly observing evidence and then acting optimally on it can never be lower than the expected utility of deciding without it, a rational Bayesian in Miriam's position is, without qualification, committed to opening the letter. He likes to add that this is really just Carnap's principle of total evidence with a proof attached, which makes Miriam's hesitation look to him less like wisdom than like a rationality error I've dressed up as characterization -- something I either need to have her overcome by the final chapter, or flag plainly as a flaw.

He's rigorous enough, and I'm undertrained enough in the mathematics, that I don't have a confident way to push back on my own. Before I revise the scene where Miriam finally decides, I'd like a straight answer: does Good's theorem really force this conclusion about a case like hers, and if so, what should that mean for how I write her? And if it doesn't force that conclusion, I'd like to understand exactly where his argument overreaches, so I can give her hesitation the intellectual backbone I originally intended it to have.
