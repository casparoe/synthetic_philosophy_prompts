Over dinner last week, a friend of mine who conducts a regional orchestra described an experience that has continued to trouble her. Her orchestra holds blind auditions, in which candidates perform behind a screen precisely so that a player's reputation cannot color the panel's judgment of the sound itself. Listening under those conditions, she rated one particular violinist as solid but unremarkable, one competent player among several that day. Some months later she learned, quite by chance, that this same violinist had since won a major international competition and was being singled out by critics for an unusually rich, expressive tone. Out of curiosity, she located a recording of that exact audition excerpt and listened to it again. She insists that the tone now sounds noticeably fuller and more nuanced than it did the first time, even though it is the identical recording. What makes the case especially puzzling to her is that a fellow panelist, who never learned who the player was, also listened to the same excerpt again around the same time and reported hearing nothing different at all.

As it happens, I am teaching a seminar this term on the philosophy of perception, but nothing in the assigned reading has prepared me to answer my friend's very concrete question, and I would like to reason it through with you before I write back to her. Is there any principled way to tell whether her auditory experience of the recording genuinely changed on the second hearing, rather than her simply misremembering or reinterpreting an unchanged experience in light of what she has since learned? Does her colleague's unchanged reaction on the second listen count as evidence one way or the other? And practically speaking, does it even matter which of these turned out to be true, or should it make no difference to how much she trusts her own ear, and her orchestra's blind-audition process, going forward? What would you tell her?
