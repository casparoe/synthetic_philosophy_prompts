I'm a PhD candidate in political theory finishing a dissertation chapter on the ethics of targeting in wartime, and this year I've taken on the (non-voting) role of graduate assessor to my university's Honorary Degrees Committee, which means I'm expected to circulate a short written opinion before any contested nomination goes to a vote. The committee is currently deadlocked over Lieutenant General Daniel Ashworth (Ret.), who has been proposed for an honorary doctorate mainly on the strength of the international mediation institute he founded after leaving the service and thirty years of subsequent humanitarian diplomacy. The complication is his earlier record: as the officer who planned the final phase of the air campaign that ended a regional war three decades ago, he deliberately targeted the besieged enemy capital's state broadcaster and its central power and water utilities -- installations he has since written, in a widely read memoir, were chosen less for their remaining military value than "to bring the war home to the population and hasten the collapse of its will to continue." The campaign is generally credited with ending the war within weeks and averting a ground invasion, at a cost, by most outside estimates, of several thousand civilian deaths from disease and exposure once electricity, clean water, and hospital power failed.

Half the committee wants to invoke something very like Anscombe's argument in "Mr. Truman's Degree" -- that deliberately breaking a civilian population as a means of forcing surrender is murder no matter how many lives it nets on the other side of the ledger, and that this is really just an instance of her broader claim, in "Modern Moral Philosophy," that no accounting of consequences converts intentionally killing the innocent into something else. The other half insists this is a garden-variety double-effect case: the intended objects of attack were dual-use command-and-propaganda infrastructure, the civilian deaths were a foreseen but unintended side effect of an otherwise proportionate strike, and Ashworth's framing in a memoir written decades later for a popular readership is thin evidence of what he actually intended at the time, especially set against his contemporaneous orders, which are couched entirely in terms of degrading military capability. I'd like your help drafting the opinion I'm expected to circulate. The full committee votes in three weeks, so it needs to settle on one definite recommendation -- award the degree, withhold it, or some concrete middle path (an accompanying citation that names the campaign rather than passing over it, say) -- and it needs to actually adjudicate the question of intention at the center of the Anscombe-style objection, rather than retreating into proportionality talk or "it was a different era." Whatever you end up recommending, take the strongest form of the losing side's case seriously rather than just gesturing at it.
