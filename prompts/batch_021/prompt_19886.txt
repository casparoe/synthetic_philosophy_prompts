"Consider the following wager, known in the literature as the Pasadena game. A fair coin is tossed repeatedly until it lands heads for the first time. If the coin first lands heads on toss number n, you receive a payoff of (−1)^(n+1) · 2^n/n dollars (so you win $2 if heads comes up on the first toss, lose $2 if it first comes up on the second toss, win about $2.67 -- that is, $8/3 -- if it first comes up on the third toss, and so on).

(a) [3 pts] Compute the game's expected value by summing, in the natural order n = 1, 2, 3, ..., the probability of each outcome times its payoff.

(b) [4 pts] Nover and Hájek use this game to argue that, unlike in the St. Petersburg paradox, the Pasadena game doesn't merely have an infinite or otherwise extreme expected value -- it has no well-defined expected value at all. Explain the mathematical basis for this claim, and explain why the St. Petersburg game does not raise the same difficulty.

(c) [3 pts] What do you take the Pasadena paradox to show about the principle that rational agents should evaluate gambles by their expected value? Defend your answer in a few sentences."

Here is the student's answer, verbatim:

"(a) The expected value is Σ (from n=1 to ∞) of 2^(-n) · (−1)^(n+1) · 2^n/n, which simplifies to Σ (−1)^(n+1)/n = 1 − 1/2 + 1/3 − 1/4 + ⋯. This is the alternating harmonic series, and it converges to ln 2 ≈ 0.693. So, added up in the order the outcomes naturally come in, the game is worth a little over 69 cents.

(b) Summed in that order, the terms of the expected-value calculation form a conditionally convergent series: it converges (to ln 2), but if you take the absolute value of every term you get the ordinary harmonic series, Σ 1/n, which diverges. Whenever a series converges only conditionally, the Riemann rearrangement theorem tells us it can be reordered to sum to any real number we like, or to diverge to +∞ or −∞, or to oscillate forever without settling on any value. Because the terms of the series shrink to zero as n grows, the payoffs themselves stay comparatively tame for large n, and it is exactly this kind of series -- small enough to sum to a finite value along one path, yet not absolutely small enough to rule out a rearrangement summing to something else entirely -- that the rearrangement theorem applies to. Nothing analogous threatens St. Petersburg, since every term in that sum, 2^(-n) · 2^n = 1, is already positive; a divergent series of non-negative terms diverges to the same value (+∞) no matter how its terms are reordered, so St. Petersburg's expected value, however hard to swallow, is at least well-defined.

(c) I take the deepest lesson of the paradox to be this: when a gamble's expected value is undefined rather than merely infinite, expected-value theory doesn't just struggle to evaluate the gamble -- it tells us the gamble is worth nothing at all. Once we see that no single number can be picked out as 'the' expected value, the only stance consistent with that fact is to treat the game as valueless, so a rational agent should decline to pay any positive amount to play it."
