I spent thirty-five years as an analytical chemist before retiring, and I have only recently had the leisure to read philosophy properly rather than admire it from a distance. A few of us who studied science together as undergraduates decades ago still meet once a month to work through a philosophy book, and we are currently partway into Kripke's Naming and Necessity. Since so many of his examples are drawn straight from chemistry — water turning out to be H2O, gold being fixed by its atomic number — I assumed this would be the one stretch of the book where my background actually helped. Instead, last month's session ended in a disagreement between two other members, Daniel (a retired high-school chemistry teacher) and Renata (a retired geologist), that none of us could settle, and it has stayed with me since.

(1) Daniel laid out the standard picture: "water is H2O" is necessary but a posteriori, on a par with "Hesperus is Phosphorus" — water's real nature just is its molecular composition, so once chemists discovered that composition they discovered something that could not have been otherwise, even though no amount of armchair reasoning could have told them so in advance. (2) Renata objected that jade should give us pause. For most of its history, she said, "jade" was treated as naming one precious stone, prized across unrelated cultures, and it was not established until the nineteenth century — if she remembered right — that the word actually covers two distinct minerals from different mineral families, jadeite and nephrite, one a pyroxene and the other an amphibole. On her view, there was no single hidden essence sitting there waiting to be discovered, the way Kripke's story about water requires; chemistry did not vindicate "jade," it merely revealed that ordinary speakers had been running two different kinds together all along. (3) Daniel replied that this only adds a wrinkle rather than breaking the picture: "jade" turns out to be a disguised disjunction, "jadeite or nephrite," and each disjunct still gets its essence fixed in the usual way. Water, he said, is untouched by this, because unlike jadeite and nephrite — which are different compounds with different crystal structures — every sample anyone would call water, however many minerals happen to be dissolved in it, shares the same molecular bonding of two hydrogens to one oxygen; chemistry itself, not philosophical stipulation, tells you to treat the dissolved minerals as impurities and the jadeite/nephrite difference as a difference in kind. (4) Renata was not satisfied. Heavy water, she pointed out, is still two hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom by Daniel's own criterion, since deuterium is simply an isotope of hydrogen with the same atomic number — yet nobody treats D2O as merely "water with a bit of impurity." It freezes and boils at measurably different temperatures, it is noticeably denser, and it is valued in the nuclear industry precisely because its behavior toward neutrons is so different from ordinary water's, not because it is a slightly dirty version of the same stuff. So, she concluded, either sameness of element settles sameness of kind, in which case heavy water simply is water and Daniel's bonding criterion was too coarse from the start, or it does not, in which case he needs some further principled cut — and at that point he is just choosing, case by case, whichever level of chemical description delivers the verdict he already wanted, for water and for jade alike.
