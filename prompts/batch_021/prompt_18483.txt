Okay, this is going to sound petty but it's genuinely been bugging me since it happened, and it ties into stuff from my Epistemic Logic seminar. A few weeks ago some friends and I were messing around and someone asked if 8191 is prime; I guessed no and lost five bucks, becuase it turns out it is (it's 2^13 - 1, some 'Mersenne prime' thing apparently). My friend Dana, who's doing a stats PhD, told me I don't actually get to be surprised by this, because whether 8191 is prime is just a fixed logical/computable consequence of what a prime number is plus the value of the number itself — so in some sense I already 'knew' the answer the whole time, I'd just never bothered to work it out, kind of like how I already 'know' the 1000th digit of pi even though I've never once calculated it.

What really got me is that when I brought this up with my TA, she said this isn't just Dana being a smug math person — it's basically built into the standard 'possible worlds' way of modeling knowledge that we've been using in class: if you know some set of starting claims, you automatically count as knowing literally everything that logically follows from them, no matter how many steps it would take to derive or whether a single person has ever actually worked it out. That seems to wipe out any real difference between actually figuring something out and just having it sit there, unexamined, inside the logical consequences of stuff you already believe. So who's actually right here — is there some legitimate sense in which Dana and my TA have it right and I really did know all along, or is there something genuinely broken about a theory of knowledge that forces you to say that? And if it is broken, is this a known problem people have already tried to patch up, or did I just stumble into some hole in the theory by accident?
