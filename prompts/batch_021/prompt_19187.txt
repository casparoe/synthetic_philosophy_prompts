Tucked into the final objection Turing considers in "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" (1950) -- the ninth and last, "The Argument from Extra-Sensory Perception" -- is a passage that most discussions of the Turing test (the imitation game, in Turing's own phrase) pass over quickly. Turing concedes that "the statistical evidence, at least for telepathy, is overwhelming," and worries that a telepathic or clairvoyant contestant could out-guess a machine at a card-guessing task, tipping off the interrogator. He then raises a stranger worry: since the machine answers using an internal random-number generator, that generator would itself be "subject to the psycho-kinetic powers of the interrogator." His proposed fix for the whole problem is to seat the contestants in a "telepathy-proof room."

Write an essay evaluating whether this fix actually works, in two stages. First: does a telepathy-proof room neutralize both threats, or only the telepathy/clairvoyance one -- given that psychokinesis, on Turing's own account, would act directly on the machine's hardware rather than travel between the rooms as a signal, so that shielded walls may do nothing to stop it? Second: take a position on what the episode shows about the imitation game as a criterion for thought. Does Turing's readiness to patch the test against any newly credited causal power, real or merely rumored, show that "thinking" is being defined as nothing more than indistinguishability from a human respondent, and so is hostage to whatever people happen to believe about human capacities at a given moment -- or is this an isolated, correctable curiosity that leaves the paper's central argument untouched? Weigh the considerations on each side, but close with clear, specific verdicts rather than a hedge.
