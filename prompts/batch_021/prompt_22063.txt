Last night my friend and I spent nearly two hours arguing about a conspiracy theory she had been reading about. Her entire case for it was that the theory neatly explained a long list of facts we had both known for years, with no novel predictions involved, and she took that explanatory fit alone to be strong confirmation. I objected that this is exactly the kind of after-the-fact accommodation that makes a theory unfalsifiable, but then it struck me that general relativity is celebrated for doing much the same thing with Mercury's perihelion precession, an anomaly Le Verrier had already flagged more than fifty years before Einstein's theory accounted for it in 1915. Neither of us could work out how orthodox Bayesian confirmation theory is even supposed to make sense of either case, since once evidence E is already certain given one's background knowledge, learning that some theory entails E seems unable to raise one's credence in that theory at all. Please describe the most important implications of the problem of old evidence for how much epistemic credit a theory deserves when it merely accounts for facts that were already on the table before it was proposed, and for whether Bayesianism gives us any principled way of treating my friend's conspiracy theory differently from Einstein's.
