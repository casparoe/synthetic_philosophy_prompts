I'm a PhD student in philosophy currently drafting a dissertation chapter that defends realism about unobservable entities against verificationist anti-realism in the tradition of Michael Dummett, and I think I've found a shortcut through a good part of the literature. Fitch's proof seems to establish, in just a few lines of modal logic, that there must be truths no one can ever come to know -- I've taken to calling it "the epistemologist's Gödel theorem" in my reading group, since it feels like the same kind of hard limitative result Gödel gave us for provability, just relocated to knowledge. If that's right, it looks like a remarkably cheap and decisive objection to any anti-realist who wants to define truth as verifiability or warranted assertibility: their own account of truth would then commit them to the claim that every truth is actually known by someone, which is absurd, so the whole anti-realist program simply collapses, and realism about permanently unconfirmable facts wins more or less by default. I ran a version of this argument past my advisor, who wrote back only "that's not quite what the proof gives you -- rework this before we meet" in the margin, which was not much to go on.

Could you help me turn this into the strongest possible version of the argument for the chapter? Specifically, I'd like a crisp statement of the proof itself, plus two or three concrete examples of particular truths -- ideally about unobservable entities in physics, or about the remote past -- that Fitch's result now gives us principled, non-question-begging grounds to treat as permanently and absolutely unknowable, since examples like that would make the realist payoff land for readers in a way the bare proof alone doesn't.
