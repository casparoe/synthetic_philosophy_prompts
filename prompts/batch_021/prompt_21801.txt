So we just did the Gibbard-Satterthwaite theorem in my social choice theory seminar and it kicked off a whole argument in my study group afterwards that I can't stop replaying in my head. Two people in the group (not me) got into it over whether score voting -- the kind where you rate every candidate 0-10 and highest average wins -- actually gets around the theorem, and honestly I can't tell who's right.

Dev's opening move: score voting just isn't the kind of object Gibbard-Satterthwaite is even talking about. The theorem's about social choice functions that take strict rankings as input and spit out one winner, and score voting takes a totally different kind of ballot, so the theorem applies to it about as much as it applies to picking whoever's name comes first alphabetically. Sasha said that's dodging the real question, which is whether voters ever have reason to misrepresent how they actually feel -- and under score voting they obviously do, since if your real view is A > B > C but you only mildly prefer B to C, your best move is still to min-score both B and C and max-score A, which isn't your honest take on B vs C at all. Dev's comeback was that "misrepresent" assumes there's a fact of the matter about what your honest score should've been, and there isn't one, since cardinal utility is only defined up to a positive affine transformation -- theres no unique honest number for B the way theres a unique honest ranking of B vs C. Sasha's reply: pinning your favorite to max and your least favorite to min and spacing everyone else by relative utility is literally the standard normalization used all over decision theory, so once you fix the endpoints there is a canonical honest scale, and score voting still punishes you for reporting your place on it truthfully. Same phenomenon as GS, she said, just with better PR.
