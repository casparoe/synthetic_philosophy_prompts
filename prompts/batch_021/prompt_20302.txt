I'm assembling a collection of linked short stories, each built around a single philosophical crux, and for the entry drawn from Nietzsche I want to steer clear of master-slave morality and ressentiment, material that's already a reflex for anyone who has skimmed the First Essay of the Genealogy of Morality. I'd rather work with the stranger, less-used passage early in the Second Essay, where Nietzsche describes the "sovereign individual": a figure who emerges at the end of humanity's long, cruel self-domestication finally capable of making a promise and standing behind it, who has won mastery over himself and circumstance, and whose resulting self-regard Nietzsche calls a conscience — a proud one, notably, rather than the bad conscience the rest of the essay goes on to anatomize. He even calls this figure the master of a free will, an odd label from a philosopher who elsewhere treats free will as a superstition invented mainly to make people punishable. Nietzsche never brings the sovereign individual back by name, and readers have never agreed on what to make of a passage so isolated from everything around it: is this his genuine ideal of self-legislating autonomy, or a symptom of the very taming he spends the rest of the essay despising — a well-trained animal admiring its own leash? Please write a short story, in whatever setting and period you choose, centered on a character who could honestly be read either way, and let the plot put real weight on that ambiguity rather than tipping the reader toward a resolution.
