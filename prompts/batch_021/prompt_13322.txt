I've been on a real Schopenhauer kick this year - picked up a beat-up copy of The World as Will and Representation after my mom's hospice stay last year, of all things, because I was trying to figure out why music seemed to be the only thing that still reached her once she'd basically stopped responding to anything else. Anway I got kind of obsessed with the part where he says music is totally different from every other art - like a painting or a play represents some "Idea" (in his weird capital-I Platonic sense) and only reaches the will, the "thing in itself," secondhand through that Idea, but music skips the middleman completely and is somehow a direct copy of the will itself, which is supposedly why it hits us so much harder than the other arts even though its "about" nothing in particular. He's also got this whole thing about how melody is basically the shape of wanting something: it wanders away from the home note and puts off getting back to it, and that wandering IS the desire, and the resolution back to the tonic is the satisfaction, except the satisfaction never lasts because the melody just wanders off again, which is supposed to mirror how our whole existence is just one craving getting replaced by the next one. And I remember him being kind of snobby about music that tries to directly copy real sounds - storms, birdcalls, that kind of thing - like that's cheating, because real music isn't supposed to be "of" any particular thing at all.

What I want is a short story that actually uses this stuff rather than just gesturing at "music is deep." Give me some kind of hospice or bedside musician (I think there's an actual name for that job, playing harp or singing for people who are dying) who gets a request from one family for a very specific, memory-loaded song - their wedding song or whatever - around the same time she's got another patient who's past recognizing words or faces and only ever settles for some wordless thing she improvises on the spot. Somewhere in there I want her to have to sit with whether the wordless improvised stuff is actually doing the deeper "direct copy of the will" thing Schopenhauer describes, or whether that's just a comforting story she's telling herself about her job, and whether the line between her and the people she plays for is thinning out in a way that feels less like connection and more like something is dissolving. Don't give me a tidy ending where she figures it out - I'd rather the story just sit in that tension.
