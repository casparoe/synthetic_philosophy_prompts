The prompt they were answering was: "In the 1844 Manuscripts and in Capital, Marx develops accounts of both alienation and exploitation. Explain how the two concepts are connected, making sure to explain Marx's account of surplus value and how it relates to the concept of species-being. In your view, does Marx think exploitation is unjust?"

Here's their answer:

"For Marx, alienation and exploitation are two sides of the same coin. In Capital, Marx explains that the capitalist buys the worker's labor on the market like any other commodity, paying its full exchange value as a wage. But labor is capable of producing more value in a day than it costs to buy for a day, and this difference is surplus value, which the capitalist keeps as profit. This is exploitation: the systematic extraction of unpaid labor time, built right into an apparently fair exchange.

This connects to alienation, which has four aspects for Marx: alienation from the product (what you make belongs to the boss, not you), alienation from the act of production (work is forced and unfulfilling rather than a free expression of yourself), alienation from other people (competition for jobs turns fellow workers into rivals), and alienation from your own species-being. Species-being is Marx's term for humanity's underlying nature, which, like the classical economists he was building on, he takes to be fundamentally self-interested and acquisitive; under capitalism this side of us is thwarted rather than expressed, since workers aren't free to pursue their own economic advantage but instead have to labor for someone else's profit.

As for whether exploitation is unjust: Marx repeatedly frames surplus-value extraction as a kind of theft and describes the capital relation as a species of robbery, so for him exploitation is a straightforward moral wrong, no different in kind from a mugging, and that's the whole basis of his call to overthrow capitalism."
