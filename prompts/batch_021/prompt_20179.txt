I'm currently drafting the climactic chapter of a literary thriller in which my protagonist, a bridge engineer, is forced into an irreversible, split-second decision that will kill one person in order to save several others. My writing group pushed back on an early draft of this scene, pointing out that as written it just reduces to the standard trolley problem everyone has already met in an introductory ethics class or a viral online quiz — a bridge collapse standing in for a rail switch, five lives against one. I think they're right, and I'd like to rebuild the scene around something philosophically sharper: specifically, a case that presses hard on the doctrine of double effect's distinction between killing someone as a means to saving others and merely foreseeing their death as a side effect of saving others, so that the moral logic driving the scene is genuinely non-obvious rather than a costume change on the same old lever.

I have a hazy, possibly unreliable memory of philosophers constructing exactly this kind of variant — something involving a side track that loops back around toward the five, such that redirecting the trolley only saves them because a person standing on the loop physically stops its momentum before it can circle back. The thought, I believe, is that pulling the very same lever one would pull in the "ordinary" bystander case now arguably counts as using that person as a means, even though the overt act is identical to the permissible switch case. I also vaguely recall a further refinement, possibly called something like a "doctrine of triple effect," meant to carve out a third psychological category between intending a death as a means and merely foreseeing it. Could you reconstruct the relevant case (or cases) as precisely as you can, correct anything I've gotten wrong or oversimplified, and explain exactly why they're supposed to be harder for double effect to handle than the classic switch-versus-footbridge contrast? I'd also be curious, from a storyteller's rather than a seminar's perspective, whether you think the distinction at stake is one a reader could actually feel the weight of in a dramatized scene, or whether it's the kind of fine-grained puzzle that only survives inside a journal article.
