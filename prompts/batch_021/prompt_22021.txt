Since my daughter was born seven weeks ago, I have been handling the 1 a.m. and 4 a.m. feeds myself, and more than once I have woken in genuine confusion about which of the two feeds I am in the middle of, since they feel identical and I have no reliable memory cue to distinguish them. This small domestic puzzle sent me back to the Sleeping Beauty problem, and from there to a less familiar relative of it: the Absent-Minded Driver problem, in which a driver on a highway with two consecutive, identical-looking exits needs to take the second one, cannot tell upon arriving at an exit whether it is the first or the second, and must therefore fix in advance a single probability of exiting that applies at both. Both puzzles turn on self-locating uncertainty — not knowing which of several subjectively indistinguishable moments one currently occupies — yet one is standardly posed as a question about credence and the other as a question about optimal action. I would like help working out how closely the two are really related. Does committing to thirder-style reasoning about Sleeping Beauty settle, or even constrain, what probability the driver should choose, or do the two problems come apart in ways that make it a mistake to carry a solution over from one to the other? I am also curious whether the tension the driver's problem raises, between the strategy that is optimal to plan in advance and the strategy that seems optimal once one is actually at the exit, has any genuine counterpart in the Sleeping Beauty debate, or whether it is simply an artifact of a decision-theoretic framing that a pure credence puzzle does not share.
