Here is the question:

"Locke argues that most parents and tutors rely on some mix of physical punishment and material bribes to shape a child's character, and that this approach is largely self-defeating. Using *Some Thoughts Concerning Education*, explain (a) the psychological mechanism Locke thinks is at work when a child is beaten or bribed in connection with some activity, and why this mechanism works against the very habits parents are trying to instill; (b) whether Locke's opposition to corporal punishment holds without exception; and (c) how Locke's insistence that children have distinct natural 'tempers,' which the tutor must study and adapt to, should be squared with his general empiricist denial of innate ideas."

Here is Alex's answer:

"Locke's argument against both whipping and bribing children rests on his associationist psychology: whatever feeling of pleasure or pain gets attached to an action becomes attached, in the child's mind, to the action itself rather than to the underlying virtue a parent is trying to teach. If a boy is caned whenever he stumbles over his Latin, he doesn't conclude that diligence is good; he concludes that Latin and books are hateful, since those are what got connected to the pain. Likewise, if a child is bribed with sweets for sitting through his lesson, he concludes that the lesson itself must be disagreeable (otherwise why the bribe?) and that sweets and idleness are the things actually worth having. Locke's preferred substitute for both is esteem and disgrace: children naturally want to be well thought of, and a good tutor can make virtue itself pleasant by attaching praise to it, instead of importing an artificial pleasure or pain from outside.

Locke is quite consistent on this point: because beating always produces the same self-defeating association no matter what it's attached to, he treats the rod as having no legitimate place in a rational education, for any offense.

This fits with Locke's broader empiricism. Since he denies that the mind comes equipped with any innate ideas, character on his view must be built up entirely through associations formed by experience — which is presumably why he treats the tutor's toolkit of esteem, disgrace, and habituation as working the same way on any child, rather than as something that has to be custom-fit to each child's natural disposition."
