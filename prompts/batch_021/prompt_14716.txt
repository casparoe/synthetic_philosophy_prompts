I'm a backend engineer at a mid-sized startup, and a disagreement keeps resurfacing on my team whenever we face a big, hard-to-reverse call. The most recent instance was whether to delay a launch by three weeks so we could gather more usage data from a limited beta before deciding whether to ship a redesigned onboarding flow or scrap it in favor of the old one. Our head of analytics has a rule of thumb he invokes in nearly every one of these debates: you should essentially never pass up a chance to look at more data before deciding, because additional information can only ever leave you as well off as you already were, or better — never worse, so long as you remain free to disregard it if it doesn't turn out to be useful. Stated that way, it sounds almost self-evidently true, and I've never managed to counter it in the moment.

And yet a few incidents from past launches have left me unconvinced that the rule is as safe as he makes it sound. On one project, waiting an extra sprint for "more signal" led us to react to what turned out to be a random early spike in a single metric, and we reversed a decision our original data had actually gotten right. On another, the delay itself gave a competitor time to ship a similar feature first, which changed the entire calculus no matter what our beta eventually showed. And more than once, the extended wait made leadership anxious enough that they overrode the final analysis and went with gut instinct anyway. My colleague insists these are all failures of execution rather than counterexamples to his underlying rule, and that in principle he's still correct that more information is never worse than less. Since I have no formal background in statistics or philosophy, I can't tell whether he's defending a genuinely airtight principle against sloppy real-world objections, or whether there's a rigorous sense in which deliberately seeking out more information before deciding can actually leave you worse off. Could you help me sort out who has the more defensible position here, and, either way, what exactly would need to be true for a rule like his to actually hold?
