hey, I TA an intro ethics course and I'm grading take-homes this week, figured I'd sanity check one with you. one of the questions was on Anscombe's "Mr Truman's Degree" pamphlet -- the one where she goes after Oxford for giving Truman an honorary degree over Hiroshima and Nagasaki. felt like a good question to assign because it's the kind of case that gets thrown around a lot in EA/rationalist arguments about whether "the math worked out in the end" can ever be a complete defense, so I wanted it to force people to locate exactly where the argument does its work instead of just gesturing at "dropping the bomb was bad."

question as given to students:

"Reconstruct Anscombe's argument for the claim that Truman's decision to drop the atomic bombs on Hiroshima and Nagasaki was murder. Explain what work the doctrine of double effect is doing in the argument, and explain why she thinks the 'it shortened the war and saved lives' defense fails on its own terms -- not merely because the casualty numbers might be wrong."

one student's answer, copied exactly as turned in:

"Anscombe's target is the idea that Truman can be excused because the bombs ultimately shortened the war and therefore saved lives on both sides. Her argument doesn't depend on disputing that estimate -- she thinks the defense is bad even if we grant it, because some acts are simply not permissible no matter how good their consequences are, and intentionally killing innocent people is one of them. This is where she introduces the doctrine of double effect, which she develops in the essay as her tool for handling exactly this kind of case. On this view, an act that has a bad effect alongside a good one can still be permissible if the bad effect isn't itself the thing you're aiming at, either as your goal or as your chosen method of getting there, but is instead something you merely foresee and accept as a side effect, provided the good achieved is proportionate to it. Anscombe's point about Hiroshima and Nagasaki is that the civilian deaths weren't a side effect of hitting some other target -- the whole strategy was to destroy the cities so that the resulting terror and devastation would force the Japanese government to surrender. So the deaths were the means, not a side effect, and double effect gives Truman no cover. Anscombe isn't a pacifist about any of this: she thinks killing enemy combatants in a just war is fine, and she'd say the same about civilian deaths that are genuinely incidental to hitting a legitimate target. But once you see that double effect turns on whether the harm was intended rather than just forseen, it also shows that any commander who knows ahead of time that civilians will die from an operation is, on her reasoning, committing murder -- which is presumably why she thought the firebombing campaigns against German and Japanese cities earlier in the war counted as murder too."
