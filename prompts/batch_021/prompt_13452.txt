I am putting together a session for my graduate seminar on formal epistemology. We have already covered James Joyce's accuracy-dominance argument for probabilism and the basic apparatus of epistemic utility theory, and I now want to present the standard objections to accuracy-first epistemology with real precision. The one I am most anxious to get right is Selim Berker's "separateness of propositions" charge: roughly, the idea that because epistemic utility theory evaluates an entire credence function by aggregating some measure of accuracy across every proposition in one's belief state, it thereby permits trading off accuracy on one proposition against accuracy on some wholly unrelated proposition, in a way that is supposed to inherit the force of the separateness-of-persons objection to utilitarianism.

Could you reconstruct this objection with some care? In particular, I would like an explanation of why aggregation over propositions is doing the objectionable work, together with a toy formal example of your own devising -- a handful of propositions, an explicit scoring rule, and two candidate credence functions -- that actually exhibits the trade-off, rather than just gesturing at the analogy with persons. I would also like you to keep this line of argument sharply separate from Berker's other complaint, about cases in which present belief-formation is supposedly vindicated purely because of the accuracy it causally promotes later on, since my students have a persistent tendency to run the two together.

Finally, I would welcome your honest assessment of the best replies available to the accuracy-first camp. A colleague of mine, who works mainly in decision theory, maintains that the objection simply dissolves once one distinguishes "epistemic consequentialism" from "accuracy-first epistemology" more carefully: on her view, one can treat accuracy as a formal, axiological tool for characterizing rational credence without committing to the further claim that doxastic states are justified in virtue of promoting accuracy. I am not yet convinced that this distinction does as much work as she thinks, and would be curious whether you find it more or less promising than replies that instead appeal to formal constraints on which scoring rules ought to count as legitimate in the first place, such as propriety or something like Joyce's "coherence-admissibility" condition.
