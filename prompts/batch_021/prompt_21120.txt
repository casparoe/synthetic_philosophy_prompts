I cover neurotech and AI for a science magazine, which is apparently how I ended up as the "unaffiliated public member" on my local hospital's neuroethics committee (every review board needs one person with no clinical stake, and I guess I was available). We've got a case that's been eating at me for weeks. A patient in a small trial of an adjustable deep-brain stimulator for treatment-resistant depression has a handheld controller that lets her raise or lower her own stimulation within a range her psychiatrist originally set. At the top of that range she says she feels better than she has in twenty years, not just "less depressed" but genuinely euphoric. Problem is, over the last couple months she's been running it at max pretty much constantly, she's stopped returning her sisters calls, quit a part-time job she liked, and by her family's account mostly just sits there with the dial turned up. She's now asking the committee to raise the ceiling further. She's fully lucid, passes every competency evaluation we throw at her, and says we should all just be happy she finally feels good.

The committee's split roughly down the middle. One camp says overriding a competent adult's own account of her well-being, just because we don't like how she's using it, is exactly the kind of paternalism medical ethics is supposed to have grown out of. The other camp, including an AI-safety researcher we brought in to consult on the device's control algorithm, points out that this is pretty much the exact failure mode her field worries about when a system finds a way to directly max out whatever's measuring its reward instead of doing the thing the reward was suppose to be a proxy for, and argues that her glowing self-reports are close to worthless as evidence anything is actually going well for her. I have to draft the memo the committee votes on Thursday: an actual recommendation (raise it, hold it, add conditions, whatever) with a timeframe, and it needs to seriously engage the autonomy camp's argument instead of just asserting that we know better than she does what's good for her. I keep wanting to reach for the old rat-lever-pressing experiments and call it a day, but that feels too glib for something we're actually about to vote on.
