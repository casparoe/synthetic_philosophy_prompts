I'm working on a piece tied to the 100th anniversary of Gottlob Frege's death this year (he died in 1925 -- yes, I had to double check), and I was covering a small conference on his legacy where, during the Q&A after one of the talks, two people in the audience -- grad students, I think, from the way they argued -- got into a proper back-and-forth. I'll just call them Marcus and Leah since it's not really about them personally. The trigger was Frege's 1924 diary, the one where, a year before he died, he wrote a bunch of virulently antisemitic, anti-democratic stuff: wanting Jews stripped of political rights, basically pining for some strongman to sweep away the Weimar Republic. It only turned up in his papers after he died, and it apparently rattled people like Michael Dummett, who's the guy most resposible for reviving Frege's reputation as the father of modern logic and analytic philosophy of language.

Marcus's opening move was that the diary is philosophically inert: Frege's logic either works or it doesn't, and whether the man who invented the Begriffsschrift also held disgusting political views is about as relevant as Newton's alchemy obsession is to the inverse-square law, i.e. not remotely, because arguments don't inherit the moral status of whoever's making them. Leah pushed back that this is too quick, because Frege's whole philosophical brand was a claim to objectivity: his career-defining move was attacking "psychologism," insisting logical laws are mind-independent and eternal, not a matter of anyone's contingent psychology, so it's atleast worth asking whether someone who mistook his own ugly prejudices for plain necessity should make us a little warier of his other claims to have found "the objective." Marcus's comeback was that this proves too much. Plenty of realists and objectivists throughout history have had garden-variety flaws or far worse, and if every thinker's private bigotry counted as evidence against their formal work we'd have to throw out huge chunks of the canon; you can't infer general untrustworthiness from a mess-up in one domain (politics) to a totally unrelated one (quantifier scope, say). Leah's reply was that she wasn't making an inference about Frege's general reliability so much as pointing at an asymmetry: everyone treats Dummett's own documented distress on discovering the diary as biographically touching but philosophically beside the point, and yet Dummett himself, of all people the one who'd invested the most in Frege's greatness, apparently didn't treat it as a mere footnote either.
