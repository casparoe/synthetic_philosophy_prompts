A few of us rewatched Oppenheimer over the weekend, and it set off a long argument at the pub afterward that I was very much a part of -- which is exactly why I don't trust my own judgment on the sharpest exchange of the night. That exchange was between two friends, Marco and Elena, on whether "murderer" is the right word for what Truman did in ordering the bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki. I've set it out below, numbered, and would like your assessment.

1. Marco opened with the familiar defense: a land invasion of the Japanese home islands (Operation Downfall) would almost certainly have killed far more people, Japanese and American alike, than the two bombs did, so ordering them was the lesser evil.

2. Elena replied that this misses the actual target of the philosopher Elizabeth Anscombe's objection to Oxford's decision to award Truman an honorary degree. Her argument was never about comparative body counts: her claim was that Truman chose to kill innocent people as a means of ending the war, and choosing to kill the innocent as a means is murder, whatever the surrounding arithmetic turns out to favor.

3. Marco pointed out that Anscombe was no pacifist -- she accepted that civilians could be permissibly, if regrettably, killed as a foreseen side effect of a legitimate military action. So maybe the disagreement is factual rather than moral: Hiroshima held a military garrison and Nagasaki housed war industry, and the civilian dead might have been an unintended side effect of striking those targets rather than the means to anything.

4. Elena answered that the record of the actual target-selection deliberations undercuts this reading: the criteria reportedly used for choosing targets explicitly weighed the psychological effect on Japanese civilian morale and the value of a visibly dramatic demonstration, which is part of why largely intact cities were chosen over isolated military installations. On her view, this shows civilian death and terror were built into the intended mechanism for extracting surrender, not merely foreseen collateral damage -- which is exactly why double effect cannot rescue Truman on Anscombe's own terms.

5. Marco responded that if "chosen partly for psychological effect on civilian morale" is the standard for murder, it convicts the firebombing of Tokyo and Dresden, and most of the Allied strategic bombing campaign that preceded Hiroshima. Either Elena is prepared to call that whole campaign murder too, or she owes a further distinction she hasn't yet supplied.

The conversation ended there, with neither side conceding.
