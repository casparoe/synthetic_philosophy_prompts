For my IB extended essay on free will, I have been working through Galen Strawson's Basic Argument for the claim that no one can ever be truly (or "ultimately") morally responsible for what they do, regardless of whether determinism is true. The crucial step is a regress argument: to be truly responsible for an action, you would have to be truly responsible for the mental character that produced it, which would require having authored that character in light of some earlier character, and so on; since nothing can ultimately cause itself, the regress cannot terminate in a self-made starting point, and Strawson insists indeterminism cannot help either, since an undetermined trait is no more "up to you" than a determined one. This regress reminds me of the regress of justification in epistemology, where foundationalists stop an analogous threat of infinite reasons by treating certain basic beliefs as justified without being inferred from further beliefs. Please argue that an analogous foundationalist strategy is available against Strawson: that some of an agent's character traits could ground genuine responsibility simply in virtue of the agent's current relation to them, such as reflective endorsement, rather than through any prior act of self-authorship. Please also explain why this does not simply amount to begging the question against Strawson, given that he could just reply that the very disposition to endorse a trait is itself one more unchosen feature of "the way you are."
