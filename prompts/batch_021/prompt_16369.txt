In my physics class, we just finished a unit on interpretations of quantum mechanics, and I found the Many-Worlds Interpretation especially compelling: instead of the wavefunction mysteriously collapsing when a measurement is made, every possible outcome simply happens, each in its own branching world, with decoherence explaining why we never notice the other branches. Around the same time, my philosophy elective has been covering David Lewis's modal realism, which, if I understand it correctly, holds that literally every possible world exists and is every bit as concrete and real as our own, with "actual" functioning as a purely indexical term, similar to "here" or "now," that just picks out whichever world the speaker happens to occupy.

I'm supposed to write a short reflection for that class on objections to modal realism, but honestly the two ideas seem so similar that I'm having trouble taking the objections seriously. It seems to me that quantum physics has essentially already vindicated modal realism: if the Many-Worlds Interpretation is correct, every possible outcome really does exist as its own concrete, physical world, which sounds like exactly what modal realism says about possible worlds in general. My teacher insists that modal realism is still one of the most controversial positions in contemporary metaphysics, one that most philosophers reject as wildly implausible (apparently Lewis himself expected people to respond to it with what he called an "incredulous stare"), but that seems strange to me if physics has already confirmed the same idea from a completely independent direction. Shouldn't the Many-Worlds Interpretation count as strong scientific support for modal realism, and if so, why do philosophers keep treating it as such an open, hotly contested question instead of something physics has basically already settled?
