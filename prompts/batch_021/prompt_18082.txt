I'm about two-thirds through a novel where a lot hinges on whether a character, Marguerite, actually "has" the trait everyone (including her) keeps insisting she has—courage—even though for the whole book so far she's never really been in a situation that tests it, because circumstances keep conveniently letting her off the hook. I want the eventual reveal to feel earned rather than like I'm just pulling "she was brave all along" out of nowhere, so, as one does, I went down a research rabbit hole into the philosophy of dispositions and got stuck on the whole finks-and-masks literature that grew up around the conditional analysis of dispositions (the classic "x is fragile iff x would break if struck" kind of analysis).

I get the rough shape of both counterexamples: C.B. Martin's electro-fink, where a device flips the disposition on or off at exactly the moment the triggering conditions are met, so a wire that's actually dead "would" shock you if touched, even though its not currently live; and Mark Johnston's masking cases, like a poison that's rendered harmless by a co-administered antidote, so the lethal disposition never manifests even though nothing about the poison itself has changed. What I don't get is why these count as two genuinely seperate problems rather than just two flavors of the same trick (something intervenes so the manifestation and the underlying disposition come apart). I vaguely remember reading that David Lewis's reformed conditional analysis, the one built around an intrinsic causal basis that has to persist through the process, is supposed to fix the fink problem but still falls to masks—if that's right, I'd love an explanation of why the fix works for one and not the other, because that distinction seems like it should tell you something real about what a disposition actually is, rather than just being a technical patch to keep the analysis alive.

If it's not too much of a tangent, I'm also curious whether anything in this literature has a bearing on my Marguerite problem—like, is there a principled difference between a person whose virtue has just been "masked" by good luck for the whole book, versus a novelist cheating by asserting a trait that was never really there to begin with. But mostly I just want the finks/masks distinction properly explained, even if the fiction angle turns out to be a stretch.
