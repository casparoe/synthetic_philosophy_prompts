As a software engineer, I've spent plenty of time building and A/B-testing the kind of choice architecture that Thaler and Sunstein call "libertarian paternalism": default settings, opt-out enrollment, pre-checked boxes, all designed to steer users toward some outcome while leaving their formal freedom to choose intact. I keep wondering how John Stuart Mill would judge this practice. He's the standard reference point for anti-paternalism, but On Liberty is more specific than the harm-principle slogan suggests: Mill holds that a bystander may forcibly stop someone from stepping onto a bridge known to be unsafe, just long enough to warn them, after which they may proceed if they still wish; and he is willing to accept labeling requirements or a record of purchase for the sale of poisons, short of an outright ban.

Given those specific positions, together with his broader case for individuality and the exercise of one's own judgment against the "despotism of custom," what do you think Mill's verdict on nudging would be? Would something like opt-out retirement enrollment count as the same sort of harmless interference as the bridge case, since the choice is technically preserved -- or would he treat the deliberate exploitation of status-quo bias as a subtler way of bypassing a person's judgment that the bridge case doesn't cover? Please work from his actual arguments rather than his general reputation, and land on a definite verdict, including roughly where he would draw the line between nudges he would endorse and ones he would condemn.
