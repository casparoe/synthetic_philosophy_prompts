I'm about a third of the way into a novel about a woman in 1850s England who loses her religion in stages rather than all at once, and I keep circling back to George Eliot as the obvious real-life template -- except I realized I'd only ever absorbed the cartoon version of her story ("brilliant Victorian, stopped believing in God, wrote big humanist novels"). So I spent last weekend actually reading up on the Rosehill circle and the translation work, and I banged out the notes below trying to get past the cartoon and say something sharper. I'd love your honest comments before this gets anywhere near the actual manuscript.

Here's the gist: everyone knows Eliot swapped Christianity for a kind of ethical humanism, but I don't think that's the interesting part. The interesting part is that the shape of her novels stays evangelical even after the content doesn't. Biographically, it's Charles Hennell's Inquiry and the Rosehill freethinkers that trigger the 1842 "Holy War" with her father over church attendance -- years before Darwin is even in the picture. Then comes the grim, "Strauss-sick" grind of translating Strauss's Life of Jesus, and then Feuerbach's Essence of Christianity, the one translation she apparently puts her own name to. Feuerbach's move -- theology is really anthropology, God is just the human species-essence projected outward and worshipped -- basically becomes her operating theology from then on (theres that Myers anecdote about her walking with him in the Fellows' Garden at Trinity, Cambridge, in 1873, solemnly laying out "God, Immortality, Duty": inconceivable, unbelievable, and yet peremptory and absolute, respectively). My claim is that once you've swallowed Feuerbach like that, you don't stop writing conversion narratives, you just relocate the object of conversion. Silas Marner loses his faith community at Lantern Yard when he's falsely accused of theft and "convicted" by his own congregation drawing lots, goes numb and miserly, and gets saved again -- same beats, crisis and all -- by a child instead of by Christ. Dinah Morris in Adam Bede is a literal Methodist preacher whose doctrine the book quietly drains out of her by the end (even the detail that she has to stop preaching lines up with the real Methodist ban on women preachers), leaving just the ministry of sympathy behind. Even Dorothea in Middlemarch is chasing what the book calls a "binding theory" of life, which reads like a deconvert's ache for the thing she gave up. So: theologically Eliot is radical, but structurally she's conservative -- same wineskins, different wine.

Does that actually hold up, or am I forcing three different novels into a pattern that's really just mine? I'm especially unsure whether I'm overplaying the Feuerbach influence relative to Comte's positivism or her Spinoza translation, which get almost no airtime in my notes but might matter just as much to the "necessity" side of her thinking. And tell me straight if this is a stale observation every Eliot scholar made back in the 1970s and I should either cite it as background or go find a genuinely different angle -- I'd rather know that now than after I've built forty pages of plot on top of it.
