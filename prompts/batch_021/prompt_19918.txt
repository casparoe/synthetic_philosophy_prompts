She retold Death in Damascus roughly like this: a man runs into Death one morning in Damascus, who gives him a strange, startled look; convinced Death has come for him, the man rides through the night to Aleppo to escape, only to learn afterward that Death's startled look was just surprise at seeing him in Damascus at all, since Death already had an appointment with him in Aleppo that very night. Her argument was that this puzzle is structurally identical to Newcomb's Problem, since both involve a near-perfect predictor whose forecast of your choice fixes the outcome before you act, and she concluded that the two stories are therefore making the same point in favor of evidential decision theory over causal decision theory: because your choice is such strong evidence about what the predictor already did, you should always reason evidentially whenever a flawless predictor is involved.

I'm supposed to turn her summary into a short write-up for the rest of the club before next week, but something about the argument nags at me, since I vaguely recall that Death in Damascus is usually brought up as a difficulty specifically for evidential decision theory, which would be an odd thing to also cite as proof that evidential decision theory is the right approach whenever a predictor is involved. Before I write this up and possibly repeat a mistake to the whole club, could you help me sort out whether she's right that any near-perfect-predictor scenario must recommend the same decision theory, or whether Death in Damascus and Newcomb's Problem actually pull in different directions despite looking so similar on the surface?
