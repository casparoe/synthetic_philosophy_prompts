I'm a linguistics PhD student working on early bilingual and twin language acquisition, and this term I've been auditing a philosophy of language seminar to strengthen my background in the philosophical literature on meaning. Last week's session covered Wittgenstein's private language argument from the *Philosophical Investigations* (roughly §243 onward), and something from my own subfield came up that I raised with the professor but don't think we fully resolved.

In the developmental literature I work with, there is a well-documented phenomenon usually called cryptophasia or idioglossia: certain twin pairs, especially those with limited exposure to older children, develop a rapid, fluent idiom between themselves that outsiders, including their own parents, cannot follow, sometimes for a year or more, until a linguist sits with the pair long enough to reconstruct the pattern. This looks to me like an actual, empirically attested instance of exactly what Wittgenstein claims is impossible: a language that only certain specific individuals, here two, are able to understand. If real cryptophasic twins already falsify Wittgenstein's central claim, I'm puzzled why the private language argument is still taught as a serious, unresolved piece of philosophy of language rather than as a thesis about meaning that developmental linguistics has simply moved past. When I raised this, the professor's answer seemed to turn entirely on twins being two people rather than one, which struck me as a technicality rather than a real rebuttal. Is that really the crux of the matter, or is there a more principled reason why cryptophasia was never the kind of case the argument was actually about?
