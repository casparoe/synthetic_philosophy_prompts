One of the questions on the exam read: "Reconstruct Parfit's Mere Addition Paradox — the argument that proceeds from population A to A+ to B and onward to population Z — and explain how it is meant to force acceptance of the Repugnant Conclusion. Then explain whether Average Utilitarianism escapes the Repugnant Conclusion, identifying which specific step of the Mere Addition argument it rejects, and state one serious cost of adopting Average Utilitarianism in place of Total Utilitarianism."

Here is the answer one of the students gave: "Parfit's Mere Addition Paradox begins with a population A in which everyone has a very high quality of life. We then construct A+, which contains everyone in A at the same level of welfare, plus an additional group of people whose lives are worth living but at a lower level of well-being; since nobody already in A is made worse off, and the additional lives are still worth living, A+ cannot be worse than A. Next we compare A+ to B, a population of the same size as A+ but in which total welfare has been redistributed so that everyone is equally well off, at an average somewhat higher than in A+ though still below the average in A. Since B beats A+ on total welfare, average welfare, and equality all at once, B must be better than A+, and therefore, by transitivity, at least as good as A. Repeating this two-step move again and again, each time producing a larger, more equal, lower-average population that is at least as good as the last, eventually yields a vast population Z in which every life is barely worth living. Because each step in the chain preserves the 'at least as good as' relation, Z comes out at least as good as A: this is the Repugnant Conclusion.

Average Utilitarianism, which ranks outcomes by welfare per capita instead of total welfare, is usually credited with avoiding this result. Because the average is, in effect, an egalitarian measure, Average Utilitarianism will always rank a more equal distribution of a given quantity of welfare over a given number of people above a less equal one, which is part of why it comes apart from Total Utilitarianism once population size is allowed to vary. What actually stops the argument, however, is that Average Utilitarianism gives up the transitivity of 'better than' once populations of different sizes are being compared, so the inference from 'B is at least as good as A+' and 'A+ is at least as good as A' to 'B is at least as good as A' no longer goes through partway down the alphabet, which is what ultimately keeps us from reaching Z. This is not a free lunch: Average Utilitarianism is standardly taken to imply the Sadistic Conclusion, the claim that it can sometimes be better to bring into existence people with negative welfare than people with positive but very low welfare, since a large number of barely-positive lives can pull the average down further than a much smaller number of clearly unhappy ones."
