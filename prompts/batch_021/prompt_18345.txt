I spent thirty-five years as an experimental physicist, and it is only since retiring that I have started reading philosophy with any seriousness, largely at the prodding of a friend from my old research group who minored in the subject decades ago. Prompted by the recent debate about whether large language models understand anything, she suggested I read Leibniz's Monadology alongside Searle's "Minds, Brains, and Programs," since more than one commentary I have since come across remarks, almost in passing, that Leibniz's "mill" argument in section 17 of the Monadology anticipates the Chinese Room by nearly three centuries. I can follow Searle's paper well enough on its own terms, but I find I do not actually understand the Leibniz passage itself well enough to judge whether the comparison is being made carelessly, and I would rather work through the old argument properly than simply take someone's word for the parallel.

The passage, in the translation I have to hand, reads: "It must be confessed, moreover, that perception, and that which depends upon it, are inexplicable by mechanical causes, that is to say, by figures and motion. And, supposing there were a machine so constructed as to think, feel, and have perception, we could conceive of it as increased in size while retaining the same proportions, so that we might be able to enter it as one enters a mill. This granted, we should, on examining its interior, find only parts which work one upon another, and never anything by which to explain a perception. Thus it is in a simple substance, and not in a compound or in a machine, that perception must be sought for."

What I would like help with is mainly two things. First, I cannot tell whether the move from "inside the mill we would only find parts pushing one another" to "perception cannot be found there" is meant to follow rigorously from something in Leibniz's wider metaphysics -- his views on genuine unities versus mere aggregates, say -- or whether it is really just an appeal to the reader's inability to picture how perception could emerge from parts in motion, in which case it looks uncomfortably like a bare argument from incredulity dressed up as a thought experiment. Second, Searle's Chinese Room is usually read as the fairly narrow claim that syntactic symbol manipulation specifically cannot suffice for semantic understanding, leaving it officially open that some other kind of machine might think by different means; Leibniz's mill seems to rule out perception in any mechanical arrangement whatsoever, computational or not, which is a considerably stronger conclusion. If the older argument is valid, does it not already give Searle everything he wants and more, and if so, should Searle's critics really be spending their time on Chinese symbols and rule books rather than on the premises of the mill? I would be grateful for help sorting out exactly how these two arguments relate, rather than a summary of either one on its own, since it is the relation between them, and not either argument in isolation, that I am struggling with.
