Question: State Aumann's agreement theorem, explaining the role of the common prior assumption and the notion of common knowledge involved. Then discuss whether the popular slogan "rational agents cannot agree to disagree" is a fair summary of what the theorem actually shows.

Submission: Aumann's theorem considers two Bayesian agents who share a common prior over some state space but who may possess different private information, represented formally by partitions of the state space; each agent's posterior for an event A at a given state is just the prior conditioned on the cell of their own partition containing that state. The theorem states that if the two agents' posteriors for A are common knowledge at some state — meaning each agent knows the other's posterior, and each also knows that the other knows theirs — then their posteriors must in fact be numerically equal, even though the private information underlying them may differ. The common prior assumption is what does the real work: it guarantees that any residual disagreement can only reflect asymmetric information, never a fundamentally different starting point, so once that asymmetry is neutralized by common knowledge of the bottom-line beliefs, no room for disagreement remains. Aumann later strengthened this into the no-trade theorem, showing that two such agents could never agree to a speculative bet based on private information, since the very willingness to take one side of the bet is itself informative and gets incorporated once it is common knowledge. Given how rarely real people share a common prior — our starting points are shaped by upbringing and disposition, not just evidence — and how hard it is to achieve genuine common knowledge of exact posteriors outside stylized models, the slogan that rational agents "cannot agree to disagree" considerably overstates what Aumann proved: it is a narrow, idealized result rather than a description of how disagreement among reasonable people actually works.
