Ok so I've been talking to chatbots way too much lately and it's gotten me stuck on this question: when people (including the AI companies themselves) say stuff like "the model believes X" or "it wants to give you a helpful answer," is that literally true in some sense or just a convenient way of talking? A friend told me about this philosopher Dennett and his "intentional stance" idea, which as far as I understand it is basically: you're allowed to describe pretty much any system as having beliefs and desires, like it's a rational agent, as long as doing so actually helps you predict what it'll do. I guess there's supposed to be three "stances" total (physical, design, and then intentional) but I don't fully get why the first two even matter if the intentional one is what does the predicting. Anyway what blew my mind is that Dennett apparently uses this to say a plain old thermostat "believes" the room is too cold and "wants" to warm it up, same idea as saying a chess program "wants" to protect its queen. Once a thermostat counts, it feels like there's basically no bar left, so saying an LLM has "beliefs" stops being an interesting claim about what's happening inside it and becomes trivially true.

Here's where I get stuck though: if the whole test is just "does treating it as a believer help you predict what it does," the LLM case seems like a total slam dunk, but that feels too cheap, like belief can't just mean "whatever's convenient to say" or the word stops meaning anything at all. I vaguely remember Dennett having a later idea about beliefs being "real patterns" rather than just useful fictions, kind of like how a shape can be a real pattern in a bunch of dots even though no single dot "is" the shape - and I wonder if that version is actually strict enough to separate the LLM case from the thermostat case in some non-arbitrary way. Also, and this is the part that really confuses me, I think I read that dennett himself got genuinely worried near the end of his life about chatbots being good enough to pass as real people, and wrote something warning about ai "counterfeit people" - which seems like a weird thing for the guy who made belief-attribution so cheap to be worried about. Is there an actual tension there or am I misunderstanding something?

Could you write me an essay that actually takes a stance on this (pun not intended, or maybe intended): using Dennett's own framework, not just the pop-science version of it, does it really follow that current AI language models have honest-to-god beliefs and desires, or is there a legitimate way to use his own theory to block that conclusion, or at least draw a non-arbitrary line between the thermostat/chess-program cases and the LLM case? I don't want a wishy-washy "well it depends what you mean by belief, everyone should define their terms" type answer - I want you to weigh the best arguments on each side and then actually land somewhere and defend it, the same way you would if you were settling an argument with a friend for real, even if your honest answer ends up being some kind of "yes, but only under these conditions."
