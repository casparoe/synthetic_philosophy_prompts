So I've been down a bit of a rabbit hole the last few weeks. A friend who did a comparative religion degree mentioned this Jain concept called anekantavada at dinner a while back — basically that reality has a bunch of facets and no single vantage point ever gets the whole of it, kinda like the blind-men-and-an-elephant story but with actual epistemological teeth to it. I went and read up on it (nayavada, syadvada, this seven-part "maybe" logic thing) and realized it maps weirdly well onto something that happens constantly at my job: blameless postmortems after an outage, where three different engineers each land on a different "root cause," and all three are fully consistent with whatever logs they personally happened to be staring at.

I wrote up a short post for our internal eng blog about this (pasted below) and would love some comments before I hit publish, specifically: (1) am I actually representing anekantavada correctly, or just butchering a couple-thousand-year-old philosophical tradition to make a cute blog post, and (2) does my postmortem analogy actually get at what's philosophically interesting about the doctrine, or is it surface-level pattern-matching dressed up in jargon ("different people notice different things" is a pretty banal observation on its own, so maybe im missing the actual substance of syadvada). One coworker who read a draft said this is basically just relativism with extra steps, and I've got a nagging feeling that's wrong but couldn't quite articulate why, so I'd appreciate you weighing in on that objection specifically too. Dont hold back, I'd rather hear it now than after I post it in front of the whole eng org.

Here's the draft:

"When Three Engineers Describe One Outage"

Last month checkout latency spiked for about 40 minutes. In the postmortem, the on-call engineer blamed a bad database migration. The frontend lead blamed a caching bug that had been dormant for weeks and just happened to get triggered that day. The infra person pointed at a noisy-neighbor pod scheduled onto the same node. All three had graphs backing them up. None of them were lying, and none of them had the whole truth either — each had just been staring at a different slice of the system.

There's an old Jain idea for exactly this situation, anekantavada, roughly "non-one-sidedness": reality is many-faceted, and any single standpoint (a naya) only ever captures part of it. What I like is that this isn't the same move as "everyone's opinion is equally valid, maaan" — Jain philosophers were apparently pretty explicit that a naya turns into an actual error the moment it gets asserted as the complete picture instead of a partial one. They even built a whole conditional grammar around this, syadvada, where claims get prefixed with "in some respect" (syat), so instead of flatly saying "the migration caused it" you'd say "in some respect, the migration caused it." It reads less like "everything's relative" and more like a mandatory scope qualifier on every claim, the way you'd scope a variable instead of leaving it global and letting it clobber everything else.

Maybe the postmortem takeaway is: stop asking "whose root cause is correct" and start asking "what's the scope within which each answer holds," and only then try to compose the partial answers into a fuller, still probably incomplete, picture.
