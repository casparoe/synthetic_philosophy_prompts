My grandpa passed away back in March and I ended up as executor of his estate, which has turned into alot more of a headache than I expected. His will was drawn up like 15 years ago and has one specific line leaving "my 500 shares of Meridian Corp stock" to my cousin Ellie, with the rest of his brokerage account (the "residue") split evenly between his other grandkids, including my cousin Marcus. Except here's the wrinkle: Meridian got bought out by a bigger company called Vantage Group in an all-stock merger back in 2016, so by the time grandpa died his 500 Meridian shares had automatically turned into 750 Vantage shares at whatever the exchange ratio was that year. He never sold or bought a single share after that, he just held whatever he recieved in the swap, and never updated the will to even mention Vantage. (For what it's worth, I'm not a beneficiary of this particular part of the estate either way — grandpa left me a flat amount separately for being executor — so I don't gain or lose depending on how the stock question shakes out, I just have to actually implement whatever's correct.)

At the lawyer's office a couple weeks ago, Ellie and Marcus got into a genuinely heated argument over what should happen to those 750 Vantage shares, and I can't stop turning it over in my head since I'm the one who has to sign off on the distribution. Roughly how it went, in order: (1) Ellie said it's obviously still her stock — the merger was totally outside grandpa's control, the company just changed its name and share count on paper, and the whole point of that line in the will was "give Ellie my position in that company." Treating the gift as if it vanished because of a corporate event he had zero say in feels like punishing her over a technicality. (2) Marcus, who's finishing his second year of law school, said wills have to be read by what they actually say, not by whoever tells the most sympathetic story about what grandpa "really" wanted, and that's exactly why the traditional rule (he called it "ademption by extinction") is that if the exact property named in the will isn't sitting in the estate at death, the gift just fails and drops into the residue. If grandpa's lawyer had wanted the gift to survive a merger, there's boilerplate for that ("or any shares I may receive in exchange therefor") and it simply isn't there. (3) Ellie shot back that the will has at least one other sloppy spot — it still lists a bank account grandpa closed like a decade ago — so his silence about mergers is obviously just him never thinking about it again, not some deliberate choice to cut her out if this ever happened. She also said her husband, who's a corporate lawyer (not trusts and estates, but still), told her most states carve out exactly this situation, that involuntary, automatic stuff like a merger isn't supposed to wipe out a specific gift the same way an actual sale by grandpa would've. (4) Marcus said sure, maybe some states do that, but that just relocates the fight instead of ending it: now we're arguing about whether "all-stock merger" falls inside whatever narrow list of exceptions the statute actually uses, which is a different question than "what would grandpa have wanted," and Ellie doesn't get to lean on a big-picture "his intent is obvious" argument and a narrow technical statutory exception at the same time, whichever helps her more in the moment. (5) Ellie said that's fair, but if the statute doesn't cleanly cover this exact situation, the tiebreaker should still go to grandpa's obvious intent instead of automatically defaulting to "the gift dies," especially since three different family members separately remember him saying some version of "the Meridian stock is Ellie's, that's for her wedding fund."

Nobody backed down and the lawyer just kind of watched it happen. Could you go through moves (1) through (5) and tell me, for each one, whether that person basically had the better of it, the worse of it, or something in between, and why? Separately, I'd like to know if there's something both of them are missing entirely that would resolve this more cleanly than either framing does. And one more thing, since it's been bugging me: part of me suspects this is basically a smaller-scale version of the whole textualism-vs-purposivism fight I keep half-hearing about in Supreme Court arguments, just playing out over a will instead of a statute — but I honestly don't know if that comparison holds up or if wills run on different logic entirely. Curious what you think.
