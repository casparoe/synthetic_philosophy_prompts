I'm a PhD student in philosophy working primarily on philosophy of physics, and for a dissertation chapter on infinite idealizations I've been re-reading Thomson's lamp. As I understand the original 1954 setup, a lamp that starts off is toggled at 1/2 minute, again at 3/4 minute, again at 7/8 minute, and so on, so that it ends up switched infinitely many times before a single minute has elapsed. Thomson's argument, as I read it, is a clean dichotomy: the lamp can't be on at the one-minute mark, since that would require some last flip from off to on that was never subsequently reversed, and a sequence constructed like this one has no last term; by exactly parallel reasoning, it can't be off either. Since those are the only two possibilities for the lamp's state, the bare supposition that the switching sequence has actually been completed seems to generate a genuine contradiction, which I'd always taken to be a formally valid proof that supertasks, in the strict sense of completing infinitely many discrete operations in finite time, are logically impossible, not merely unrealizable given the physical constraints of our world.

My advisor keeps telling me that this reading is too quick, but after several passes through the paper I haven't been able to locate the gap myself, and it bothers me for a further reason: so much subsequent work treats the coherence of completed supertasks as a live, substantive question rather than one Thomson's dichotomy already closed. Benacerraf's own reply to Thomson, Earman and Norton's discussions of spacetime structures like Malament–Hogarth spacetimes that have been proposed as physical implementations of infinite computations, and the literature on "Zeno machines" that perform a countable infinity of steps in finite time all proceed as though there's genuine work left to do in saying when a supertask is or isn't coherent. If Thomson's dichotomy really does derive a contradiction from the bare supposition that the switching sequence gets completed, I don't see why any of that later work would still be worth doing rather than being a settled non-starter. Could you help me see whether the reductio actually goes through as I've stated it, and if not, where exactly it breaks down?
