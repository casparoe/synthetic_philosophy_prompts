For my AP U.S. History class, I have to write a short analysis of a paragraph from Frederick Douglass's 1860 speech "The Constitution of the United States: Is It Pro-Slavery or Anti-Slavery?," which he delivered in Glasgow to rebut a speech the British abolitionist George Thompson had just given at Glasgow's City Hall arguing that the U.S. Constitution was irredeemably pro-slavery. Here's the paragraph I'm stuck on:

"In all matters where laws are taught to be made the means of oppression, cruelty, and wickedness, I am for strict construction. I will concede nothing. It must be shown that it is so nominated in the bond. The pound of flesh, but not one drop of blood. […] But where human liberty and justice are at stake, the case falls under an entirely different class of rules. There must be something more than history — something more than tradition. The Supreme Court of the United States lays down this rule, and it meets the case exactly — 'Where rights are infringed — where the fundamental principles of the law are overthrown — where the general system of the law is departed from, the legislative intention must be expressed with irresistible clearness.' The same court says that the language of the law must be construed strictly in favour of justice and liberty. Again, there is another rule of law. It is — Where a law is susceptible of two meanings, the one making it accomplish an innocent purpose, and the other making it accomplish a wicked purpose, we must in all cases adopt that which makes it accomplish an innocent purpose."

I understand his general point -- that the Constitution's actual text doesn't protect slavery -- but three things about how he builds the argument are tripping me up. First, what work is the "pound of flesh" line doing there, and why bring in what sounds like a Shakespeare reference in the middle of a legal argument? Second, isn't "strict construction" normally the approach a slaveholder's lawyer would want, since it reads a document narrowly instead of finding new rights in it -- so why does Douglass, who wants the Constitution read as protecting enslaved people, plant his flag on strict construction rather than some looser, more generous method of reading? Third, could you walk through how the three rules he invokes -- strict construction, "irresistible clearness," and resolving ambiguity toward the "innocent" reading -- actually reinforce each other as a single method, rather than being three separate arguments just stacked on top of one another? I'd like to understand the actual mechanics of the argument, not just the conclusion he's driving at.
