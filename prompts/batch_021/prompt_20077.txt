I've been teaching a graduate seminar on metaphysical dependence this term, and we recently spent two sessions on Kit Fine's and Jonathan Schaffer's efforts to rehabilitate grounding as a primitive, explanatory relation of metaphysical priority, distinct from causation on one side and mere modal notions on the other. One of my sharper students raised an objection afterward that I haven't been able to fully answer. She pointed out that when we say the mental is grounded in the physical, or that a disposition is grounded in its categorical basis, we seem to be saying nothing more than that the lower-level facts necessitate the higher-level ones and that this dependence runs asymmetrically from lower to higher. But necessitation plus asymmetry is exactly what supervenience already gives us once you build in a direction, and supervenience has the considerable virtue of being a modal notion philosophers understood perfectly well before anyone introduced grounding-talk. On her view, grounding is therefore at best a redundant label for asymmetric supervenience, and at worst an unearned bit of ideology that lets metaphysicians sound as though they have discovered a new relation in the world when they have merely renamed an old one.

I tried gesturing at the standard reply that grounding is meant to be a worldly, explanatory relation rather than a mere pattern of modal covariation, but I couldn't produce a clean example on the spot to show the two relations actually coming apart, and I would rather not send my students to office hours with only a hand-wave. I would like a more rigorous answer to bring back to seminar: is my student basically right that talk of grounding can be paraphrased away, without loss, in favor of suitably asymmetrized supervenience claims? Or is there a principled reason -- ideally illustrated with a concrete example -- for thinking that a fact can stand in a relation of strict, asymmetric supervenience to some base without being grounded in that base?
