Over the past few months I have been sorting out my late grandmother's estate, which included a small oil-on-panel interior scene that has hung in the family dining room for as long as anyone can remember and was always spoken of, half-seriously, as "our little Old Master." When I finally had it examined for insurance purposes, the appraiser's technical report concluded, on the basis of pigment and panel analysis, that the picture is almost certainly a skilled later copy rather than an autograph work by the artist to whom it had always been attributed, and the insured value dropped by roughly ninety percent overnight. Nothing about the physical object changed in the process: the wood, the paint, and the image are exactly what they were the day before the report arrived. That gap between the object and its worth has been nagging at me ever since, and I have started reading around the philosophy of art's treatment of forgery to try to make sense of it.

Two accounts I have come across don't quite line up, and I would like your help comparing them. Nelson Goodman, discussing the difference between autographic and allographic arts in Languages of Art, argues that even a copy that is currently indistinguishable from an original need not be aesthetically equivalent to it, because our perceptual discrimination could in principle be trained to notice a difference that escapes us today; on his view, authenticity remains aesthetically relevant even where perception currently fails us. Alfred Lessing, in his essay "What Is Wrong with a Forgery?", takes the opposite tack: he grants that a copy's aesthetic properties can be entirely on a par with the genuine article, and locates the wrong of forgery instead in originality as a historical achievement, since the copyist is merely imitating a creative feat that belongs to someone else. Could you compare these two accounts and tell me which one gives the more convincing explanation of a case like mine, where nothing perceptible about the painting has changed but everything about its status has? I would also be curious whether either theory actually licenses the odd impression I have that the painting looks slightly different to me now that I know what it is, or whether that is simply a psychological trick I am playing on myself. Please justify whichever assessment you give.
