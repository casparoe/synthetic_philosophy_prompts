I sit on my university's academic integrity board, and this week's case has turned into something closer to a seminar than a hearing. A philosophy PhD student doesn't dispute that he lifted several paragraphs, nearly verbatim, from a manuscript he had reviewed anonymously for an academic journal and incorporated them into a chapter of his own dissertation. His written response to the charge, however, is built almost entirely around Galen Strawson's Basic Argument: he could not have been truly or ultimately responsible for what he did, since that would require him to be truly responsible for the way he is, which would in turn require some earlier act of deliberate self-formation undertaken in light of values he'd already have had to possess without ever having chosen them himself, and so on backward without end. Because the argument, on his account, holds whatever the truth about determinism turns out to be, he insists it isn't the kind of thing our usual excusing categories -- duress, diminished capacity, mistake of fact -- were built to address. He isn't asking to be found "not responsible" for the incident in our procedural sense; he concedes the board can note that he did it. He is asking us not to treat whatever sanction follows as punishment for a moral failing, since on his view nothing anyone does is ever, in the relevant sense, ultimately theirs to answer for.

I spent most of last night arguing about this with a colleague who also sits on the board. Her view is that the case dissolves on contact: if the student is right that no one is ever ultimately responsible for anything, he is no more entitled to complain that punishing him would be unfair than we are entitled to blame him, so the board can simply proceed exactly as it would have without the essay. I think that move is too quick -- it answers a philosophical argument with a debater's trick, and it lets the board avoid ever saying what a sanction is actually for, if not desert. Could you draft the memo I'll circulate to the rest of the board before we meet, given that his prospectus defense is three weeks out? It should recommend a specific finding and sanction, or explain clearly why none would be warranted, on a realistic timeline, and it should take the strongest version of his argument seriously enough that it wouldn't make the philosophers on the board wince, while still giving the historians and chemists on it something they can actually act on.
