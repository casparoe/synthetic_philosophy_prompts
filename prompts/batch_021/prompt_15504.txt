At my company we just missed a major product launch, and leadership wants a clear account of what "caused" the delay before our postmortem. Two separate things happened during the final month. First, about three weeks before launch I recommended cutting a large, unfinished feature; our own project-tracking estimates say that if it had been cut, we would have shipped exactly on schedule. My director rejected that recommendation and kept the feature in scope. Second, and entirely independently, our database lead was hospitalized for the last ten days before launch, and losing her for that stretch was, by itself, enough to cost us the same amount of time — the estimates say that even if the feature had been cut, her absence alone would still have pushed us past the deadline. My director now says his decision to keep the feature "wasn't really the cause" of the delay, since we would have missed the deadline anyway because of the hospitalization. That already bothers me, but it seems to force an equally strange conclusion in the other direction: by the same logic, the hospitalization "wasn't really the cause" either, since we would have missed the deadline anyway because of his decision. Having both of those things be true at once feels wrong, since the delay obviously did have a cause, or causes — it didn't just happen for no reason.

A friend of mine who studied philosophy said this sounded like a well-known puzzle about how to define what it is for one event to bring about another, though she couldn't recall the details. I would like help actually working through it: is the instinct to ask "would the delay still have happened without this particular factor?" simply the wrong test to apply in a case like this? Is there a more careful version of that question that would restore the obvious verdict that both decisions mattered? And, practically speaking, once this problem is taken seriously, how should someone in my position think about dividing up responsibility for the delay?
