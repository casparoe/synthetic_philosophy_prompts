I've spent the past few weeks reporting on corporate bidding wars and arms-race dynamics for a piece on escalation of commitment, and I keep circling back to Martin Shubik's old "dollar auction" as the canonical toy example — a device he introduced, if I remember correctly, around 1971. The rules are deceptively simple: a dollar bill goes to the highest bidder, bidding rises in fixed increments, and, crucially, the second-highest bidder also has to hand over their final bid while getting nothing for it. Every popular account I've come across treats the resulting spirals — bids that blow past the dollar's actual worth many times over — as a case study in irrationality: sunk-cost thinking, loss aversion, and a reluctance to be seen backing down, all overriding what a sensible agent would presumably do.

What I can't settle for myself is whether that "irrationality" framing survives being translated into an actual game-theoretic model. If I try to work through the dollar auction as a contest between two fully rational bidders using backward induction, I get pulled in two directions. On one hand, a bidder who can foresee the whole tree should apparently never enter a fight they already know will be destructive, which suggests the equilibrium should just involve someone conceding almost immediately, with no real escalation at all. On the other hand, once a bidder is in second place, topping the other side's bid by one small increment is nearly always cheaper than eating the sunk loss outright, and that local logic seems to reproduce itself at every subsequent round no matter how sophisticated the players are — which suggests equilibrium play might license escalation nearly as far as any behavioral account does. My hunch is that the resolution turns on things I don't fully understand myself: whether there's a commonly known ceiling on how far either side can or will go, whether each side actually knows how resolved (or how deep-pocketed) the other one is, and how the size of each bid increment relative to the dollar's value factors in.

Could you walk me through, in some depth, what a rigorous game-theoretic treatment of the dollar auction actually predicts once assumptions like these are made explicit, including how it connects to the broader "war of attrition" family of games from evolutionary biology and economics? I'm especially interested in whether there's a coherent equilibrium story that reproduces the destructive escalation everyone associates with the game, or whether genuine rationality, plus common knowledge of it, should instead make the whole contest unravel before it even starts. Ultimately, I'd like to come away with a clearer sense of whether escalation of commitment, in cases like this, is best understood as evidence of a cognitive bias, or as a structural feature of certain competitive situations that fully rational agents could fall into as well.
