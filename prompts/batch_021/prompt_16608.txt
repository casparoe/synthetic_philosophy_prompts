the question was: "Explain Williamson's anti-luminosity argument, using his example of a person gradually warming up after being out in the cold. What does the argument show about the KK principle, and do you find it convincing?"

here's the student's answer:

"Williamson wants to knock down the 'luminosity' thesis, the Cartesian idea that we can't be wrong about our own mental states — if you're in pain, you know you're in pain, no gap between having a mental state and being aware of it. His argument runs through a case where someone starts out freezing cold and slowly warms up over an hour, in changes too small to notice from one moment to the next. He then uses a margin-for-error requirement on knowledge: for a belief to count as knowledge it has to be safe, meaning it would still be true in very similar nearby cases, not just the actual one. So if at minute n you know you feel cold, you must still feel cold at minute n+1, since your evidence at the two moments is indistinguishable — otherwise you could so easily have been wrong that it wasn't knowledge to begin with. Run that step forward far enough, though, and you get the absurd result that the person is still cold an hour later, once they're sweating. Since that's false, the assumption that they always know they're cold whenever they are cold must break down somewhere — luminosity fails.

Williamson then points out that 'knowing that p' is itself a mental condition, so the same reasoning applies to it: there can be cases where you know p but the margin-for-error setup means you can't know that you know p. That directly refutes the KK principle, which classical epistemic logic (going back to Hintikka) had basically built in as an axiom. Since introspection turns out to be exactly as fallible and margin-dependent as any other kind of knowledge, Williamson concludes that we can never really be sure of our own minds — genuine self-knowledge turns out to be a myth, just a comforting leftover from Descartes.

I find the argument pretty convincing, though the margin-for-error premise is doing a lot of work, and a Cartesian could just reject it outright."
