im TAing a grad seminar on philosophy of math and grading short-answer responses to this prompt: "Constructivists have always been unhappy with the Axiom of Choice. Explain the usual rationale for that unhappiness, and then say whether AC's non-constructiveness is basically the same kind of move as assuming excluded middle, or whether there's a tighter logical relationship between the two."

Here's what one student wrote:

"Constructivists reject the Axiom of Choice for basically the same reason they reject nonconstructive existence proofs generally: AC says that for any family of nonempty sets there is a function picking an element from each one, but it doesn't give you any rule for how to do the picking. For a constructivist, to prove something exists you have to be able to produce it, or at least a procedure that would produce it, and a bare choice function satisfies neither. This is exactly what made Zermelo's 1904 proof of the Well-Ordering Theorem so controversial -- Borel, Baire, and Lebesgue all objected that a 'well-ordering' of, say, the real numbers that nobody could ever actually describe wasn't a mathematical object at all, just a form of words. Notably, this worry is specifically about infinite families: nobody objects to choice functions over finite collections, since there you can just list the choices one by one, so the finite version of AC isn't really an extra assumption at all.

Whether this is 'the same move' as assuming excluded middle, though, I'd say no. Rejecting excluded middle is about refusing to assert 'P or not-P' before you have a proof of one of the disjuncts -- a claim about when you're entitled to assert a disjunction. AC is a claim about when you're entitled to assert an existential, over a family of sets rather than a single proposition. So they're both examples of the same general constructivist scruple about unproven assertions, but they're logically independent principles: you could have a logic with excluded middle but without AC, or in principle a choice principle without committing yourself to excluded middle for arbitrary propositions. There's no formal derivation of one from the other -- it's really a family resemblance, not a logical dependency."
