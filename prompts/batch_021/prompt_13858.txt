After forty years running experiments in condensed-matter physics, I have spent much of my retirement finally reading the economics and epistemology I never had time for during my career, together with two former colleagues who share my belated enthusiasm and with whom I now meet as an informal reading group. We recently spent several sessions on Aumann's agreement theorem and its market-oriented descendant, the Milgrom–Stokey no-trade theorem, and I found the argument quite elegant: if a set of traders share a common prior over some uncertain event, if each is known by the others to be rational and risk-averse, and if the only conceivable motive for a proposed trade is one side's private information, then the mere fact that a counterparty is still willing to take the other side of the bet should itself update your beliefs enough to make the trade unattractive again — so that, starting from an already efficient allocation, no purely speculative trade should ever be mutually agreed to.

What I have not been able to work out is how this squares with what actually happens on, say, the NASDAQ on an ordinary Tuesday, where hundreds of billions of dollars change hands, a great deal of it presumably driven by funds and trading desks acting on proprietary signals rather than on any liquidity or life-cycle need to rebalance a portfolio. If the theorem is right that mutually rational traders should not be able to find a counterparty for a purely informational bet, doesn't the sheer scale of observed trading volume amount to fairly direct empirical evidence that most participants in modern markets are simply not rational in the sense economists require of them — and, by the same token, that the efficient-markets hypothesis, which is usually said to rest on precisely this kind of rational information processing, is in empirically worse shape than its defenders tend to admit? I would be curious how you think this apparent tension between the theorem and the trading floor ought to be resolved.
