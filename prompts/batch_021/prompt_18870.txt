Last week I tried a sensory-deprivation flotation tank for the first time: floating in body-temperature salt water, in total darkness and silence, with my arms and legs positioned so that no part of me touched any other part. Somewhere around the twenty-minute mark, with no felt boundary telling me where my body began or stopped, I found myself thinking about Avicenna's flying man argument, which I had led a discussion section on only the week before as a TA for our department's graduate seminar on classical Islamic philosophy. I was in no doubt at all that I existed and was the one floating there, even though I had, for practical purposes, no sensory information about my body whatsoever. It was an odd thing to feel a thousand-year-old thought experiment from the inside rather than just teach it.

Less romantically, I now have a stack of midterm essays to grade on exactly this argument, and there's one answer I keep going back and forth on. It reads confidently and hits most of the standard points, but something about it nags at me, and I don't entirely trust my own judgment here, partly because the flotation-tank experience has left me unusually sympathetic to anything that takes the argument's conclusion seriously. I'd like an independent read before I write a number on the page.

The question on the exam was: "Reconstruct Avicenna's flying man argument in your own words, stating its premises and its conclusion. Then assess whether the argument succeeds in establishing dualism about the soul, or whether it establishes something more modest than that -- and say how its aim and structure compare to Descartes' cogito."

Here is the student's answer, verbatim:

"Avicenna's flying man thought experiment asks us to imagine a person created all at once, fully grown, but suspended in empty air with his limbs held apart so that they never touch each other or anything else, and with every one of his senses blocked -- he cannot see, hear, touch, taste, or smell anything, including his own body. Avicenna's claim is that even in this state, the man would still affirm his own existence: he would know that he is, that he is a self, even though he would have no grounds at all for affirming that he has hands, feet, or any other organ. Since he can be certain of himself while having no awareness of his body whatsoever, Avicenna concludes that the self -- what we would call the soul -- cannot be identical to the body or to any part of it. The body turns out to be wholly inessential to who 'I' am.

This is essentially Avicenna's version of the cogito: just as Descartes will later argue that 'I think, therefore I am' gives him one indubitable fact even after he has doubted the existence of his body and the external world, Avicenna's flying man remains aware of himself after everything bodily has been stripped away by hypothesis. Descartes uses this same move -- self-awareness surviving the doubt of everything corporeal -- to conclude that mind and body are two really distinct substances, and Avicenna is making exactly the same inference roughly six hundred years earlier. So although the vocabulary is different, it is the same argument, and Avicenna deserves much more credit than he usually gets for anticipating an idea normally taught as purely Cartesian.

Does the argument succeed? I think it does. If the man is aware of himself without being aware of his body at all, then his self and his body must be two different things, full stop -- you cannot have a case where X is aware of itself but not of Y, and X turns out simply to be Y. So the flying man does establish genuine dualism: for Avicenna, soul and body are separate substances with no essential relation to one another, which is also why he can go on to argue that the soul survives the body's death. The main weakness I can see is that the thought experiment does a lot of work through a single intuition pump: we are just trusting that the imagined man really would be self-aware under those conditions, and not everyone will grant that."
