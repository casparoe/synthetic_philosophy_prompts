I'm chairing the question period after a colloquium talk our department is hosting next week, and the speaker has built much of her recent work around a "restriction strategy" reply to Fitch's paradox of knowability. Rather than giving up the knowability principle -- the claim that every truth is in principle knowable -- she holds that it survives once restricted to what she calls "Cartesian" propositions: roughly, those whose being true doesn't itself preclude their being known. On her view, Fitch's troublesome instance -- the conjunction of an unknown truth with the further truth that it is unknown, which collapses into contradiction the moment you suppose it could be known -- simply isn't Cartesian, and so was never really covered by the principle in the first place. I taught the original Fitch paper alongside her replies in a graduate seminar this term, and my students' recurring complaint was that the Cartesian/non-Cartesian line looks tailor-made to exclude exactly the one case that breaks the argument, so I'd like the Q&A to actually test that rather than let her simply restate the maneuver for a room that has already read the relevant papers.

Could you draft eight questions for me to put to her? I'd like one that presses on whether the Cartesian/non-Cartesian distinction can be characterized independently of Fitch's proof, so that the restriction isn't just read off the one counterexample it's designed to block; one on why the factivity of knowledge and the distribution of knowledge over conjunction -- the two other premises doing real work in the derivation -- are left untouched while the knowability principle itself takes the hit; one on whether intuitionistic logic, which anti-realists tend to favor on independent grounds, does any work of its own in blocking the paradox, or whether the restriction has to do all the labor regardless of one's logic; and one asking her to weigh her strategy against Dorothy Edgington's rival proposal to save knowability by reformulating it with an "actually" operator rather than restricting its domain. For each of these, add a natural follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give, and close the sequence with a question asking what a "revenge" version of the paradox -- one built entirely out of propositions her restriction was designed to count as safely Cartesian -- would show about her view, if anyone could construct one.
