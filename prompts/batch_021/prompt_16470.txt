I'm a novelist, and in ten days I'm delivering the eulogy at the memorial service for Marisol Duarte, the professor who ran the small fiction-and-translation seminar where I effectively learned to write, and who spent nearly forty years of her career teaching and translating Borges. This wasn't academic enthusiasm worn lightly: she organized her actual sense of her own mortality around Borges's essays on time, in a way that everyone close to her found either bracing or exasperating depending on the week. She could recite long stretches of "A New Refutation of Time" ("Nueva refutación del tiempo") from memory, and in the last year of her illness, whenever a visitor tried to comfort her with the thought that she would somehow "live on," she liked to counter with the argument of "The Immortal" — that given enough time, any life dissolves into every other life, so that what exactly survives and what exactly is lost stop lining up with our ordinary intuitions about persons and their endings.

What makes the eulogy hard to write is that she was explicit, more than once, about what she did not want said over her coffin, and her prohibitions cut in two directions at once. Nothing about her watching over us, obviously — she had no patience for that, and would have taken it as a betrayal to let grief smuggle it back in. But also nothing that reached for the opposite, equally cheap comfort: not the idealist shortcut where, because Borges's arguments cast doubt on temporal succession and on any self persisting through it, her death could be waved away as a kind of grammatical illusion; and not the humanist fallback that she "lives on in our memories," which she liked to call a consolation prize dressed up as metaphysics. What she wanted, I think, without ever quite putting it this way, was for someone to sit with the same tension Borges himself sits with at the end of that essay, where all the argument marshalled against time's reality turns out not to touch how it actually feels to be a person that time is running out on.

The room will hold something like seventy people: a dozen former students and fellow translators who will know exactly which essay is being paraphrased if I get a detail wrong, and the rest cousins, neighbors, and old friends who have never read a word of Borges and simply need to feel that the loss has been spoken to honestly. Could you write this eulogy for me? I'd like it to run about six or seven minutes, to open on something small and concrete about her before it earns its way into any of the philosophy, and, above all, to let her actual views on time and selfhood shape what comfort is and isn't available here, rather than quietly reaching for any of the three things she ruled out.
