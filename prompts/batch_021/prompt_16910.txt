In reporting a story on the psychology of aging, I keep returning to Cicero's De Senectute, in which Cato the Elder sets out to rebut the four standard complaints against growing old — that it withdraws us from active affairs, weakens the body, deprives us of pleasure, and brings us nearer to death — arguing chiefly that a mind cultivated over a lifetime compensates for physical decline, and that the waning of the passions is itself a form of liberation rather than a loss. Compare Cato's reasoning with Laura Carstensen's socioemotional selectivity theory, which attributes the well-documented rise in emotional well-being among older adults to a shrinking time horizon that reorients people toward emotionally meaningful goals and relationships. In which respects does Carstensen's psychological mechanism vindicate Cicero's conclusions about old age, and in which respects does it suggest that he was optimistic for the wrong reasons? Defend your answer with specific reference to Cato's arguments and to the mechanism that socioemotional selectivity theory proposes.
