Two people in my Descartes reading group got into it over whether he actually thought animals feel pain. Colin said the beast-machine stuff in the Discourse isn't just about reasoning, it's total: Descartes denies animals any inner life whatsoever, so a dog yelping when you step on it is exactly as meaningful as a hinge creaking, nothing going on behind it. Sasha said that's the cartoon version -- what the Discourse actually argues is that animals lack language and general reasoning, which is a claim about "thought" tied to having an immaterial rational soul, and that doesn't automatically mean zero sensation: in the letters to Newcastle and to More, Descartes talks about animal fear and hunger as running on pure bodily mechanism, which sounds like some inner state is happening, just not one attached to a self that's aware of having it. Colin replied that an "inner state nobody's aware of" isn't feeling in any sense anyone actually cares about, so Sasha's "sensation without consciousness" is just a comforting category Descartes never really commits to. Sasha said it's not invented -- his own dualism needs some distinction between sensation as a mode of body and sensation as a mode of mind anyway, to explain why plenty of human pain-type responses (reflexes, flinching) happen without the mind doing anything. Colin's last move was that Descartes defines "thought" (cogitatio) to cover every kind of awareness a mind has, sensing included, so if the Discourse denies animals thought at all, it's denying them awareness full stop -- Sasha's body/mind distinction just smuggles the awareness back in through the side door.

Neither budged, and I don't know Descartes well enough to referee this myself. Can you go through it move by move -- who's got the better of each exchange, not just who wins overall -- and say whether Descartes' actual texts settle this or whether its genuinely underdetermined by what he wrote?
