For thirty-two years I ran a neurophysiology laboratory, working first with rats and then, through most of the 1990s, with macaques, before I closed it down and retired. Only since then have I had the time to read philosophy with any real care, rather than in the fragmentary way I managed as a student, and a former colleague from my old journal club recently talked me into working through Robert Nozick's Anarchy, State, and Utopia.

I went in expecting to spend my time on the parts of the book everyone quotes — the experience machine, the Wilt Chamberlain argument, the case for the minimal state — and was caught off guard by a much shorter and more tentative passage, tucked into his discussion of rights as "side constraints" on action, in which Nozick worries openly about whether the Kantian rationale he has just given for those constraints — that a person may never simply be used as a means to some aggregate good, however large — extends to non-human animals at all. He seems to end up half-endorsing, and half-recoiling from, a picture in which our actual practice amounts to a basically utilitarian, costs-and-benefits treatment of animal suffering sitting alongside a much stricter, non-aggregative, rights-based treatment of human beings, without ever quite explaining what could justify drawing the line exactly there, or whether it can be drawn in a principled way at all.

What I would like from you is a description of the most important implications of this part of Nozick's discussion — not simply the position itself, but the instability in it that he half-notices — for how someone in my former position ought to think, in retrospect, about the ethics of the work I did. Concretely, I am thinking of studies that caused the macaques significant but non-lethal, time-limited suffering, such as surgical implantation of recording electrodes, undertaken for basic research whose eventual medical payoff was genuine but diffuse and far from guaranteed at the time. Does Nozick's picture offer any real, non-arbitrary guidance on cases of that kind, and if so, what is it? I am less interested in a balanced survey of considerations than in your own considered judgment, defended in some depth, about what follows from taking his view seriously, including what it would have to say about beings of intermediate cognitive complexity, such as great apes or human infants, where any line drawn by appeal to capacities like self-consciousness or the ability to frame a life plan becomes especially hard to hold steady.
