The following question and answer are from a graduate seminar on the philosophy of causal inference. Please grade the student's answer.

Question: A hospital is comparing a new stroke treatment ("Treatment X") against the standard treatment. Among patients with severe strokes, 100 of 250 patients given Treatment X recovered (40%), compared with 15 of 50 given the standard treatment (30%). Among patients with mild strokes, 45 of 50 patients given Treatment X recovered (90%), compared with 200 of 250 given the standard treatment (80%). Yet when the two severity groups are pooled together, only 145 of 300 patients given Treatment X recovered (48.3%), compared with 215 of 300 given the standard treatment (71.7%).

(a) Explain why this reversal occurs.
(b) Should the hospital adopt Treatment X hospital-wide? Defend your answer using the numbers above.
(c) More generally: whenever an aggregation reversal like this one appears, does the stratified, subgroup-level breakdown always give the causally correct answer, as against the pooled figure? Explain why or why not.

Student's answer:

(a) This is a case of Simpson's paradox. Stroke severity is a confounding variable here: it affects both which treatment a patient is likely to receive and how likely that patient is to recover, independent of which treatment is used. Treatment X was given disproportionately to severe cases (250 of the 300 severe patients, versus only 50 of the 300 mild patients), so its overall numbers are dragged down by the fact that severe strokes recover at a low rate no matter what treatment is used, while the standard treatment's overall numbers are inflated by having mostly been used on the easier, mild cases. Pooling the two severity groups conflates the effect of the treatment with the effect of severity, which is why the aggregate comparison points in the opposite direction from both severity-specific comparisons.

(b) Yes, the hospital should adopt Treatment X. The comparison that actually isolates the treatment's effect is the one within each severity stratum, since that holds severity fixed and lets only the treatment vary. Treatment X outperforms the standard treatment by ten percentage points among both severe patients (40% vs. 30%) and mild patients (90% vs. 80%). The pooled figures are the misleading ones here, precisely because they average over two groups that were not given the two treatments in equal proportions; once that imbalance is corrected for by stratifying, Treatment X comes out ahead in every comparison that matters.

(c) Yes. Simpson's paradox always has this structure: some lurking variable is unevenly distributed across the groups being compared, and pooling the data lets that imbalance masquerade as an effect of the variable we actually care about. So whenever pooling and stratifying disagree, it is the stratified comparison -- the one that holds the lurking variable fixed -- that should be trusted, exactly as in part (b). This is really the same lesson as the ecological fallacy: a relationship that holds at the level of the aggregate need not hold, and should not be assumed to hold, at the level of the subgroups that make it up, so we should always prefer the finer-grained analysis when the two come apart.
