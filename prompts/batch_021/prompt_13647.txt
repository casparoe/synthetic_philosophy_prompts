I'm working on a feature about the current vogue for invoking "the Turing test" every time a chatbot says something uncannily human, which sent me back to Turing's actual 1950 Mind paper, "Computing Machinery and Intelligence." Most retellings never get past the imitation game itself, but the paper spends real space, in its survey of objections near the end, on what Turing calls the argument from extrasensory perception, where he states that the statistical evidence for telepathy is "overwhelming" and proposes running the game in a "telepathy-proof room" as a safeguard. I put the passage to two people I was interviewing for the piece, a philosopher of mind (Renata) and a historian of science who has written on the history of parapsychology research (Oskar), expecting a shrug, and instead got an email argument running to several rounds that I haven't been able to referee myself.

(1) Renata opened by arguing that the passage is not throwaway color: Turing genuinely believed the evidence for telepathy was strong, and treating that as live while designing a test for "machine thought" is a real admission that his famous operationalization was never as clean as it's usually presented, since it quietly depends on a background assumption — no paranormal leakage between interrogator and contestant — that the rest of the paper never has to state. (2) Oskar agreed Turing meant what he said about the evidence but denied this does the work Renata wants: Turing's own fix, the shielded room, is a purely practical patch, which shows that even granting ESP he thought the game's basic logic survived untouched — an engineering nuisance, not a crack in the foundation. (3) Renata's rejoinder was that a "patch" requiring literal shielding against an unexplained force isn't in the same league as ordinary assumptions like "the equipment isn't broken," and that needing it at all shows the test smuggles in assumptions about the physical world that Turing elsewhere treats as irrelevant to defining thinking. (4) Oskar's next move brought in history: the card-guessing results almost certainly behind Turing's confidence were the Soal-Shackleton experiments then circulating in Britain, which serious statisticians of the day treated as about as tightly controlled as parapsychology research got, so Turing was tracking informed opinion, not indulging personal credulity. (5) Renata answered that this cuts the other way: those experiments were shown, in 1978, by Betty Markwick's analysis of the scoring records, to almost certainly involve manipulated data, so the "overwhelming" evidence Turing leaned on was most likely an artifact of fraud, whatever excuse hindsight offers him personally. (6) Oskar's closing move was that faulting Turing for not catching a fraud that fooled the field for over two decades after his death is hindsight bias dressed up as rigor, and that the real question — whether provisionally trusting decisive-looking statistics while building your own theory to survive even if they held up was good reasoning in 1950 — has an answer that doesn't depend on how the Soal affair later turned out.
