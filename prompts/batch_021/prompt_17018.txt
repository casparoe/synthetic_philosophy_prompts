Next month my father turns ninety, and as the philosophy professor among his children I have been asked to give the toast at his birthday dinner -- a task that feels pointedly Ciceronian, since he taught Latin for nearly forty years and has treated Cicero's De Senectute, the dialogue in which an aged Cato the Elder defends old age to the much younger Scipio and Laelius, less as a text he assigned than as something close to a personal creed. My own field is contemporary ethics rather than ancient philosophy, so I know the dialogue the way a careful reader does rather than the way he does, which is presumably why I would like some help doing it justice.

Here is the complication I would like the toast to take seriously rather than smooth over. Cato spends most of the dialogue answering three of the four standard complaints against old age -- that it withdraws a man from active affairs, weakens the body, and empties life of pleasure -- and my father finds these arguments completely convincing: the case that judgment and standing matter more than physical strength (his favorite illustration is Fabius Maximus saving Rome through patience while younger men wanted a fight), the story of Sophocles reading a jury his newly finished play to prove his mind hadn't gone, the long and genuinely tender praise of farming as the pleasure suited to an appetite no longer in charge of you. But when Cato turns to the fourth complaint, that old age is close to death, he gives two answers rather than one. The first is fully secular: even a short life can be complete, the way a play needn't run to its last act to have been a good performance. The second is not -- he goes on to build a hopeful case for the soul's outliving the body out of the Pythagoreans, Socrates' arguments on the day of his own death, and Cyrus's deathbed speech in Xenophon, and closes the dialogue with the image of leaving life as one leaves an inn rather than a home. My father, a cheerful and entirely committed materialist, has told all four of us more than once that this second answer is Cicero comforting himself with something he could not actually have been sure of, and that he doesn't want it borrowed on his behalf, this birthday or any other occasion.

Could you write me that toast? It should run four to five minutes, build on Cato's actual arguments about the first three complaints, and feel free to use the play image for the fourth -- but nothing about the soul's immortality should sneak in anywhere, however tempting a tidy Ciceronian ending would be. About a third of the room will be his former colleagues from the classics department, who will notice immediately if I have gotten the dialogue wrong; the rest is family who know only that he loves "that old Roman book" and would like to leave the toast understanding a little better why.
