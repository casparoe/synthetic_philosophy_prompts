Ok so I got into it with my friend Dana last night about the absent-minded driver problem — the Piccione & Rubinstein highway thing, not Sleeping Beauty, though we kept dragging Sleeping Beauty into it too since the two are obviously cousins. This was extra dumb because we were literally on a road trip when it came up and almost missed our actual exit arguing about hypothetical ones. Quick recap in case it matters for how you answer: a driver needs to take the second of two identical, indistinguishable exits to get home; exiting at the first exit pays 0, exiting at the second pays 4, missing both pays 1, and because he genuinely cannot tell the two decision points apart, the "planning-optimal" mixed strategy is to continue with probability 2/3 at either one (giving an expected payoff of 4/3 — worse than what he'd get with perfect recall). Everyone agrees on that part. Where Dana and I couldn't agree, like, at all, for over an hour, is what the driver should actually believe, mid-drive, about whether he's at the first or second intersection, and more generally which resolution of that interpretive puzzle is the sane one. She thinks the question is basically confused; I think theres a real fact of the matter, and by the end neither of us could name a resolution we'd fully bite the bullet on, which was kind of embarrassing given how confidant we'd both started out.

Below are the main positions on offer for what, if anything, the driver should believe (not what he should *do* — that's settled — but what he should *think*), mostly reconstructed from a decision theory seminar I audited a couple years back, so forgive me if I'm butchering anyone's actual view:

- Planning-only (à la Piccione & Rubinstein themselves): there's no well-defined interim credence here at all; only the ex-ante optimal plan is a meaningful object, so "what does he believe at the intersection" is close to a category error.
- Modified-multiself Bayesianism (à la Aumann, Hart & Perry): each "temporal self" is treated as its own agent who forms beliefs by ordinary Bayesian updating on the equilibrium strategy, which does pin down a determinate credence.
- Naive indifference / "halfer" reasoning: the two intersections are subjectively identical to him, so plain symmetry says 1/2 each, and anything cleverer is overthinking a simple case.
- Visit-frequency / SIA-style reasoning: credence should track how often each node actually gets visited under the equilibrium strategy, so more weight goes on the first intersection since he's guaranteed to pass through it.
- Evidential self-location: treat the choice you're about to make as evidence about which node you're at, the way evidential decision theory treats acts as evidence about the world in Newcomb-style cases.
- Eliminativism about imperfect-recall credence: there just isn't a fact of the matter — agents who can't distinguish their past selves don't have well-defined degrees of belief, and decision theory should be rebuilt without requiring one.
