I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and I've been reading around the scientific realism literature for a longer piece on what, if anything, we're entitled to carry over when a fundamental theory gets overturned. In the course of that I ran into an internal split among structural realists themselves that I hadn't appreciated going in. Epistemic structural realists, in the tradition of John Worrall, hold that we can know the mathematical structure a theory shares with its successor even after the theory's own ontology is abandoned — the standard example being Fresnel's equations for reflected and refracted light, which reappear, reinterpreted, as a limiting case within Maxwell's electromagnetism — while remaining agnostic about whatever further, structure-independent nature the underlying objects might have. Ontic structural realists, following James Ladyman and Steven French, think that this residual "further nature" is either unintelligible or simply dispensable: for them the relations are what's fundamental, and objects are at most derivative nodes within them.

Could you write me a dialogue between one proponent of each view, working through exactly where they part ways rather than just restating their slogans at each other? I'd like the ontic structural realist to press the old Newman objection against the epistemic side — the worry, dating to Newman's 1928 review of Russell, that "knowing only the structure" collapses into an almost empty claim about cardinality unless something more is smuggled back in — and I'd like the epistemic structural realist to have a real rejoinder rather than folding immediately. It would also help if the two of them tested their positions against a concrete case, such as how each should interpret the failure of the identity of indiscernibles for particles obeying quantum statistics. This needn't be long — a dozen or so exchanges that actually earn their keep would serve better than an exhaustive treatise — but please make sure both characters are arguing at full strength and genuinely responding to each other's last point, rather than delivering two self-contained position statements side by side.
