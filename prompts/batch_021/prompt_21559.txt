So I've been down a bit of a Frank Ramsey rabbit hole lately -- the kind of thing that happens when you keep seeing "Ramsey-de Finetti" and "Dutch book" thrown around in decision theory threads and finally decide you should know more about the actual guy behind the name. Turns out he was a convinced atheist (his own brother became Archbishop of Canterbury later, which is a fun detail) who died at 26, and people who knew him kept remarking on how wierdly untroubled he was by all of it -- not in a repressed way, more like it was an actual considered position. There's a passage of his (I've seen it paraphrased a bit differently in different places so don't hold me to the exact wording) where he says he doesn't feel humbled by the vastness of the heavens, because stars are physically enormous but can't think or love, and thinking and loving are the sort of things that actually make something matter, not sheer size. He's also got this image of the world as a picture drawn in perspective rather than to scale, with human beings filling up the foreground and the stars shrunk down to the size of coins. As a one-liner its a great quip, but it kind of reads like a mood dressed up as an argument, and I want to know if that's unfair.

What I'm actually after is whether there's a real argument underneath it, and how it fits with the rest of his views. He was tangled up with the Tractatus early -- as a teenager he worked on the English translation and then wrote a pretty sharp critical review of it for Mind -- and Wittgenstein's famous move at the end of that book is basically the opposite of Ramsey's: the sense/value of the world can't be found anywhere inside the world of facts, so it has to sit entirely outside it, in the mystical part that can only be shown and not said. Ramsey just doesn't seem to need that move at all; on his view human importance is fully available from inside an ordinary human-scale perspective, no exit from the world required. But this is also the same guy whose whole account of probability grounds it in human betting behavior rather than in anything mind-independent, and who's got a deflationary 'redundancy' theory of truth where there's no substantive property of truth to discover either. So is his stance on meaning/importance really one unified naturalist package -- probability, truth, and importance are all just facts about creatures like us, all the way down, and that's fine -- or is there a real tension in being that deflationary about truth and probability while still wanting human importance to come out feeling robust instead of equally deflated? I'd like the actual reconstructed argument here rather than a Wikipedia-summary version, and some sense of whether it holds together as philosophy or is basically just Ramsey's temperament talking.
