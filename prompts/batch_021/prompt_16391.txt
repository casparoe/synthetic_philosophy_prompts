Ok here's a decision theory rabbit hole I've been stuck in. In the acausal-threats literature (Oesterheld, the CLR crowd) there's this idea of "surrogate goals": an agent facing possible extortion commits in advance to caring just as much about some cheap, basically made-up goal as it does about the thing it actually values, so that a rational threatener redirects their leverage onto the fake goal instead, and the agent's real values never get touched. I suspect this trick is fundamentally unstable once you take seriously that a good enough threatener can reason about *how* the surrogate got picked in the first place, not just about the surrogate itself. Can you lay out, step by step, an argument for the claim that surrogate goals don't actually solve the threat problem, they just push it up a level -- i.e. a sufficiently sophisticated threatener will threaten the mechanism/policy that generates the surrogate goal rather than the surrogate goal itself, and once that's on the table the whole scheme unravels? Don't just assert the regress, actually argue for why it doesnt bottom out somewhere safe.
