I'm about a third of the way into a historical novel set in the Leipzig book trade of the 1790s, following a young clerk whose master, an old-fashioned publisher, is drafting a pamphlet against a rival house that has just brought out a pirated edition of one of his bestselling authors. Because no single German territory's printing privileges reached beyond its own borders in this period, such Nachdruck was rampant and more or less unpunishable, and publishers had to rely on moral suasion rather than law.

I want my master printer's pamphlet to voice a genuinely serious philosophical argument rather than mere commercial huffing, so I went digging into what contemporaries themselves argued on the matter, and found that Kant and Fichte each wrote an essay on exactly this question within a decade of each other, in the same journal, under almost identical titles. Fichte's 1793 essay, "Proof of the Unlawfulness of Reprinting," splits a book into a "content" (the ideas, which supposedly become the reader's own property the moment they are understood) and a "form" (the exact sequence of words, held to be so bound up with the author's individuality that no one else could ever have produced it), and locates the whole wrong of piracy in the theft of form. Kant's own essay on the subject, written in the mid-1780s and later revisited in the Doctrine of Right, denies that a book is "property" in the relevant sense at all, and instead treats unauthorized publication as a wrong against the author's person: the pirate publisher is, in effect, impersonating an agent authorized to carry the author's words to the public, without ever having received such a mandate — which a friend who trained in law tells me is essentially the old Roman notion of negotiorum gestio dressed up in Kantian language.

Before I put words in my printer's mouth, I would like to actually understand which of these two arguments is doing more real philosophical work. Concretely: what precisely makes reprinting wrong on each view, and does either one manage to avoid smuggling in a hidden property-conception of ideas despite officially disclaiming it? Fichte's form/content split is often credited as an ancestor of the modern idea–expression distinction in copyright law; is that credit deserved, or does Kant's proxy-and-mandate argument actually capture something about the wrong of piracy that Fichte's account misses? Please don't simply set the two arguments side by side and leave it there — tell me which one you find the more defensible foundation for a right against literary piracy, and justify that verdict rather than hedging.
