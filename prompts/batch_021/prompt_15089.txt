I've spent enough time reading Slate Star Codex, LessWrong, the EA forum, and similar corners of the internet that I've internalized one of their central norms almost as a reflex: you should always be willing to put a number on your uncertainty, however rough, rather than taking refuge in vague talk about things being "too uncertain to quantify." The reasoning, as I understand it, is that declining to name a number doesn't make the underlying uncertainty disappear; it just hides whatever number is implicit in how you actually end up acting. I've found this a genuinely useful habit of mind, but a recent disagreement with a friend has left me less sure it's the right norm for every situation.

The disagreement came up while I was deciding how to split my annual charitable giving between a handful of well-evidenced global health charities and a couple of organizations working on more speculative risks from advanced AI. My problem isn't a shortage of estimates for how likely an AI-driven catastrophe is this century; if anything, there are too many, and they range from well under one percent to well over thirty percent. What strikes me is that this spread doesn't come from people feeding different inputs into a model they otherwise share; it comes from people relying on fundamentally different stories about how AI development will unfold, several of which strike me as reasonable on their own terms. My friend's position is that none of this should change what I actually do: I should still combine the estimates into a single figure, even if it's just a crude average, and multiply through, because refusing to settle on one number doesn't make the problem go away — whatever I end up doing will reveal some implicit number regardless, so I might as well choose it deliberately. My own instinct is that treating this like ordinary statistical uncertainty, where I trust a single model and I'm only unsure about its inputs, loses something real: I don't just have uncertainty about parameters, I have uncertainty about which story is even approximately right, and that seems like a good reason to hedge across causes rather than commit everything to whatever conclusion the single number recommends.

So: is my friend correct that any hesitation to settle on one number is just a failure of nerve dressed up as epistemic caution, or is there a principled difference between the two kinds of uncertainty I've just described, one that would justify acting on them differently? I'm less interested in which charities you personally think I should fund than in which side of this argument is actually right.
