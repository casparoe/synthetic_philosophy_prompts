So I'm working on a piece about scientists and intellectuals who moonlighted in the paranormal, and Henry Sidgwick has turned into my favorite (and most confusing) case study. He's apparently the guy philosophers point to as one of the most careful, rigorous utilitarian thinkers of the 19th century — Methods of Ethics gets described as almost painfully thorough and precise. And yet this same guy co-founded the Society for Psychical Research and spent the last two decades of his life investigating mediums, telepathy, and haunted houses, which seems like a totally seperate thing from writing dense treatises on hedonism and the greatest happiness principle. My working theory for the piece is basically a compartmentalization story: Sidgwick lost his religious faith as a young man, and the psychical research was really just this brilliant guy's way of coping with that loss, quietly hoping to find scientific proof of an afterlife so the universe still felt meaningful — totally separate from, maybe even in tension with, the actual arguments in his philosophy.

Before I write it up that way, I wanted to sanity check it with you, since I can already picture my editor asking if I'm sure. Is the "brilliant philosopher, one irrational hobby" framing basically right? Or is there something in the actual structure of his ethical theory itself that the ghost hunting was a response to, that I'd be flattening if I treat it as just a side project or a personal blind spot?
