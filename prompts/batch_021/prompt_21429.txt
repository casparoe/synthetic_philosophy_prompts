So I've been down a bit of a rabbit hole on Bayesian philosophy of science lately, which started because of some old Overcoming Bias/LessWrong-era post I stumbled back into about how Einstein already knew about the weird excess precession in Mercury's orbit before he'd even finished General Relativity, and how people still treat that as one of the strongest confirmations GR ever got. Turns out this is apparently the textbook example for something called "the problem of old evidence" in Bayesian confirmation theory: if your credence in E is already 1 because you've known about E for years, conditionalizing on E can't move your credence in H at all (P(H|E) just collapses to P(H)), which seems totally at odds with the intuition that the perihelion thing really did confirm GR alot. This bugs me more than it probably should, because that whole "beliefs need to pay rent in advance" idea from the Sequences is such a load-bearing piece of how I think about evidence, and old evidence seems to break the formal machinery that's supposed to cash that intuition out.

Digging around, there's apparently no single agreed fix, just a few competing patches, and two that keep coming up are: (1) Garber's move of dropping the logical omniscience assumption, so what you're actually updating on isn't E itself but the newly-noticed fact that H entails E, and (2) some kind of "counterfactual" solution (I've seen it pinned on Maher, and I think theres a newer version from Sprenger?) where you measure confirmation by asking what your credence in H would've been if you hadn't already known E, and treat that hypothetical jump as the real confirmation value instead of the actual, current one.

Could you walk me through how these two solutions actually differ mechanically, and then give me your honest take on which one comes closer to genuinely rescuing Bayesian confirmation theory as a normative account of scientific reasoning, vs. which one is more of a band-aid over the same hole? Extra credit if you can say something about whether either solution has any teeth against the sketchier case of a theory that's basically reverse-engineered after the fact to fit data that's already fully baked into everyone's priors, since that feels like the version of this problem that actually matters for stuff like evaluating some new economic or forecasting model that conveniently "explains" a crisis after it already happened.
