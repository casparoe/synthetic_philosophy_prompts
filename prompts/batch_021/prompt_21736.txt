The exam question was: "In 'The Idea of Public Reason Revisited,' Rawls introduces the 'proviso' as a way of specifying how citizens may appeal to their comprehensive religious or philosophical doctrines within public political debate about constitutional essentials and matters of basic justice. (a) State what the proviso requires. (b) Rawls illustrates the proviso using the historical examples of the abolitionists and of Martin Luther King Jr.'s civil rights movement; explain how he uses these examples and what conclusion he draws about whether their advocacy was compatible with public reason. (c) Explain the distinction Rawls draws between the 'public political forum' and the 'background culture,' and why it matters for the scope of the 'duty of civility.'"

Here is the student's answer in full:

"Rawls's later work locates the question of religion within his broader theory of public reason. Because citizens in a modern constitutional democracy affirm a plurality of reasonable but conflicting comprehensive doctrines — religious, philosophical, and moral — and because, after the Reformation, we can no longer expect convergence on comprehensive religious or metaphysical truth, Rawls argues that when citizens debate and vote on constitutional essentials and matters of basic justice they should so far as possible appeal only to considerations that other reasonable citizens could accept regardless of which comprehensive doctrine they hold. This is what he calls public reason, and it is meant to satisfy a criterion of reciprocity: political power is legitimate only when it is exercised on terms that all reasonable citizens could endorse.

Because this ideal might seem to exclude religious citizens from politics altogether, Rawls adds the 'proviso': citizens may always introduce their comprehensive religious (or secular) doctrines into public political debate, provided that, in due course, they also present properly public reasons — reasons that do not depend on any particular comprehensive doctrine — sufficient by themselves to support whatever the comprehensive doctrine was invoked to support. When citizens on all sides do this successfully over time, Rawls says the result is an overlapping consensus: the same political conclusion turns out to be endorsed from within each citizen's own comprehensive doctrine, so that satisfying the proviso in public debate is really just the day-to-day mechanism by which an overlapping consensus gets built.

Rawls's own illustration is the abolitionists and Martin Luther King Jr., both of whom relied heavily on explicitly religious language and premises to argue against slavery and segregation. Rawls's verdict is that, strictly speaking, this advocacy went against the ideal of public reason, since it relied on comprehensive doctrine without yet supplying the independent public reasons the proviso requires; he treats the case as a limiting one in which pressing comprehensive doctrine into service ahead of public reason is excusable, even necessary, given the urgency of ending slavery and segregation, but not as an example of the proviso actually being satisfied.

Finally, Rawls restricts the strict duty of civility — the duty to be guided by public reason — to what he calls the public political forum: judges giving their decisions, government officials and legislators acting in their official capacities, and candidates for public office in their campaigns, along with citizens themselves when voting on constitutional essentials. This is distinct from what he calls the background culture: churches, universities, professional and voluntary associations, and the informal public sphere generally, where citizens remain entirely free to reason from their comprehensive doctrines without any constraint from public reason at all. The distinction matters because it shows that Rawls's account is not asking religious citizens to bracket their faith in ordinary life, or even in most political speech, but only in the specific, narrow context of justifying the exercise of coercive political power over constitutional essentials."
