I'm doing an AI safety fellowship project where I built a tiny toy agent that's supposed to trust a future, self-modified version of itself enough to hand off control -- basically a baby version of that old Yudkowsky/Herreshoff tiling agents paper (or whatever it's actually called, I read it kind of fast). I kept slamming into this thing people call the Löbian obstacle: if your agent only lets its successor act once the successor has *proven* the action is fine, and you want the current agent to trust "anything my successor proves must be true," Löb's theorem says a consistent system can't establish that trust schema for arbitrary statements without basically already proving the statements itself. So the whole "I can fully vouch for my future self" idea seems to collapse, or you just get an infinite regress of weaker and weaker successors.

I brought this up with my mentor and another fellow and it turned into a real arguement. She thinks its a genuinely deep limit on self-trust for any sufficiently powerful reasoner, human or AI, and that stuff like parametric polymorphism or logical induction just relabels the problem instead of solving it. He thinks the obstacle is mostly an artifact of insisting on strict, all-or-nothing provability inside one fixed formal system, and that moving to something more probabilistic (reflective oracles, logical induction) makes most of the problem dissolve.
