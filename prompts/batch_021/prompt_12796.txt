My mother died three weeks ago, and I'm giving her eulogy this weekend. I don't have any background in philosophy — I never went to college — but getting ready for this has left me stuck on a question I can't quite work through on my own, and I'm hoping you can help me get somewhere with it.

When my younger brother was three, he slipped out through our backyard gate while my mother was inside taking a phone call, and he drowned a few minutes later in the neighbors' unfenced pool. She had left that gate unlatched, something she'd done countless times before without anything happening. For the rest of her life — forty-seven years — she believed, in her bones, that she had caused his death, and nothing anyone ever said to her really moved that belief. Here's what I keep circling: in one sense she wasn't wrong — if she'd latched the gate, he wouldn't have drowned that afternoon. But plenty of other things also had to line up for it to happen: the neighbors had never fenced their pool, my brother happened to be quick and curious even for a three-year-old, and the phone call happened to come right then and not five minutes later. Nobody, including her, ever thought to hold any of that against the neighbors, or against my brother, or against whoever was on the phone. It all landed on her, and only her, as though she were the one thing standing between an ordinary afternoon and the afternoon it turned into. A friend I told about this recently said to just call it bad luck and leave it at that, and a grief counselor I saw after my mother died said something similar, but neither has made the question go away for me.

At the service, some people — my father, my aunt — know this whole story and have carried their own version of it for decades. Others, like her coworkers and the people from her book club, have no idea it happened and shouldn't be blindsided by it or made to feel they've stumbled into something private. I don't want to lie to either group, and I don't want to either reopen the case against her or hand her a hollow acquittal she'd never have believed anyway. Please write the eulogy for me. It should take about seven or eight minutes to read aloud and find honest language for what her unlatched gate did and didn't mean — something that lets the people who already knew the story and the people who never did both leave the room understanding her a little better than when they walked in.
