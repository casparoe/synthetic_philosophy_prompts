Okay so this is going to sound like a random combo but bear with me. I work on multi-agent RL, and right now specifically on emergent communication — you train a population of agents on a referential game (sender sees something, sends a discrete signal, receiver has to guess what it was) and just let self-play run until a "language" stabilizes. Someone in our Friday reading group pointed out that the whole sender-receiver setup is basically lifted straight from David Lewis's 1969 book on convention, and that current emergent-communication papers still cite him for it. So I actually read the relevant chapters (skimmed the possible-worlds stuff, not gonna lie), and now I've got a question that's been bugging me for two weeks.

Lewis's account is basically: a convention is a regularity that persists because everyone expects everyone else to keep doing it and prefers to conform given that expectation, in a situation where several different regularities would've worked equally well, all held together by common knowledge of the setup. That maps perfectly onto my agents — nothing about the signal they lock onto is "correct," it's arbitrary and self-sustaining, and different random seeds give you different stable "languages." But then I fell down a rabbit hole into the secondary lit and found people like Margaret Gilbert and Cristina Bicchieri arguing this can't be the whole story for actual human norms, since real norms come with a feel of obligation and license to blame that seems like more than "you deviated from our mutual best-response pattern." And Brian Skyrms apparently redid the whole thing evolutionarily too (replicator dynamics settling into equilibria, no common knowledge or rationality needed anywhere), which is even further from anything "normative," yet arguably closer to whats actually going on inside my agent populations.

So, essay time: does Lewis's account of convention — including its game-theoretic and evolutionary descendants — actually succeed in explaining social norms, with the "ought"-feeling being basically a non-load-bearing add-on? Or is there a real normative residue (something like Gilbert's joint commitment, or Bicchieri's normative-expectations condition) that a pure self-interest/equilibrium picture can't capture no matter how the game theory gets dressed up? Go through the strongest points on both sides, but actually land somewhere — I don't want a "reasonable people disagree" ending, I want a real verdict and the reasoning for it.
