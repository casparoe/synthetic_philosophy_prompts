Since retiring from a career in experimental optics, I have spent the last couple of years working through the philosophy of science, and I keep returning to Larry Laudan's pessimistic meta-induction: if so many empirically successful past theories—caloric, phlogiston, the luminiferous ether—turned out to be false in their central claims rather than merely incomplete, what entitles us to think our current best theories are even approximately true? I am especially interested in whether John Worrall's epistemic structural realism actually defuses this argument or merely relocates it. Write an essay that tests structural realism against the specific case Worrall leans on: the survival of Fresnel's equations, essentially intact, inside Maxwell's electromagnetic theory, despite the outright abandonment of the mechanical ether they were built to describe. Take seriously the Newman objection that a purely structural claim about the world is thin enough to be almost trivially satisfied by any sufficiently large domain, and weigh it against the "no miracles" intuition that such precise predictive success would otherwise be a staggering coincidence. Don't hedge: tell me whether structural continuity of this sort is enough to rescue realism, and defend the verdict against the strongest objection to it.
