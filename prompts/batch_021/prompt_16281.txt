So I've been falling down a rabbit hole on old Turing papers for a piece and I can't shake this one bit of "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" that everyone treats as an embarassing throwaway. In section 9, "The Argument from Extra-Sensory Perception," Turing flatly states the statistical evidence for telepathy is "overwhelming," and he seriously proposes testing contestants in a "telepathy-proof room" so the interrogator can't just ask something like 'what card am I holding' and unmask the machine that way. Every writeup I find treats it as a rare lapse from a guy who was rigorous about literally everything else. But the actual best evidence going around in 1950 was stuff like Soal's card-guessing experiments, which serious people at the time considered close to fraud proof, and which didn't get exposed as rigged until decades after Turing was already dead. Can you build the strongest possible case that Turing's call here was epistemically reasonable, not credulous, given what a careful thinker could've actually known in 1950? I don't want the safe "he had a blind spot but its understandable" answer -- I want the real argument that he got it right by the lights of his evidence.
