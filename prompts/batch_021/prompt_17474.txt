My uncle was drafted in 1968 and served two tours in Vietnam. By all accounts he was scrupulous in the field, but he spent the rest of his life unconvinced that this squared him with having fought in a war he'd come to think was unjust. He used to say he'd earned the right to be judged on how he fought, not on what the war was -- then admit, in the same breath, that he never quite believed his own line. He died last week, and I'm giving the eulogy this weekend to a room that includes men who served with him and would bristle at any suggestion he'd done wrong. While writing it I've been reading about the traditional view, associated with Michael Walzer, that a soldier's conduct can be judged independently of his side's cause, and the newer argument, from philosophers like Jeff McMahan, that this 'moral equality of combatants' doesn't hold: an unjust cause taints even scrupulous conduct, whatever excuse the soldier has for not knowing better. I'm a software engineer, not a philosopher, so I may have some of this slightly wrong, but I don't want the eulogy to quietly settle a question he died without settling himself. Please write it so it comforts that room honestly, without pretending the question was ever simple, and without lecturing anyone on just war theory.
