So I'm on this small internal committee at work (mid-size logistics company, maybe 800 people) that decides who gets our twice-a-year "Integrity Champion" award. It's basically a certificate plus a $2,000 bonus, but people take it seriously since it gets read out at the all-hands. The policy describing it is like four sentences long and says it goes to whoever reports serious wrongdoing "in good faith, for the right reasons, and at some personal risk to themselves." Nobody ever bothered defining "for the right reasons," because up until now it's always been obvious.

This cycle's nominee, Dana, reported that her department head was falsifying safety inspection logs, which turned out to be completely true and pretty serious -- it led to real fixes before anyone got hurt. The wrinkle is that Dana was also next in line for that same department head's job, and she got it about a month after he was pushed out. In her nomination statement she says she came forward purely out of concern for the warehouse crew's safety. A couple people on the commitee who've worked with her for years say, off the record, that she'd wanted that promotion for ages and this was obviously her opening. There's genuinely no way for us to settle what was actually going on in her head -- all we've got is her statement, the timing, and a track record that's honestly pretty good.

Half the committee thinks the motive question is a red herring: the report was true, the danger was real, and turning it in is exactly what someone who cared about the crew would've done, so it doesn't matter what else was mixed in -- she acted on a reason that genuinely justifies the act, full stop. The other half thinks that if the promotion was really doing the motivational work, handing her an "integrity" award basically launders politics as principle, and teaches the next ambitious person exactly the wrong lesson about how to get ahead here. I keep flip-flopping, but I think we're circling around a version of a debate philosophers actually have, about whether acting "for the right reasons" is about the reasons that explain why you did something or just the reasons that would justify doing it. Can you write the short memo I'd bring to our meeting next week? I want it to recommend whether we grant the award, deny it, or grant it with some kind of modification, and I want the reasoning to be something the half of the committee that loses this vote could still walk away thinking was fair.
