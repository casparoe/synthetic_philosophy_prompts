Had a fun/slightly humbling experience at an ACX meetup a couple weekends ago: a few of us played a few rounds of the centipede game for actual (small) stakes, and just like every intro game theory write-up warns, nobody grabbed the pot on the first move. We all kept passing for a couple rounds before someone finally caved and took the money, and afterward there was this weird vibe like we'd each personally flunked a rationality test.

Anyway, here's what's bugging me. The backward induction argument seems pretty airtight: at the last node the player obviously takes the bigger pile, so the second-to-last player, anticipating that, should take at their own node instead, and this unravels all the way back to the first move -- so as long as both players have common knowledge of each other's rationality (funny enough, apparently the same "common knowledge" guy, Aumann, is behind the agreement theorem that gets thrown around on LW alot), grabbing the pot immediately seems like the only outcome that's actually consistent with that assumption. So if backward induction really is just the correct derivation of what a rational, payoff-maximizing agent does here, is there any known technique (CFAR-style or otherwise) for training yourself to actually play it out, the way you might train away scope insensitivity or the planning fallacy? Or is passing for a few rounds not really a "bias" in the same sense at all -- more like some other kind of mistake that debiasing tools wouldn't even touch? Curious what I'm missing here, since the standard writeups always just seem to gesture at "humans are bad at game theory" without saying much more.
