For a magazine feature on how experimental philosophy is complicating old armchair debates about action and intention, I've been retelling Joshua Knobe's well-known "side-effect effect" case to the people I interview: a company chairman is told that a new program will raise profits but, as a side effect, harm the environment; he says he doesn't care about the environment at all, only the profit, and green-lights it; asked afterward, most people say he harmed the environment intentionally. Swap "harm" for "help" throughout, keeping the chairman just as indifferent, and most people say he did not help the environment intentionally. At a workshop dinner, two of my sources, a cognitive scientist named Elena and a philosopher named Marcus, ended up seated together and spent the rest of the meal disputing what this asymmetry actually shows. I took notes afterward because I wanted to use the exchange in the piece, but I honestly can't tell who came out ahead.

Elena opened by calling the effect a straightforward bias: people first decide, on moral grounds, that the chairman deserves blame for the harm, and then adjust their judgments of his intention — and, she added, even of what he "knew" or "believed" would happen — to rationalize that blame, the same way assigning blame has elsewhere been shown to inflate judgments of skill, foresight, and causal control after a bad outcome. Marcus objected that calling this a "bias" quietly assumes there is some pure, morality-free fact about intentional action that the moral judgment is distorting; but if the ordinary concept of "intentional action" is partly constituted by moral appraisal, the way a concept like "reckless" plainly is, then the moral judgment is not corrupting anything — it is doing exactly what the concept asks of it. Elena's comeback was that this cannot be the whole story, because the very same asymmetry shows up for verbs with no obvious moral content built into their meaning at all — whether the chairman "knew," "decided," or "was in favor of" the side effect — and once moral valence can swing something as flatly factual as a belief attribution, that looks like blame leaking outward, not a concept doing its job. Marcus's reply was that the spread to belief and knowledge need not mean bias either: perhaps we infer an agent's hidden mental states from what we take to be his settled character, so that indifference reads as concealed disregard when it precedes something bad and as sheer luck when it precedes something good — a general, and perfectly respectable, principle for reading minds from actions, not a distortion of anything. Elena's last word was that this "deep self" move quietly abandons Marcus's own earlier point, since it explains the whole pattern without any moral component built into the concept of "intentional" at all, so he cannot consistently hold both.

Could you go through their exchange move by move and say where each of them is right and where each overreaches, rather than just naming an overall winner? I would also like your take on whether Elena is correct that Marcus's two points are in tension, and on whether there is a better diagnosis of the original asymmetry that neither of them considered.
