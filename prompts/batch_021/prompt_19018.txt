My grandparents are celebrating their fiftieth wedding anniversary next month, and my mom has volunteered me to give the toast, since I'm apparently the only grandchild who has taken a philosophy elective at school and can "speak his language." The trouble is that my grandfather, who taught metaphysics at the community college for thirty years before retiring, is a committed perdurantist. He genuinely maintains that a persisting thing like a person is never wholly present at any single moment, but is instead stretched across its whole lifetime as a series of temporal parts, in the way a road is stretched across space rather than sitting entirely in one place. On his view, strictly speaking, "the young man who proposed in 1975" and "the man being toasted next month" are two different temporal parts of one four-dimensional whole, not one and the same thing fully present at both dates. He has been known to bring this up at family dinners just to tease my grandmother about it, and roughly a third of the guests will be his former philosophy department colleagues, who will certainly notice if I get the view wrong, while everyone else at the party just wants a toast warm enough to make my grandmother tear up.

Could you write me a four-minute toast that takes his perdurantism seriously as a genuine bit, rather than flattening it into "philosophers say strange things," and that is built around one real conceit: something like what it means for a vow made by one temporal part of a person to bind every later temporal part, and what it says about my grandfather that he has kept faith with my grandmother across every temporal part he's had so far. I would like it to land as a sincere tribute rather than a metaphysics lecture, and if you can work in something the non-philosophers already half-know, such as the ship of Theseus, as a bridge into the jargon, that would help it land for both halves of the room.
