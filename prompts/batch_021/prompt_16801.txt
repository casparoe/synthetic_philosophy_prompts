So I spent thirty-five years running a molecular biology lab before I retired, and now they've got me on the university's research integrity committee. Current case: the core imaging data behind Figure 3 of a well-cited paper from the lab can't be reproduced -- a grad student building on it went back to the raw files and the numbers don't match whats published. The postdoc who generated the data left research years ago and wont answer emails. The two remaining co-authors, still faculty here, say they simply trusted her the way you'd trust any careful colleague you'd known for a decade, no red flags at the time, and shouldn't be held responsible for data they never personally generated or checked. A few younger members want a blanket rule instead: nobody may ever rely on a co-author's word about data alone, no matter how trusted, without an independent check. Funnily enough I've been reading about this exact fight in epistemology over testimony lately -- whether trusting what someone tells you is its own basic source of justification, or whether it only really counts once you can cash it out in evidence you could've checked yourself -- and I can't settle that even in the abstract, let alone here. Could you draft the memo I'll circulate before next week's meeting? I need an actual recommendation -- clear them, find them at fault, or something in between -- with a concrete timeframe, plus a call on what the policy should be going forward, and it has to take the "reasonable reliance on a trusted colleague" side seriously, because if that's never enough I don't see how any collaborative lab ever gets anything done.
