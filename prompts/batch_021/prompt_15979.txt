I'm a junior at my high school, and this year I was elected chair of our Honor Council, the student-run body that hears academic-integrity cases and forwards a recommended finding to the dean of students. The full council has eleven members, but I don't sit on individual hearing panels: each case is heard by five members drawn from the group, and our bylaws just say the panel decides "by majority vote," without ever specifying majority vote on what. That gap actually mattered for a case we heard last week. The handbook defines a "sourcing violation" as requiring two separate things to both be true: (A) the submitted work is substantially similar to a source the student wasn't authorized to use, and (B) the student had access to that source before the assignment was due (this second condition exists so that two people who independently land on the same idea aren't treated as if one copied the other). The handbook is explicit that the verdict is a strict function of (A) and (B): violation if and only if both hold.

As chair, I asked each panelist to record their own view of (A), their own view of (B), and the overall verdict those views implied for them personally, before anyone saw anyone else's answers. Three of the five panelists concluded (A) was satisfied; three also concluded (B) was satisfied, though not necessarily the same three. But when we tallied each panelist's own overall verdict, only two came out to "violation," so the panel forwarded a 2-3 finding of "no violation" to the dean. (I'd just started a unit on voting rules in my AP Government class when this happened, which is probably why I noticed the tallies at all instead of just accepting the result.) Some council members think the panel's own numbers show that a majority actually believed both elements of the offense, so reporting "no violation" doesn't reflect what the panel collectively found, and they want future bylaws to require voting on (A) and (B) separately and letting the verdict follow automatically. Others think that's backwards: the verdict is the only thing the panel actually has authority to decide, and it would be strange to tell a student the council found against them when three of five panelists, each fully aware of the handbook's test, personally voted "no violation." I can see real force in both positions, which is exactly the problem: whatever I recommend, roughly half the council will think I've ignored the obvious answer.

I need to write the memo, probably a page or so, that the council will discuss and vote on at our meeting next Wednesday, and I'd like your help drafting it. It should take a clear position on two things: what to do about the pending case, and what our standing bylaw on panel voting should say going forward. And it should represent the side it doesn't adopt seriously enough that those council members would recognize their actual argument in it, rather than a weaker version of it.
