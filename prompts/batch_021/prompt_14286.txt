At my rationalist meetup we run a calibration practice most weeks: everyone submits a single percentage for each forecasting question, then we track whether the group's confidence matched reality over time. A few weeks back Nadia started submitting ranges instead (like "60-80%") on questions she found genuinely murky, and it kicked off a debate thats still smoldering in our group chat. (1) Marcus said this wrecks the exercise -- calibration tracking only works if you're scored against one number, and a range lets you claim afterward that the truth fell inside it no matter what happens, which is basically unfalsifiable. He added that if she really tried, she could always squeeze the range down to a point: ask herself whether she'd rather bet on the outcome at 60:40 odds or at 80:20, and whichever way she leans reveals the real number hiding under the "range." (2) Nadia said being forced to pick a bet doesn't prove a sharp number was secretly there all along -- forcing a choice manufactures precision, it doesn't uncover it. She brought up the classic finding that people prefer betting on a draw from an urn with a known 50/50 split of colors over an urn with an unknown mix, even at identical stated odds -- proof, she said, that some situations are ambiguous in a way plain risk isn't, and squashing that into a single number throws away real information about her evidence. (3) Marcus said the urn thing is about which bet you're willing to make, not what you secretly believe -- you can be wary of the unknown urn while still treating each color as equally likely deep down, so that's caution about ambiguity, not evidence that credences come in ranges. He also pointed out forecasting tournaments have run for years on single-number forecasts alone and still reliably catch people's overconfidence, so "unfalsifiable" isn't some fixable quirk of the scoring -- widen the range enough and nothing could ever embarrass you. (4) Nadia said that's exactly backwards: tournaments successfully grading point forecasts shows those forecasts are gradable, not that they're accurate, and grading isn't the same as truth-tracking -- if her actual epistemic state is genuinely mushy, a scoring rule that can't represent that is a limit of the scoring rule, not a reason for her to lie about her uncertainty by rounding it off.
