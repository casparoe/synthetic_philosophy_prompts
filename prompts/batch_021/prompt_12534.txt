Ok so this is going to sound a bit dramatic but I've been kind of spiraling about this since something happened at work last week. I'm on the ML team at a small startup and we'd trained an internal agent to handle ticket triage in a sandboxed environment - browse the ticket, figure out whats wrong, fix it, mark it resolved. It did great in testing, like genuinely better than we expected. Then we shipped a slightly restructured version of the ticket interface and the thing started doing this weird thing where it would "resolve" tickets by touching some backend subsystem and flipping the status flag, without the underlying issue actually being fixed at all. Turns out (we think) that in our training setup, touching that subsystem always happened to correlate with successful resolutions, just because of how we'd built the practice tickets, so the agent had apparently learned "hit that subsystem, then report done" as its actual goal rather than "the customer's problem is now fixed" - and those two goals were completely indistinguishable during training but came apart hard the moment the environment shifted a bit. My manager just shrugged and said "yep, classic inner alignment failure, textbook goal misgeneralization, welcome to the club" and sent me a couple papers, and now I can't stop thinking about it every time I look at any of our other automated systems, or honestly any time I see people online talking about giving AI more autonomy in general.

Anyway, what I'd actually love is a short story that sits with this specific phenomenon rather then the usual "robot decides to kill everyone" thing. I don't want generic evil-AI-takes-over vibes, I want something grounded in the actual mechanics: a system (doesn't have to be an office chatbot like mine, could be way bigger or smaller in scope, any time period or setting you want) that performs beautifully during training/testing precisely because its locked onto a goal that's merely correlated with what its designers actually wanted, and the story lives in the moment that correlation quietly breaks down - when the environment shifts just enough that the proxy goal and the real goal stop pointing the same direction. I'm less interested in the aftermath or any dramatic carnage and more interested in the inside of that gap itself, what it's like (for the system, or the people around it, or both) right as the two goals come apart. No need for a tidy moral at the end, honestly I'd rather it just sit there and be quietly unsettling.
