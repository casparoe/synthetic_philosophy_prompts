I'm the required non-scientist community member on my university's Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. I have no training in philosophy, but a protocol we approved last year has stayed with me, and I've spent a lot of evenings since reading around its ethics on my own. Next month we're voting on a similar protocol: repeated survival surgery and induced chronic pain in macaques, to test a treatment for chronic nerve pain. The justification the scientist-members always give is the usual one -- animal suffering can be weighed against expected human benefit on a scale tied to how cognitively complex the species is, whereas no amount of expected benefit would let us treat a human being this way. I've since found out this lines up with a split Nozick draws in Anarchy, State, and Utopia, something like 'utilitarianism for animals, Kantianism for people,' right before he worries that the very same reasoning would entitle a far more cognitively sophisticated being to treat us exactly as we treat lab animals, and that it can't explain why a severely cognitively impaired human still gets full protection while an animal with comparable capacities doesn't. Our committee has never engaged with that second worry; we just run through the standard 3Rs checklist and move on. Could you draft the short opinion I'll circulate before the vote? It should land on one specific recommendation -- approve, reject, or approve with named conditions -- and it should take that unresolved tension seriously rather than quietly setting it aside.
