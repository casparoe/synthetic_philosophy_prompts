I spent thirty-five years as an analytical chemist before retiring, and I have only taken up philosophy seriously in the last couple of years, so I hope you will forgive any imprecision in how I describe the background here. My oldest friend, Walter, died two weeks ago at eighty-four. For as long as I knew him he kept a small ledger, ruled exactly as Franklin describes in his autobiography, tracking the same thirteen virtues week by week and marking a dot against himself for every daily lapse. He loved Franklin's admission that he never conquered his own worst habit, and Franklin's joke about settling for a "speckled axe" rather than a perfectly bright one; he was also, half-seriously, wary of being praised for his Humility specifically, since Franklin had noted that a man convinced of his own perfect humility would, in that very conviction, have found a new occasion for pride. I am to give the eulogy this Saturday, to a room divided fairly evenly between the Thursday-evening reading group he ran out of his kitchen for thirty years, who will catch every one of these references, and grandchildren who mainly remember a man who made them write notes of apology for arriving late to dinner. Walter always insisted that the whole point of Franklin's scheme was that one never arrives, and that a eulogy claiming he had finally mastered himself, or praising his humility too plainly, would have offended him more than any candid mention of his well-documented faults. Please write the eulogy: about five minutes long, warm enough for people who have never heard of Franklin, and honest enough that Walter, hearing it, would not have winced.
