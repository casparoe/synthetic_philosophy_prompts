Hey, so I'm outlining a new novel set in the mid-50s and I've been going down a rabbit hole of old Fortune and Life essays for research, which is how I stumbled onto von Neumann's "Can We Survive Technology?" piece from '55. I'd only ever known the guy as the game-theory/bomb guy, so I wasn't expecting the essay itself to be so weirdly resigned in tone. The part that's stuck with me isn't the nuclear stuff, which is what you'd expect a doom essay from that era to lead with — its his aside about geophysical and climate warfare, the idea that messing with weather or climate could become an even cheaper, sneakier weapon than the bomb, precisely because you wouldn't need a huge industrial base to pull it off. And then there's his bigger, gloomier point, that technology has basically shrunk the globe for good (no more empty space or distance left to act as a buffer) and that there's no one-time fix for any of this, just an endless run of decisions we have to keep getting right, forever, with no finish line. I brought this up with my writing group last week and half of them had never even heard of the essay, so now I want to actually use it for something.

What I'd like is a short story, maybe 1,500-2,500 words (dont stress about hitting that exactly), close third person, following an engineer or lower-level scientist at some Southern California aerospace outfit — Convair, Douglas, something like that — who reads the essay in late '55 or early '56 and can't shake it. I don't want a generic Cold War paranoia piece where von Neumann is just a scary name dropped for atmosphere; I want the actual substance of his argument — the climate-as-weapon bit specifically, the "shrinking globe" idea, and that grim "no permanent solution, only successive correct decisions" note he ends on — to be the thing the character is actually chewing on, and ideally the thing that pushes him toward some small, concrete decision by the end.

One more note: I'd rather the ending stay a bit ambiguous instead of resolving into either "he becomes an activist" or "he numbs out and forgets about it" — both feel like the easy version of this story to me. And picture him as someone technical but not a physicist himself, more a structures or propulsion guy who's good at compartmentalizing, which is part of why the essay rattles him so much in the first place.
