I'm about two-thirds into a campus novel and I keep chickening out of one scene: my protagonist Petra, a philosophy PhD student TAing a grad seminar on Gödel, is up way too late grading response papers, including one she's not quite sure how to score. I don't want to fake the philosophy just to move my plot along -- if Petra's going to scribble comments in a margin, I want those to be the comments a real logician or historian of philosophy would actually make, not something I invented because it sounded smart to a novelist. So I drafted the seminar prompt and a sample response myself, based on some reading I did on Gödel's stranger theological stuff (sorry if there's typos below, its almost 1am here), and I'd love it if you'd grade the response the way Petra would -- rigorously, like someone who has to justify the mark to a fussy professor, not like someone being kind to a student.

Here's the prompt I invented for the fictional seminar: "Gödel is known for two separate excursions into philosophical theology: a formal modal-logic proof of God's existence that he kept private for decades, and a quite different, informal argument -- laid out mainly in private correspondence and in conversations later recorded by the logician Hao Wang -- for why an afterlife is more probable than not. In 300-400 words: (a) reconstruct the afterlife argument in your own words and identify the single premise doing most of the work; (b) explain how, if at all, this argument depends on or connects to the ontological proof; (c) state what you take to be the argument's biggest weakness."

And here's the "student" response (I wrote this too, so go easy on me, not on it):

"Gödel's argument for an afterlife starts from his general conviction that the world is rationally constructed rather than absurd or the product of blind chance -- the same conviction that made him a mathematical Platonist. Given that, he found it implausible that a person's intellectual and moral capacities, which take a whole lifetime to even partly develop and are barely tapped by the time we die, should simply be annihilated at death; a rationally ordered world wouldn't produce beings with that much unused potential only to waste it, so the balance of probability favors some kind of continued existence in which that development carries on. Importantly, this doesn't rest on any specific religious doctrine or a personal God -- it's closer to a general metaphysical principle, almost a version of the principle of sufficient reason, than to scripture. This is of a piece with, and actually formally builds on, the ontological proof Gödel dictated to Dana Scott: since the modal argument establishes that a being possessing all positive properties necessarily exists, the afterlife falls out as a consequence of the same S5 axioms, guaranteeing that whatever happens to us happens within the scope of that necessarily existing being. Gödel worked all this out in letters to Einstein, his closest friend and walking companion at Princeton, who by all accounts was far more skeptical of the whole thing than Gödel was. The weakest point, I'd say, is just that 'the world is rational' is doing an enormous amount of work for a premise nobody is required to grant."

Could you grade this the way a properly rigorous TA would? I'd want an overall grade (letter, or out of 20, whatever feels natural) plus specific point-by-point comments tied to parts (a), (b) and (c), noting what the student gets right as well as wrong. And if you're able to also give me a short rubric as JSON -- something like part, points possible, points awarded, comment -- that'd be great, since there's a decent chance Petra's actual grading sheet ends up on the page.
