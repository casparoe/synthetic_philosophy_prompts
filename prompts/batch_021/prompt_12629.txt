Last night, a close friend of mine who is finishing a PhD in decision theory and I spent the better part of two hours arguing about the Allais paradox, which she had first posed to me over dinner, almost as a parlor game, before either of us realized how much we would end up disagreeing about it. She gave me the two classic choice problems: in the first, a certain $1 million against a gamble offering an 89% chance of $1 million, a 10% chance of $5 million, and a 1% chance of nothing; in the second, an 11% chance of $1 million (and otherwise nothing) against a 10% chance of $5 million (and otherwise nothing). Like most people who are asked this — and, notoriously, like Savage himself, when Allais first put the problem to a roomful of economists that included three future Nobel laureates — I took the certain million in the first pair and the shot at the larger prize in the second. She then walked me through why no expected-utility function can accommodate both choices at once, so that the pair of preferences, taken together, violates the independence axiom. Where we couldn't agree was on what to make of that result. Her view was that the only honest response, once the inconsistency has been laid out algebraically, is to do what Savage himself eventually did: treat one of the two preferences as a mistake and revise it, especially given that holding onto both leaves you exploitable across a sequence of trades. My view was closer to the line taken by theorists like Mark Machina or Lara Buchak: that the independence axiom quietly assumes there is nothing rationally relevant about the overall shape of a gamble beyond a probability-weighted sum of outcomes considered in isolation, and that a considered concern for security, or for the worst case, is a genuine reason to reject that assumption rather than a bias to be corrected. We spent the last half hour going in circles, restating our own positions without either of us really landing a blow on the other's strongest point.

I would like you to write the dialogue we should have had instead. Set it as a conversation between two decision theorists, one who defends the independence axiom as a real requirement of rationality and treats the Allais preferences as an error to be corrected, and the other who defends those preferences as rational by way of some non-expected-utility framework in the spirit of Machina's or Buchak's work. Please don't let the second side lean on Kahneman and Tversky's prospect theory, since that's explicitly offered as a descriptive rather than a normative theory and would be answering a different question. I want the independence-axiom defender to press the dynamic-consistency or money-pump worry as hard as it can honestly be pressed, and I want the other side to give it the strongest reply actually available in the literature rather than a hand-wave. Have each of them respond to what the other has just said, rather than deliver prepared remarks past one another, and please don't engineer a tidy concession at the end — I would rather the dialogue close by making clear exactly where the disagreement still stands once both sides have made their best moves.
