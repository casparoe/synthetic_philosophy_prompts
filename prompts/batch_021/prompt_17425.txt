I don't have any formal training in computer science or philosophy, but over the past year I've become increasingly absorbed in reading about how large language models work on the inside, mostly through AI lab blog posts and long-form explainers rather than anything academic. What keeps nagging at me is a disagreement I run into between the people who work on "interpretability." Some of them will say, quite matter-of-factly, that they've found "the feature for sycophancy" or "the circuit for deception" inside a model's weights, as if they had located a genuine internal belief sitting there waiting to be read off, the way you might point to a neuron and say "this is the concept of a dog." I remember watching Anthropic's "Golden Gate Claude" demo, where they took a feature their tools had identified as corresponding to the Golden Gate Bridge and turned it up artificially, and the model started bringing the bridge into almost every answer, at one point insisting it was the bridge; this was presented as evidence that the feature was really there in the network, not just a story researchers were telling about it. But I've also come across people who are much more skeptical, arguing that tools like sparse autoencoders may just be carving the network's activity into whatever categories happen to be convenient for humans to talk about, without those categories corresponding to anything the network itself relies on when it actually computes an answer.

What I can't work out on my own is how much this dispute actually matters outside of academic bookkeeping. So I would like you to describe the most important implications of this realism-versus-anti-realism disagreement about neural network representations for a very concrete worry of mine: how much weight I should put on interpretability results when they're offered as reassurance that we'll be able to "look inside" some future, more capable model and catch it lying, scheming, or pursuing a hidden goal before any damage is done. If what these tools find turns out not to be, in any robust sense, the network's "real" representations, but rather useful fictions shaped by our own choice of methods, does that undercut safety arguments that lean on interpretability, or can those arguments survive on some weaker notion of "representation" that doesn't require full-blown realism in the first place? I would rather you walk through the reasoning than simply announce which side of the debate is correct, since my hunch is that the honest answer depends heavily on exactly what job we need the word "representation" to be doing.
