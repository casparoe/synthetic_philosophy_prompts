So I went down a rabbit hole this week that started, kind of embarrassingly, from "Neurath's boat" — the sailors-rebuilding-their-ship-at-sea thing that gets quoted in like every third LW post about epistemology — and ended up somewhere I didn't expect: Otto Neurath's other project, the Isotype system (originally called the "Vienna Method of Pictorial Statistics"), which he ran out of the Gesellschafts- und Wirtschaftsmuseum in Red Vienna in the 1920s, with Gerd Arntz doing the actual icon designs and Marie Reidemeister as his "Transformer" (that was literally her job title — translating raw statistics into the picture system). The bit that hooked me is one of his core design rules: to show a bigger quantity you repeat the same-size icon more times, you never just draw one icon bigger, because scaling a single picture up warps its area and basically lies to the eye. Thats a 1925 version of "don't use exploding pie charts," decades before Tufte. And it wasn't just a design quirk, it was load-bearing for the whole project — Vienna-Circle, unified-science Neurath genuinely thought ordinary prose and statistical tables were class barriers keeping non-literate workers out of facts about their own housing, wages and health, and he wanted Isotype to be a strictly fact-transmitting "helper language" that stayed on the informing side of the line, not the propaganda/advertising side.

Anyway, here's what I can't stop thinking about: basically everything Neurath wanted — a wordless, standardized, cross-language picture system that lets a mass, none-too-literate audience absorb statistical facts fast — has come true, just absolutely not on his terms. We've got COVID dashboards, Our-World-in-Data-style charts, and an entire meme/infographic/emoji economy moving "facts" (and plenty of non-facts) across language barriers way faster than his museum exhibits ever could. Given what you actually know about his commitments, what do you think he'd say if you sat him down in front of a viral bar-chart-race video or a deceptively-scaled infographic? Would he take it as vindication (his dream, just running on phones instead of museum walls), or would he think the whole modern picture-economy collapsed exactly the distinction he cared about most? And separately — do you think his specific 1920s design rules actually give him principled, non-anachronistic grounds to criticize particular modern practices, or is that just me projecting present-day data-viz ethics onto a guy who's been dead since 1945?
