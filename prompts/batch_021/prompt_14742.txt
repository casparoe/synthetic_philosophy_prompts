For a piece I'm reporting on the long afterlife of the ENCODE controversy, I've been working through the argument that broke out after the consortium announced, in 2012, that roughly 80 percent of the human genome could be assigned a "biochemical function." The backlash from evolutionary biologists was fierce: critics accused the consortium of using a definition of "function" so permissive — any measurable biochemical activity at a given stretch of DNA counted, whether or not that activity was ever the target of natural selection — that it was bound to inflate the functional genome far beyond what comparative-genomics estimates of evolutionary constraint would support. Working through both sides of the dispute, it struck me that they weren't really disagreeing about the data so much as each side was quietly presupposing a different philosophical theory of what it even means for something in biology to "have a function."

I'd like to get that underlying philosophy straight, in some detail rather than in slogan form. Could you compare the etiological, or "selected-effects," account of biological function associated with Larry Wright, Karen Neander, and Ruth Millikan with the causal-role account associated with Robert Cummins? I'm especially interested in the mechanics of each theory rather than the one-line summaries: what work the appeal to natural selection is actually doing in the etiological story, how each theory does or doesn't license claims about malfunction and dysfunction, and how each one handles a trait that arose as a developmental or structural by-product of selection for something else — a "spandrel," in Gould and Lewontin's sense — that is later co-opted for a new use (what Gould and Vrba called an exaptation).

With that machinery on the table, I'd be grateful for your own assessment: does either theory offer a more defensible reconstruction of what the two camps in the ENCODE dispute were actually disagreeing about, and of whose notion of "function" was the better-suited tool for the biological question at hand? Please argue for your answer rather than simply asserting a preference, and say so if you think the honest conclusion is that neither theory maps cleanly onto how working biologists actually deploy the term.
