I'm writing a novel in which Robert Nozick — who in my story is still alive today, some twenty years after his actual death in 2002 — spends a country-house weekend arguing with his marine-biologist granddaughter about the moral status of octopuses and lobsters. For the scene to earn its place, his side of the argument has to be built out of his actual, specific views on animals in Anarchy, State, and Utopia: the "utilitarianism for animals, Kantianism for people" formula, his suggestion that side constraints ultimately track a capacity for self-conscious, plan-forming rational agency, and his own acknowledgment that this line sits uneasily with our confidence that severely cognitively impaired humans still merit full side constraints. Reconstruct how he would most plausibly respond to the UK's 2022 Animal Welfare (Sentience) Act, which extended legal recognition of sentience to cephalopod molluscs and decapod crustaceans on the strength of evidence about their capacity to feel pain — and to his granddaughter's charge that grounding protection in the capacity to suffer, rather than in rational agency, quietly dissolves his whole way of drawing the line. Give me his strongest, most in-character reply, not a survey of his premises followed by a bland verdict.
