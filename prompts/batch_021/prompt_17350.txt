I write novels that tend to circle around crime, blame, and their long domestic aftermath, and for the past year I have also hosted a modest podcast where I interview specialists whose work feeds directly into whatever book I am drafting. The novel underway follows a woman across the decade after her younger brother is killed by a driver who fell asleep at the wheel during an undiagnosed narcoleptic episode -- a case that sits awkwardly between an ordinary excuse ("he did not choose to fall asleep") and something closer to an exemption ("is he even the kind of agent resentment is fittingly aimed at?"). To make sure the philosophical scaffolding under the novel is sound before I finish it, my next episode is with a philosopher who has built her career defending and extending P.F. Strawson's naturalistic account of moral responsibility in "Freedom and Resentment" -- the claim that our practices of resentment, gratitude, guilt, and indignation do not answer to any further fact about whether determinism is true, because they belong to the fabric of interpersonal life rather than to a theory about it. When previous interviewers have pushed on this, she tends to retreat to some version of the line that asking whether the whole reactive-attitude framework is "justified" is a bit like asking whether promising is justified: the question can only be raised from inside a practice of that general kind, so the demand for an external justification is confused rather than simply unmet.

Could you draft nine interview questions, arranged so that retreat to that line gets progressively harder to sustain, with a brief follow-up after each keyed to the answer she is most likely to give? I would like at least one question that presses the excuse/exemption boundary using a case like my narcoleptic driver; one that uses Gary Watson's attributability/accountability distinction to ask whether Strawson's naturalism has quietly smuggled in a justification ("these attitudes are fitting") under cover of a mere description ("these attitudes are natural and near-inescapable"); one on the objective attitude, asking whether full knowledge of anyone's causal history would rationally push us toward treating everyone objectively rather than just the cases we currently exempt; and one that puts to her R. Jay Wallace's fairness-based alternative -- responsibility as answerable to whether an agent had the general power to grasp and act on the relevant reasons -- and asks whether that is not a rival account doing the very justificatory work her naturalism insists is unnecessary. Somewhere in the second half, include a question drawing on the broadly Pereboom-style hard-incompatibilist argument that people can moderate supposedly inescapable resentment through sustained practice, which would seem to undercut the inescapability premise her position leans on so heavily. Close the sequence with a question asking her to specify something that would count as evidence against her naturalism, as distinct from one more occasion to restate it.
