I'm a PhD student in behavioral economics, and in my own research on charitable giving I've grown used to treating "impure" motives as an unremarkable modeling assumption: donors are typically moved partly by concern for the beneficiary and partly by the "warm glow" that giving produces for themselves, and nobody in my field worries much about whether this makes the giving somehow less real. A situation in my own family has left me less sure this is just a technical nicety, though. My aunt has been the primary caregiver for her elderly mother-in-law for four years now, managing medications, handling disorienting middle-of-the-night episodes, and putting her own career on hold to do it. When I asked her about it recently, she told me, unprompted, that a large part of what keeps her going is that she couldn't tolerate the guilt, and the rest of the family's judgment, if she stopped, and that she is also quite aware that the arrangement will likely put her in a favorable position once her mother-in-law's estate is eventually settled. In the same breath, though, she said she genuinely loves the woman and doesn't trust any other relative to give her the attention she needs.

I mentioned all this at dinner with some housemates recently, and it turned into a genuine argument. One of them insisted that, since the caregiving is sustained partly by guilt-avoidance and self-interest, it doesn't really speak well of my aunt as a person, however much good it does her mother-in-law. Another said this whole line of thought is a mistake: what ultimately matters is that a vulnerable person is being well cared for, and rummaging around in someone's psychology for "purer" motives is beside the point. Both positions strike me as too quick, but I couldn't articulate why, or what a more careful alternative would look like. Is there a more precise way of thinking about cases like my aunt's, where self-interested and other-directed motives are thoroughly intertwined and possibly not even fully transparent to the agent herself? I'd like to have something more exact to say than "it's complicated" the next time this comes up.
