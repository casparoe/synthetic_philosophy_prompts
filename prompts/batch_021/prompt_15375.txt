I spent about thirty-five years as an experimental physicist (mostly detector instrumentation work at a couple of the big accelerator labs), and now that I'm retired I've been filling the time by finally reading all the philosophy I ignored during my career. Last month I sat myself down and worked through Godel's ontological proof line by line — the "positive property" axioms, "God-like" defined as having all of them essentially, the essence and necessary-existence definitions, the whole S5 modal apparatus (had to relearn my modal logic from scratch, it'd been decades since grad school). I was pretty pleased with myself for following it all the way through to "necessarily, God exists."

Then a friend from grad school who ended up teaching logic told me over dinner that the proof, exactly as Godel wrote it, has this notorious side effect called "modal collapse" — apparently the very same axioms let you derive that literally every true proposition is a *necessary* truth, i.e. there's no contingency left anywhere in reality, which is a much bigger and stranger conclusion than "God exists" and honestly seems like it should definately worry a defender more than it seems to. She said there are various "emended" versions around that tweak the definition of essence or get pickier about which properties count as positive specifically to block the collapse, but that critics of those patches argue they either quietly let the collapse back in through a side door, or only avoid it by baking the desired conclusion into the choice of axioms in the first place.

I'd love to actually watch this fight happen instead of getting it secondhand over dessert. Could you write me a dialogue — say, two philosophers of religion arguing it out over coffee after a logic seminar — where one is defending some emended version of Godel's argument as still doing genuine metaphysical work, and the other thinks the modal collapse problem sinks the whole project no matter which patch you pick? I want them equally sharp, really engaging with each other's specific moves rather than trading prepared talking points, and I'd like the collapse objection itself to become clear through the back-and-forth — what exactly it shows and why it's supposed to be so damaging — since my friend's dinner-table version honestly left me only about 70% following it.
