In my spare time I have been teaching myself decision theory, starting with Newcomb's Problem and the disagreement it produces between evidential and causal approaches to choice. Recently I came across a related thought experiment called "Death in Damascus," in which Death is a perfectly reliable predictor of my movements, and I must decide whether to stay in Damascus or flee to Aleppo, surviving only if I end up somewhere other than wherever Death has already gone. Most discussions I have found treat this as essentially Newcomb's Problem with new labels attached, but that does not seem right to me: with Newcomb's Problem, the standard recommendation to take both boxes stays put once you commit to it, whereas here whichever city I tentatively settle on immediately seems to become the wrong choice to have settled on, so the reasoning just keeps flipping between staying and fleeing without ever landing anywhere. My understanding is that this instability is supposed to be a genuinely distinct, and in some sense deeper, problem than the ordinary evidential/causal split, and that one proposed fix is to let the agent choose randomly rather than commit outright to either city. Could you explain, in intuitive terms, what actually makes this flip-flopping different in kind from the familiar Newcomb disagreement, and why the random-choice fix strikes some decision theorists as more of a technical patch than a genuine resolution?
