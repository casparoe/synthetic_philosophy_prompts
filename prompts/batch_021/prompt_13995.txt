Question: Max Black's famous 1952 thought experiment asks us to imagine a universe containing nothing but two qualitatively identical iron spheres, a fixed distance apart, and nothing else in it. Explain how Black uses this scenario against Leibniz's Principle of the Identity of Indiscernibles (PII), and explain why Black thinks the "relational properties" defense fails to save the principle.

Student's answer: "Leibniz's Principle of the Identity of Indiscernibles (PII) says that no two distinct substances can share literally all their properties - this is the same principle usually called Leibniz's Law, that if x and y are identical then whatever is true of one is true of the other. Black's universe has just two spheres, same size, same mass, same composition, same temperature, symmetric relative to each other, with nothing else around to break the symmetry. Since there is nothing else, every purely qualitative, non-relational property true of one sphere is true of the other too - so if PII were correct, the two spheres would actually have to be one and the same object. But Black insists there really are two spheres, not one, so PII must be false.

The obvious way to save PII is to point to relational properties: sphere A has the property of being some distance from B, and B doesn't have quite that property, since it isn't that distance from itself. Black's response is that this relation is perfectly symmetric - each sphere is that same distance from a sphere - so appealing to it doesn't actually discriminate between them the way the defender needs. Once that move fails, the only thing left to distinguish the spheres is brute numerical difference that doesn't supervene on any quality at all. This is also why people bring Black's spheres into the older debate over absolute space: if two things can be numerically distinct while completely indiscernible, space itself can't be merely relational in the way Leibniz argued against Clarke and Newton, so something like Newton's absolute space is needed after all to ground the difference."
