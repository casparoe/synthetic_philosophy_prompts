For an AI safety and rationality club at my high school, a few of us have been working through Wei Dai's original proposal of updateless decision theory (UDT) so that we can lead a discussion on Newcomb's Problem and the "counterfactual mugging" thought experiment next month. Here is what I think I've understood: the whole point of UDT is that an agent settles, once and for all and before making any observations, on whichever single policy maximizes expected utility with respect to its prior over how the world and its own future observations could turn out, and then it simply executes whatever that policy prescribes; it never recomputes a "better" response after actually seeing what happened, even in a case like counterfactual mugging, where, once you've learned the coin landed tails, handing over the $100 looks like pure loss with nothing left to gain. If that's right, then "updatelessness" is really just a formal label for telling an agent to disregard whatever it subsequently learns, which seems to directly contradict one of the first principles of rational belief I picked up from the LessWrong sequences: that you can never predict, in advance, which direction new evidence is going to move your beliefs (what Yudkowsky calls the conservation of expected evidence). A theory that instructs an agent, ahead of time, to act as though certain future observations won't matter looks like it's built to violate that principle on purpose.

Given this, I don't understand why anyone doing serious work on decision theory or AI alignment treats UDT as a live contender for how a rational agent ought to reason, rather than as a curious but ultimately confused idea that looks appealing only because it happens to win on a couple of cherry-picked thought experiments. Two people in my club have pushed back and insisted that "updateless" doesn't mean what I think it means, but neither of them could pin down exactly where my reasoning goes wrong. Could you help settle this for us?
