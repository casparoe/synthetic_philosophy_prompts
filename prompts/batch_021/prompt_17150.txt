My mother died two weeks ago, and I am giving her eulogy this weekend. She spent her working life as a school librarian and was married for more than forty years to a philosophy professor; although she never took a philosophy class herself, decades of faculty dinners left her able to accuse us, in the middle of an argument, of lacking any real "alternative possibilities" whenever we insisted we could not have done our chores differently. A handful of my father's former colleagues will be among the mourners, alongside people who have never heard of Harry Frankfurt and never need to. The one thing both groups will agree on is that my mother was constitutionally incapable of lying — not honest as a hard-won virtue, but honest the way a compass points north. Last night at dinner, my sister, who is finishing a doctorate in philosophy, gently pointed out that this is more or less the structure of a Frankfurt case, even though the nearest thing to a counterfactual intervener here is forty years of settled character rather than a neurosurgeon: a situation in which an agent could not have done otherwise, which the classical Principle of Alternative Possibilities says should undercut moral credit, yet in which withholding that credit feels exactly backwards. She said, half joking, that a eulogy praising our mother's honesty without addressing this would not really be entitled to the praise it was handing out.

I do not want to turn a funeral into a seminar on moral responsibility in front of eighty grieving relatives, and I am not going to stand up and claim our mother could have lied and simply chose not to, since everyone present would know that this was not true. But I would like the eulogy to survive contact with the three or four people in the room who will actually be turning this over in their heads during the reception afterward. Could you write it? It should run about five minutes, take her incapacity for dishonesty as its central, celebratory theme, and find a way to make that theme land as the highest possible praise for both audiences at once — ideally leaving my sister nodding rather than composing a rebuttal.
