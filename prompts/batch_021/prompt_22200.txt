Explain the difference between Wright's etiological account of biological function and Cummins' causal-role account. Then discuss whether the Swampman thought experiment (a molecule-for-molecule duplicate of a person that springs into existence by freak chance, with no evolutionary history) creates a problem for either account, and if so, which one and why.

That's the question I gave for my phil of bio seminar, and here's what one of my students wrote:

"On the etiological view, something's function is whatever effect explains why that trait is still around today — e.g. the heart's function is pumping blood because ancestral hearts that pumped blood were favored by selection, so the trait got passed down. This ties function tightly to history: you can't say what something's function is without knowing the selection history that produced it. Cummins rejects this and says a part's function is just its current contribution to keeping the organism alive and able to reproduce, whatever job its doing for the system right now, regardless of how it got there. This is usually why Cummins's account is accused of being too liberal, since almost any effect a part has on the organism's present survival could count as a function on this view.

Swampman is actually a bigger problem for Cummins than for the etiological view. Swampman has no evolutionary history at all, so a causal-role theorist has to say his heart has no function, even though its pumping blood just like any normal heart would — which seems like exactly the wrong result. The etiological theorist doesn't run into this problem, since they can just look at what the heart is currently doing to keep Swampman alive."
