I'm moderating a colloquium Q&A with a philosopher who has just published a book defending expressivism in metaethics, claiming to have solved the Frege–Geach problem with a 'hyperplan' semantics: normative sentences express acceptance of a maximally specific plan for living, with negation, disjunction, and the conditional defined over combinations of such plans rather than truth values. Whenever she's pressed on an embedding puzzle, her stock reply is that this machinery secures the inference's validity, and that validity is all the problem ever demanded. Write six to eight questions that make that reply progressively harder to sustain: start with an ordinary moral modus ponens, where her semantics plausibly works; move to negated and disjoined moral claims, where 'approving of' and 'disapproving of' are not obviously exhaustive or exclusive attitudes; then to mixed inferences embedding a moral predicate under an epistemic operator such as 'probably' or 'Ann believes that'; and finally to the worry — sometimes called 'creeping minimalism' — that her apparatus for handling these cases becomes formally indistinguishable from an ordinary truth-conditional semantics. End with a question asking what would actually count as evidence against her solution.
