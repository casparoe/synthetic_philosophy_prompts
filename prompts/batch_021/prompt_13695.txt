Ok so I've fallen into a bit of a Sidgwick hole the last couple weeks — it started because someone in my rationalist reading group mentioned that Parfit apparently called the Methods of Ethics something like "the best book on ethics ever written," which sent me down a wiki-spiral, and I ended up writing a short post connecting two sides of Sidgwick that I don't think usually get put together: the "dualism of practical reason" stuff at the end of Methods, and the fact that the same guy spent basically the last two decades of his life as the founding president of the Society for Psychical Research, doing statistics on ghost sightings with his wife. I want to post it (probably just as a forum shortform, not a real essay) but would love a gut check first. Draft's below.

Sidgwick spends the ~500 pages of Methods of Ethics building the most careful case for utilitarianism in the literature, and then in the final chapter basically admits defeat on one point: he can't get rational egoism (maximize your own good) to yield to rational benevolence (maximize the good, impartially) using practical reason alone. Both look like self-evident axioms to him, they can conflict in real cases, and nothing "above" the two principles settles the dispute. He more or less calls this the profoundest problem of ethics, and says the only real fix would be some moral order to the universe — basically God plus a future life — that guarantees virtue and self-interest coincide eventually, even when they obviously don't in this life.

Everyone treats that as a purely academic embarrassment for the book, and treats Sidgwick's other big project — co-founding the SPR in 1882 (he was its first president), feeding apparition cases into stuff like Phantasms of the Living with Myers and Gurney, later running the big Census of Hallucinations, chasing mediums for two decades with his wife Eleanor — as unrelated Victorian eccentricity, the kind of thing Wallace and Crookes also did that everyone politely forgets about. But once you know about the dualism it stops looking unrelated. Sidgwick had already resigned his Trinity fellowship in 1869 because he couldn't honestly keep signing the 39 Articles, so the normal route to "there is a moral governor and a future life" was closed to him on the religious side. Psychical research reads like his attempt to get the same postulate in the back door, empirically: actual evidence of telepathy or survival would hand him exactly the kind of fact his ethics needed.

What makes this more than a cute psychoanalytic story, I think, is how rigorous the Sidgwick-era SPR actually tried to be — he was famously the one in the room pushing for controls, which is presumably how you end up with the SPR letting Hodgson torch their own most famous case (Blavatsky, in '85). Honestly it reads a lot like a 19th-century rationalist community trying to Bayesian-update its way through claims everyone else was tribally for or against. And the ending is almost too neat: by his own account Sidgwick never got evidence solid enough to settle anything, and died in 1900 still stuck in the dualism. Myers died the next year, and that's when the cross-correspondences start — the multi-medium automatic-writing puzzles some SPR people read as Myers, and eventually Sidgwick himself, trying to finish the job from the other side.
