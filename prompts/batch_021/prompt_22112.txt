I host a small podcast on philosophy of science that started as a way to procrastinate during my physics PhD, but it has become something I take considerably more seriously now that I have committed to writing my own dissertation on the foundations of statistical mechanics. Next month I am recording an episode with a philosopher of physics who is known for defending the view that the "Past Hypothesis" -- the assumption that the universe began in an extraordinarily low-entropy macrostate -- should be treated as a fundamental law of nature in its own right, rather than as a brute initial condition that itself stands in need of explanation. I have read most of her published work and understand the broad shape of the position, but I am wary of asking only the questions she has clearly answered many times before, since that would just produce a rehearsed restatement rather than anything that tests the view under real pressure.

Could you help me draft eight to ten interview questions that go beyond having her restate the position? I would like at least one question on whether elevating the Past Hypothesis to the status of a law actually explains the entropy gradient or merely relabels our ignorance of why the early universe was so special; one on how her view is meant to bear on the Boltzmann brain problem, since it is unclear whether treating low entropy as lawlike helps or worsens the worry that, in the relevant cosmological models, genuine observers with reliable memories should be vastly outnumbered by disembodied "brains" that arise from spontaneous thermal fluctuations rather than from any actual low-entropy past; one on the difficulty of even assigning a probability, or a "typicality" measure, to the universe's initial macrostate when there is no surrounding ensemble to sample from; and one on whether the psychological and causal arrows of time are supposed to reduce to the thermodynamic one on her view, or whether that is a further, independent commitment. For each of these, please add a brief follow-up question keyed to the answer she is most likely to give, and close with a question about what kind of empirical or theoretical discovery would actually count as evidence against her position.
