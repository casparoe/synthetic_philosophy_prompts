I don't have any formal background in philosophy — I'm a self-taught reader who picked up Pascal's Pensées a while back, mostly out of curiosity about the wager, and ended up getting pulled into the rest of the book. For the last couple of weeks I've been stuck on one of the shorter fragments, the one usually given the header "Justice, force" in modern editions. As best I can reconstruct it from the copy I own, it reads: "It is just that what is just should be followed; it is necessary that what is strongest should be followed. Justice without force is powerless; force without justice is tyrannical. Justice without force is contradicted, because there are always wicked men; force without justice is condemned. We must therefore combine justice and force, and for that end make what is just strong, or what is strong just. Justice is subject to dispute; force is easily recognized and beyond dispute. So we could not give force to justice, because force contradicted justice, claiming that justice was unjust and that force itself was just. And so, unable to make what is just strong, we have made what is strong just." I mentioned this passage to a friend who studied a bit of philosophy at university, and the only thing he offered was that Pascal was "basically agreeing with Hobbes," which felt like it skated past whatever is actually strange about the argument rather than explaining it.

What I would like help with is working out what kind of claim Pascal is actually making, rather than being handed a label. Is he saying that "justice," as it actually functions among people, is really nothing but the name the strongest party gives to its own will, so that the passage amounts to a flat moral relativism or even nihilism about justice — or is he holding onto some independent, dispute-free standard of justice that force merely fails, as a matter of fact, to track reliably? I've also read that elsewhere in the Pensées Pascal describes something he calls the raison des effets, the "reason of effects," where a popular belief that looks naive once a clever person debunks it turns out, on a second and deeper look, to deserve protecting anyway, for reasons hidden from the very people who hold it. Is the "justice, force" fragment meant to work the same way — is Pascal hinting that the fiction of a law being "just," as opposed to merely backed by force, is precisely the sort of useful illusion that a truly wise person should quietly preserve in others rather than expose? And does this connect at all to his separate distinction between the order of bodies, the order of minds, and the order of charity — is force simply confined to the lowest of those orders, with any justice worth the name only available in an order that political force can never reach?
