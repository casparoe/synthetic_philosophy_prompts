Hey, need a devil's advocate for something I'm chewing on for my decision theory seminar.

The standard money-pump story against cyclic preferences assumes a naive agent who doesn't see the pump coming and just keeps trading. McClennen's old response is that a sophisticated chooser -- one who can foresee their own future choices -- just refuses to step onto the path in the first place, so no pump ever happens. A resolute chooser goes further still, committing to a whole plan up front and sticking to it even at the very last step, where, taken on its own, they'd actually rather deviate. Everyone's first reaction to that last bit is that its fishy: isn't choosing against your own preferences in the moment just irrational, full stop, whatever you resolved to do earlier?

Argue that the resolute chooser is not being irrational at that last step, and that the objection only seems compelling because it quietly assumes rationality has to be evaluated synchronically, choice by choice, rather than as something that can be governed by a rational plan extended over time. Give me the strongest version of that case, not just a rehash of the standard positions -- I already know those.
