I've been down a modal metaphysics rabbit hole lately for a project (long story, literally) and I keep getting stuck on this old disagreement between David Lewis and Saul Kripke about what's called transworld identity, basically whether the "you" in another possible world is literally you or just a look-alike. Lewis's answer is counterpart theory: he thought possible worlds are concrete, solid places, just not connected to ours in space or time, and because of that nothing can literally exist in two of them at once, so when I say "I could've become a violinist," what's true on his view is that some sufficiently similar "counterpart" of me plays violin over there, not that the literal me does. Kripke thought that was basically a dodge, something like: if its not really me, just some guy who resembles me, why would I care what happens to him? He used Hubert Humphrey and the 1968 election as his go-to example, Humphrey himself couldn't care less whether some similar fella wins an election in a seperate world, since it isn't him doing the winning. I brought this up at my writing group and got a lot of blank stares, so now I'm bringing it to you instead.

Here's the premise I want to hang a short story on: a shady little company builds a machine, think an old switchboard covered in dials, that opens a brief window onto "the nearest possible world" and lets someone talk to the version of themselves over there, once, for a few minutes, ever. My protagonist books a session the night before she has to make an irreversible call, turning down a risky surgery, say, or taking a job overseas, whatever ends up working best dramatically, hoping the call will tell her how things turn out "for her" on the other end.

Could you actually write that story, somewhere around 1200-1800 words? I don't want a cute gadget-of-the-week sci-fi piece, I want the gut-punch at the end to turn on the real philosophical fork: does hearing from this other woman tell my protagonist anything true about her own fate, the way transworld identity would want, or is Kripke basically right that she's eavesdropping on a stranger and the comfort she feels is some kind of category mistake, which is what counterpart theory implies? Maybe give the machine's operator, a burnt out technician who's clearly read too much philosophy on the job, a moment where he needles her about which side of the debate she's unknowingly leaning on. I'd rather the ending commit to that tension honestly than dodge it with something vague and poetic.
