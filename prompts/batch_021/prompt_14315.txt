I've spent the past couple of weeks reading deeply into the literature on hard choices and value incomparability, partly spurred by a disagreement with a friend over whether "shut up and multiply"-style expected-value reasoning ever really breaks down, or whether any two options are always comparable in principle once you specify a fine-grained enough utility function. Ruth Chang's work keeps coming up as the canonical rebuttal: she argues that in cases like the stock career-choice examples common in this literature (a demanding, lucrative path versus a much less lucrative but more intrinsically fulfilling one, say), the two options are neither better, worse, nor equally good, and yet still fully comparable, because they stand in a fourth basic relation she calls being "on a par." This is meant to be an alternative both to the traditional "Trichotomy Thesis" (that comparability requires one of better/worse/equal) and to Joseph Raz's older claim that such pairs are simply incommensurable, related by no evaluative relation at all.

What I haven't managed to reconstruct cleanly for myself is the actual logical structure of Chang's "small-improvement argument" for why parity has to be a distinct, sui generis relation, rather than just a loose way of describing rough or imprecise equality. As I understand it, the argument turns on the idea that a small enough improvement to one of the options is guaranteed to break a relation of exact equality, but need not break parity — yet I can't quite see why that asymmetry is supposed to rule out the two options simply being vaguely equal rather than on a par in Chang's stronger sense. Could you walk me through the argument step by step, and then help me weigh how much force it really has against John Broome's rival proposal that what Chang calls "parity" is really just vagueness in the predicates "better than" and "worse than," rather than evidence of some fourth determinate relation? I'd especially appreciate it if you could say what, if anything, would count as evidence capable of adjudicating between the two views, since from where I'm sitting they seem to predict much the same pattern of first-order intuitions about particular cases.
