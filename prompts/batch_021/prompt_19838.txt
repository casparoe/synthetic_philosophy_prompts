So I'm about two-thirds through a first draft of a near-future novel where the emotional climax hinges on two rival AI systems -- built by companies that don't trust each other an inch -- reaching a genuine, unforced peace deal by literally showing each other their source code before deciding whether to cooperate. I fell down a rabbit hole a while back on something called "program equilibrium" (Tennenholtz, I think, coined the term) and then a follow-up idea where instead of the two programs just comparing code line by line, each one searches for an actual proof that the other cooperates before committing to cooperate itself -- and somehow that doesn't spiral into infinite regress, because of Löb's theorem. I've only got one intro logic course from a decade ago to my name though, so I wrote the mechanism up in my own words for a scene where an old engineer explains it to a very skeptical negotiator, and I'd love a sanity check before I build three more chapters on top of it.

Here's the actual paragraph from my notes, rough, not final prose:

"Picture two AIs, A and B, about to play a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma, and each can read the other's complete source code first. Just hard-coding 'cooperate if your code is identical to mine' is too brittle -- rename one variable and the whole deal's off. So instead each is built to reason: I'll cooperate if I can prove, using ordinary arithmetic, that the other one cooperates with me. That looks circular on its face, since what A does depends on what B does and vice versa, forever. But apparently if you build the proof search the right way (I've seen the cooperating program called something like "FairBot" in one write-up), each one actually does find a short, finite proof that the other cooperates -- and its not a loophole, it's forced by the logic itself, the same way Löb's theorem forces a system to be able to prove that its own consistency is unprovable, or something like that. The punchline the philosophers and decision-theory people draw from this is that you get real cooperation between two parties with zero enforcement and zero repeated interaction, purely from mutual transparency, which is supposed to be a genuinely big deal for what we even mean by trust or commitment."

Could you tell me where I've mangled the actual logic -- I've got a nagging feeling my Löb's-theorem gesture is doing work it isn't entitled to, but I can't pin down exactly what's wrong with it. And separately, the more literary question: is that closing "punchline" (real trust, not just a clever trick) something serious people in this area actually endorse, or would I be overselling it if I have a character say that line out loud? I'd rather find out now than have a reader who does logic for a living email me after the book's out.
