I'm a PhD student in philosophy working on formal epistemology, and this term I've been sitting in on a seminar on epistemic logic that has spent several weeks on the problem of logical omniscience: the awkward consequence of the standard possible-worlds treatment of knowledge and belief, going back to Hintikka, that any agent who knows anything at all thereby knows every valid formula of the underlying logic and every logical consequence of what she knows, however difficult the derivation would be for any actual reasoner to carry out. Over the course of the term we've worked through most of the well-known strategies for blocking that consequence without simply giving up on having a formal logic of knowledge at all: Fagin and Halpern's awareness logic, which relativizes explicit knowledge to a prior set of formulas the agent is even capable of entertaining; impossible-worlds semantics, which allows non-normal worlds where valid formulas can fail and contradictions can be true, so that a tautology need not hold at every world compatible with what the agent knows; Levesque's distinction between explicit and implicit belief, modeled through partial, possibly locally incoherent situations; so-called step or active logics, which identify an agent's beliefs at a given time with whatever has actually been derived through a bounded number of inference steps rather than everything derivable in the limit; a framework usually credited to Halpern and collaborators called algorithmic knowledge, which ties what an agent knows to the output of a resource-bounded algorithm running on her local state rather than to truth at every world she cannot rule out; and Artemov's justification logic, which replaces the bare knowledge operator with explicit justification terms, so that having a reason to believe p needn't amount to having a reason to believe everything p entails.
