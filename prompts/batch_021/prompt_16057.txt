After forty years as a research chemist, I have been using retirement to work through philosophers I previously knew only secondhand, and Rousseau's Lettre à d'Alembert sur les spectacles struck me as far more pointed and specific than the vague "Rousseau distrusted civilization" impression I had going in. I would like to see its central quarrel dramatized properly. Please write a dialogue between Rousseau and Diderot debating whether the theatre corrupts public morals or cultivates them. Rousseau should press his concrete complaints: that spectators discharge their whole capacity for virtue in tears over a fictional sufferer and walk out no more virtuous than they walked in; that the actor's craft of seeming what he is not corrodes his own character; that Molière's Misanthrope proves the point, since the play makes the blunt, honest Alceste ridiculous while vindicating the socially pliable Philinte; and that Geneva's public festivals, where citizens are simultaneously actors and spectators to one another, show what a genuinely transparent civic culture could look like instead. Diderot should answer with his own considered theory of bourgeois drama and sympathetic identification, including a frank reckoning with the fact that his account of great acting concedes the actor is a calculating dissembler rather than someone who feels what he shows. Neither figure should simply concede or win by default: have them genuinely engage each other's strongest points, with at least one moment where a character revises a specific claim under pressure without abandoning the overall position.
