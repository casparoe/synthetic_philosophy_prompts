So I'm taking this ancient political philosophy seminar, and we just spent two classes on the bit of Aristotle's Politics about money and exchange, and its the first thing from that class that's actually mapped onto my real life. My fuzzy version of it: he thinks getting stuff for your household is natural up to a point, and even exchange is natural when it's solving a real problem (you've got extra wine, not enough grain), but at some point people start chasing money itself instead of the stuff money is supposed to get you, and that's the "unnatural" version. The worst case of that, the one he seems to have the most contempt for, is charging interest -- apparently the Greek word for interest is basically the same word as "offspring," like the money is having babies, and money isn't supposed to be able to do that, it's just supposed to get exchanged.

This all came back to me because my friend Jake has gotten really into crypto staking this year and won't stop telling people "his money works for him while he sleeps," and at game night last week when he said that again, two of our other friends, Mara and Ben, got into this whole thing about it that I haven't stopped thinking about. Mara said staking isn't "work" in any sense -- nothing gets made, sold, or done for anybody, it's just money making more money out of nothing, unlike even a sketchy business that's at least selling something people want. Ben said that description fits any interest-bearing savings account, bond, or index fund in anyone's retirement account, so if passive return is illegitimate then basically the whole financial system is illegitimate, which is too strong to accept -- and anyway staking isn't nothing, since the protocol needs people to lock up capital to stay secure, so stakers are providing a real service, you just don't see it, same as you don't see what your bank does with your deposit. Mara came back that a bank's opacity is atleast hiding a real chain where your money ends up funding an actual house or business, whereas "keeps a protocol secure" is just a fact about one system's design, not a defense of what Jake actually said, which was about money growing because he owns some, not about any service. Ben's reply was that if that's really the objection, it applies just as much to a boring index fund, and nobody thinks their 401k is shady, so either bite that bullet everywhere or admit there's no real principle here. Mara's last word was that maybe this was never about the mechanics of any specific transaction, but about treating money as something owed to keep growing just because you own it, rather than its growth being incidental to getting or making something you actually need -- and that attitude doesn't get let off the hook just because you can dig up some technical justification underneath it.

Nobody won, everyone just went back to the board game, but I can't tell if Mara's closing point is actually the more Aristotelian one or if I'm just pattern-matching off the money-having-babies thing, and Ben's 401k point is bugging me just as much because I don't see how you get around it. I'd also like to know how well each side's position actually tracks what Aristotle thought versus where they've drifted into a more modern argument he wouldn't recognize, and whether there's some obvious consideration neither of them raised that would settle this.
