Quick recap in case it's useful: Williamson calls a condition "luminous" if, whenever it obtains, you're automatically in a position to know that it obtains, and then argues that basically no interesting mental condition is luminous — he leans on margin-for-error/safety reasoning plus a sorites-style induction over tiny changes (the classic case is "feeling cold" as the temperature ticks down degree by degree) to squeeze a contradiction out of assuming some condition like that is luminous. He then reuses the same machinery against the KK principle — i.e., you can know that p without being in a position to know that you know p, because your own knowing isn't luminous either.

What's been bugging me in our seminar is whether the safety/margin-for-error premise he needs is doing way more work than people give it credit for, like he's basically assuming something close to what he wants to prove. And does the argument actually generalize the way he wants, to all non-trivial mental states, or does it only really bite for comparative/borderline conditions like "feeling cold" and loose its grip once you get to something like being in pain?
