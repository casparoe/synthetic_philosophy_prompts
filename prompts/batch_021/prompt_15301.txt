"In Guide of the Perplexed III:32, Maimonides explains why God commanded animal sacrifice even though, on his account, God doesn't actually want or need it. Summarize that explanation, then explain Nahmanides' central objection to it, and briefly say how damaging you think the objection is."

Here's the student's answer, verbatim:

"Maimonides argues that sacrifice was a concession to the Israelites' upbringing: they'd grown up in a world (Egypt and the surrounding Chaldean/Sabian cultures) where sacrificing animals to idols was just how you worshipped, and human nature can't drop something that ingrained overnight -- you have to wean people off it gradually rather than yank it away all at once. So instead of banning sacrifice outright, which nobody would've obeyed anyway, God redirected the very same practice toward Himself and hedged it with restrictions (one central Temple, no images, etc.) so it couldn't shade back into idolatry. This fits Maimonides' larger project in Book III of insisting that even the ceremonial commandments have real historical reasons rather than being arbitrary divine whim. And because the whole point of sacrifice was only ever to stamp out idolatry, Maimonides thinks that once that danger has passed, sacrifice has basically done its job and prayer -- "service of the heart" -- becomes the real, lasting form of worship; that's why the Mishneh Torah makes prayer a standing daily obligation while leaving the sacrificial laws as more or less frozen historical law. Nahmanides objects that this can't be right, since it makes the sacrifices look like a shameful compromise rather than something God actually delights in. I think Nahmanides has a point, but Maimonides' account is still the more psychologically realistic one."
