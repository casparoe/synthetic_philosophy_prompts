In a graduate seminar on philosophy of language, we spent a session contrasting epistemicist and supervaluationist responses to the sorites paradox, and I want to make sure I've actually understood the contrast before it comes up again in discussion. As I understood the lecture, epistemicism (the view associated with Timothy Williamson) holds that a predicate like "heap" or "tall" really does have a sharp cutoff — some exact number of grains, some exact height in millimeters — that we are simply constitutionally incapable of ever knowing, so classical logic and bivalence survive completely intact. Supervaluationism (associated with Kit Fine and Rosanna Keefe, among others) struck me as the more revisionary of the two views: since it denies that every sentence built from a vague predicate is determinately true or false, treating borderline cases somewhat the way a three-valued logic treats a sentence assigned the "indeterminate" value, I assumed it has to give up the law of excluded middle in those cases too, in exactly the way Kleene's three-valued semantics does whenever both disjuncts of "P or not-P" come out indeterminate.

What's now puzzling me is that several of the assigned readings instead describe supervaluationism as staying unusually close to classical logic, in some cases closer than you'd expect from any theory admitting genuine truth-value gaps, while epistemicism's "fully classical" status gets mentioned almost as an afterthought rather than as its whole distinguishing feature. So where exactly does my reasoning break down: doesn't admitting that borderline instances of "heap" are neither true nor false force a supervaluationist to reject excluded middle, the way it would in an ordinary gappy logic like Kleene's? And if it turns out both views end up preserving classical logic to more or less the same degree, what is the substantive disagreement between epistemicism and supervaluationism actually supposed to consist in?
