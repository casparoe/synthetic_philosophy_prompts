In a graduate seminar on Kant's practical philosophy, we recently spent a session on his scattered but striking remarks on animals in the Lectures on Ethics and the Metaphysics of Morals. Because animals lack self-consciousness and the capacity for rational self-legislation, Kant denies that they can be "ends in themselves," and so denies that we have any direct duties toward them. What we do have, on his view, are indirect duties: duties that are at bottom owed to humanity, but that concern our treatment of animals, because cruelty toward them tends to dull or coarsen the very disposition of sympathy that sustains proper conduct toward other human beings. The stock illustration is the elderly dog: a master who turns out or destroys a faithful animal once it can no longer work commits no wrong against the dog itself, on Kant's account, but does wrong nonetheless, in that he damages his own moral character -- the very character on which his duties to other people ultimately depend.

One of my students raised an objection I haven't been able to shake off since. Every one of Kant's own examples, she pointed out, involves a single identifiable person directly and visibly mistreating an animal that is, in some sense, personally known to him. But the great bulk of animal suffering caused by human beings today looks nothing like that: animals raised and slaughtered by the billions annually in industrial agriculture, say, or wild populations driven toward collapse by cumulative, anonymous human activity, are harmed through processes that are systemic, bureaucratized, and largely invisible to those who benefit from them. No single farmhand, executive, or consumer personally performs, witnesses, or is coarsened by any particular cruel act toward any particular animal.

I would like your considered view on how Kant's framework should be expected to cope with this disanalogy. Would he, confronted with modern industrial animal agriculture and anthropogenic mass extinction, have to concede that his account simply has nothing critical to say about them, since no individual's moral character is being visibly corrupted by any single identifiable act? Or is there a plausible way of extending his own premises -- concerning complicity, habituation, the shaping of character by institutions rather than by individual acts, or some other resource internal to his ethics -- that would let him condemn such practices on recognizably Kantian grounds after all? Please commit to a specific answer rather than simply cataloguing considerations on both sides.
