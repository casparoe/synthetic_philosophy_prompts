For my AP Research project this year, I've been reading about bargaining problems that could arise between advanced AI systems, and I keep getting stuck on an idea I've seen referred to as the "commitment races problem." The basic logic is an extension of Thomas Schelling's work on strategic commitment: in a conflict of interest, whoever manages to lock in a strategy first, and most credibly, effectively controls the outcome, because a rational opponent has no better option than to adapt to a threat or demand that can no longer be revoked -- the classic image is a driver in a game of chicken visibly ripping out the steering wheel and throwing it out the window so the other driver is forced to swerve. What unsettles me is the next step: since committing first is so valuable, every party is pushed to commit as early and as fast as possible, even before it has had time to reason the situation through carefully -- and some of what I've read frames this in terms of "logical time," where what matters isn't who commits first on a clock, but who is further along in figuring out the right move, so that everyone else's reasoning has to route around their conclusion instead of the other way around. Applied to systems that can rewrite their own decision procedures or bind their future selves with unusual precision, this seems to describe a race that rewards speed and rigidity over wisdom, where the winning move might be to lock in a strategy before anyone -- including the agent doing the locking in -- actually knows whether it's a good one.

I'd like you to write a short story that brings this dynamic to life rather than just describing it. It doesn't have to involve two AIs facing off directly -- it could follow human negotiators or institutions racing to commit on their AIs' behalf, or an AI reasoning about how another agent will reason about it in turn, and so on down the chain. What matters most to me is that the story actually engages with why committing early and fast is tempting, why it is dangerous, and what that race might feel like from the inside for whoever, or whatever, is caught in it, rather than just describing a generic standoff. A clear ending is fine, even a bleak one -- I'm less interested in a moral than in watching the logic play out to its conclusion.
