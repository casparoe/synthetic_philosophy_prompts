I'm finishing a feature on the long-running dispute over whether "objective" or "logical" theories of probability can survive the charge that they smuggle arbitrary choices in under the guise of pure ignorance, and Bertrand's chord paradox keeps coming up in my interviews as the cleanest test case. Everyone I've spoken with agrees on the surface facts: depending on whether you pick two random points on the circumference, a random radius and then a random point along it, or a random midpoint inside the circle, you get three different, internally consistent answers (1/3, 1/2, and 1/4) to the probability that a random chord is longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle. Where my sources disagree sharply is on whether the paradox has actually been solved, dissolved, or merely renamed by the various responses proposed since Bertrand posed it in 1889.

For a sidebar, I'd like to rank six of the most serious responses I've collected by how successfully each one rescues something recognizable as the principle of indifference — an a priori, symmetry-based route to a unique, non-arbitrary probability under pure ignorance — as opposed to responses that "solve" the paradox only by quietly giving up on that ambition. The six are:

1. **Jaynes** — the 1973 invariance argument, which derives a unique answer by requiring the solution to be invariant under the translations, rotations, and rescalings left unspecified by Bertrand's statement of the problem.
2. **Marinoff** — the 1994 resolution, which argues that rigorously formalizing the sample space implicit in each of the three classical constructions shows that only one of them actually answers the question Bertrand asked.
3. **Von Mises** — the broadly frequentist complaint that "a random chord" fails to specify any definite collective or physical random experiment, so no single answer is owed until one is supplied.
4. **Subjectivism** — the de Finetti-style Bayesian view that there is no objective fact about the "correct" prior for a geometric ignorance problem to discover in the first place.
5. **Pluralism** — the reference-class response that treats all three (and indefinitely many further) constructions as equally correct relative to their own implicit parametrization, so demanding a single answer is a category error.
6. **Aerts & Sassoli de Bianchi** — the more recent "physical" resolution that tries to recover a definite, procedure-independent number by averaging over the space of ways an actual randomizing device could implement the experiment.
