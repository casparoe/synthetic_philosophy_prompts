"In lecture we used Noggle's 'bypassing' account of manipulation: persuasion gives the target reasons she can weigh for herself, manipulation moves belief, desire, or emotion through some route that skips that weighing. Consider a bednet charity (think AMF) whose fundraising letter leads with a photo and first name of one real child it helped, right next to the true aggregate cost-effectiveness stat from GiveWell. Internal A/B testing shows the individual story triples donations; exit surveys show most of those donors later say they wish they'd 'just been given the real numbers.' Using Noggle's criterion, is leading with the story manipulative or legitimate persuasion? Does it matter that nothing in the letter is false?"

and here's the student's answer, verbatim:

"On Noggle's view, persuasion works by offering the target reasons she can assess, while manipulation moves belief or desire through some route that skips that assessment. By that test the letter is manipulative: the child's story doesn't make bednets more cost-effective, it just produces a much stronger response than the statistic does, because identifiable victims move us more than statistical ones do, a bias Kahneman and Tversky first identified in their work on scope insensitivity. Since the letter is optimized to trigger that bias rather than to help the reader reason about where to give, it bypasses rational agency even though every fact in it is true, which shows manipulation doesn't require deception on Noggle's view. The exit-survey result confirms this: if donors wish afterward they'd been given the real numbers, their in-the-moment choice isn't one they'd endorse on reflection, and that gap is basically the signature of a manipulated rather than a persuaded belief. More generally, this shows that any charity appeal built around a story instead of a statistic is manipulative, since a story always works through emotional identification rather than argument."
