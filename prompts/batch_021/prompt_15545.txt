I've been teaching a graduate seminar on the Vienna Circle this term, and a couple weeks ago we spent a whole session on Neurath's Isotype project -- the pictorial statistics work he started at the Gesellschafts- und Wirtschaftsmuseum in Vienna and kept developing after he fled first to the Netherlands and then Oxford. One of my students made an offhand comment that's honestly been bugging me since -- I havent been able to shake it: the whole design philosophy behind Isotype is built around rules like "repeat the icon rather than resizing it" to show quantity, precisely so a viewer, whatever language they speak or whatever their education level, can just read the quantity straight off the picture with no interpretation required. That sounds a lot like Neurath is quietly reintroducing exactly the kind of theory-neutral, unmediated "given" that his mature epistemology was supposed to have banished for good -- the boat metaphor, the fight with Carnap and Schlick over protocol sentences, the insistence that even observation reports are revisable and only ever justified by their fit with the rest of the web of belief.

So here's what I want: actually build that argument, don't just gesture at it. Work from specific features of how Isotype charts get made -- the repetition-over-resizing rule, the role of the "transformer" whose job is to turn raw statistics into a picture, the fact that every chart still needs a verbal key or legend to be read correctly, the whole aspiration to a picture-language that works across literacy and language barriers -- to an actual conclusion about whether this practice is in tension with, or maybe even undercuts, his anti-foundationalist, coherentist epistemology. And don't let yourself off easy: before you land on that conclusion, lay out the strongest reply a sympathetic Neurathian could give, and only then tell me whether you think the tension survives it anyway.
