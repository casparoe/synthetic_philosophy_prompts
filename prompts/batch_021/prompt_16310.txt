I've been working through a unit on confounding in an introductory causal-inference course, and Simpson's paradox keeps coming up as the go-to illustration of why raw, pooled data can be misleading. The example that has stuck with me concerns the effect of maternal smoking on infant mortality: across the population as a whole, babies born to mothers who smoked die at a higher rate than babies born to non-smoking mothers, which is roughly what one would expect. But when the same data are broken down by birth weight, the pattern reverses within each weight class — among low-birth-weight infants specifically, those born to smokers have a lower mortality rate than those born to non-smokers in that same category, and something similar holds among normal-birth-weight infants. Our instructor presented this as a textbook Simpson's-paradox reversal and used it to motivate a general rule: whenever stratifying by a third variable flips the direction of an association, it is the stratified, subgroup-specific figures that reflect the real underlying relationship, since they have been purged of whatever was confounding the pooled comparison.

Given that rule, the honest conclusion from the birth-weight data would seem to be that maternal smoking is actually protective against mortality once birth weight is properly controlled for, and that the original pooled result was simply an artifact of not stratifying finely enough. Is that the right way to read a case like this, and more generally, is it true that whenever Simpson's paradox produces a reversal of this kind, we should always defer to the subgroup-level estimates over the aggregate one, on the grounds that finer conditioning gets us closer to the truth?
