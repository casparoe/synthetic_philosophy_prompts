I've always been struck by a strange wrinkle in Turing's "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" (1950): of the nine objections to the imitation game he considers, the one he treats most credulously is the last, the argument from extrasensory perception. He judged the statistical case for telepathy built up by card-guessing researchers in the tradition of J. B. Rhine to be overwhelming, and worried enough about a telepathic participant giving the game away that he proposed shielding the test against psychic interference to keep it fair. In the decades since, that evidential case has been dismantled by failed replications and exposed practices like optional stopping and file-drawer effects, culminating in the furor over Daryl Bem's 2011 precognition studies, now widely cited as a catalyst for psychology's replication crisis; parapsychology has since become something of a stock example, in Bayesian and rationalist circles, for how far rigorous-looking statistics can mislead absent a sufficiently skeptical prior. Had Turing lived to see this trajectory unfold, would he have revised his assessment, and roughly when would that update have occurred? Does his willingness to take Rhine-style evidence at face value also expose a real soft spot in his epistemic practice elsewhere in that paper — contrast his much brisker dismissal of the argument from consciousness a few pages earlier — or is it better read as a forgivable product of its time, without much bearing on the rest of his argument?
