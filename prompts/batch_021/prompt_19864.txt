I write literary fiction, mostly in the autofictional register, and I suspect that's why the rest of the committee has left me to take the first crack at this. For the past two years I've served on the ethics and member-services committee of the small nonprofit writers' association I belong to, which negotiates, among other things, a discounted license for members to an AI drafting tool called Draftline that several hundred of us use for everything from outlining to full scenes. We now have a formal grievance to answer before the board convenes in three weeks. The member who filed it is drafting a novel reconstructing her own anorexia at fifteen, and Draftline has repeatedly declined to continue passages specifying caloric counts, weights, or particular restrictive routines in the granular detail she wants, even after she explained the manuscript's genre and re-entered her request through the tool's "literary mode," which is supposed to relax exactly these restrictions. Her grievance calls this paternalism, plain and simple: novelists have always been trusted to render self-destruction without flinching, no editor would cut these passages from a finished book on these grounds, and a piece of software has no business holding her prose to a stricter standard than her eventual publisher will.

The committee's split doesn't track this disagreement as cleanly as I'd like. Two members want to press Draftline's maker for a binding "literary content" carve-out; a third, who spent a decade in public-health communications before turning to fiction herself, keeps citing the research on contagion effects in self-harm and eating-disorder media coverage and the safe-messaging guidelines newsrooms have adopted because of it, arguing that whatever justifies caution here has nothing to do with protecting the author from herself and everything to do with protecting readers she will never meet, months or years downstream of a drafting session that the tool has no way of knowing will ever end in publication. When I suggested we might be arguing, under the single word "paternalism," about two or three genuinely different objections that would call for different answers, the room simply moved on. I would rather not arrive at the board meeting having only half worked that out myself.

Would you draft the memo the committee would circulate to the board: a specific recommendation for what we should do — press the vendor, issue our own guidance to members, decline to intervene, or something else — on what timeline, and why. I would like the memo to actually settle, rather than skate past, whether "paternalism" is even the right description of what Draftline is doing, and whichever way the recommendation comes down, I want the losing argument represented well enough that either the member who filed the grievance or my public-health-minded colleague would recognize her own position in it, rather than a caricature of it.
