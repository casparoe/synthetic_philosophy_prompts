In "Criteria of Negro Art" (1926), W. E. B. Du Bois argued that art by and about Black Americans is never aesthetically neutral, insisting that "all art is propaganda" and must be judged by its service to truth, beauty, and racial justice. At the same time, Du Bois was committed to racial uplift through respectability, and grew uneasy with Harlem Renaissance writing that lingered on the poorer or more disreputable sides of Black urban life, fearing it would feed white prejudice rather than advance the race. Suppose Du Bois, who died in 1963, had lived to see the emergence of "conscious" hip-hop: music explicitly devoted to Black political liberation, yet often delivered through profanity, violence, and unsparing depictions of poverty. Would he regard this as fulfilling his own theory of art as propaganda, or would his commitment to respectability lead him to condemn it much as he condemned kindred tendencies among his Harlem Renaissance contemporaries? Justify your answer with reference to the specific standards for judging art that he lays out in the essay.
