In my seminar on rational choice and decision theory this term, we recently discussed Douglas Hofstadter's notion of superrationality: the idea that two agents who are each fully rational, and who each know the other to be fully rational, ought to cooperate in a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma, since whatever reasoning leads one of them to a particular choice must, by symmetry, lead the other to the very same choice, making mutual cooperation the only self-consistent outcome. This followed on from earlier sessions on Newcomb's problem and Parfit's Hitchhiker, where we had already seen how a correlation between an agent's disposition and a predictor's behavior can matter for rational choice even when no causal arrow runs from the agent's present decision back to the predictor's earlier prediction.

After class, a classmate mentioned that superrationality has been generalized, in parts of the decision theory and rationalist literature, into the concept of "acausal trade": the claim that agents who cannot causally influence one another at all, whether because they are separated by unbridgeable distances, occupy different branches of a multiverse, or exist at different points in time, can nonetheless benefit one another by each acting as though the other will reason similarly and reciprocate. I would like to understand this generalization more precisely. Could you compare Hofstadter's original superrationality argument with the broader notion of acausal trade?

In particular, I am interested in which additional assumptions acausal trade needs beyond those required for superrationality in a symmetric one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma — for instance, assumptions about the agents' capacity to predict or model one another, about how correlated their underlying decision procedures must be, and about what should count as "the same" choice once the agents are exchanging different favors rather than playing a symmetric game. Feel free to touch on how causal, evidential, and functional or updateless decision theories differ in how comfortably they can accommodate acausal trade as genuinely rational. Finally, in which ways, if any, is acausal trade a distinctive extension of superrationality rather than merely a relabeling of the same idea for a cosmic setting, and does the case for it strike you as resting on firmer or shakier ground than the original argument for superrationality?
