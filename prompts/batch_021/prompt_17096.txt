For my philosophy elective we spent a week on modal realism, and out of curiosity I went looking for other things David Lewis had written. That's how I found out that the same philosopher famous for arguing that every possible world is just as real as ours also wrote a short, strange paper (I believe it's called something like "Do We Believe in Penal Substitution?") asking whether Christians are even entitled to believe that Jesus was punished in our place so that God could justly forgive us. The part that stuck with me is a distinction he draws between two kinds of penalty: a fine, which anyone can pay on your behalf so that your debt to the law counts as settled, and a prison sentence, where it seems absurd to say that a volunteer serving your time somehow clears your record, since you would still be the person who actually committed the crime and got away with it. He uses that gap to ask whether punishment, unlike debt, is even the kind of thing that can be handed off from the guilty party to a willing substitute, or whether penal substitution quietly asks us to believe in a moral impossibility dressed up in theological language.

Could you write me a dialogue between two contemporary philosophers who have each read this argument and walked away with opposite conclusions? One should argue that penal substitution can be made coherent, engaging seriously with the fine-versus-imprisonment gap rather than brushing past it, and spelling out what is special about the Christ case (consent, representation, an unusual theory of what punishment fundamentally is, or whatever move actually works) that lets substitution succeed where it wouldn't in an ordinary criminal trial. The other should argue that no such move works, and that the doctrine is either incoherent or rescuable only at the cost of giving up whatever it is that makes punishment, rather than mere suffering, matter morally in the first place. I would like them to genuinely engage with each other's specific moves instead of talking past one another. Feel free to set the scene however you like, whether that's a seminar room after class or a long email exchange, but I want to see the strongest version of both sides, since I am honestly trying to work out which way this argument goes rather than collect a soundbite for each team.
