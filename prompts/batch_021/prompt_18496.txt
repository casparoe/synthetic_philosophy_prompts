I spent almost forty years as a geologist - rocks, faults, the occasional volcano - so philosophy of mind is about as far from my old wheelhouse as it gets, but since retiring I've gone down a total rabbit hole with it. It started when my son showed me this youtube series where Keith Frankish and a panpsychist philosopher (Philip Goff, I think - they call the show Mind Chat or something close to that) go back and forth about theories of consciousness, and I got completely hooked on Frankish's illusionism, the claim that phenomenal consciousness, the "what it's like"-ness of experience, isn't actually real, it just seems that way to us.

Here's the bit that's been bugging me. Doesn't the very fact that it seems like there's something it is like to be me already presuppose the thing illusionism is trying to deny? If I have an illusion that I'm conscious, isn't undergoing that illusion itself a conscious event - i.e. something it is like to have? It feels like the theory eats it's own tail: you can't have a mere seeming of phenomenality without that seeming itself being a phenomenal fact. I gather this move against illusionism actually has a name, something like "the paradox of illusionism," and that Frankish tries to head it off by saying introspection represents our experiences as having full-blown phenomenal properties when really they only have "quasi-phenomenal" (purely functional) properties. When I try to picture what a mere seeming would even be if it wasn't phenomenal in some sense, though, I just draw a blank.

So here's what I'd like from you: build the strongest version you can of the argument that illusionism is self-undermining for exactly this reason, and actually engage with the quasi-phenomenal-properties move rather than just brushing past it. Then give me a real verdict, not a "well, reasonable people disagree" - on balance, does illusionism actually survive this objection, or does it secretly help itself to the very thing it claims doesn't exist?
