Question: "Explain how Solomonoff induction is meant to formalize Occam's razor. Your answer should (a) define Kolmogorov complexity, (b) explain how the universal prior is built out of it, and (c) discuss one major limitation of Solomonoff induction as an account of inductive inference."

Student answer: "Solomonoff induction tries to make Occam's razor -- the idea that, other things equal, we should prefer simpler hypotheses -- mathematically precise. It does this by putting a 'universal prior' over hypotheses (equivalently, over the programs that could be generating our data), where a hypothesis h gets prior probability roughly proportional to 2^-K(h), K(h) being the Kolmogorov complexity of h: the length of the shortest program that outputs h on some fixed universal Turing machine. So shorter, simpler hypotheses automatically get more prior weight, and you update this prior on evidence with Bayes' theorem as usual. Kolmogorov complexity is computable -- you find K(h) by just running longer and longer candidate programs and checking which ones output h -- so the whole scheme is, in principle, a well defined algorithm, just an extremely slow one, which is part of why its sometimes called an 'ideal' theory of inductive inference. The limitation people usually raise is basically practical rather than principled: even granting computability, searching through all programs consistent with the data so far is completely intractable, so nobody thinks Solomonoff induction is something you could actually run -- it's a normative benchmark that things like the Minimum Description Length principle try to approximate."
