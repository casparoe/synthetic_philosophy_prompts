Last weekend a friend and I got into a long, occasionally heated argument about the Judy Benjamin problem, and neither of us walked away fully persuaded by the other's position. As background: Judy Benjamin starts out with a uniform prior over four equally likely locations, formed by crossing a Red/Blue territorial boundary with a boundary separating the areas held by Headquarters Company and Second Company, so each of the four quadrants gets credence 1/4. She then receives a radio message that says nothing about her overall odds of being in Red versus Blue territory, but does tell her that, conditional on being in Red territory, the odds are 3:1 that she's in the Headquarters Company area rather than the Second Company area. My friend's position was that, since the message is entirely silent on the Red/Blue question, the only defensible update is to leave P(Red) fixed at one-half and simply redistribute probability inside Red to reflect the stated 3:1 split, leaving Blue untouched. I objected that this isn't what actually falls out of the "minimum relative information" rule — the natural generalization of Jeffrey conditionalization to a constraint on a conditional probability rather than on a partition, and the very procedure van Fraassen used this example to criticize — since running the cross-entropy minimization moves P(Red) to somewhat under 0.47, even though the message never mentions Red versus Blue at all. Neither of us could give a satisfying account of why the math does this instead of the "obvious" thing my friend wanted.

I'd like your help understanding this properly rather than just refereeing who won the argument. Could you give me an intuitive, gut-level explanation — rather than a full Lagrangian derivation, which I'm capable of grinding through myself if needed — of why minimizing relative information subject to a constraint on a conditional probability forces the marginal probability of the antecedent to move at all, given that ordinary Jeffrey conditionalization has no opinion on the matter in the first place? I'm especially interested in what it is about treating "the odds are 3:1 within Red" as a constraint on the posterior, combined with a closest-to-the-prior criterion, that rules out my friend's invariant-P(Red) answer as a candidate. If you can, I'd also appreciate a word on why this example was treated as a serious objection to relative-information-minimizing approaches to updating in general, rather than dismissed as a mathematical curiosity, and on whether anyone in the literature has proposed an updating rule that vindicates my friend's intuition without simply building it in by fiat.
