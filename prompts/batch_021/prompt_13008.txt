A few of us in my political philosophy reading group spent way too long arguing last night about whether Nozick's Wilt Chamberlain argument actually holds up, and we couldnt agree on which of the usual objections actually land versus which ones are kind of cheap shots that don't touch the real core of the argument. This isn't for an assignment or anything, I just want to get my own head straight before we meet again next week instead of going by vibes.

Quick recap in case it pins down exactly what I mean: Nozick imagines a society starting out in some distribution D1 that satisfies whatever patterned principle of justice you like (say, strict equality). A million people each freely choose to pay 25 cents to watch Wilt Chamberlain play basketball, so he ends up with $250,000, way more than anyone else, giving us a new distribution D2. Nozick's point is that D2 is just as just as D1, since it arose purely through a chain of free, uncoerced transfers, so keeping D1's pattern in place forever would require constantly interfering with people's voluntary choices, which is supposed to show that patterned theories of justice are untenable in a way historical/entitlement theories aren't.

Here are 7 standard-ish objections to that argument. Can you sort them into a tier list by how much actual damage each one does to Nozick's conclusion (not just by how popular or well-known the objection is)?

1. Equivocation on liberty: the "liberty" doing the work in the premises isn't obviously the same thing as the liberty that would be threatened by maintaining D1, so the argument trades on a shift in meaning.
2. Unjust baseline: Nozick's own theory requires D1 to be free of any historical injustice in acquisition or transfer, and basically no real-world starting point satisfies that, so the argument doesn't actually bear on real tax policy.
3. Known rules of the game: if the tax-and-transfer scheme is a public, standing rule that Wilt knows about before he ever steps on the court, taxing the $250,000 isn't a "further transfer" imposed on top of a completed transaction, it was baked into the deal from the start.
4. Myth of ownership: "his" pretax $250,000 isn't some pre-legal fact independent of the property and tax system; what counts as Wilt's in the first place is partly constituted by that very system, so calling taxation a "taking" from him begs the question.
5. Rent, not desert: a good chunk of that income reflects the scarcity of Wilt's talent (plus league rules restricting supply) rather than any extra effort or desert on his part, and scarcity rents are the kind of thing plenty of otherwise liberty-respecting views think can be taxed.
6. Proves too much: if the argument actually works, it rules out any taxation of any voluntary transfer whatsoever, always and everywhere, which looks a lot more like a reductio of the argument than something we should just accept.
7. Patterns don't have to be frozen: a patterned view doesn't need to lock in one distribution forever, it can redistribute periodically (say, once a year) while leaving transfers free in between, so "liberty vs. patterning" was never really an all-or-nothing choice.
