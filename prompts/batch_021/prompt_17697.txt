I'm a PhD student in History and Philosophy of Science, currently drafting a dissertation chapter on how Kepler policed and repeatedly redrew the boundary between astronomy and astrology. A few of us in the department also run a small history-of-science podcast, and for next month's episode we're trying a scripted "imagined interview": a colleague who has spent the better part of a year inside Tertius Interveniens and Book IV of the Harmonices Mundi is going to answer questions in character as Kepler, restricted to positions he can actually document from the primary texts. She already has a favorite move for closing down easy objections: comparing traditional astrology to a dung-heap in which an industrious hen occasionally scratches up a pearl, so that she gets to keep the pearls (the aspects) while disowning the dung (the zodiac signs, the houses, the planetary rulerships) without ever quite saying what licenses the sorting. I don't want each question in the episode to be dismissible with some restatement of that same image.

Could you draft eight or nine interview questions, sequenced so that the dung-heap move gets progressively harder to lean on, each paired with a short follow-up keyed to whichever deflection she reaches for next? Please build in at least one question, with follow-up, on each of the following: (1) how she can dismiss the zodiac signs, houses, and rulerships as arbitrary human bookkeeping while insisting the aspects alone answer to something real in nature; (2) what work is actually being done by her claim that an aspect is not a physical reality the way light is, but a mere geometrical relation that exists only in the perceiving of it -- and whether that move genuinely escapes the occult-influence picture Kepler officially scorns, or simply relocates the mystery from the planets into the soul; (3) why the purify-rather-than-abolish stance of Tertius Interveniens should count as a principled philosophical position rather than the position of someone who wrote the book as a self-appointed mediator between two other men's pamphlets, and who separately earned his living from nativities and calendars; and (4) why the constructibility of a regular polygon by compass and straightedge -- the very reasoning that let Kepler add aspects like the quintile instead of merely pruning Ptolemy's traditional five -- should be diagnostic of anything psychological or causal, rather than just a fact about Euclidean geometry. If a ninth question fits naturally, have it press her on whether this harmonic, perceptual story about the soul connects at all to the physical force Kepler was busy assigning to the sun in the Astronomia Nova during those very same years, or whether she thinks she's doing one physics or two.
