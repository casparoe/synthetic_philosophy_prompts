I've been reporting on the prediction-market boom (Polymarket, Kalshi, that whole world) and keep bumping into a theoretical snag none of my sources can explain to me in plain english. Take Bayesian rationality seriously, add something economists call the "common prior assumption," and you apparently get results like Aumann's agreeing-to-disagree theorem and the Milgrom-Stokey no-trade theorem, which basically say two people who trust each other's reasoning shouldn't be able to knowingly keep betting against each other for purely speculative reasons. And yet I watched two genuinely sharp, well-credentialed forecasters, each familiar with the others track record, put real money on opposite sides of the same contract on when AGI arrives, and neither budged an inch after hearing the other's reasoning. One economist I interviewed just shrugged and said real Bayesians all start from basically the same prior, so persistent disagreement like that has to mean somebody's sitting on private information, or fooling themselves (she called it a "self-favoring prior," I think that's a Robin Hanson term?). Another source thought that was close to nonsense, and said two equally rational people can start from genuinely different priors without either one being irrational -- something closer to what philosophers call permissivism about rational belief.

I'd love to see these two positions actually fight it out properly instead of getting ninety seconds each on a recorded call -- doesn't have to be my actual two sources, just two people who each hold their position at full strength. Could you write me a dialogue between a game theorist who defends the common-prior assumption in the Aumann/Harsanyi tradition, and a philosopher who thinks that whole tradition smuggles in a kind of view-from-nowhere that doesn't survive contact with real disagreement? Set it right after the two of them have each taken opposite sides of that same AGI-timeline contract, arguing about whether what they just did was even coherent for two mutually respectful rational agents to do. I don't want either of them to just cite their favorite theorem and walk off -- I want the philosopher to genuinely grapple with the force of the no-trade result instead of waving it away, and the game theorist to seriously engage with why a prior might not be the kind of thing that's rationally required to converge, rather than just insisting that it does.
