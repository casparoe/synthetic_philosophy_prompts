I'm auditing a graduate seminar on philosophy of history, and during the session on historical explanation, two of the enrolled students got into an extended disagreement that the professor never really settled before time ran out. The trigger was a case one of them raised: the opening of the Berlin Wall on the night of November 9, 1989, which is often told as a story of sheer contingency. East German spokesman Günter Schabowski, under-briefed on new travel rules the government had just approved, fumbled a reporter's question at a press conference and implied the rules were already in effect; crowds surged to the checkpoints that night, and the unbriefed border guards, receiving no orders from above, eventually just opened the gates rather than confront them. The question that split the room was whether telling the story this way — as a chain of particular events — actually explains why the Wall fell, or whether it only counts as an explanation at all because it's secretly leaning on some general law.

(1) Renata, defending something like Hempel's covering-law view, argued that the story only explains anything because it implicitly relies on regularities — something like "unbriefed guards facing large, non-violent crowds tend not to fire," or "when a regime's leadership won't back forceful suppression, local officials default to standing down." Absent some such regularity, she said, you don't have an explanation of why the Wall fell rather than, say, a massacre happening instead — you just have a sequence of events. She pointed out that Hempel himself called ordinary historical narratives "explanation sketches" for exactly this reason: they gesture at laws too complicated to state in full, but a law is still what's doing the explanatory work. (2) Tobias replied that this gets historians backwards: what actually explains the guards' behavior isn't a covering regularity but a reconstruction of their reasoning in that specific moment — their actual orders, their specific confusion about the chain of command that night, Schabowski's specific slip. That's what Dray called a "rational explanation": you explain an action by showing it was the sensible thing to do given the agent's own situation as they understood it, not by subsuming it under a generalization about how guards or regimes behave on average. (3) Renata answered that this doesn't escape covering laws, it just hides one: calling the guards' inaction "the rational thing to do" given their beliefs already assumes something like "agents tend to act on their beliefs and goals in a means-ends-coherent way," which is itself a general psychological regularity — we've just stopped noticing it as one. Hidden laws, she said, are still laws. (4) Tobias granted that some thin background assumption about agency is doing work, but denied it was doing the work Hempel needs: knowing guards are means-ends reasoners doesn't tell you why these particular guards opened the gate rather than radioing for reinforcements. The actual explanatory work, he said, is carried by the narrative's specific ordering — Schabowski's slip makes the crowd's rush intelligible, which makes the guards' paralysis intelligible, which makes the opening intelligible — the sort of retrospective sense-making Danto had in mind with what he called "narrative sentences," descriptions of an earlier event that are only available once a later one has occurred (no one at the press conference could have described it as "the moment the Wall fell," but we can). (5) Renata pushed back that this retrospective quality is exactly what worries her: it's always possible to arrange events into a satisfying story once you already know how things turned out — that's more or less how hindsight bias works — and without some law-like regularity to check the story against, nothing stops a well-told sequence of events from being mistaken for an explanation of why that sequence, rather than some other, had to unfold. At that point the section ended, and as far as I could tell neither of them thought they'd lost.

I'd also be curious what you think this whole back-and-forth is missing, if anything, and, if you had to weigh it up, who you think ends up closer to being right.
