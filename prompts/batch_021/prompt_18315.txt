I'm about two-thirds through a hard science-fiction novel set some four centuries from now, after a stable and broadly benevolent superintelligence has assumed custodianship of human affairs and established, through its cosmological modeling, that the reachable universe is almost certainly a small region within a far larger — quite possibly infinite — structure, meaning there exist other civilizations and other decision-making agents with whom Earth's descendants will never have any causal contact whatsoever. A recurring institution in the book is the Concordance Directorate, a standing advisory body charged with reasoning about the civilization's obligations toward those permanently unreachable others. In the chapter I'm drafting now, the Directorate must rule on a proposed Common Charter: a costly, binding commitment to set aside a fixed share of the civilization's resources for the presumed interests of agents elsewhere, and to accept certain constraints on its own conduct, including in its treatment of minds it simulates, on the strength of evidential cooperation in large worlds — the idea that because one's own decision procedure is correlated with the decision procedures of relevantly similar agents elsewhere, choosing to cooperate is itself evidence that those other agents cooperate too, even though nothing causal ever passes between them. One faction on the Directorate treats the Charter as simply the rational response to living in a world this large; another regards the whole argument as an elegant means of extracting real, immediate sacrifices in exchange for a benefit that can never, even in principle, be confirmed or reciprocated.

Could you draft the memo the Directorate's chair circulates ahead of the vote — the actual in-story document my characters will be arguing over? I need it to commit to something specific (adopt the Charter as proposed, reject it outright, or adopt some scaled-back version, with whatever conditions or a sunset-and-review clause seem warranted), and to defend that recommendation using the genuine decision-theoretic substance of the idea rather than vague appeals to cosmic neighborliness. Please give the skeptical faction's position its full due — the objection about unverifiability and one-sided sacrifice is, I think, the strongest card they hold, and I don't want it dispatched in a sentence before the memo moves on. It needn't run long; a page or two, in the dry, faintly bureaucratic register of an internal policy memo, is exactly the texture I'm after. I would much rather the reasoning be genuinely sound than merely sound impressive, since some of this book's readers will know the underlying decision theory, and I don't want the seams to show.
