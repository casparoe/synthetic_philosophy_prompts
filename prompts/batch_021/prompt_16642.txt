Last night, a friend and I got into a long argument about teleosemantics. She thinks the trouble made vivid by Millikan's frog-and-fly case—where the frog's snapping mechanism fires just as readily for BB pellets as for flies, even though, intuitively, flies are the only thing it's supposed to detect—is basically a quirk of that one crude reflex, and that the problem dissolves once you consider organisms whose relevant traits were shaped by richer, more finely differentiated selection pressures. I think the underlying problem is structural and reappears no matter how sophisticated the mechanism gets. Construct a new example—avoiding frogs and any other stock case from the literature—of a trait or cognitive capacity whose proper function could be specified, with equally good historical justification, in terms of two properties that were co-extensive throughout the relevant selection history but diverge outside it, such that a selected-effects theory of content has no principled basis for choosing one over the other as the trait's actual content. Then briefly explain why appealing to Millikan's notion of "Normal conditions" doesn't settle which one wins.
