My friend Priya (she's finishing up a logic PhD) and I got into it for like two hours after our philosophy of logic reading group last night, over way too much wine, and I don't think either of us actually won. The topic was Fitch's paradox of knowability -- the proof that if you assume every truth is knowable in principle, which is basically the anti-realist's whole "knowability thesis," you can derive the much stronger and obviously false claim that every truth is already known. Something in that setup has to give, and that's where we started disagreeing.

I was defending the standard-ish anti-realist comeback that the paradox only really kills the naive, fully unrestricted version of the thesis, and that you can instead restrict knowability so it doesn't range over weird self-referential, "Moore-paradoxical" instances like p & ~Kp (the exact conjunction the proof exploits) -- basically the Tennant-style move, though I'll admit I was fuzzy on the actual details in the moment. Priya wasn't buying it. Her first objection was that any restriction cooked up specifically to exclude the one instance that breaks your theory is just special pleading with extra formalism bolted on. Then when I brought up the other standard escape -- switching to intuitionistic logic so you can't run double-negation elimination on the final step -- she said that just leaves you defending the weaker claim that no truth can ever be verified to be unknown, and thats basically the same uncomfortable commitment wearing a trenchcoat.

I want to see this fought out properly by someone who isn't half-drunk and improvising off half-remembered seminar readings. Could you write a dialogue between the Verificationist (the anti-realist, trying to rescue some non-trivial version of "all truths are knowable") and the Realist (who thinks Fitch's proof is basically fatal to any interesting knowability thesis), where they actually work through both moves -- the self-referential-restriction strategy and the intuitionistic-logic strategy -- rather than picking one and letting the other slide? I want the Verificationist to give a real, principled defense of each move rather than just asserting it, and I want the Realist to engage with the specifics instead of just chanting "ad hoc" -- an actual diagnosis of what each move costs or fails to deliver. Somewhere in there they should state the core proof explicitly (the p & ~Kp step is the crux, and my summary above is definitely fudging it). Neither one should cave just to give the dialogue a tidy ending -- if it's honestly a standoff over which cost is worse to swallow, that's a completely fine place to leave it.
