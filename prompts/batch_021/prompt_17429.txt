A home DNA-ancestry kit I took last month revealed that the man my family has always called my great-grandfather has no biological relation to us whatsoever: the story of his emigration from Sicily, retold across three generations, in fact belongs to someone else's family and was somehow grafted onto ours. The discovery has left me unexpectedly unsettled about how much of what I take myself to know rests on nothing more than other people's word. Since then I have been reading in the epistemology of testimony, where I keep encountering the long-standing dispute between reductionists, who hold, following Hume, that testimonial justification must ultimately be grounded in the hearer's own independent perceptual, inductive, or inferential evidence for the testifier's reliability, and anti-reductionists, who hold, following Reid, that testimony is a basic, autonomous source of warrant on which a hearer is entitled to rely absent specific grounds for doubt. I would like a concrete example — something other than the well-worn case of knowing one's own birthdate — of a specific situation in which a reductionist and an anti-reductionist would reach opposite verdicts about whether a hearer's testimony-based belief counts as justified, together with a brief explanation of why each position rules as it does there.
