I'm a PhD student in philosophy working on formal and social epistemology, and this term I've been sitting in on a game theory course that covered Aumann's agreement theorem and the Milgrom-Stokey no-trade theorem back to back. This has left me wanting much clearer sense of how these results actually bear on debates about the epistemology of disagreement, a connection that gets gestured at constantly in survey articles but is rarely unpacked with any care. I follow the formal statements well enough: under a common prior, common knowledge of two agents' posteriors on some event forces those posteriors to be equal, and Milgrom-Stokey extend this to show that, starting from an ex ante Pareto-efficient allocation, it can never become common knowledge that a newly proposed trade is mutually beneficial, so long as the only thing motivating the trade is an asymmetry in private information. What I can't yet articulate precisely is why the second result is a genuine strengthening of the first rather than just a restatement of it in the language of trade -- my sense is that Milgrom-Stokey need an extra step in which the very fact that a trade is being offered and accepted functions as a public signal that gets folded back into everyone's beliefs, in the spirit of the Geanakoplos-Polemarchakis result on iterated announcements of posteriors. I'd like a careful, intuitive walkthrough of that unraveling, ideally anchored to a toy example -- three or four states of the world, two traders with different information partitions -- small enough to track by hand rather than just algebraically.

Once that mechanism is pinned down, I want help thinking through what it actually licenses philosophically, since this is where I'm really stuck. The equal-weight view in the peer-disagreement literature is sometimes bolstered by gesturing at Aumann-style results, as though rational agents confronted with a disagreeing peer are thereby shown to be rationally required to converge -- but the common-prior assumption seems to be doing enormous and rarely-examined work here, and I suspect it quietly builds in exactly the prior epistemic symmetry between agents that the peer-disagreement debate is supposed to be adjudicating rather than presupposing from the start. Separately, I'd like your take on how to read the mismatch between the theorem's prediction and observed markets: real asset markets exhibit enormous trading volume, and I don't know whether that should count as evidence against treating the common prior as a genuine requirement of rationality, or merely as evidence that actual traders routinely violate a requirement that remains normatively intact regardless. Those strike me as quite different morals to draw, and I haven't come across a source that keeps them apart cleanly.
