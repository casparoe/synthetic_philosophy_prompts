Me and my friend Priya got into it pretty hard last night. It started as one of those dumb dinner table arguments about whether you should believe your cousin's story about a faith healer, and somehow by 1am we were deep into Hume's essay on miracles, both talking over each other about testimony and probability like we were being graded on it.

Priya's whole point is that Hume rigs the game from the start, he sets the bar for miracle-testimony so impossibly high that nothing could ever clear it, so the "argument" is really just a fancy way of assuming the conclusion. I pushed back and said no, he's doing something more careful with weighing evidence, not just stacking the deck. To back myself up I went back and reread Part 1 of "Of Miracles" this morning (hungover, not my finest scholarship) and ran straight into a passage that honestly made my side feel shakier:

"Though the Indian prince, who refused to believe the first relations concerning the effects of frost, reasoned justly; and it naturally required very strong testimony to engage his assent to facts, that arose from a state of nature, with which he was unacquainted, and which bore so little analogy to those events, of which he had had constant and uniform experience. Though they were not contrary to his experience, they were not conformable to it."

Right after that, Hume defines a miracle as a violation of the laws of nature, and pretty soon after he drops the famous line about how no testimony could ever establish a miracle unless its falsehood would be more miraculous then the fact it's trying to prove. Here's what's bugging me: with the frost case Hume seems totally fine saying that strong enough testimony, plus some analogy, really can rationally move someone to accept something totally outside their past experience. But a couple paragraphs later, with actual miracles, it sounds like testimony can never pull that off, full stop, because "a firm and unalterable experience" of the laws of nature just automatically wins. Is that a straight up double standard between the frost case and the miracle case, basically proving Priya's point, or is there some principled reason, internal to Hume's own theory of probability, for treating those two situations so differently? Neither of us studied this stuff formally so there's no professor to bug about it, I just want to know if I actually have a real answer here or if I lost the argument and don't want to admit it.
