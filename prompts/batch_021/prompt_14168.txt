This term I'm taking a seminar on classical American pragmatism, and this week's readings paired Peirce's 1905 Monist article "What Pragmatism Is" with the sixth lecture of James's 1907 book Pragmatism, titled "Pragmatism's Conception of Truth." What surprised me is how personally stung Peirce seems to have been by what became of his idea: James is generous about crediting Peirce's 1878 paper "How to Make Our Ideas Clear" as the source of the pragmatic principle, yet Peirce clearly thought James (and Schiller) had turned a rule for clarifying meaning into a theory of truth that was really just psychology in disguise — reason enough for him to rebrand his own view "pragmaticism," a term he deliberately made ugly so that, in his words, it would be "safe from kidnappers." Our professor mentioned that the two remained warm personal friends throughout the dispute, which makes me think it's worth taking seriously as philosophy rather than writing it off as a personal spat over credit.

For the seminar's take-home exercise I have to write a dialogue between the two men, and I'd like your help drafting a version I can work from. Please set the scene shortly after Peirce has read James's Pragmatism, and keep the exchange confined to their competing accounts of truth rather than the pragmatic maxim itself, since the maxim is the part they actually agreed about. I'd like Peirce to press James on whether cashing truth out in terms of an idea's satisfactoriness, its verifiability, or its capacity to "work" collapses the difference between a belief's being true and its merely being convenient or comforting to hold, and to tie this to his worry that James's account leaves no room for a belief that satisfies everyone today nonetheless to turn out false. James should press back by asking what work Peirce's "final opinion," fated to be agreed upon only at the unreachable limit of inquiry, is actually doing if it can never be cashed out or checked against anything — isn't it just as much a human postulate as anything in James's own account?

Beyond those two central moves, I'd like each figure to introduce at least one further objection genuinely drawn from his own body of work, and I want the other to answer it on the merits rather than deflect or change the subject: both men were far too sharp for that, and I don't want either one reduced to a strawman for the other to knock down. Please write it as prose dialogue rather than a list of talking points, somewhere in the range of 700-800 words, and don't feel obliged to manufacture a tidy resolution at the end — I'd rather it close with both of them seeing clearly, and perhaps for the first time saying aloud, exactly where their disagreement actually bottoms out.
