Two friends of mine, Nate (an ICU nurse) and Selin (a bioethics PhD student), got into a long argument at dinner about donation-after-circulatory-death (DCD) protocols, and I still can't decide who came out ahead.

(1) Nate opened by noting that in DCD, a patient is declared dead about five minutes after the heart stops and organ procurement follows immediately -- but that span of asystole doesn't mean the heart could never have been restarted, only that no one is going to try. Calling that "irreversible" cessation, he said, is a polite fiction that lets transplant teams satisfy the dead donor rule (the principle that organs may only be taken from patients who are already dead) on paper while doing something else in practice. (2) Selin replied that "irreversible" has never meant "could not conceivably be reversed by any intervention" -- every real determination of death, brain-based or circulatory, is made against a backdrop of which interventions won't be attempted, not which are metaphysically impossible. (3) Nate granted that, but said there's still a difference in kind, not degree, between "no technology on Earth could revive this person" and "working technology is sitting right next to the bed and is being withheld by the very people signing the death certificate." (4) Selin answered that once a patient or surrogate has the right to refuse resuscitation, the mere availability of the withheld intervention is morally beside the point -- otherwise a hospice patient who dies at home under a DNR, with nobody attempting CPR, wouldn't count as really dead either, just "unresuscitated," which proves too much. (5) Nate's closing move was that this conflates two separate questions -- whether withholding treatment is permissible, and whether the resulting state is death -- and that the concept doing the real work in DCD is a fact about human decisions, which can't be the same "irreversible loss of function" that whole-brain criteria are supposed to track. On his view, the Uniform Determination of Death Act just uses "irreversible" in two different senses and calls it one criterion.

Could you adjudicate the exchange move by move -- who has the better of it at each of (1) through (5), not just who wins overall -- and say whether there's a consideration neither of them raised that would actually settle the dispute?
