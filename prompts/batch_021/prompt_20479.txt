I'm compiling a set of case-study dialogues for my philosophy of biology seminar on evolutionary explanation. I'd like the next one to apply Gould and Lewontin's spandrels critique to a case their 1979 paper never actually discussed: the human female orgasm. Write a dialogue between two evolutionary theorists arguing over this case. One is a careful adaptationist who argues that a reflex this anatomically elaborate — involving coordinated uterine, vaginal, and pelvic-floor contractions and a distinctive hormonal signature — is unlikely to be selectively inert, and proposes that it serves some further function such as sperm retention, mate choice, or pair-bonding. The other defends the "spandrel" reading, on which orgasm is a nonadaptive byproduct of the shared embryological development of male and female genitalia, on a par with male nipples, and points to its notoriously unreliable elicitation through intercourse alone as exactly what one would expect from an unselected byproduct rather than a fine-tuned adaptation. Both have read Elisabeth Lloyd's complaint that most of the proposed adaptive stories here were never tested against rivals, so neither may simply dismiss the other's position as an unfalsifiable just-so story without saying what observation would actually change their mind. Have them work out where the burden of proof really lies, and whether "it's a spandrel" is itself a testable claim or just the byproduct camp's own version of Panglossian storytelling.
