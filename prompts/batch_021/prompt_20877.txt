This term I'm taking a seminar that pairs philosophy of mind with mechanistic interpretability, and we've spent the last few weeks asking whether talk of neural networks "having representations" amounts to warranted realism or just a convenient way of describing causal structure. For our next discussion post, I'd like you to write a dialogue between two interpretability researchers standing in front of a dashboard displaying the "features" a sparse autoencoder has extracted from a language model's residual stream. The realist holds that these features are discovered, mind-independent representations, pointing to the fact that ablating or steering along a feature's direction reliably changes the model's behavior in the predicted way, and defending a Dennett-style "real patterns" standard on which a pattern counts as real once it earns its keep predictively and causally. The skeptic thinks this realism is unearned, pointing to how sensitive the extracted dictionary of features is to training seed, sparsity penalty, and dataset, and invoking the lesson from probing-classifier research that a method can appear to recover structure that is really an artifact of its own capacity rather than of anything already in the network. Have them engage with each other's strongest evidence — the ablation results against the instability and probing worries — rather than talk past one another, and see whether they can locate a further experiment that would settle their disagreement, or explain why none could.
