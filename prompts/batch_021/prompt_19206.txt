I'm a retired physicist (condensed matter, mostly) and for the past year or so I've gotten seriously into philosophy for the first time, partly through a little discussion group that meets at my local library on Tuesdays. We've been slowly working through computability theory and I got stuck on something about the Church-Turing thesis that I can't quite let go of. Everyone treats it as bedrock, and I get why - Turing's machines, Church's lambda calculus, and Gödel and Kleene's general recursive functions were all worked out independently and turned out to pick out exactly the same class of functions, which really is striking. But that convergence is still evidence, not a proof, since nobody ever gave an independent, formal definition of "effectively calculable" against which to prove those formalisms equivalent. So the thesis sits in this odd spot where its not a theorem the way the equivalence results themselves are theorems, but it also doesn't feel like an ordinary empirical claim you could just go out and test.

What's bugging me is that I keep seeing the thesis get invoked, on both sides, in arguments I'm not sure it actually settles. My nephew, who does ML for a living, argues that since brains are presumably Turing-equivalent, there's nothing in principle stopping a big enough model from really thinking; a colleague of his counters that biological neurons might do analog, continuous-valued computation that lets the brain compute things no Turing machine can, so digital AI is out of luck no matter how big it gets. Seperately, I keep running into people online claiming quantum computers already show the thesis is false, and other people (rightly, I think, though I can't quite articulate why) saying that's a confusion between computability and complexity. And then there's a whole further literature on deliberate "hypercomputers" - Turing's own oracle machines, analog neural nets with arbitrary real-valued weights, even these relativistic setups (Malament-Hogarth spacetimes, I believe they're called) where an observer supposedly watches an infinite computation finish in finite proper time - and people disagree about whether any of that, if physically realized, would actually refute the thesis, or whether it's not even the sort of thing that could.

So here's what I'd like from you: could you lay out the most important implications of the Church-Turing thesis's peculiar epistemic status - neither a proven theorem nor a garden-variety empirical hypothesis - for figuring out which of these arguments (the brain/AI ones, the quantum computing one, and the hypercomputation ones) are actually engaging with the thesis versus which are just trading on a loose sense of what kind of claim it even is? I'm not after a refresher on what a Turing machine is, I'm fine there. I want to know what would count, even in principle, as evidence for or against the thesis, and whether "it's just a thesis, not a theorem" is carrying real philosophical weight in these disputes, or whether people - myself very possibly included - are just leaning on that phrase rhetorically.
