During my years as an experimental physicist, I grew accustomed to explanations of complex behavior that invoke nothing beyond the interactions of a system's parts. Since retiring, I have been reading philosophy of mind, and I keep noticing how closely Searle's Chinese Room parallels a passage in section 17 of Leibniz's Monadology, where he imagines walking through a mechanical mind enlarged to the size of a mill and finding only parts pushing against parts, with no perception anywhere to be discovered. I would like you to compare the two arguments rather than simply note the resemblance. Leibniz denies perception to any aggregate whatsoever, on the principled mereological ground that only simple, genuinely unified substances (monads) can perceive; Searle denies understanding only to symbol-manipulating systems specifically, while wanting to preserve brains, as biological mechanisms, as sufficient for genuine intentionality. Given that brains are, on Leibniz's own terms, just another aggregate of interacting parts, does his mereological argument, if sound, prove too much for Searle's purposes by ruling out brains alongside computers as loci of real perception? Please justify your answer, and say which of the two arguments strikes you as the more defensible challenge to the claim that organized mechanism could ever suffice for mind.
