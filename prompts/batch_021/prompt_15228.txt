For a near-future novel I'm drafting, one pivotal scene turns on a video, seen by millions before anyone can verify it, that appears to show a miraculous healing. My protagonist, a philosopher, wants to resist believing it on explicitly Humean grounds, and I need her reasoning to genuinely hold up rather than gesture vaguely at "Hume would be skeptical of miracles." Specifically: if Hume himself were confronted with a world in which moving images, and not only spoken or written testimony, can be fabricated cheaply and undetectably, how would he revise the actual argument of "Of Miracles" — the maxim that testimony can establish a miracle only if its own falsehood would be more improbable than the miracle itself, together with his separate observations about our appetite for the marvelous and about how rival religions' miracle-claims undercut one another? Does the mere possibility of undetectable fabrication strengthen his case against trusting the video, by further degrading the background reliability of the relevant class of evidence, or does it threaten to undercut his whole framework, which quietly presupposes a stable track record against which the reliability of testimony can be calibrated? Work through this with real fidelity to the argument's actual structure, and commit to a specific verdict about which way it actually cuts.
