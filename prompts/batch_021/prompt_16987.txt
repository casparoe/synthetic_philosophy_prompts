My philosophy of language seminar has spent the last few weeks on the semantics of conditionals, and one example from the reading keeps nagging at me: Ernest Adams' pair contrasting "If Oswald didn't kill Kennedy, someone else did" with "If Oswald hadn't killed Kennedy, someone else would have." The first sounds obviously true, the second sounds like baseless speculation, and yet the two sentences differ only in the mood of the conditional. A classmate and I got into an argument after class about whether this really shows that indicative and counterfactual conditionals need two fundamentally different kinds of treatment, or whether a single unified semantics could still cover both if it's set up cleverly enough, and neither of us could settle it, partly because our readings tend to present Stalnaker's side and the Adams/Edgington side as two self-contained summaries rather than as a real exchange between them.

Could you write me a dialogue between two philosophers holding these opposing positions, in which they actually engage with each other's strongest moves rather than just restating their own views side by side? One character should defend a Stalnaker-style unified account, on which a single selection-function mechanism picks out the relevant "closest" antecedent-world for both indicative and counterfactual readings, with indicative conditionals constrained to stay inside the live epistemic possibilities of the conversation and counterfactuals free to reach outside them. The other should defend the Adams/Edgington line that indicative conditionals have no classical truth conditions at all and are governed instead by something like Adams' Thesis, so that indicative and counterfactual "if" turn out to be different in kind rather than two settings of one dial. Have them work through, in order: first, how each side accounts for the Oswald/Kennedy contrast and what each thinks the other gets wrong about it; second, whether Lewis's triviality result really forces anyone who wants truth conditions for indicatives to give them up, and how the Stalnaker-style character tries to resist that conclusion; and third, whether the no-truth-conditions view can make sense of indicative conditionals embedded under negation or nested inside further conditionals, which is usually said to be the price of denying them truth conditions in the first place. End the dialogue with each character naming one specific piece of linguistic or logical evidence that would actually change their mind, rather than letting either retreat to a bare difference in starting intuitions.
