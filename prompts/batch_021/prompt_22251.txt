"Wittgenstein's private language argument is best understood as a continuation of the point that meaning is constituted by use rather than by any act of inner ostension. In §243 Wittgenstein raises the idea of a language whose individual words refer to what can be known only to the speaker of it — his immediate private sensations — and asks whether such a language is conceivable. His answer, worked out through §258, is negative, and the diary case shows why. A speaker resolves to write 'S' in a diary whenever a certain sensation recurs, fixing the sign's meaning by concentrating his attention on the sensation as he writes it down. But concentrating attention cannot supply a criterion of correctness for future uses of 'S', since there is nothing to distinguish his correctly remembering the sensation from merely seeming to remember it: whatever is going to seem right to him is right, which Wittgenstein takes to show that talk of 'right' and 'wrong' has lost its normal foothold here.

The beetle-in-the-box passage in §293 extends this point. We are asked to imagine everyone possessing a box that only they can look into, and calling whatever is inside it a 'beetle.' Wittgenstein's target is the picture of sensation-language on which a word like 'pain' functions by naming an inner object that only its owner can inspect, much as each person's beetle is known only to them. The upshot of the passage is that on this picture we could never actually know whether what is in your box resembles what is in mine, so the assumption of a shared inner object behind our shared word is something we are in no position to verify. This is, in effect, a private-language version of the classical problem of other minds: the private-object model makes the sameness of our sensations permanently unknowable, and it is this unknowability that should make us suspicious of the model in the first place.

Kripke's *Wittgenstein on Rules and Private Language* gives the argument its sharpest formulation. Kripke shows, via the deviant 'quus' function, that no fact about my past behavior or mental history can determine whether by '+' I have always meant addition rather than some function agreeing with it only on numbers I have actually computed, so that ordinary rule-following turns out to be paradoxical: no fact makes it the case that I am following one rule rather than another. Kripke's skeptical solution holds that meaning-ascriptions are not made true by any such fact but are warranted by an individual's agreement with the practice of the community around him; it is this communal check that gives content to the idea of following a rule correctly. Applied to the private linguist, the result is exactly Wittgenstein's own conclusion in §258 and §293: cut off from any community that could correct him, the diarist has no way of drawing the distinction between using 'S' correctly and merely seeming to, so his private language collapses into no language at all.

Not every commentator accepts this alignment. Baker and Hacker object that Kripke's picture turns Wittgenstein into a meaning-skeptic who then requires a communitarian solution to a paradox, whereas Wittgenstein's actual aim in these sections is therapeutic: to dissolve the idea that following a rule, whether alone or in company, requires some further fact — mental, behavioral, or social — to interpret or fix it. On their view, the request for such a fact is already a confusion, and pointing to communal agreement simply relocates the confusion rather than removing it. This is a serious objection, but it does not affect the main line of argument developed above, since whether one calls the result a 'solution' or a 'dissolution,' the conclusion is the same: an isolated individual could not sustain a private language of the kind §243 imagines."
