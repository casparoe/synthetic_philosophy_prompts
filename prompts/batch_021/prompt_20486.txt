I'm writing a feature on the history and philosophy of mathematics that has led me deep into Thomas Hobbes's writings on geometry. Hobbes regarded geometry as the one branch of natural philosophy in which human beings had achieved genuine, causal certainty, precisely because we generate its objects ourselves through motion — a line traced by a moving point, a plane by a moving line, and so on. That same conviction underlay his notorious, decades-long feud with John Wallis, in which Hobbes insisted he had squared the circle by classical construction and dismissed Wallis's algebraic and infinitesimal methods as empty symbol-manipulation with no genuine geometric content.

Now suppose Hobbes could be briefed on two later developments: the discovery of internally consistent non-Euclidean geometries, which dethroned Euclid's parallel postulate as the sole true description of space, and Hilbert's formalist axiomatization of geometry, on which terms like "point," "line," and "plane" become placeholders satisfying purely formal relations — so that, as Hilbert reportedly put it, one could substitute "table," "chair," and "beer mug" for them without any loss of rigor. Would Hobbes most plausibly have read these developments as belated vindication of his nominalism about mathematical terms, or as demolishing the physical, motion-generated foundation on which he thought geometry's certainty actually rested? Please argue for one of these readings, engaging directly with the tension between the nominalist and materialist strands of his philosophy of mathematics.
