I'm a novelist and one of five judges for a small but well-established short-fiction prize, and this year's shortlist has split the panel. One finalist's cover letter discloses that the story was produced by prompting a language model to write "in character" as an invented, wholly fictional author-persona -- complete with a biography that shifted in small ways between the two drafts the submitter shared with us -- while the human's role was limited to writing the persona's brief, choosing among generations, and light editing. Two judges call this an unusually literal version of something novelists already do -- write convincingly in a voice unlike their own -- and say the human is plainly the directing intelligence regardless of the tool. Two others invoke the "simulator" framing some researchers use for base language models: on that view the model isn't an agent with its own communicative intentions but a process that can instantiate many different personas on demand, none of which is "the model itself," so a piece produced this way may have no authorial agency behind it at all, human or otherwise -- which would fail our rule that entries be "conceived and executed by the entrant." The fifth judge wants a standard we can reuse rather than a one-off ruling. Please draft the short memo I'll bring to Tuesday's call: recommend whether to admit the entry, disqualify it, or admit it subject to conditions, propose criteria future panels could actually apply, and engage seriously with the disqualification argument rather than dismissing it.
