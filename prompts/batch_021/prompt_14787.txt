So this is a slightly weird ask but I promise it's a real philosophy problem and not just a practical one. I'm doing a philosophy PhD, and a few months back I got roped onto the family advisory council at the memory care home where my grandma lives — once I mentioned what I study they basically unofficially deputized me to draft our policy recommendations. Right now the home has no actual policy on something staff do constantly and totally inconsistently: telling residents with moderate-to-severe dementia comforting falsehoods. My grandma asks for my late grandfather most afternoons; some aides tell her "he's at the shop, he'll be back soon," which settles her down in about two minutes and she doesn't remember asking again an hour later, while others follow a stricter validation-therapy line and won't say anything false, so every so often she relives finding out he died, fresh, like it just happened. The council is genuinely split — some families want the comforting-lie approach made standard practice, others are kind of offended by the idea of routinely lying to their parent even when "it works" — and the four-principles stuff from the bioethics course I TA doesn't get any real traction here. It hit me a couple weeks ago that this is basically Nozick's experience machine playing out for real, except its someone else choosing it for her, over and over, and she can't remember or consent to the choice either way.

Could you draft the actual memo I'd bring to the council? I want a real recommendation, not "it depends" — ideally something close to a decision rule staff could apply on the floor case by case (what should trigger permission to use a comforting falsehood versus requiring redirection or honesty, who signs off on it, how often it gets reviewed). But please also seriously engage, in writing, with the strongest argument for whichever side you don't end up recommending. I don't want a memo that quietly just assumes felt comfort is what matters, or one that quietly just assumes some dignity-of-truth view, without earning it first.
