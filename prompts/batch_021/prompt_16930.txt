Over four decades in experimental physics, I invoked the phrase "laws of nature" constantly without ever pausing to ask what, metaphysically, makes something a law rather than a mere accidental regularity. Since retiring, I have been reading my way through the metaphysics of science, and I keep getting stuck on David Armstrong's proposal that a law consists in a second-order relation of necessitation, N(F,G), holding between the universals F-ness and G-ness, which is supposed to necessitate the corresponding first-order regularity that all Fs are Gs. What I cannot get past is David Lewis's objection that this necessitation relation cannot actually do the work Armstrong needs of it: however N is characterized, it seems possible for N(F,G) and F(a) to hold without G(a), unless the entailment linking them is simply stipulated by fiat, in which case the label "necessitation" is doing no real explanatory work. Could you walk me through this "inference problem" in enough depth that I could reconstruct it for a colleague, and then tell me whether dispositional essentialists, who locate the necessitating power inside the properties themselves rather than in a further relation between them, genuinely avoid the problem or merely relocate it?
