Last night, over dinner, a friend and I spent a couple of hours arguing about whether Goodman's "grue" paradox and Kripke's "quus" paradox are, at bottom, the same philosophical problem wearing different clothes. My friend, who is finishing a dissertation in philosophy of language, insisted that the two puzzles share exactly the same logical skeleton: in each case, infinitely many hypotheses (or rule-interpretations) fit any finite stretch of evidence (or past linguistic usage) equally well, so nothing in the evidence itself can privilege the "natural" candidate, green or plus, over the gerrymandered alternative, grue or quus. On her view, Goodman's talk of "projectible" predicates and "entrenchment" in Fact, Fiction, and Forecast, and Kripke's talk of dispositions and communal assertability conditions in Wittgenstein on Rules and Private Language, are just two vocabularies laid over one and the same underlying skeptical gap.

I disagreed, on the grounds that Goodman's new riddle is a genuinely epistemological problem about which projections from past to future are justified, whereas Kripke's rule-following paradox is a metaphysical problem about whether there are any meaning-constituting facts at all: a far more radical form of skepticism, and one that is not really about evidence or confirmation in the first place. We went back and forth for a while, dragging in emeralds, "quaddition," and even Quine's indeterminacy of translation, without either of us budging.

I would like your help settling this properly. Please reconstruct both puzzles with enough precision to make their structural parallels vivid: Goodman's definition of "grue" and its mirror image "bleen," together with his entrenchment-based resolution; and Kripke's deviant addition function "quus," together with the skeptical solution he reconstructs on Wittgenstein's behalf. Then, rather than splitting the difference diplomatically, please defend a verdict on two questions. First, is Goodman's appeal to entrenchment, if it succeeds at all, the kind of move that could in principle be redeployed against Kripke's skeptic about meaning: could one simply say that "plus" is more entrenched than "quus," and would Kripke have any principled reason to reject that response? Second, does the distinction I want to draw, between an epistemic puzzle about which projections are justified and a metaphysical puzzle about whether meaning-constituting facts exist, actually survive scrutiny, or does it collapse once one looks closely at what "justification" is doing in each case? I would rather you take a side than tell us we were both partly right.
