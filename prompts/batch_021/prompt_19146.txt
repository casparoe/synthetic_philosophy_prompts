I'm putting together a linked collection of short stories that each grow out of some genuinely obscure philosophical snag rather than the greatest-hits version of an idea, and I'm stuck on the one I want to write next. A few weeks back I fell down a rabbit hole reading about how a blind person's visual cortex basically gets colonized by touch and sound -- braille readers whose occipital lobe lights up when their fingers are on the page, and that guy who mountain bikes solo by clicking his tongue and reading the echoes off trees and boulders (Daniel Kish? I might be misremembering the name) apparently processes those clicks in what used to be his "seeing" brain. That sent me into this old fight in philosophy of biology over what a body part's function actually is -- I think one side (etiological, maybe? there were two guys, Wright and Cummins, back in the 70s) says the function is whatever the part was historically shaped or selected to do, full stop, no matter what it's doing for you today, and the other side says forget the history, the function is just whatever the part currently contributes to keeping the whole system running.

Here's my rough seed, tell me honestly if it can carry a whole story: a woman blind since early childhood has spent decades turning that "unused" visual cortex into something like a sixth sense -- she reads braille at absurd speed and rides mountain trails solo by echolocating off the terrain -- and a new experimental treatment can reclaim that cortical territory and give her literal sight, except the surgeon's own data suggests it will likely wreck the rewired ability shes built her whole adult identity around. Could you actually write the story itself (not a pitch, the real thing, something like 1200-1800 words) where the people pulling at her -- the surgeon, her mother, a friend from the blind cycling club -- end up embodying those two rival theories of function without either camp ever getting named out loud, and where the story never quietly sides with one of them by the end? I'd like it to land more like a quiet Ted Chiang gut-punch than an inspirational disability story, if that makes sense -- I want readers arguing afterward about which brain is the "real" one, not nodding along to a moral.
