I have recently been re-reading Eliezer Yudkowsky's old metaethics sequence on LessWrong, where he leans on something like G.E. Moore's open-question argument to motivate his view that "right" rigidly designates a specific, complex computation rather than anything definable in a short natural-language formula. He never actually quotes Moore directly, though, so I went back to Chapter 1 of Principia Ethica itself, where Moore writes:

"My point is that 'good' is a simple notion, just as 'yellow' is a simple notion; that, just as you cannot, by any manner of means, explain to any one who does not already know it, what yellow is, so you cannot explain what good is."

A little later he runs what has become known as the open-question argument: for any proposed definition of "good" in terms of some natural property (he uses "pleasant" as the test case), it always remains a meaningful, non-trivial question whether a thing with that property is in fact good, and he takes this persistent openness to show that no such definition, and hence no naturalistic identification of goodness with a natural property, can be correct.

Could you first reconstruct that argument as an explicit series of premises and a conclusion, and then tell me whether, so reconstructed, it actually threatens a naturalist who claims only a synthetic, a posteriori identity between goodness and some natural property, on the model of "water is H2O," rather than a definitional one? It is not obvious to me why a competent speaker's being able to coherently ask "is the pleasant good?" should be any more damaging to that kind of view than the coherence of "is water H2O?" is damaging to the claim that water is H2O. If Moore's argument can be repaired against this objection using only what is in the passage above, I would like to see how; if it cannot, I would like to know whether this is essentially the gap that later naturalists, Cornell realists and others, actually exploited.
