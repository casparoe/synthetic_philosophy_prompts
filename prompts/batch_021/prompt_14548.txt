Here's the question as given to the class: "Explain Putnam's multiple realizability argument and what it's supposed to show about the relationship between mental states and physical/neural states. Then explain why Ned Block's 'China Brain' thought experiment is meant to pose a problem for functionalism specifically, rather than for identity theory. Finally: does functionalism, as a view about the nature of mental states, entail physicalism? Why or why not?"

And here's the student's answer, copied over exactly, typos and all:

"Putnam's multiple realizability argument says that the same mental state, like pain, can be realized by different physical states in different organisms or systems - octopus pain might be realized in a totally different neural (or even non-neural) substrate than human pain, but both organisms can still be in pain. If mental states were strictly identical to specific types of physical states (as type-identity theory claims), then only creatures with that exact physical state could have that mental state, which seems too restrictive. Since pain seems to be realizable in many different physical substrates, Putnam concluded that mental states can't be type-identical with brain states, and instead should be understood functionally - in terms of their causal role (what typically causes them and what they typically cause), not their physical composition. This causal-role notion is what grounds functionalism: mental states are defined by their functional/causal profile, so anything that plays the right causal role - silicon chip, brain, or otherwise - counts as being in that mental state.

Block's China Brain thought experiment tries to show that functionalism, not just identity theory, has a problem. Block imagines the entire population of China organized so that each person acts as a neuron, communicating with each other by radio according to the same functional organization as a human brain, thereby realizing the same functional states as a person in pain. Block's intuition is that its implausible that this system is really conscious or feels pain, even though it satisfies the functional definition - so functionalism over-generates mental states, counting things as having feelings that intuitively shouldn't count. This is different from a problem for identity theory, because identity theory doesn't have this issue - since the China Brain isn't made of neurons, it simply wouldn't count as being in pain according to identity theory, so no problem arises for that view.

Finally, even though functionalism is officially just a claim about the causal/functional definition of mental states rather than what they're made of, this still commits functionalists to physicalism: since any system that actually realizes the right causal roles has to do so through some concrete mechanism, and the only concrete mechanisms we have any real reason to believe exist are physical ones, functionalism collapses into a form of physicalism once you spell out what could actually do the realizing."
