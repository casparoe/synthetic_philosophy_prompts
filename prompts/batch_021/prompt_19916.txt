A long-running reading group I've been part of for years recently spent several months working through Yudkowsky's old "A Human's Guide to Words" sequence, and the argument that stuck with most of us is the picture of categories as devices for tracking real clusters in "thingspace" — the idea that calling something a fish or a planet is only useful insofar as the label picks out things that actually clump together in the relevant respects, and that when a category fails to correspond to any such clustering, the right move is to taboo the word and argue about the underlying facts rather than the label. Naturally, this came up again when someone brought in the standard philosophical claim that categories like gender, race, and money are "socially constructed." Several people in the group took this as basically confirming the sequence's warnings: if these categories aren't discovered clusters that would exist whether or not anyone classified things that way, but are instead imposed by us for social, legal, or economic convenience, then by the sequence's own standard they're exactly the kind of gerrymandered, non-joint-carving categories we're supposed to distrust — useful fictions at best, with no more claim to "carving reality at a joint" than an arbitrary line on a map.

That conclusion sits badly with me, since it seems to prove far more than anyone in the room actually believes (nobody wants to say money isn't "real"), but I haven't been able to identify exactly where the inference goes wrong, and the usual replies I've encountered just assert that construction doesn't imply unreality without explaining what, if anything, is doing the joint-carving in cases like these. Within something like the thingspace framework, or any similar account on which good categories are supposed to track mind-independent statistical structure, is there a principled way to distinguish a socially constructed kind that still carves out something real from one that's simply invented wholesale, or does taking social construction seriously mean abandoning that picture of categorization altogether?
