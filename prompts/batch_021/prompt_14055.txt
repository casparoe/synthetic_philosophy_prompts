Over the last few months I've been working through Robert Nozick's Anarchy, State, and Utopia on my own time, without anyone around to check my understanding against, and I keep circling back to the entitlement theory of justice and the Wilt Chamberlain example. As I understand it, the story goes like this: a million fans each freely drop an extra twenty-five cents into a box to watch Wilt play over the course of a season, so he ends up with a quarter of a million dollars, far more than anyone else has; because every one of those transactions was voluntary and grew out of a distribution nobody had grounds to object to, the resulting inequality counts as just.

The way I've come to read this argument is that it's really a desert-based argument wearing procedural clothes: Wilt ends up rich because he has a rare talent that people are willing to pay to watch, so in some real sense he deserves his fortune, the way we might say a brilliant surgeon deserves a high salary or a straight-A student deserves their transcript, and taxing that fortune away amounts to punishing him for something he rightfully earned. On that reading, entitlement theory is basically desert theory dressed up in more sophisticated vocabulary.

If that's basically right, I've been trying to work out what it implies for cases where the recipient hasn't shown any special talent or effort at all, like a large inheritance or a lottery jackpot. Shouldn't the entitlement theory treat a windfall like that quite differently from Wilt's earnings, given that nobody can really be said to deserve a lucky number coming up, even though both look like the same kind of voluntary, uncoerced transfer from Nozick's point of view? I brought this up with a friend who took some philosophy courses in college, and she said I was running together two things Nozick is actually careful to keep separate, though she had to leave before she could spell out exactly what she meant. I'd like to actually understand the distinction rather than just take her word for it.
