I'm taking a seminar on early modern political thought and we just got to the section of Pascal's Pensées that's literally titled "Justice and the Reason of Effects." My friend has been quoting the "justice without force is tyrannical" line at me approvingly for years, like its some timeless bit of hardnosed realism, so I finally went back and read the actual fragments around it, and now I don't think it means what he thinks it means, or what I always assumed either.

Here's the run of fragments as I have them (Trotter's translation, and I think this is Brunschvicg's numbering rather than Lafuma's, if that even matters). 293: "Why do you kill me? What! do you not live on the other side of the water? If you lived on this side, my friend, I should be an assassin, and it would be unjust to slay you in this manner. But since you live on the other side, I am a hero, and it is just." Then 297, titled "Veri juris": "We have it no more; if we had it, we should take conformity to the customs of a country as the rule of justice. It is here that, not finding justice, we have found force, etc." Then 298, "Justice, might": "It is right that what is just should be obeyed... Justice is subject to dispute; might is easily recognised and is not disputed. So we cannot give might to justice, because might has gainsaid justice and has declared that it is she herself who is just. And thus, being unable to make what is just strong, we have made what is strong just."

On its own, 298 just sounds like flat cynicism, basically Thrasymachus with a French accent. But its sandwiched between the river anecdote and that clipped "veri juris" line, and a few sentences earlier in fragment 294 he'd already written "Doubtless there are natural laws; but good reason once corrupted has corrupted all" -- so he isn't denying real justice exists, just that we've lost access to it. This whole section also keeps circling back to what Pascal elsewhere calls the "reason of effects," this move where a custom that looks totally arbitrary turns out on a second look to have a real function -- and per fragment 328 there's supposedly a third look that undercuts even that one too. So is 298's punchline the cynical "second look," with some further twist I'm missing, or is Pascal just letting the cynicism stand because it's doing apologetic work for him, priming you to feel the loss of true justice so you're more receptive to the "without God there's no secure justice" move he makes elsewhere in the book? I don't need a full line-by-line commentary, just help working out what's actually being argued here instead of what the quote sounds like on a coffee mug.
