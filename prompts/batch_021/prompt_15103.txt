"Socrates argues that wine is useful for testing and cultivating self-control (sōphrosynē), because it loosens our normal inhibitions the way a frightening situation strips away our normal composure. Just as a general can't know whether a soldier is genuinely brave until he's actually been placed in a frightening situation, a lawgiver can't know whether a citizen has real self-mastery until he's been placed in a situation, like intoxication, that strips away his usual defenses of shame and habit. Sober, almost anyone can put on a decent show of virtue; it's only under the loosening effect of wine that a person's true character comes out, which is why he treats wine as a kind of truth serum for the soul -- the same insight that shows up when a very drunk Alcibiades crashes the party in the Symposium and, freed of his usual reserve, ends up blurting out his real, humiliated feelings about Socrates instead of the encomium to Eros everyone else gave.

He thinks the Spartans and Cretans are wrong to avoid these communal drinking parties altogether, because their policy assumes that the way to be safe from a temptation is to never encounter it. But this confuses avoiding a danger with conquering it: on his view, real courage and real temperance can only be produced and demonstrated through controlled exposure, not permanent abstinence, so their caution actually leaves their own citizens comparatively untested and untrained."
