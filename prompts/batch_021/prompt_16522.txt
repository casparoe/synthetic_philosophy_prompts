I've spent a lot of time reading causal-graph discussions on LessWrong and Overcoming Bias, where Pearl's do-calculus is often treated as having settled the metaphysics of causation -- but I've never been convinced that interventionism cleanly beats the older counterfactual theory it's supposed to have superseded. On Lewis's view, c causes e iff e counterfactually depends on c (later patched, to handle preemption, with a notion of "influence": patterns of counterfactual dependence across whole ranges of alterations to c and e, rather than just the single actual counterfactual). On the Woodward/Pearl interventionist view, c causes e iff some possible surgical intervention on c -- one that changes only c and whatever is downstream of it, with everything else held fixed -- would change e. The two views converge on easy cases but pull apart on harder ones. Take Schaffer's trumping-preemption case: a major and a sergeant simultaneously order "Charge"; the soldiers are trained to act on the highest-ranking order they receive and ignore any lower-ranking one, so the charge intuitively happens because of the major's order -- yet it doesn't counterfactually depend on that order, since the sergeant's order alone would have produced the identical charge. Or take symmetric overdetermination: two simultaneous, individually lethal shots, where neither shot is counterfactually necessary for the death either. Write an essay on how each theory should handle at least these two cases, including whatever ad hoc machinery each needs to reach the intuitive verdict (Lewis's move to "influence"; the interventionists' recourse to machinery like Halpern and Pearl's "contingencies"), and on whether those patches look like principled repairs or symptoms of a deeper problem. Reach and defend a definite verdict on which theory gives the more adequate account of causation -- a domain-relative answer is fine, but only if you say exactly which domain favors which theory; don't settle for "both have strengths and weaknesses."
