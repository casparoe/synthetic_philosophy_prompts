I was appointed six weeks ago as the junior biostatistician on the Data and Safety Monitoring Board for a phase III trial of a novel immunomodulatory antibody (the protocol calls it BRX-19) in patients with septic shock, and last week's interim analysis meeting turned into the first real methodological dispute I've had to help referee. The pooled, intention-to-treat comparison (964 patients) shows a clear benefit: 28-day mortality is 21% on BRX-19 versus 27% on placebo (p = 0.04), and baseline characteristics — age, comorbidity burden, SOFA score at enrollment — are well balanced across arms, so this isn't a case of randomization having simply failed. But the analysis plan also pre-specifies a severity-adjusted sensitivity analysis that stratifies patients by their SOFA score at 48 hours, and within every stratum of that analysis the benefit vanishes: mortality is statistically indistinguishable between arms in the low- and medium-severity bands, and if anything slightly worse on BRX-19 among the sickest patients (44% versus 41%).

Someone on the call invoked "Simpson's paradox," and the board split down the middle. Half, including our lead biostatistician, want to treat the stratified analysis as authoritative, on the grounds that Day-2 severity is by far the best predictor of death and that reporting the unadjusted pooled figure lets a lurking variable masquerade as a drug effect. The other half, including our independent trial statistician, want to treat the pooled intention-to-treat estimate as authoritative, on the grounds that SOFA at 48 hours is measured after two days of exposure to the study drug — so if BRX-19 works partly by altering the trajectory of organ failure, stratifying on it doesn't remove a confound, it removes part of the very effect the trial exists to measure. I had only ever met Simpson's paradox in its textbook form (kidney stones, Berkeley admissions), and hadn't appreciated how unhelpful the standard "trust the disaggregated groups" moral becomes once the stratifying variable might itself be a downstream consequence of treatment rather than a cause of who received it. Our charter instructs the board to report "the most statistically appropriate primary estimate of effect" to the sponsor, without specifying which one that is.

I need to circulate a short memo to the rest of the board before we reconvene next Tuesday, recommending which estimate we should treat as primary for our efficacy determination, what concrete action follows for the trial (continue as planned, pause enrollment, request specific additional analyses, or something else), and on what timeline. I'd like the memo to actually settle the disagreement rather than defer it by calling for "more data" in the abstract, and to engage honestly with the strongest version of whichever side's argument it doesn't adopt — both halves of this board are competent statisticians, and neither will be persuaded by being told they've simply confused correlation with causation.
