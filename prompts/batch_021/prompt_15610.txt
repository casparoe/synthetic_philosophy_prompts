I'm a senior taking a seminar cross-listed between computer science and public policy called "Algorithmic Fairness and Public Policy," and we just spent two weeks on the Chouldechova and Kleinberg-Mullainathan-Raghavan impossibility results, which show, in slightly different ways, that whenever an outcome has different base rates across two groups, a risk score cannot simultaneously be calibrated within each group (so that a given score means the same actual probability of the outcome regardless of group) and have matching false-positive and false-negative rates across those groups, except in the degenerate case of a perfect predictor. I found the math itself clean, but a few of us got into a long argument after section about whether that leaves any principled way to choose a fairness criterion once you've accepted that the trade-off is real rather than just a fixable flaw in the model.

That argument turned out to matter more than I expected, because I also represent undergraduates on my university's Learning Analytics Oversight Committee, which votes next Wednesday on whether to renew the contract for "Persist," the predictive-analytics tool our advising office uses to flag incoming students as high-, medium-, or low-risk of leaving before sophomore year, so advisors know whom to prioritize for mandatory check-ins. Base rates differ substantially here too: roughly 24 percent of first-generation students don't persist to sophomore year, versus 11 percent of continuing-generation students. The vendor's technical report shows the tool is well-calibrated with respect to that status -- among students given any particular score, about the same share actually leave regardless of group -- but the Faculty Senate's data-equity subcommittee found that among students who do go on to persist, first-generation students are flagged high-risk almost three times as often as their continuing-generation peers who also persist: a much higher false-positive rate falling on exactly the group the advising program is meant to support rather than stigmatize. The committee is now split three ways: keep the tool as-is, since calibration is what makes a score mean what it claims to mean; suspend it until the disparity is resolved; or adopt it only with conditions, such as group-specific thresholds or a rule that a high-risk flag may add resources but never restrict them (no registration holds, for instance). As the student representative, I've been asked to draft the memo we'll circulate before the vote. I'd like it to reach one concrete recommendation with next steps and a review date, rather than just describing the trade-off, and to fairly represent the strongest case for whichever option it doesn't end up choosing. Could you write that memo for me?
