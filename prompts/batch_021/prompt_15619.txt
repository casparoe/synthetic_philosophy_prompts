I keep coming back to a particular move in the long-running dispute between Bradley and Russell over the unity of the proposition. In §54 of the Principles of Mathematics, Russell argues that 'A differs from B' is a genuine unity, not the bare list 'A, difference, B', because a relation has a twofold nature: taken 'in itself' (as when we say 'difference is a relation') it is a mere, inert term, but taken as it occurs 'actually relating' A and B within the proposition, it does the uniting. He leans on essentially the same distinction a few years later against Bradley's regress, insisting that a relation simply does its relating job when it occurs in the second way, so that the regress of further connecting relations never gets started. Please argue that the distinction cannot bear this weight: that calling an occurrence 'actually relating' rather than 'in itself', without saying what makes it one rather than the other, leaves it entirely open which occurrences do the relating and which don't, and so, in effect, concedes Bradley's objection rather than answering it.
