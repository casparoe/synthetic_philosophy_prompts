The question was: "Explain Williamson's anti-luminosity argument, using his 'feeling cold' case, and say precisely what (if anything) it shows about the KK principle -- the thesis that knowing P puts you in a position to know that you know P. Is 'luminosity fails' the same claim as 'KK fails,' or does one follow from the other only as a special case? Defend your answer, illustrating with the feeling-cold case."

And here's the student's answer, word for word:

"Williamson's argument targets 'luminous' conditions: a condition C is luminous just in case, whenever C obtains, the subject is thereby in a position to know that C obtains -- not that she does know, if she isn't even attending to the question, just that she could come to know merely by reflecting. The feeling-cold case is meant to show that almost nothing satisfies this, not even paradigmatically 'inner' states like how cold you feel.

Set up a long sequence of moments, a second apart, running from a moment when you're freezing to a moment, hours later, when you're perfectly warm; the temperature changes by such a tiny amount each second that you genuinely can't feel any difference between adjacent moments. The key premise is a margin-for-error principle: because your only access to how cold you feel is a fallible discriminative capacity that can't tell adjacent moments apart, any method reliable enough to produce knowledge at one moment has to still get things right at the very next moment too, otherwise it could easily have gone wrong, which disqualifies it as knowledge-producing even at the moment it happens to get things right. So: if you know, at moment n, that you feel cold, you must actually still feel cold at moment n+1.

Now suppose for contradiction that feeling cold is luminous. At the first moment you're clearly freezing, so, attending to the question, you know you feel cold. By margin for error you still feel cold one moment later. By luminosity you know that too. Repeat a few million times and you get that you still feel cold at the very last moment, even though by then you're comfortably warm. Contradiction -- so feeling cold isn't luminous.

I think the deeper point is that this is really an argument against the KK principle: the whole apparatus is designed to show that knowing p doesn't guarantee knowing that you know p, and 'feeling cold' is just a vivid everyday dressing for that underlying point. That's also why it matters for epistemic logic, where positive introspection (Kp -> KKp) is often just built in as an axiom -- Williamson is arguing that this isn't a general truth about knowledge, because knowledge, like feeling cold, needs a margin for error that iterated knowledge can't survive at the margins.

The upshot is a real blow to the Cartesian picture of the mind as transparent to itself: we like to think that whatever we believe, feel, or intend, we're automatically able to know that we do, but Williamson shows this can't be right in general. Since even our access to our own minds runs through a fallible discriminative capacity, there's always pressure from the margins, and the conclusion is that we can never really be sure we know our own mental states, only that we're in them."
