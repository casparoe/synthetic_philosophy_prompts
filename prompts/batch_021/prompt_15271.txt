So my partner lives in this ~30-person co-op house (shared kitchen, shared expenses, monthly house meetings, the whole deal), and they've had this recurring fight over whether to let people sublet their rooms on Airbnb while traveling. Everyone got sick of the shouting matches, so one of the more finance-brained housemates pitched a new way to decide: instead of voting on the sublet rule directly, the house first votes on what they actually care about (they landed on a blended score of "average happiness-survey rating" plus "house savings balance," both measured 12 months out), and then they open an internal points market — fake currency that converts into real rent discounts at the end of the year — where anyone in the house can bet on what that blended score will end up being, conditional on subletting being allowed vs. banned. Whichever policy has the higher expected score according to the betting odds automatically becomes the rule, no separate vote on the actual policy needed. My partner is kind of into it, since it feels less personal than yelling at your neighbor, and asked me what I thought, since I'm the one doing a philosophy PhD (I work on stuff adjacent to political epistemology, so people assume I have opinions about this kind of thing now, lol).

Honestly my gut says somethings off about this, or maybe even self-undermining once you sit with it, but I could only get as far as mumbling "Goodhart's Law" before I lost the thread. Is my unease actually justified here, and if so what's the deepest issue with a setup like this — is it more of an implementation/incentive-design problem that a cleverer version of the scheme could iron out, or is there something more structural that dooms this whole "vote on what you want, then bet on how to get it" approach once you're not talking about a huge, liquid market of strangers with no stake in the actual outcome? I'd love help pinning down what's really going on here philosophically, beyond just "well, people might cheat."
