I've been slowly working through the Gongsun Longzi this month, mostly as an excuse to finally read primary sources from the Warring States "School of Names" instead of just knowing them by reputation, and the "White Horse Dialogue" immediately reminded me of Yudkowsky's old "Taboo Your Words" post and the broader Sequences point that most "is X really a Y" disputes — is Pluto really a planet, does a tree falling in an empty forest really make a sound — dissolve completely once you stop fighting over the word and just describe the underlying facts or the anticipated experience directly. Gongsun Long's argument that "a white horse is not a horse" seems to turn entirely on treating "horse" as naming shape alone and "white horse" as naming shape-plus-color, so that whatever satisfies a request for "a horse" (any color at all) won't automatically satisfy a request for "a white horse." That looks to me like exactly the same move as the Pluto case: define two slightly different bundles of criteria, notice they don't perfectly overlap, and then act surprised that one word can't cover both bundles. If you taboo "horse" and just talk about the actual animal standing in the field, the whole puzzle should evaporate the same way the Pluto dispute evaporates once everyone agrees on the orbital mechanics and stops fighting over the label.

Given that this looks like a Warring States-era instance of an entirely dissolvable definitional dispute, why do historians of Chinese philosophy still treat the White Horse Dialogue as a genuine milestone, with real disagreement (if I'm remembering the secondary literature right, something like Chad Hansen against A. C. Graham) over whether it anticipates the intension/extension distinction, reveals something specific about how classical Chinese common nouns function semantically, or is just a fallacy of equivocation dressed up as an argument — rather than filing it away, the way we'd file the Pluto dispute, as a cute but fully resolved case of two people talking past each other?
