Question: In the Seventeenth Discussion of the Tahāfut al-Falāsifa, al-Ghazālī denies that fire is the true agent responsible for the burning of cotton. (a) Reconstruct his argument against the philosophers' account of natural causation, including the role played by the notion of "custom" (al-ʿāda). (b) Explain what is theologically at stake in the argument for al-Ghazālī. (c) Compare his position to Hume's and to Malebranche's treatments of causation, noting genuine similarities as well as differences.

Student answer: Al-Ghazālī's target in the Seventeenth Discussion is the philosophers' claim that when fire is brought into contact with cotton, the fire itself necessarily produces the burning. Against this, he points out that experience only ever shows us that burning occurs when contact occurs, never any necessary tie between the two; what we take for a causal power is really just constant conjunction. He pushes the point further by arguing that fire, being inanimate, lacks the will that genuine agency requires, so it cannot be a true agent at all; the real agent behind the burning is God, acting either directly or through the angels entrusted with such matters. The apparent lawlikeness of nature is, on this view, simply God's "custom" (al-ʿāda): He regularly creates burning to accompany contact but is free to withhold it, which is precisely what allows for miracles such as a righteous person emerging unharmed from fire. The theological stakes are high, since if fire burned by an inherent necessity, God could not suspend that necessity, and the miracles reported in scripture would be not merely rare but strictly impossible; denying necessary connections is what keeps such events open as possibilities and keeps God's power unconditioned by the natures of things. This is essentially the same insight Hume reaches: he too denies that experience reveals any necessary connection between cause and effect and traces the impression of necessity to habit rather than to the objects themselves, so that al-Ghazālī and Hume converge, five centuries apart, on the same underlying account of why we mistake regular succession for necessary causation. His occasionalism is likewise of a piece with the doctrine Malebranche later defends: both thinkers deny that any creature has genuine causal efficacy and reserve true agency for God alone, having each been led to that conclusion by the same difficulty of explaining how a power in one finite thing could reach across to determine what happens in another, whether the things in question are fire and cotton or mind and body. Averroes' rejoinder in the Tahāfut al-Tahāfut, that this view leaves nature without stable essences for science to grasp, remains the classic objection to al-Ghazālī's position.
