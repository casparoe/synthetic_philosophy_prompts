For my elective on Jewish philosophy, I've been puzzling over what looks like a genuine inconsistency in Maimonides, and I would like help constructing the strongest possible case for it before I write my term paper. In the Guide for the Perplexed (III:32), he argues that the sacrificial cult has no inherent value whatsoever: because every ancient nation worshipped through animal sacrifice, God could not simply have commanded a purely spiritual, sacrifice-free service for the newly freed Israelites, so the Torah redirected that existing practice toward Him instead of abolishing it outright, much as a skilled physician breaks a patient of a harmful habit through gradual stages rather than all at once. Yet in the closing chapters of the Mishneh Torah (Hilchot Melachim, chapters 11-12) - writing there in his voice as legal codifier rather than as philosophical exegete - Maimonides describes the Davidic king as rebuilding the Temple and fully reinstating that very same sacrificial order once the messianic age arrives, rather than retiring it, as the logic of gradual weaning would seem to predict. Please argue that these two texts are genuinely, and not merely apparently, in tension, and that this inconsistency exposes a real problem with the concession theory of sacrifice itself, rather than being an incidental wrinkle that a sympathetic reader could iron out. Engage seriously with the strongest harmonizing reply available before you dismantle it.
