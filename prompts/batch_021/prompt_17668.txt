I had a long argument with a friend last night about whether Daniel Bernoulli's resolution of the St. Petersburg paradox is genuinely an early statement of expected-utility theory, or whether something gets lost when people compress it into "just use a concave utility function." Trying to settle it, we dug up Isaac Todhunter's 1865 "History of the Mathematical Theory of Probability" and its summary of Bernoulli's original 1738 memoir (Arts. 379–380, in the chapter on Daniel Bernoulli), and got stuck on this passage:

Suppose a person to possess a sum of money x, then if it receive an increment dx, Daniel Bernoulli estimates the relative value of the increment as proportional to dx directly and x inversely; that is, he takes it equal to k dx/x, where k is some constant. Put this equal to dy; so that dy = k dx/x; therefore y = k log x + constant = k log (x/a), say. Laplace calls x the fortune physique and y the fortune morale. We must suppose a some positive quantity, for as Daniel Bernoulli remarks, no man is absolutely destitute unless he is dying of hunger. Daniel Bernoulli calls y the emolumentum, a he calls summa bonorum, and x − a he calls lucrum.

Suppose then that a person, starting with a for his fortune physique, has the chance p1 of gaining x1, the chance p2 of gaining x2, the chance p3 of gaining x3, and so on; and suppose the sum of these chances to be unity. Let

Y = k p1 log (a + x1) + k p2 log (a + x2) + k p3 log (a + x3) + ... − k log a.

Then Bernoulli calls Y the emolumentum medium, and Laplace still calls Y the fortune morale. Let X denote the fortune physique which corresponds to this fortune morale; then Y = k log X − k log a. Thus X = (a + x1)^p1 (a + x2)^p2 (a + x3)^p3 ... And X − a will be, according to Laplace, l'accroissement de la fortune physique qui procurerait à l'individu le même avantage moral qui résulte pour lui, de son expectative. Daniel Bernoulli calls X − a the lucrum legitime expectandum, seu sors quaesita.

My friend's reading is that once Bernoulli has Y, introducing X is mere relabelling: the real work of taming the paradox happens inside the logarithm, and translating back into X is just a cosmetic flourish. I think X is doing something Y can't do on its own, but I couldn't make the case precisely. Could you explain what X actually represents, why Bernoulli bothers converting the "moral" quantity Y back into the "physical" quantity X instead of stopping at Y, and what work the throwaway-looking remark about a needing to be positive is quietly doing in the argument?
