I'm about two-thirds through a novel about the slow-motion breakup of a small, well-regarded family publishing house that two adult siblings inherited from their mother, and I've hit a scene I can't fake convincingly. The siblings can't agree on how to split things up (one wants out with cash, the other wants to keep the imprint alive), so per the terms of the mother's will they're stuck bringing in a neutral arbitrator who has to write up an actual recommendation for how to divide what's left. While digging around for realistic material on how mediators handle exactly this kind of standoff, I fell down a rabbit hole into "bargaining solutions" in game theory (I took exactly one economics class in college and it was not this), and kept running into this contrast between the Nash bargaining solution and something called the Kalai-Smorodinsky solution, which apparently disagree about what counts as fair in situations shaped just like my siblings': Priya, who wants out, has a good job waiting for her no matter what happens here, while Theo, who wants to stay, has nothing else lined up but is also the only one who actually knows how to run the place, so the whole thing is worth more in a world where he ends up in charge than one where she does. I got lost somewhere around "monotonicity" and "the ideal point" and gave up trying to teach myself the math, but it feels like too good a wrinkle to leave out.

Could you draft the actual memo my arbitrator character would send to both siblings' lawyers? I'd like it to lay out, in plain enough language that a non-technical reader could follow the scene, what really distinguishes the Nash and Kalai-Smorodinsky solutions and why they can point to different splits in a case as lopsided as this one, then have her actually commit to one (or own up to splitting the difference some other way, if that's the more honest call) and defend it on the merits rather than just procedurally. And because whichever sibling looses that call is going to push back hard through their lawyer, I want the memo to already take their best objection seriously instead of waving it off -- if the strongest objection actually exposes a real weakness in whatever solution she lands on, the memo shouldn't pretend otherwise.
