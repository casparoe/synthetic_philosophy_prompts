My friend and I spent a long evening arguing over Maimonides' account of animal sacrifice in the Guide for the Perplexed. As I understand him, Maimonides holds that the sacrificial cult was never something God desired in itself: it was a concession to the idolatrous worship the Israelites had grown up with in Egypt, which could not be abolished outright, so it was redirected toward the one God and hedged about with restrictions (a single sanctioned site, a dedicated priestly line) instead of eliminated. My friend found this deflationary and pressed Nahmanides' objection: sacrifice long predates any distinctly Israelite exposure to idolatry — Cain and Abel, Noah, and the patriarchs all sacrifice before Egypt enters the story — and its minutiae (the blood, the burning, the specific portions) seem too deliberately symbolic to be mere weaning-off strategy, so Nahmanides reads the rite instead as a vivid enactment of the sinner's own deserved fate, meant to provoke repentance. Compare these two accounts of why the Torah commands animal sacrifice, and say which one offers the more defensible explanation of why the sacrificial laws take the specific form they do. Defend your answer.
