I'm a junior, and I've been in my school's philosophy club since freshman year. A couple of weeks ago the father of one of our members, who's a retired actuary, came in to run a session on rational choice theory, and he spent most of it on the classic "money pump" argument: the idea that if your preferences over a set of options are cyclic instead of transitive, and you're willing to pay a little to trade up at each step, someone could walk you around the cycle indefinitely and take your money while you end up exactly where you started. The example that actually got people arguing afterward was real: our friend Maya had three options for spring break, each with a deposit and a genuine cancellation fee attached — a community-service trip through a local nonprofit, a ski trip with her cousins, and a weekend portfolio-intensive at an art school for her college applications. She started out committed to the portfolio program, switched to the ski trip once it sounded more fun, switched again to the service trip when a friend pointed out it would look better on applications, and switched back to the portfolio program about a week later when the deadline suddenly felt closer than she'd realized. Three switches, three cancellation fees, and she landed back exactly where she'd started, out around $140.

Two people in the club, Derek and Naomi, argued about this for days afterward. Derek's position was that Maya's spring break is a real-life version of the lecture's whole point: her preferences went in a circle, she acted on whichever one she held at the time, and that pattern is exactly what makes cyclic preferences a genuine failure of rationality rather than a harmless quirk, regardless of how much she actually cared about any of the three options. Naomi said that gets the diagnosis wrong: the fees only got taken because Maya reopened the question every time a new option appeared; if she had picked one plan at the very start and simply refused to reconsider no matter what came up later, she'd have paid nothing at all, while her underlying preferences stayed exactly as cyclic as before — so the real problem, on her view, is the habit of re-deciding, not the shape of the preferences themselves. Derek's comeback was that this doesn't solve anything, it just relocates it: if Maya's preference for the ski trip over the portfolio program really was her preference in the moment, then a version of her who sticks with the portfolio program anyway is choosing against what she actually wants right then, which is an odd thing to call rational — and if it wasn't a genuine preference, he wants to know what a "preference" is even supposed to mean if it's never allowed to guide anything. Naomi's answer was that this only looks strange if you assume the same standard has to apply both to a single moment and to a whole plan stretched out over time; on her view, the money-pump story only ever demonstrates something about the second case — don't let yourself be led in a costly circle — and a person who commits to a plan and follows through satisfies that without their moment-to-moment preferences ever needing to line up into a consistent order. Treating those two standards as one and the same, she said, is exactly the assumption the lecture's argument needs and never actually defends. Derek's last word was that this proves too much: if simply refusing to reconsider always gets an agent off the hook, then no cyclic set of preferences could ever count as irrational, so long as the agent is also stubborn enough about ignoring them, and that can't be the moral of a story about spring break deposits.

Nobody in the club could agree on a winner, and I don't have a strong opinion myself, since each move seems to land at least partly. Could you go through the exchange step by step — Derek's opening diagnosis, Naomi's point about the habit of re-deciding versus the preferences themselves, Derek's objection about what a preference even is if it doesn't guide the choice, and Naomi's distinction between the two standards of rationality — and say who has the stronger position at each stage and why, rather than just declaring an overall winner? I'd also like to know whether there's some further move or distinction that neither of them thought to raise, one that would actually push the disagreement somewhere new rather than just sending it around for another round.
