"Daniels borrows Christopher Boorse's biostatistical theory of disease, on which health means functioning within the species-typical range for one's age and sex, and disease is a statistical shortfall relative to that reference class. His key move is to connect this natural baseline to Rawls's original principle of fair equality of opportunity, which Rawls had already extended to health care in A Theory of Justice: since disease and impairment narrow the range of life plans realistically open to a person, treatments that restore normal functioning are matters of justice, protecting a person's fair share of the normal opportunity range, while enhancements that push someone above the species-typical range aren't owed as a matter of justice. Basically, the theory says nature sets the correct standard and medicine's job is to bring people back to it, not past it. Idiopathic short stature is a good test case: since the child isn't diseased in Boorse's sense (there's no dysfunction, just a low-but-non-pathological height), prescribing growth hormone looks like enhancement by Daniels' own definition, yet insurers and doctors often treat it as legitimate therapy anyway. This shows that in practice the line is far blurrier than the theory suggests, since psychosocial disadvantage from extreme shortness can matter for opportunity even without any underlying disease."
