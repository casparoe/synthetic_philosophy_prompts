For my History of Educational Thought seminar we just spent two weeks on Whitehead, and everyone has to bring a short "provocation" connecting him to something going on right now for Thursday's discussion. I got assigned the essay "Universities and Their Function" from The Aims of Education, where his big claim is basically that what justifies a university existing at all -- as opposed to just a really good secondary school, or a stack of textbooks you could read on your own -- is that teaching there stays fused to live research. Students pick up something like imagination or "zest" off scholars who are still actively finding things out, and the second that fusion breaks, the institution has kind of lost its reason for being.

What I can't stop thinking about is how much undergrad teaching in the US now runs on exactly the disconnection he was warning about: intro courses taught mostly by adjuncts and grad students with no research line of their own and often no realistic path to one, whole degree programs outsourced to asynchronous online platforms built around lecture videos recorded once and reused for years, and now, on top of all that, students (myself included, not gonna lie) leaning on AI chatbots as an on-demand tutor for stuff office hours used to cover -- something with zero research life and zero "zest" behind it, just very good pattern-completion over old material.

Could you write up what you think Whitehead would actually say about all this, working as much as you can from his own concepts (inert ideas, the rhythm of education, the research/teaching fusion, "style," whichever fit) instead of a generic "he'd be horrified by AI" take?
