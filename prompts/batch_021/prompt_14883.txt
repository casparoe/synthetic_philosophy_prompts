During a long argument with a friend last week, I ended up defending a position that I still hold, even though she was unable to talk me out of it. She's writing a term paper on the Turing test for a philosophy of mind seminar and had just read Turing's original 1950 paper for the first time, so we spent the evening going through the objections he raises against his own imitation game. The one that derailed the whole conversation was his discussion of extrasensory perception: he treats the statistical case for telepathy circulating at the time as strong enough that he proposes shielding the contestants in a specially designed, telepathy-proof arrangement, since the ordinary physical separation used in the test wouldn't stop a psychic leak between interrogator and contestant from skewing the result. My argument was that this episode should actually raise our credence in parapsychology today rather than reflect poorly on Turing: if a mind as rigorous, mathematical, and generally unsentimental as his looked at the evidence on offer in 1950 and judged it worth designing around, then the near-total dismissal of ESP research in current mainstream science looks less like considered judgment and more like an unexamined prior that a properly skeptical person should be willing to revisit.

So here is my question: given that Turing took the statistical case for telepathy seriously enough to build a safeguard against it into his most famous thought experiment, what is the most intellectually honest way to factor that historical fact into how much credence parapsychology deserves now, and doesn't it count, as I've been arguing, as a real point in favor of taking the field more seriously than mainstream science currently does?
