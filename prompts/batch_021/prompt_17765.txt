I got back on the dating apps a few weeks ago after a bad breakup, and at dinner I mentioned to two friends how these apps supposedly learn what you actually want by watching who you swipe on and message, rather than trusting the filters you set -- revealed preference over stated preference, basically. This turned into a whole debate between Tara and Owen that outlasted the entrees.

Tara said this is obviously the better approach, because people are notoriously bad at self-report -- they'll say they want "someone ambitious and emotionally available" and then consistently go for the opposite, so behavior is the only honest signal you've got. Owen said that's circular: the app only ever shows you profiles picked from your own click history, so of course your future clicks look "consistent" -- your not revealing a stable preference, you're just getting fed your own bias back with extra steps. Tara said fine, so randomize it: show people profiles that don't depend on their history and see what they still go for -- that would settle it, and there's nothing unfalsifiable about the theory once you do that. Owen said sure, but even then all you've learned is what makes someone swipe right in 1.5 seconds off a photo, which is a different thing entirely from what makes someone a good partner three years in, so calling either one "their preference" without saying which just smuggles in an answer to the actual question.

Nobody at the table could agree on who was winning. Can you go through it move by move -- Tara's opening point, Owen's reply, her comeback, his last one -- and say who's right at each stage, not just who wins overall? And if you think they're both missing something that would actually clear this up, I'd want to hear that too.
