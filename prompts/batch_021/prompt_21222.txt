I'm a retired experimental physicist (spent about thirty-five years doing condensed matter work, of all things, so general relativity was never really my day job) and since I retired a few years back I've been filling the extra hours with philosophy, which I never had time for before. My niece is finishing up a philosophy PhD and keeps sending me papers to "keep my brain limber," and last month it was something on Bayesian confirmation theory that's been bugging me ever since: Clark Glymour's "problem of old evidence."

As I understand it, the puzzle goes like this. In standard Bayesian confirmation theory, evidence E is said to confirm hypothesis H when learning E raises your credence in H, i.e. P(H|E) > P(H). Fine so far. But by Bayes' theorem, that gap depends on E actually being uncertain beforehand — and if E was already fully established, if your credence in E was sitting at 1 long before H was even proposed, then P(H|E) just collapses to P(H) no matter what H predicts. The textbook case is Mercury's perihelion precession: that anomaly had been sitting on the books for over half a century before Einstein derived it from general relativity in 1915, so by Glymour's math it literally cannot have confirmed GR, even though every physics student (me included, back in the day) gets taught that it's one of the strongest pieces of evidence we have for the theory.

This has gotten weirdly personal for me because I've been arguing by email with an old colleague who's turned into a real MOND evangelist, and half his pitch for MOND over dark matter is that it fits the galaxy rotation curve data beautifully — data that was already sitting there years before Milgrom proposed MOND in the 80s. Given Glymour's result, it seems like the Bayesian math has basically caught scientists (all of us, really) in a giant self-deception: we FEEL confirmed by old data that a theory explains after the fact, but that feeling is apparently unearned, its really just a bias, kind of in the same family as what the Ellsberg paradox is supposed to show about ambiguity aversion. So, assuming I'm reading this right, what's the principled way to correct for it? Should I tell my colleague to throw out the rotation-curve argument entirely and only count MOND's genuinely novel, in-advance predictions, and should I go back and mentally downgrade Mercury's perihelion to "not actually evidence" for GR too?
