So I've been TAing a Victorian ethics seminar this term (I'm a phil PhD student, mostly working on 19th century moral philosophy) and I went down a rabbit hole on Sidgwick that I can't shake. Everyone reads him for the Methods of Ethics and treats him as basically the last really careful classical utilitarian, but almost nobody mentions that for like the last two decades of his life he was also co-founding and running the Society for Psychical Research -- personally sitting in on seances, designing telepathy experiments, chasing down haunted houses, the whole deal -- and by his own admission never found anything he'd call solid proof of survival after death. What makes this weirder to me is that this is the same guy who resigned his Trinity fellowship back in 1869 because he couldn't honestly keep affirming the religious tests anymore, so he clearly wasn't just a credulous gentleman with a hobby. And if you go back to how the Methods of Ethics actually ends, with that whole unresolved "dualism of practical reason" business -- he can't get a purely rational proof that duty and self-interest ultimately line up, and says that without some further guarantee (traditionally God plus an afterlife) the whole "cosmos" of ethics just collapses into "chaos," or some phrase real close to that, I'm paraphrasing from memory -- it starts to look like the ghost-hunting wasn't a weird side hobby at all but him trying to finish the ethics project by empirical means, since he'd already ruled out just helping himself to the theological answer on faith.

Can you walk through what you think are the most important implications of this for two things: first, how seriously we should take the dualism of practical reason as a genuinely open problem in Sidgwick's system, given that his own multi-decade empirical bet to close it came back basically empty; and second, a more personal version of the question -- for someone today (me, and honestly most of my EA-adjacent friends) who takes impartial utilitarian-ish demands seriously but definitly doesn't believe in personal survival, what does Sidgwick's failed rescue attempt actually imply about whether that kind of impartial altruism is rationally required of us, versus just one live option among others? I'm less interested in the generic self-interest-vs-morality debate than in what specifically follows from the Sidgwick case -- like, did he basically prove his own theory needs a metaphysical postulate it can't have, or is there a good reading of the whole episode that leaves classical utilitarianism in better shape than that makes it sound?
