Here is the question as I posed it:

Consider Bostrom's "Presumptuous Philosopher" case: two physical theories, T1 and T2, are equally well supported by all the evidence gathered so far and were equally probable a priori, except that T1 entails a universe containing a trillion times as many observers as T2 does. Physicists are preparing a decisive experiment to distinguish the theories.

(a) Explain why the Self-Indication Assumption (SIA) implies that the philosopher can already declare T1 a trillion times more probable than T2, before the experiment is run.
(b) Explain why the Self-Sampling Assumption (SSA), on its own, does not license the same move.
(c) State the Doomsday Argument, explain why it is standardly derived from SSA rather than SIA, and explain what becomes of the Doomsday shift for a reasoner who accepts both SSA and SIA together.
(d) In Anthropic Bias, Bostrom comes down in favor of a (reference-class-restricted) version of one of these two principles and against the other. Say which, and briefly assess whether the way he treats the two principles' respective "costs" is well-motivated.

And here is the answer I received:

(a) SIA tells us to weight hypotheses not just by their prior probability but by how many observers they predict, because finding yourself existing at all is more likely on a hypothesis where there are more observers to be. Concretely: if T1 predicts a trillion times more observers than T2, and the two theories were equally likely before taking our own existence into account, then after conditioning on "I exist" the likelihood ratio in favor of T1 is a trillion to one, since under T1 there are a trillion times as many "observer slots" for me to have turned out to occupy. That swamps anything a physics experiment could tell us, which is exactly why the philosopher in Bostrom's example thinks he can skip the experiment.

(b) SSA doesn't have this feature, because it only asks you to imagine yourself as randomly drawn from the reference class of observers who actually exist given a particular theory, not to compare how many observers exist across theories. Under SSA, T1 having more observers overall doesn't automatically make it more probable, because your evidence is "I am this observer, with this rank and these characteristics," and that evidence isn't automatically more expected under T1 just because T1's population happens to be bigger — you'd need some further reason (e.g., that your specific characteristics are typical under T1 and atypical under T2) to get a shift. So the philosopher's trillion-to-one move simply doesn't go through for a pure SSA-reasoner.

(c) The Doomsday Argument runs the same kind of self-locating reasoning in the other direction. If I treat myself as a random sample from the reference class of all humans who will ever live, then my birth rank is far less surprising if the total number of humans who will ever live, N, is small than if N is astronomically large: under SSA, P(my rank | N) = 1/N, so small-N hypotheses ("Doom Soon") get a likelihood boost over large-N hypotheses ("Doom Late") purely from my rank, no facts about actual extinction risk required. That's structurally the same move as the Presumptuous Philosopher, just run on population size instead of universe size, which is why critics say SSA buys its immunity to the Presumptuous Philosopher at the cost of an equally "free lunch" conclusion elsewhere. Because SIA is motivated as a principle about whether you exist at all, while the Doomsday Argument is a claim about when you exist given that you already do, adding SIA on top of SSA doesn't touch the argument: the doomsday update survives fully intact even for a reasoner who accepts both principles together. If anything, this is a point in SSA's favor: unlike the Presumptuous Philosopher, which you can only avoid by rejecting SIA outright, the Doomsday Argument is a freestanding commitment of SSA that doesn't get quietly patched over just by also accepting the rival principle.

(d) Bostrom's own verdict in Anthropic Bias is to keep a version of SSA, with the reference class restricted to observers subjectively indistinguishable from oneself, and to reject SIA outright. His reasoning is that SIA isn't just unpalatable but actually incoherent as a probabilistic principle: because it weights possible worlds by the number of observers they contain rather than treating "I exist" as ordinary conditioning evidence, it ends up double-counting the evidence and violates the probability calculus in a way the Presumptuous Philosopher case merely makes vivid. I think this is basically the right call: a principle that can be shown to be formally inconsistent should be dropped regardless of how well it performs in any particular thought experiment, so the asymmetry in how Bostrom treats the two costs — living with the Doomsday Argument, refusing SIA outright — is well-motivated rather than a matter of taste.
