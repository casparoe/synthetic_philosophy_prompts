A friend of mine, who prides himself on following an argument wherever it leads, made a claim at dinner last week that I have not been able to shake. I mentioned I was still weighing a job offer that would require relocating within the month, and that I wanted to think it over carefully before deciding anything. He replied that the sentence "you will take the new job" is, right now, either true or it is false -- not neither, and not both, since that is simply how declarative sentences work. But, he went on, if it is already true today which one it is (or already false, whichever the case may be), then the matter has already been settled, and nothing I do while weighing the offer next week can be what actually settles it, since things were already going to go one way rather than the other as soon as the sentence had a truth value at all -- which, on his view, it always has, right back to before either of us was born. He was happy to extend this to any future event whatsoever: whether it rains during my sister's outdoor wedding in September, whether a given patient recovers, who wins next year's election. In every one of these cases, he says, it is already true or already false how things will go, so the future is exactly as fixed as the past, and what we call deliberating, planning, or trying is merely what an already-fixed outcome feels like from the inside.

I could not immediately locate the flaw in this, but it still strikes me as absurd that so mundane a fact as sentences being true or false should carry this kind of consequence. Was my friend right, and if not, precisely where does his reasoning break down? I would also like to know whether, and how, the answer to that should change how seriously I take my own deliberation over the job offer -- or whether, as he insists, fretting over it really is a waste of time.
