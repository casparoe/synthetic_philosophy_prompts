For my high school's "Introduction to Philosophy" elective, we just wrapped up a unit on epistemology that covered two rival explanations for why we seem more willing to say someone "knows" something when little rides on it than when a lot does. Epistemic contextualism holds that the word "knows" itself picks out a stricter or looser standard depending on the conversation in which it is used, so that the very same person in the very same evidential position can be truly described as knowing by someone in a low-stakes conversation and truly described as not knowing by someone in a high-stakes one. Pragmatic encroachment (sometimes called subject-sensitive invariantism) instead holds that the standard for knowledge itself never budges, but what it takes to meet that standard depends on what is actually at stake for the person whose knowledge is being assessed, no matter who is talking about them or how casually. These looked easy enough to tell apart in the abstract, until our teacher gave us a case designed to pull them apart, and it turned into an argument between two classmates, Malik and Sofia, that the rest of us just ended up watching.

The case: Naomi has a mild shellfish allergy that has never given her more than an upset stomach, and at a potluck she eats a dish whose index card reads "shrimp-free," reasoning from the card together with the fact that the same dish never bothered her at past potlucks; confident, she tells a friend, "I know this one's fine," and eats it without hesitation. Nobody at the potluck knows, including Naomi, that she had started a new medication that week which can turn even a mild allergic reaction into a medical emergency, so far more was actually riding on her being right this time than on any ordinary occasion, even though her evidence for "shrimp-free" was exactly as strong as always. As it happens, the dish really was shrimp-free and nothing went wrong, but when Naomi mentioned the new medication to a couple of classmates the following week, they realized in retrospect just how much had been riding on that meal, and got into an argument at lunch about whether she had "known" the dish was safe when she ate it. (1) Malik said she hadn't: if Naomi had somehow found out about the medication beforehand, everyone would agree she should have gone to double-check with whoever made the dish rather than resting on a card and a track record, and what is good enough to act on, he said, shouldn't depend on whether she happened to find out something that was true regardless. (2) Sofia said that's beside the point, since whether it is true to call Naomi a knower depends on the standards in play in whatever conversation is actually happening about her, and since neither she nor Malik has anything on the line while chatting at lunch, ordinary standards apply, under which a card plus a track record is plenty. She added that it would be strange if the very same evidence made Naomi a knower or not depending on a fact that wasn't on anyone's radar, least of all Naomi's, at the moment she made the claim. (3) Malik replied that this can't be right: on Sofia's view, two people gossiping safely in the cafeteria would have been just as correct to call Naomi a knower even if the dish had, in fact, sent her into anaphylactic shock a minute later, and that is exactly the kind of claim we should want to take back rather than stand behind, once we see how easily it could have gone the other way. (4) Sofia granted that the claim sounds bad in hindsight, but said the same is true of plenty of innocent, true knowledge claims that turn out badly through sheer luck, like someone truly saying "I know my tire is fine" right before a freak blowout, and accused Malik of conflating "this will look regrettable later" with "this was false when it was said." (5) Malik answered that the two cases aren't parallel, because the tire's failure happens after the claim is made, whereas the medication interaction that raised the stakes was already true at the very moment Naomi said "I know" — so unlike the tire, there is no new fact appearing afterward, only a stake that was already in place and simply wasn't being tracked by Sofia's lunchroom standard.

Neither of them budged, and I've gone back and forth myself without landing anywhere. Could you go through the exchange move by move, (1) through (5), and say who has the better of it at each step rather than just declaring an overall winner? I'd also be curious whether, on balance, this particular case actually manages to favor pragmatic encroachment over contextualism, or vice versa, and whether there is a consideration bearing on the two views that neither Malik nor Sofia thought to raise.
