And here's what my classmate handed in:

"Under moral uncertainty we don't know which ethical theory is correct, but we can still have credences (like probabilities) spread across different theories — say 40% utilitarian, 35% Kantian, 25% virtue ethics. The 'maximize expected choiceworthiness' view says we should treat this exactly like decision-making under empirical uncertainty: each theory tells you how choiceworthy each option is, you weight that by your credence in the theory, add it up across all the theories, and pick the option with the highest total. It's basically expected utility theory except the 'states of the world' are moral theories instead of facts about the world.

The problem is that most theories don't hand you a number. A utilitarian can say option A produces 40 utils and option B produces 10, but a Kantian theory might only say that lying is forbidden, full stop, without telling you how forbidden it is compared to some other violation. So when you average across theories you're forced to invent an exchange rate between utils and 'wrongness', and there's no principled way to do that — any answer you pick is arbitrary, which makes the whole expected value calculation kind of made up.

The moral parliament model fixes this by giving each theory a number of delegates proportional to your credence in it. Then, just like with MEC, each delegate reports how much value they assign to each option, weighted by the size of their delegation, and whichever option gets the highest weighted score wins the vote. Since its still just the same credence-weighted-average procedure underneath, the parliament model ends up recommending exactly the same actions as MEC would, just dressed up in voting language instead of decision-theory language — so I'd say its a nice intuition pump but it doesn't actually solve the comparison problem, it just hides it."
