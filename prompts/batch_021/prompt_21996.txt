(1) Dana opened by saying that, now that the loophole-free experiments have been done, Bell's theorem settles the matter: the universe really is nonlocal, and entangled particles genuinely affect one another instantaneously, at any distance. (2) Marcus pushed back that this overstates the result: Bell's theorem only rules out local realism, and you can keep locality if you're willing to give up realism instead -- on something like the Copenhagen view, a particle has no definite property before it's measured, so there's nothing sitting there that would need to be influenced from a distance. (3) Dana answered that this doesn't actually get you off the hook: the correlations are far too exact to be coincidence, hidden variables or not, and declining to say the particles 'have' properties beforehand doesn't make the pattern any less in need of explaining. (4) Marcus then brought up the no-signaling theorem: whatever produces the correlation, you can't use it to send a message faster than light, since each side's own results look completely random on their own, no matter what's done on the other end -- so relativity isn't actually threatened. (5) Dana replied that this conflates two different questions -- whether you can exploit an effect to communicate, and whether the effect is there at all -- and added that Bell himself, late in life, said he found explicitly nonlocal theories like Bohm's more honest about what's going on than interpretations that dodge the question, and was never won over by Everett's many-worlds alternative. (6) Marcus's closing point was that Bell's personal preference isn't part of the theorem: what you're forced to give up -- locality, realism, or the assumption that measurement settings are chosen 'freely,' independent of whatever the hidden variables are -- is a choice among interpretations, not something the mathematics itself decides.
