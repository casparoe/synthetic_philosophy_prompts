I'm several chapters into a novel set in and around Geneva in the years just after Rousseau's *Lettre à d'Alembert sur les spectacles* touched off a pamphlet war over whether the city should permit a public theatre, and I want one of my characters — a bookish young woman caught between the pro- and anti-theatre factions — to actually reckon with Rousseau's argument rather than just gesture at it. As I understand it, his case isn't simply that plays are frivolous: he argues that actors are trained to counterfeit passions they don't feel, that spectators get to discharge their compassion safely on invented suffering instead of on the neighbors who actually need it, and that the whole institution depends on a kind of sanctioned collective dishonesty he contrasts with the transparency of a Genevan civic festival, where there's no fixed line between who performs and who watches.

What I can't get my character to say convincingly is why any of this doesn't apply just as much to Rousseau himself, who within a few years was the author of *Julie, ou la Nouvelle Héloïse* — an epistolary novel expressly engineered to make its readers weep over people who never existed — and later of a *Confessions* that is arguably its own performance of sincerity. I put this to some novelist friends over dinner, and the consensus was just "well, he was a hypocrite," which might be true but isn't a reading of the actual argument, and I'd rather not hand my heroine a cheap shot. Did Rousseau ever offer a real, non-question-begging basis for exempting the novel (or the private letter, or autobiography) from the objections he presses against the stage — something turning on solitary reading versus collective spectatorship, say, or on the presence of real bodies performing before a crowd — and if so, does that distinction actually hold up, or does it quietly smuggle back in everything he claimed to rule out? I'd like an explanation that works through the structure of his own position rather than one that just lands on the verdict that he was inconsistent.
