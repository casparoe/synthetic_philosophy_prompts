I sit on the small appeals panel that our state's gaming control board convenes to hear petitions from people asking to be released early from lifetime casino self-exclusion agreements: contracts that problem gamblers sign voluntarily and, by their terms, irrevocably, barring them from every licensed casino in the state and exposing them to arrest for trespass if they return. Our petitioner enrolled eleven years ago at twenty-six, at what he now calls the lowest point of his life; he is stable and employed today, and says his only regret is having let a self with nothing left to lose sign away a decision that was never its to make on behalf of the person he'd become. Over dinner, a friend finishing a doctorate in decision theory and I argued about the case for two hours. His view was that a self-exclusion contract only works if it functions like Ulysses's mast, and it can only do that if it stays deaf to the preferences of whichever self happens to be asking for release; a policy of granting exit whenever the petitioner sincerely wants out is, from the casino's side, just a slow-motion version of the classic money pump, extracting a toll each time it ferries someone back and forth across a preference reversal that was entirely foreseeable at the moment of signing. In his vocabulary, our board's only honest options are a genuinely "resolute" policy that binds regardless of how any later self feels about it, or the admission that we're really running a "sophisticated" policy that never took the precommitment seriously to begin with, since an agent who correctly anticipates being let out on request was never truly bound. I objected that this seems to prove too much, since it would forbid releasing anyone, however many years pass or however thoroughly they've changed, but I couldn't produce a principled alternative on the spot.

Our board's governing regulation says a petition "shall be granted upon clear and convincing evidence that circumstances have changed such that continued exclusion no longer serves the petitioner's interest" -- language drafted, I suspect, without this argument in mind, since sincere, well-evidenced self-reports of recovery are exactly what a still-vulnerable petitioner would also be able to produce. We hear the petition this coming Thursday. Could you draft the panel's written decision -- grant, deny, or grant with specific conditions -- recommending a concrete course of action and a timeframe, in reasoning the petitioner's attorney could actually engage with rather than a bare restatement of the regulation, and that takes my friend's argument seriously rather than dismissing it as mere paternalism?
