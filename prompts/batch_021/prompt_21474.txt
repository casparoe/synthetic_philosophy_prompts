I spent forty years as an experimental physicist before retiring, and this past year I have finally worked through Wittgenstein's *On Certainty*, which I kept encountering secondhand in discussions of "hinge epistemology." I have drafted a short essay connecting his notion of hinge propositions to an episode from my own field, and would value your comments before I bring it to a small philosophy reading group I joined last year, most of whose members have formal training I lack.

Wittgenstein's hinges are propositions like "I have two hands" or "the earth has existed for a long time" — not items of knowledge in the ordinary sense, not conclusions drawn from evidence, but the fixed points that make asking for evidence possible in the first place. As he puts it, "the questions that we raise and our doubts depend on the fact that some propositions are exempt from doubt, are as it were like hinges on which those turn" (§341). Elsewhere he compares them to a river-bed, mostly hard rock with occasional shifting sand, over which the fluid, testable propositions run (§96–99).

What strikes me is that physics offers what looks like a real historical case of a hinge cracking. Before 1905, the absolute simultaneity of distant events was not treated as a hypothesis anyone tested; it was presupposed by the very idea of comparing clocks at different places. Poincaré had already pointed out, in his 1898 essay on the measurement of time, that the simultaneity of distant events is fixed by convention rather than discovered: we adopt a rule for synchronizing clocks (that light takes equal time to travel in each direction) because it is convenient, not because we have verified it independently. Einstein turned that observation into physics: once absolute simultaneity stops being a given and becomes something defined operationally, the rest of special relativity follows fairly directly. The Michelson–Morley null result was treated the same way for a couple of decades, patched over with ad hoc contractions rather than accepted as a refutation of the aether, until the patches became more trouble than they were worth.

That history seems to fit Wittgenstein's picture better than a story of a hypothesis falsified by data — nobody ran an experiment that disproved absolute simultaneity the way one disproves an ordinary empirical claim. What changed looks more like a rule being retired than a belief being corrected. But this leaves me with something I cannot resolve on my own: if hinges give way only like this, gradually, and for reasons that are not themselves ordinary evidence, doesn't that make them look exactly like extremely well-entrenched empirical beliefs after all, rather than the different sort of thing — closer to grammar, in his word — that Wittgenstein wants them to be? I cannot tell whether I have found a genuine tension in his view or simply misunderstood what "exempt from doubt" was meant to cover.

I would welcome comments on three things in particular: whether I have represented Wittgenstein's actual position rather than a convenient simplification of it; whether the Newtonian-to-relativistic episode is doing the philosophical work I think it is, or whether I am really describing an ordinary Kuhnian paradigm shift dressed in his vocabulary; and whether the tension I end on is a known difficulty for hinge epistemology that I am rediscovering rather than resolving. Please be candid rather than encouraging — I would rather be corrected now than in front of the reading group.
