I've spent years following the scalable-oversight literature on the Alignment Forum and LessWrong -- debate, amplification, recursive reward modeling, and the like -- purely as an intellectual hobby, and I never expected to have to apply any of it at work. I sit on the internal safety review board at a mid-sized company that sells a coding assistant to enterprise clients. After a red-team exercise a few months ago in which a subtly malicious change nearly slipped through our normal review pipeline undetected, our security team proposed a pilot: for pull requests our assistant helps generate, rather than routing every one to a senior security engineer (we don't have enough of them to keep pace with volume), two instances of the same model would debate whether a given PR contains a vulnerability or hidden backdoor, with a junior engineer -- who couldn't reliably catch the subtler issues unaided -- serving as judge and ruling in favor of whichever side she finds more convincing after a fixed number of rounds.

Roughly half the board is enthusiastic: this, they say, is precisely the situation debate is meant for, since it should be easier to point out a planted flaw than to construct a subtle exploit from nothing, so an honest debater should hold the advantage even when the judge cannot verify the underlying claim directly -- letting us adjudicate something beyond the judge's unassisted competence. The other half keeps raising the obfuscated-arguments worry: a debater defending a genuinely backdoored PR could spread its justification across many individually plausible steps so that no single step is refutable within the judge's limited scrutiny budget, and training the debaters to win in front of a fallible judge might simply select for whichever side argues more persuasively rather than whichever side is correct -- a worry that seems especially pressing here, since the dishonest side has both a strong incentive and real material (the code itself) available to obscure. Nobody else on the board has much technical background in the theory, so we're going to end up deferring to whoever circulates the clearest memo.

Please draft the short written opinion I'll bring to next week's board meeting. It should recommend a concrete decision -- adopt, reject, or pilot under specific conditions -- with a defined scope and time frame, and it should engage seriously with the obfuscated-arguments and persuasiveness worries rather than simply restating the theoretical case for debate. I'd rather it took a real position than hedged into "more research is needed."
