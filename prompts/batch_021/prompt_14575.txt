My mother spent thirty-four years as an organ procurement coordinator, the person who sits down with a family in the hours after a hospital has pronounced their relative dead and asks whether they would consider donation. Sometime around her twentieth year on the job she became, in her own words, 'the office heretic': she had read enough of the neurology literature on so-called chronic brain death, including case reports of bodies that keep healing wounds and fighting off infections for years after the diagnosis, and of pregnant women maintained on life support long enough to deliver a viable infant months after being declared brain dead, to stop believing that the whole-brain criterion picks out a single, real biological event rather than a useful legal line. What she talked about even more, at our kitchen table, was the other half of her job, the circulatory-death cases, and her insistence that calling the heart's stopping 'irreversible' was usually just a polite word for 'we have collectively decided not to try,' since a crash cart and a few more minutes would have restarted plenty of the hearts that get pronounced dead in that operating room. She thought the difference between a cessation being permanent because everyone present has agreed not to reverse it, and a cessation being irreversible in principle, was one of the few places where staying precise with a grieving family was itself an act of respect rather than cruelty, and she spent decades being gently mocked by colleagues who thought she was overthinking a settled question.

She died two weeks ago, and I have been asked to give her eulogy. The room will be split roughly down the middle: procurement staff, ICU nurses, and transplant surgeons who spent years arguing this exact material with her over coffee, and family and old friends who have never heard the phrase 'dead donor rule' and have no need to learn it now. I don't want to flatten what she actually believed into the kind of comforting certainty about 'the moment she died' that she spent her whole career gently declining to offer other people's families, since that would feel like its own small betrayal of her; but I also cannot deliver eight minutes of transplant-ethics jargon to a room that came to mourn my mother, not to sit through a seminar. Could you write the eulogy for me? I would like it to run about seven or eight minutes read aloud, and if there is a way to build in one moment that rewards the people who know exactly what she meant when she rolled her eyes at the word 'irreversible,' without losing everyone else in the room, I suspect she would have valued that more than anything else we could put in it.
