Over the last several months I have been teaching myself some ancient Stoicism in my spare time -- library books and secondhand anthologies, nothing formal -- and one argument that has stayed with me is the so-called "Lazy Argument" that the Stoics had to answer. As I understand it, the worry was that if everything is fated, then something like calling a doctor when you are ill is pointless: you will recover if you are fated to recover regardless of the doctor, and you will not if you are not, so the visit changes nothing either way. Chrysippus's way of blocking this, from what I have read, was to say that some events are only fated together, or "co-fated" (if I have the term right), so that your recovery, if it comes, is fated to happen precisely by way of the doctor's visit rather than around it -- fate fixes the whole chain leading to the outcome, not just the outcome on its own.

Around the same time, a friend who studied philosophy in college mentioned a decision-theory puzzle called "Death in Damascus" that she said still does not have a settled answer among specialists. The setup, as she described it to me, is that a man learns Death has already chosen which city to be in tomorrow -- Damascus, where he currently is, or Aleppo -- based on a prediction of where the man himself will decide to go, and Death predicts such things essentially without error. The unsettling part is that whichever way the man leans, that very leaning is what gives Death the information it needs, so some decision theorists conclude that the rational move is to flip a coin rather than actually choose, on the idea that randomizing severs the link between his reasoning and Death's prediction -- though even that looked shaky to me once I asked myself whether a predictor good enough to have earned that reputation could not also predict the coin.

It struck me that Chrysippus had already built machinery for exactly this shape of problem: a case where some prior fact -- fate, or here, Death's decision -- seems to rearrange itself around whatever the agent is going to do anyway, in a way that threatens to make ordinary deliberation either pointless or unstable. So I would like your best reconstruction of how Chrysippus, reasoning in the same way he did against the Lazy Argument, would have analyzed Death in Damascus. Would he say that the man's choice and Death's location are co-fated, and if so, does that dissolve the paradox the way it dissolved the doctor case, or does it just move the problem somewhere else? Would that answer effectively side him with the modern "evidential" camp that favors randomizing, with the "causal" camp that does not, or does his framework cut across that whole distinction? And does his notion of co-fatalism have any resources for the further worry about a predictor sophisticated enough to predict the coin flip itself, or is that exactly the point where his machinery runs out?
