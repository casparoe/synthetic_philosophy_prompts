My friend and I got into a long argument about algorithmic risk-assessment tools like COMPAS, and by the end I had more or less come around to my friend's position, though it still nags at me. They walked me through the Kleinberg–Mullainathan–Raghavan impossibility result: whenever two groups have different base rates for the outcome being predicted, no risk score can be simultaneously well-calibrated within each group and have equal false-positive and false-negative rates across groups, except in the degenerate cases where the base rates already match or the score predicts with perfect accuracy. Since that trade-off is a mathematical fact about any imperfect predictor rather than a defect specific to some particular algorithm, my friend concluded that algorithmic risk assessment is irreducibly discriminatory in a way that ordinary judicial discretion simply isn't. Given that, why hasn't every jurisdiction still using these tools switched back to unaided judicial discretion, if the mathematics really does show that the algorithmic approach bakes in discrimination that human decision-making somehow escapes?
