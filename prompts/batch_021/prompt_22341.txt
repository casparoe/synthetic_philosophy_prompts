I've been falling down a rabbit hole this week for a newsletter piece I'm noodling on about something called "ergodicity economics," and I want to run the core argument by you because its either a genuinely huge deal or I'm missing something. The basic setup: you've got a coin that lands heads 51% of the time, and a gambler who can wager any fraction of their bankroll on each flip, double-or-nothing. If you just want to maximize your expected winnings after, say, a thousand flips, the math says bet your entire bankroll every single time, since each individual bet has positive expected value. But do that and you go broke the moment you hit your first tails, which over a thousand flips is all but guaranteed. Meanwhile a "Kelly" bettor who only ever stakes about 2% of their bankroll per flip almost surely ends up unboundedly richer in the long run.

The physicist I'm interviewing for this piece says this proves that "expected value" and "expected utility" reasoning -- the stuff modern economics has been built on since von Neumann and Morgenstern -- has basically been mathematically refuted, and that what he calls "geometric rationality" (maximize the growth rate, not the mean) is what rational agents should actually be doing instead. That's a pretty massive claim to hang on a coin-flip toy example, and it would mean generations of economists built decision theory on a foundational error that a bit of algebra just dissolves. Is that really the right way to read the coin-flip result? And if it is, why hasn't "geometric rationality" already taken over as the standard framework in econ and decision-theory departments, instead of being this fringe-y thing physicists on Substack are excited about?
