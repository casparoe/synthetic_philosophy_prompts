Okay so this is a weird one but bear with me. I'm a software engineer (backend, mostly), not an economist or historian, but a while back I got into an online argument about Bitcoin and "sound money," and it somehow led me down a rabbit hole into David Hume's old economics essays -- Of Money, Of Interest, Of the Balance of Trade, Of Public Credit, that whole cluster from the 1750s. What stuck with me is that Hume already had this pretty elegant "specie-flow" idea about how gold and silver would automatically slosh between countries and correct trade imbalances on their own, meaning mercantilist attempts to hoard treasure forever were basically doomed from the start. But at the same time he was oddly anxious about Britain's growing national debt, and suspicious of banks issuing too much paper credit -- even though he also admitted a growing money supply could juice trade and industry for a while before prices caught up.

So here's the question that's been bugging me: if you resurrected 18th century Hume with all his actual arguments intact (not some cartoon "money is just a veil" version) and showed him todays world -- floating fiat currencies with zero specie backing, the US running a multi-decade trade deficit with China that never self-corrects the way his mechanism predicts, central banks doing QE at a scale that would've seemed insane to him, and crypto people pitching Bitcoin as "hard money" free from government meddling -- what would he actually conclude? Would his own logic push him toward liking Bitcoin, as a hedge against exactly the paper-credit-and-public-debt spiral he was already worried about in 1752? Or would his instinct that money needs to be grounded in something "real" make him just as suspicious of Bitcoin as he was of overextended bank notes? And honestly, does the specie-flow story even still work once you take specie out of the picture entirely, or does he need a totally different mechanism for a world of floating exchange rates? I'd rather get an answer that actually wrestles with the tension in his own arguments than one that just cherry-picks whichever Hume quote is convenient.
