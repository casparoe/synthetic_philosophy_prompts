For the past year, a few of us who share an interest in philosophy but have no formal training in it have kept up a small monthly correspondence: one person sets a short question on something they have been reading about, and another writes up an answer, which the rest of the group then discusses. This month I set the question below on the backward-induction paradox in the centipede game, and I have just received the answer copied underneath it. None of us has the background to be fully confident grading this sort of thing ourselves, so before I bring it back to the group I would like a rigorous, claim-by-claim check: please note which parts of the answer are accurate, which are shaky or overstated, and which are simply wrong, with a brief reason for each, and finish with an overall grade out of 20.

The question I set was:

The centipede game is usually presented as a paradox of backward induction: game-theoretic reasoning appears to force two fully rational players to end the game on the very first move, even though continuing together would leave both of them better off, and people who actually play the game in practice tend to keep going for a while. Explain why backward induction generates this result, why many people find it paradoxical rather than merely surprising, and what role the idea of "common knowledge of rationality" plays in the debate over how much weight the argument deserves.

The answer sent back to me reads:

The centipede game was devised by Robert Aumann to dramatize a tension inside classical game theory. Two players alternate the choice to end the game and pocket a growing pile of money, or pass the turn to the other player, with the total on the table growing each round it survives; but the game is built so that whoever's turn it is at the very last round always does better for themselves by ending it there than by passing, since passing at that point only leaves them with a smaller share of a pot that, by then, cannot grow any further. Reasoning backward from that last decision, the player one step before it should also end the game rather than pass into an outcome that is already settled against them, and this unravels all the way back to the first move, so two textbook-rational players are predicted to stop the game before it has properly begun, walking away with far less than they would have collected by cooperating to the end.

That is what makes the result paradoxical rather than just counter-intuitive: rationality is supposed to help agents get what they want, yet here it seems to actively bar two of them from an outcome that is better for both by their own lights, using nothing but the assumption that each is a good reasoner who expects the other to be one too. It is also strikingly at odds with how people actually behave: when the game is played for real money, most participants keep cooperating for several rounds rather than stopping at the first opportunity, which is usually read either as evidence that ordinary people fall short of the relevant standard of rationality, or as a reason to be suspicious of the standard itself.

The philosophical weight of the argument increased once Robert Aumann showed, in a well-known formal paper, that the unravelling result doesn't actually need anything beyond the two players simply being rational: because if each of them is rational in the ordinary sense of maximizing their own payoff given their beliefs, the "common knowledge of rationality" that these proofs invoke turns out to add nothing beyond that brute fact, and the backward-induction outcome falls out as a straightforward consequence of two rational agents facing one another. Part of what makes the paradox so hard to dismiss is that it isn't a fact about unsophisticated players, but seemingly a consequence of rationality as such.

None of this has fully settled the matter. Critics have pushed back on whether the notion of rationality being used here is doing legitimate work once it is asked to cover what a player should infer from being at a point in the game that, on the argument's own terms, a rational opponent would never have allowed to be reached; defenders reply that this is exactly the kind of counterfactual discipline a serious theory of rational choice has to be willing to impose. Either way, the centipede game remains one of the cleanest illustrations of how the formal apparatus of game theory can pull apart from both our intuitions about cooperation and our observations of how people actually behave.
