I'm one of five student members of my university's Honor Committee, which is entirely student-run and hears all academic integrity cases without any faculty or administrators present. This semester I'm also taking a seminar on formal methods in political philosophy that recently covered judgment aggregation theory — Kornhauser and Sager's doctrinal paradox and List and Pettit's discursive dilemma — and I've realized, a little uncomfortably, that our own hearing procedure is a live instance of exactly this problem. Under our code, a finding of "responsible" requires the panel to establish two independent elements: (1) that the student's work incorporated someone else's language or ideas without attribution, and (2) that the student acted knowingly or recklessly rather than merely carelessly. If either element isn't established, the case is downgraded to an educational referral rather than a sanctioned violation.

At our most recent hearing this produced a genuine split-decision problem. Polled issue by issue, a 3-2 majority found the attribution element satisfied, and a different 3-2 majority found the intent element satisfied — so voting proposition-by-proposition and then applying the rule mechanically yields "responsible." But when I separately asked each member what verdict they personally reached for the case as a whole, only two of the five actually concluded the student was responsible; the other three each rejected at least one element on their own view and so judged "not responsible," which is also the majority position if you poll bottom-line verdicts directly rather than the elements that supposedly generate them. We have no written rule saying whether the Committee should aggregate opinion issue-by-issue and derive the verdict mechanically, or instead poll members on the verdict itself and let each reconcile the elements privately, and this exact fact pattern is likely to recur before the procedures subcommittee finalizes its bylaws revision at the end of the month.

Could you draft the short memo I'd bring to that subcommittee, recommending which of these two procedures — or some alternative, such as a sequential priority rule — the Committee should formally adopt for cases with this two-element, conjunctive structure? I need it to actually defend a choice rather than just lay out the tradeoff, so please engage seriously with the strongest objection to whichever procedure you end up recommending. If you favor voting issue-by-issue, you'll have to address the discomfort of binding the Committee to a verdict that a majority of its own members explicitly reject for the student sitting in front of them; if you favor voting on the verdict directly, you'll have to address the worry that this lets the Committee's collective "doctrine" drift free of any consistent position on the elements it claims to be applying case after case.
