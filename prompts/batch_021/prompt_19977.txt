I'm a PhD student working on personal identity and philosophy of mind, and I'd like to test a specific extrapolation of Ayer's own arguments, rather than the loose appeal to his general materialism that this kind of question usually invites. Ayer's case against surviving bodily death rested on more than the verification principle: even granting some intelligible description of a post-mortem experience, he pressed the further question of what would justify counting its subject as identical with the person who died, rather than merely a similar successor. Suppose you put to him today's transhumanist proposal that a person might survive death by having their brain scanned at the connectome level and the resulting model run as an emulation on different hardware. Would this identity argument tell against such 'digital immortality' exactly as it tells against a disembodied soul, or does the causal and structural continuity between the emulation and the original brain give the emulation a stronger claim to being the same person? Finally, does his own widely discussed 1988 near-death experience, after which he said he was only slightly less confident that death is the end, give him any principled basis for treating the two cases differently?
