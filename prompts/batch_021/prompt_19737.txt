A friend of mine who works in decision theory and I have spent the last few weeks going back and forth about backward induction in the centipede game, and one exchange from the literature keeps nagging at me even though neither of us can fully agree on who comes out ahead. Robert Aumann showed, in a now-famous result, that common knowledge of rationality among the players is formally sufficient to force the backward-induction outcome throughout the entire game tree, including at nodes that the argument itself predicts will never be reached. Ken Binmore, among others, objected that this is less reassuring than it sounds: if play does reach, say, the third or fourth move of the game, that already means someone failed to do what backward induction said was required, so it is no longer obvious what a player who finds herself at that node is entitled to believe about her opponent's rationality going forward, or why she should keep reasoning by backward induction from there once the very assumption that generated the argument appears to have already broken down. Most treatments of the centipede game I come across skip past this and go straight to the experimental-economics headline — that real people cooperate far more than the theory predicts — which strikes me as a different and much less interesting puzzle than the purely internal question of whether the backward-induction argument can even coherently tell a rational player what to believe and do once play has left the path that the argument itself said was the only rational one.

Could you write me a dialogue between two philosophers of game theory who find themselves, for the sake of the thought experiment, standing together at the third move of a six-move centipede game: both players have already passed once, the pot has grown from $2 to $8, and Player 1 must now decide whether to take $6 for herself (leaving Player 2 with $2) or pass again and let it grow to $16. One philosopher should defend the Aumann line — that common knowledge of rationality is a perfectly coherent idealization, that it does pin down backward induction as the uniquely rational path, and that a question like "what should a player believe once she is off that path" is not one rational-choice theory owes anyone an answer to, since it concerns a contingency the theory itself rules out. The other should press the Binmore-style worry — that a theory of rational play which falls silent exactly at the nodes where players actually end up has not really earned the claim that backward induction is uniquely rational, and that any honest attempt to say what a player should believe at such a node seems to either quietly abandon common knowledge of rationality or make backward induction something less than forced. I would like the two of them to genuinely engage with each other's strongest moves rather than restate their own positions past one another, and to let the dialogue end wherever the arguments honestly lead rather than forcing a tidy resolution.
