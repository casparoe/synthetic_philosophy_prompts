The Milgrom–Stokey no-trade theorem holds that, given a common prior and common knowledge of rationality, private information alone can never motivate trade: an offer to trade is itself informative, and a rational counterparty updates on the fact that it was offered before accepting it, so any apparent gain from trade dissolves before it can be realized. I can follow the formal proof, but I want a sharper, gut-level grip on why common knowledge of rationality, rather than each trader's rationality taken in isolation, is doing the real work, and on how that same feature ties the result to Aumann's agreement theorem that Bayesians with a common prior cannot agree to disagree. Concretely, in what sense is "agreeing to trade" just "agreeing to disagree" in a different guise, and, granting that parallel, should we read the huge trading volumes seen in real markets mainly as evidence that traders' priors aren't nearly as "common" as the theorem needs, or as evidence of the mundane, non-informational motives—liquidity needs, hedging, and the like—the theorem was never meant to rule out?
