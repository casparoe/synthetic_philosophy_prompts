I'm a software engineer who first ran into predictive processing while reading about variational inference for a machine learning project, and following that thread into the philosophy of mind literature, I hit something I can't square. Andy Clark and Jakob Hohwy both build on what looks like the same core formalism -- hierarchical generative models minimizing prediction error, or variational free energy, across levels of processing -- yet they end up defending opposite pictures of the mind. Clark treats predictive processing as vindicating an extended, action-involving mind: since perception and action are both just error-minimization, the line between the "inner model" and the "outer world-probing loop" blurs, and cognition leans on body and environment as much as on the brain. Hohwy, apparently working from the same math, describes the brain as "self-evidencing" and inferentially sealed off from the world -- it only ever has access to its model's guesses about hidden causes, never the causes themselves -- and takes this to support a more classically internalist, brain-bound view of mind, with the Markov blanket marking a genuine inside/outside boundary.

I don't have the technical background to tell whether this is a real disagreement forced by the formalism itself -- say, by different ways of deciding what counts as "inside" the generative model -- or whether it's two incompatible metaphysical add-ons layered onto an otherwise neutral piece of statistics, with each side reading its preferred philosophy into shared equations. Could you walk me through what's actually driving the split, and tell me whether the predictive processing framework itself favors one reading over the other, or is simply silent on the question and leaves it to be settled on independent philosophical grounds?
