I was at a talk last week for a piece I'm writing (nominally about blame and moral responsibility) and the Q&A got way more interesting then the talk itself. Two audience members, not the speakers, ended up arguing about whether someone can have a reason to do something they genuinely don't care about.

One guy, lets call him Ravi, raised his brother-in-law as an example: totally checked out when it comes to his aging parents, never calls, treats their problems as an imposition, no sign of any pull to be different. Ravi said the guy "has every reason to treat them better whether he feels it or not, that's just true independent of his psychology." The woman next to him, Sana, disagreed: a reason has to connect to something the person already cares about, or to what he'd come to care about through correct reasoning from what's already in him (she called this Bernard Williams's internal reasons point) — if nothing in the guy links up to his parents at all, he doesn't have a reason, we just have a reason to wish he were different. Ravi said that makes moral criticism toothless for exactly the people who need it most, since the coldest people become the ones "reason" talk can never reach. Sana said we can still call him cruel and blameworthy without pretending he's ignoring a reason he secretly has. Ravi's last word: her "correct reasoning from what's already there" clause is doing a lot of unearned work, since she can always just say he'd care if he reasoned properly — which smuggles the external reason back in through the side door.

Nobody budged and the moderator moved on to the next question. Could you go through their exchange move by move, say who's got the better of each specific point, and flag anything important neither of them thought to raise?
