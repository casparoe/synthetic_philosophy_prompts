After thirty-five years as a condensed-matter physicist, I retired and began reading seriously in philosophy of biology, largely because an old graduate-school friend who became an evolutionary ecologist keeps emailing me papers to argue about. The dispute we keep circling back to is the units-of-selection question: whether natural selection is best understood as acting on genes, on individuals, or on groups. Coming from physics, the shape of the disagreement feels familiar to me: it echoes debates I know well about levels of description, such as whether thermodynamic quantities are "nothing but" statistical facts about micro-level dynamics, or whether the higher-level description is doing genuine explanatory work in its own right -- and I keep wondering whether that analogy genuinely holds here, or whether I am merely importing a habit of mind from physics where it does not belong.

My friend's position is that once everything is rewritten in the common currency of the Price equation, inclusive-fitness (kin-selection) accounting and multilevel-selection accounting turn out to be interchangeable: the same predictions emerge, only the covariance terms get partitioned differently. On their view, the whole controversy -- including the fight touched off by Nowak, Tarnita and Wilson's 2010 attack on inclusive fitness theory and the backlash it drew from the kin-selection camp -- is ultimately a dispute over heuristics and rhetorical framing rather than biological fact. I am not so sure. It seems to me that if two frameworks disagree about which entity is the real locus of causal action in selection -- gene, organism, or group -- that is a substantive disagreement even when the arithmetic can be made to agree.

Could you write me a dialogue between two evolutionary biologists, one defending each side of this, who work through the disagreement using the eusociality controversy as their running example? I would like each to engage directly with the other's strongest move -- what the equivalence theorist can say to blunt the "merely notational" charge, and what the causal realist can say against being reduced to a matter of bookkeeping -- rather than talking past one another. Neither character should come across as the obviously weaker debater; I want to see the dispute in its strongest form on both sides before I decide what I think.
