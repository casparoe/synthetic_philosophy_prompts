Three weeks ago my father received a specific, fairly sobering statistical prognosis from his oncologist, and ever since I have not been able to stop returning to a puzzle that first unsettled me years ago in a seminar on the philosophy of time and modal logic: is it already true, right now, that he will (or will not) still be alive in five years? That seminar was built around Aristotle's sea-battle passage in De Interpretatione 9 and Diodorus Cronus's Master Argument, and what has stayed with me is not Aristotle's own notoriously ambiguous solution but the later technical machinery developed to save both bivalence and an open future. In particular, I keep thinking about Prior's Ockhamist branching-time semantics, which evaluates a sentence like "it will rain" relative to a history running through a given moment, together with the further move — what Belnap and Green baptized the "thin red line" — of privileging one such history as the one that is actually going to happen. Rereading my old notes alongside Belnap and Green's "Indeterminism and the Thin Red Line" and John MacFarlane's "Future Contingents and Relative Truth," I have gotten stuck on whether MacFarlane's alternative to the thin red line — relativizing the truth of an utterance to a later context of assessment, rather than to a single privileged future fixed already at the context of utterance — really earns the advantage over Ockhamism that he claims for it, or whether it just relocates the same difficulty elsewhere in the model.

I would like you to argue for the following specific claim, as rigorously as you can. Belnap and Green's central objection to the thin red line is that, within a genuinely indeterminist branching model, nothing in the model's own resources privileges one history through the moment of utterance as "the" actual future; picking one out is arbitrary and unmotivated. Argue that MacFarlane's assessment-sensitive semantics inherits exactly this problem rather than escaping it: since branching-time models in this tradition never allow branching toward the past, any later moment of assessment lies at the end of a unique history running back through the original utterance, so evaluating a future-tense claim relative to that later context just is picking out a thin red line relative to the assessor. On this view, MacFarlane's explanation of why "it will rain," uttered today, becomes assessable as true tomorrow once it rains is purchased at exactly the metaphysical price he charges Ockhamism for paying, contrary to his own framing of relativism as avoiding any commitment to a privileged future. Please engage seriously with MacFarlane's actual reasons for thinking assessment-sensitivity is principled where absolute privileging is not, rather than simply asserting that the two proposals amount to the same thing — and if you can, say something about whether this dispute should affect how much existential comfort a technical defense of bivalence for future-tense claims can really offer someone in my father's position.
