A friend and I spent most of dinner last night arguing about whether the Church-Turing thesis could, even in principle, be refuted: he thinks any purported counterexample — some exotic supertask machine that decides the halting problem, say — would just get redefined as "not really computation," which makes the thesis unfalsifiable by construction, while I thought that was too quick. We never resolved it, and the argument was still nagging at me this morning when I sat down to grade a stack of exams that happened to include a question on nearby territory. I'd like a disinterested second opinion on one student's answer before I write anything in the margin.

The question was: "The Church-Turing thesis identifies the informal notion of an 'effectively calculable' function with the formal notion of a recursive (equivalently, Turing-computable) function. (a) Explain why the thesis cannot be proven the way a theorem is proven. (b) Explain what nonetheless makes us confident in it, with reference to Gödel's change of mind about the adequacy of proposed formalizations of 'effective calculability.' (c) Is the thesis empirical, in the sense of being confirmable or disconfirmable by evidence? Defend your answer."

The student wrote: "The thesis can't be proven because 'effectively calculable' is an informal, intuitive notion — roughly, what a human could compute by rote, given a fixed set of instructions — while 'recursive function' and 'Turing-computable function' are precisely defined mathematical notions. A proof needs two rigorously defined objects on either side of the identity sign, and here we only have one, so at best we can be persuaded rather than shown a demonstration. What does the persuading is convergence: Church's lambda-definability, Herbrand-Gödel general recursiveness, and Turing's machines were developed independently and turned out to pick out exactly the same class of functions, and every formalism proposed since — Post systems, register machines — has agreed. This is why Gödel, who had first proposed identifying effective calculability with his own notion of recursiveness, quickly grew confident once Church's 1936 proof appeared, later remarking that this gave the notion of mechanical procedure an absolute definition, independent of whichever formalism one starts from. As for whether the thesis is empirical, I'd say yes, in the same way a law of nature is: we test it by throwing new models of computation — quantum computers, DNA computing, neural networks — at it and checking whether any of them ever compute a function outside the recursive ones. None has, which is inductive evidence for the thesis in exactly the way repeated experiments confirm a physical law, and a single counterexample, such as a device that computably solved the halting problem, would refute it."

Could you grade this out of 20 and give point-by-point comments, keyed to (a), (b), and (c), that justify the grade — flagging anything the student gets subtly wrong as well as what they get right? A short rubric as JSON (criterion, points possible, points awarded, comment) would also be useful.
