I'm drafting a novel with a long central scene set at the opera, and before writing it I've been reading through Bernard Williams's essays on the form, collected in On Opera. Williams seems reluctant to treat the medium's stylizations—ensembles in which several characters sing contradictory thoughts at once, heroines who deliver extended arias while dying, plots visibly distorted to make room for set pieces—as charming absurdities redeemed only by the music; he appears to think these conventions do genuine dramatic and psychological work that a more naturalistic medium could not do as well. Drawing on his discussion of Don Giovanni, could you explain what exactly that work is supposed to consist in, and how his notion of operatic truth is meant to differ from straightforward realism? I'd also be curious, if it bears on his argument, how this connects to the long-standing debate over whether the opera's final, moralizing sextet should be staged or cut. I ask partly because I suspect an analogous question applies to conventions in prose fiction—close third-person narration, for instance—that novelists tend to use without examining.
