A friend and I spent a long evening arguing about Frederick Douglass's break with William Lloyd Garrison over how to read the U.S. Constitution, and neither of us gave any ground. My friend defended the Garrisonian line that the document was irredeemably proslavery — "a covenant with death, and an agreement with hell," as the disunionists called it — given provisions like the three-fifths clause and the fugitive-slave clause. I kept siding with the later Douglass, who argued that a text which never once uses the words "slave" or "slavery" should be read, in its silences and generalities, as what he called a "glorious liberty document."

Write a dialogue in which Douglass and Garrison debate the question face to face, each trying to win the other back to his side and engaging one another's actual arguments rather than talking past each other. Have Garrison press the case from the framers' documented intentions and from how courts — Dred Scott among them — actually construed the text; have Douglass answer with a textualist, natural-law case, including the interpretive maxim he borrowed from Lysander Spooner that ambiguous law should be read in favor of liberty. Neither man was anyone's fool, so don't let either concede too quickly or serve as a strawman for the other to knock down — let the dialogue end with the disagreement still genuinely alive, as it was between them in life.
