I teach a graduate seminar on the Vienna Circle, and this year — unusually, since we normally hurry past it on the way to Carnap and Schlick on protocol sentences — I gave a full session to Otto Neurath's Isotype project, the "Vienna Method of Picture Statistics" that he directed from the Gesellschafts- und Wirtschaftsmuseum in Red Vienna before continuing the work in exile, first in the Netherlands under the Isotype name and then, after 1940, in Oxford. What struck the students was how many hard rules Neurath built into the system. A change in quantity had to be shown by repeating a standardized icon at a fixed size rather than by shrinking or enlarging a single icon, since area and volume distort in ways an untrained eye can't correct for. The underlying statistics had to pass through a dedicated "transformer" — a role Marie Reidemeister literally held — before an artist like Gerd Arntz was allowed to touch them, precisely so that pictorial charm couldn't quietly change what was being claimed. And the whole apparatus was pitched, in texts like his 1936 International Picture Language, as a "helper language" for a semi-literate, multilingual, largely working-class public, meant to sit alongside verbal argument rather than replace or prettify it.

I'd like to close the unit with a harder question than a summary, and I want to test it on you before I put it to the class. Suppose Neurath were shown two features of our present visual culture. First, the enormous volume of statistical infographics that now circulate as screenshots and memes, routinely breaking exactly the rules he insisted on: icons rescaled instead of repeated, axes truncated to exaggerate a trend, charts increasingly generated wholesale by AI tools with nothing resembling a transformer checking them against any actual data. Second, emoji and standardized icon sets, often described, informally, as a kind of universal picture language for people who share no written one. Would he recognize either as a real heir to Isotype, or would his own commitments — not just the house rules of chart-making, but his physicalism, his preference for statements that are intersubjectively checkable over anything irreducibly private, and the "boat" picture of knowledge as something repaired plank by plank rather than built from a clean slate — force him to disown both, for different reasons? Don't just lay out considerations on each side: tell me what you think his actual verdict would be, and build the case for it.
