Free will is one of those topics where I absorbed a kind of rationalist-blogosphere party line well before I had read much of the primary literature on it. As I understand it, the line runs through Dennett and was popularized further by Eliezer Yudkowsky's old Overcoming Bias and early LessWrong posts on the subject: the sense that "I could have done otherwise" is just an artifact of not yet having computed your own output; determinism isn't something imposed on you from outside, since you simply are the physical decision process in question; and the libertarian demand to be an "uncaused cause" is incoherent anyway, since any candidate source of an action is either fixed by prior causes (in which case it isn't ultimate) or undetermined (in which case it's noise rather than agency). Having made these moves, the traditional problem gets treated as dissolved rather than solved: "free will" is relabeled as the operation of a self-modeling deliberative process not subject to any obvious external coercion, and the original puzzle is diagnosed as a confusion between levels of description rather than a real question.

I recently put this whole picture to a friend who is more sympathetic to Derk Pereboom and Galen Strawson, and she argued that the dissolution is essentially a bait-and-switch. On her view, it answers a question about mechanism that no source-incompatibilist was actually pressing, while leaving completely untouched the worry that basic-desert moral responsibility — deserving blame or credit for an act just in virtue of having performed it, rather than because assigning blame happens to have good forward-looking consequences — requires being the ultimate source of one's own values and deliberative dispositions in a way that no deterministic causal history can secure, however elegantly that history gets relabeled.

I'd like you to build her case as rigorously as you can rather than mine. Please argue that the rationalist "dissolving" strategy fails to defeat the source-incompatibilist challenge to moral responsibility. In making the argument, engage seriously with at least one specific compatibilist rejoinder of the sort used to blunt exactly this kind of manipulation-style challenge — for example, Fischer's defense of guidance control and reasons-responsiveness, or a Frankfurt-style hierarchical-mesh account of the sort Dennett's own view draws on — and explain concretely why it still fails to supply the kind of authorship the objection is demanding. I would rather not receive a "reasonable people disagree" summary; I want the sharpest version of this objection you can construct, since this is exactly the sort of question on which I notice I'm persuaded by whichever side I've read most recently.
