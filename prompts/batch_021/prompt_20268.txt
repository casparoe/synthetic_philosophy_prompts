I've spent the better part of the last decade reading LessWrong, the EA Forum, and the s-risk-adjacent corners of the blogosphere that grew out of that scene, and about eighteen months ago it turned into an actual job: I'm one of five researchers at a small nonprofit working on cooperative AI and the reduction of suffering risk, in roughly the same niche as the Center on Long-Term Risk. I sit on our four-person internal publications committee, which has to sign off on anything that goes out under the org's name, and this week we're stuck on a case I can't resolve on my own.

Some background, since the case won't make sense without it. A "surrogate goal" is a proposed defense against threats, including acausal ones, where an agent (say, a future misaligned AI reasoning about how to influence present-day actors with no causal channel back to them) tries to extract concessions by threatening something the target values. The idea is that an agent known to be vulnerable to threats against X can commit in advance to treat any such threat exactly as though it were a much cheaper threat against some unrelated variable Y, so that a threatener who has correctly modeled the commitment gains nothing extra from targeting X rather than Y, and rational threats gravitate to the cheap surrogate instead. One of our researchers has drafted a public post that goes further than anything we've put out before: rather than present surrogate goals as a hypothetical illustration, it proposes that we adopt and name one on the record, publicly committing that any attempt to pressure us by threatening large-scale suffering will be met by us exactly as we'd meet a threat against, say, next year's discretionary travel budget. Our publication policy bars anything that counts as a "clear and substantial infohazard," a phrase that's been on the books for years and has never once had to be applied.

The committee is split two and two. Two of us think this is a natural, overdue extension of work we already publish, and that an on-the-record commitment is strictly more credible than a private disposition or a purely theoretical write-up, which is presumably the whole point on any decision theory where credibility does the work. The other two are uneasy for reasons that aren't just squeamishness: one thinks this is a fresh version of the old Roko's-basilisk mistake, that publicly specifying exactly which threats we'd take seriously, and how, is itself a way of feeding the acausal channel rather than closing it off; the other doubts the surrogate would survive contact with a sufficiently capable adversary, since anything smart enough to be deterred by our commitment is also smart enough to notice that Y isn't what we actually care about, and to threaten the real target regardless — in which case we'd have advertised the shape of our vulnerability for nothing. Could you draft the memo the committee will actually use to decide this on Friday? I want a concrete recommendation — publish as drafted, publish a substantially revised version, hold it back, or something else entirely — with a timeframe attached, and I'd like you to take the strongest objection to whatever you recommend seriously rather than wave it away; I'd rather you reach your own view than just split the difference between the four of us.
