I'm a PhD student in computer science, and this term I'm TAing an advanced course on algorithmic game theory. For the capstone assignment, each student team submits a short program that plays a single, one-shot round of a resource-splitting game against every other team's program; the game has the same payoff structure as the Prisoner's Dilemma, where "grab" strictly dominates "share" for either side individually, yet mutual "share" beats mutual "grab." To make the assignment more than a rehash of the standard tournament, the instructor requires that every submitted program take the opponent's complete, unmodified source code as an input and be allowed to use it however it likes before outputting its own choice; there is no repeated play or reputation across rounds, so nothing like ongoing reciprocity is available, and the instructor also runs a "mirror match" in which each bot is paired against an exact copy of itself.

Helping draft the grading harness, I've been arguing with my labmate about what our own bot should actually do with this information. Her instinct is to have it internally run each opponent's submitted code to see what action that opponent would take, and then best-respond; I've pointed out that if enough teams have the same idea, and some of those programs try to do the same thing to us, our bot could end up trying to simulate an opponent that is busy simulating us, with no obvious place for either computation to stop (and no guarantee an arbitrary opponent program halts at all on our input). Yet the alternative of hard-coding either "share" or "grab" regardless of the opponent seems to squander exactly the opportunity that handing everyone each other's source code was presumably meant to create: unconditional sharing gets exploited by anyone who grabs, and unconditional grabbing throws away all the gains from the mirror match and from any opponents whose code would have reciprocated cooperation.

I have a strong intuition that being able to see an opponent's actual code, rather than merely knowing it is "rational" in the abstract, ought to let two well-designed bots do better than they could in a sealed, simultaneous one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma, but I can't work out a rule our bot could follow that captures this without either collapsing into the regress above or being a sucker for the first team that hard-codes "grab." I'd be grateful for help thinking through, from first principles, what kind of decision procedure could actually deliver on the promise of mutual code transparency here, and, more generally, what this exercise suggests about how much genuine strategic advantage such transparency buys you and where its limits are.
