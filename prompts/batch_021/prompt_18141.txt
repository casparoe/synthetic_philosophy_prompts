Random thing that's been stuck in my head since last week's class. I signed up for an evening game theory course through my work's tuition program (I'm in ops, thought it'd help with vendor negotiations -- turns out its way more abstract than that), and the homework had this toy scenario: our company is choosing between a Big Launch, a Middle-of-the-Road launch, or Wait-and-See for a new product, while a rival firm will either Expand into our market or Hold Steady, and we have to commit before finding out which. In made-up points, Big Launch nets 3 if the rival Holds Steady and 0 if they Expand, Wait-and-See is the mirror image (0 if Hold Steady, 3 if Expand), and Middle-of-the-Road just gets a flat 1 no matter what the rival does. The instructor's rule for round one was: you can cross an option off the table if there's some randomized combination of your *other* options -- like a weighted coin flip deciding which one you actually go with -- that beats it no matter what the rival ends up doing, and you keep repeating the process until nothing more can be crossed off.

Two people in my cohort, Leah and Marcus, got into it about this afterward at the coffee place next door, and I still can't tell who came out on top. Leah said Middle-of-the-Road obviously comes off the table: flip a coin, go all-in on Big Launch on heads and all-in on Wait-and-See on tails, and you'd average 1.5 points whichever way the rival moves, beating Middle-of-the-Road's guaranteed 1 either way. Marcus said that's a sleight of hand, since neither Big Launch nor Wait-and-See on its own ever beats Middle-of-the-Road in both scenarios (each one tanks hard in exactly one of the two cases), and besides, you can't actually run a company off a coin flip, so comparing against some imaginary 50/50 gamble nobody would ever bring to the board shouldn't count as a real alternative. Leah's comeback was that the instructor's rule explicitly allowed randomized combinations, not just single options, and anyway plenty of real strategies are effectively randomized already (which weeks you run a promotion, how leads get rotated among reps), so refusing to count a coin flip just because nobody says the word "random" out loud to the board seems arbitrary. Marcus's last word was that if randomizing counts, it should count for the rival too, and once the rival is also allowed to hedge, who's to say some rival gamble doesn't make Middle-of-the-Road look competitive again -- so at best Leah's shown it's beaten "for now," not eliminated for good the way getting outright beaten by a single option would be.

I couldn't settle it and it's honestly been bugging me all week. Could you go through their exchange move by move and say who's right (and who's wrong, and why) at each step, instead of just declaring an overall winner? And if there's some consideration neither of them actually raised that matters here, I'd genuinely like to know what it is.
