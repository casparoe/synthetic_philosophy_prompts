I trained as an experimental physicist and, like most of my generation, spent a career using quantum mechanics without ever asking what it means. Retirement has finally let me read the interpretive literature properly, and one result has left me stuck. In 1992, Englert, Scully, Süssmann and Walther described a which-path setup in which a particle's Bohmian trajectory arrives at the detector for the wrong slit, flatly contradicting what an independent which-path marker recorded; something close to this was later confirmed experimentally, using genuine weak measurements, by Steinberg's group in Toronto. On the face of it this looks like a serious empirical embarrassment: the theory's signature commitment, that particles have real continuous trajectories, appears to be lying to us. Yet committed Bohmians do not treat this as a refutation. Please explain, in real depth rather than the usual popular-science gloss, what their rejoinder actually is and what work the notions of contextuality and nonlocality are doing in it. I'd also like to know why Copenhagen and Everettian accounts, which posit no such trajectories to begin with, never face this problem at all, and whether, in your honest assessment, the Bohmian rejoinder vindicates the trajectories' claim to reality or quietly concedes the critics' point.
