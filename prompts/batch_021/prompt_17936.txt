I've spent a long time reading rationalist blogs and forums, where a recurring theme is discomfort with claims that seem to rest on evidence that can never quite resolve one way or the other — the intuition that if something important and relational were really true, whoever or whatever is responsible for it ought to make that truth more legible to someone honestly looking for it. A situation involving two friends of mine has been nagging at me in a way that feels connected to this, and I'd like a careful outside take, since the people I've discussed it with have mostly just talked past each other for weeks without resolving anything.

My friend Priya and her brother Dev grew up being quietly supported by a great-uncle neither of them has ever met. He emigrated decades ago and cut off almost all direct contact, but money still arrives through a family lawyer whenever it's badly needed — school fees, a medical bill, once a down payment on a house — always with a short, unsigned note in the same handwriting, and always the same message relayed secondhand: that he loves the family very much, that he is fully capable of visiting or writing openly if he chose to, but has his own reasons for keeping his distance, and that he would rather they come to trust and love him through how they live their lives than through direct proof of who he is. Priya has built a real relationship out of this: she writes him letters she never sends, feels she has occasionally sensed his presence at important moments, and reads the very mysteriousness of the arrangement as a sign of how much care has gone into it. Dev isn't hostile to any of this and says he'd be genuinely glad to learn it's all true, but he finds the evidence too thin and too easily explained another way — he thinks it's at least as likely to be an impersonal trust their late grandmother set up — and he can't understand why someone who supposedly loves them this much wouldn't just show up now that they're adults who could handle whatever his reasons are. Priya's answer is that showing up on demand would cheapen the relationship, or might even rule out the kind of relationship she believes they're meant to have.

I don't think this is really a dispute about inheritance law, and generic advice about family communication feels beside the point. It seems to me to be circling a much older and more specific philosophical puzzle, though I can't quite name it or say who actually has the stronger position. Could you draw out the underlying structure of Priya and Dev's disagreement, present the strongest version of each side, and say what should actually move a reasonable person in Dev's position — someone who isn't hostile or looking for excuses, just unconvinced? I'd especially like to understand whether Priya's line, that he must have his reasons for staying distant, actually answers Dev's worry or just restates the puzzle instead of resolving it.
