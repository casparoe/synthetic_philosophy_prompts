I'm a philosophy PhD student working on personal identity and the metaphysics of mind, currently doing a year-long clinical ethics practicum at a children's hospital, where I sit as the graduate-student member of the pediatric Ethics Committee. Next Thursday the committee is reviewing what looks, on paper, like a routine case: an 11-year-old with Lennox-Gastaut syndrome and several injurious drop attacks a day, refractory to every anticonvulsant regimen tried so far, for whom the epilepsy team is recommending an anterior corpus callosotomy. The parents are enthusiastic, the recognized neurosurgical risks (infection, disconnection effects such as interhemispheric transfer deficits and the well-documented episodes of intermanual conflict between the two hands) have been disclosed, and the standard consent form has been signed.

Having just finished a seminar unit on Nagel's "Brain Bisection and the Unity of Consciousness," Puccetti's two-minds hypothesis, and Bayne's "switch model" as a rival to the standard two-streams reading of Sperry and Gazzaniga's original split-brain data, I pointed out at the pre-meeting that the consent form is silent on something the philosophical literature treats as genuinely unresolved: whether a completed callosotomy produces two separate loci of experience and agency where there was one, merely unmasks a duality of hemispheric minds that was arguably present all along, or leaves a single subject whose access to information simply becomes more effortful and lateralized -- and that these readings are not equivalent for thinking about whose welfare and whose agreement we are actually securing once the corpus callosum is cut. Two of the attending physicians on the committee think this is an interesting seminar question with no real purchase on a well-established, low-risk palliative procedure that this particular family has every reason to want, and worry that raising it will only frighten the parents or delay a beneficial surgery without changing anything anyone would actually do differently.

Could you draft the roughly one-page written opinion I'll circulate to the committee before Thursday's meeting? It should give a concrete recommendation -- with a specific time frame -- on whether and how our consent/assent process and post-operative follow-up should change, both for this family and as a template for future callosotomy referrals, and it should take the physicians' "we are overthinking a beneficial, well-established surgery" objection seriously on its own terms rather than answer it by simply asserting that the metaphysics matters; if the recommendation can't survive contact with two skeptical, non-philosopher colleagues, it isn't going to survive the meeting.
