In 2016, a ProPublica investigation into COMPAS, a recidivism-risk-assessment tool used by several U.S. courts in bail and sentencing decisions, found that Black defendants who did not go on to reoffend were flagged "high risk" at a substantially higher rate than white defendants who likewise did not reoffend. Northpointe, the company behind COMPAS, replied that its tool satisfied a different, equally natural standard of fairness: it was well calibrated across race, so that among defendants assigned any given risk score, roughly the same proportion actually went on to reoffend regardless of race. Subsequent formal work, most notably by Alexandra Chouldechova and by Jon Kleinberg, Sendhil Mullainathan, and Manish Raghavan, showed that this was not a disagreement that better data or a cleverer model could dissolve: whenever the base rate of reoffending differs between two groups, as it did here, no risk score can be calibrated within each group while simultaneously equalizing false-positive and false-negative rates across the groups, except in degenerate special cases. Calibration and equalized error rates turn out to be strictly incompatible demands under those conditions, so that satisfying one requires sacrificing the other, at least in part.

This is a case I keep returning to in a seminar on distributive justice, since it looks like exactly the sort of dispute Rawls's machinery should be able to adjudicate, and yet he obviously never had it, or anything resembling it, in view. Suppose Rawls, who died in 2002, had lived to confront this result directly. His resources include the original position and veil of ignorance, the two principles of justice, the difference principle, and his considered distinction between merely formal equality of opportunity ("careers open to talents") and fair equality of opportunity, which additionally corrects for the arbitrary advantages of social background. Drawing on this apparatus, which of the competing statistical criteria at stake here — calibration, equal false-positive rates, equal false-negative rates, or some alternative such as demographic parity — do you think Rawls would treat as the more defensible demand of justice once it is clear that they cannot all be jointly satisfied, and why?

Two further wrinkles are worth addressing in your answer. First, one standard criterion in this literature is already named "equality of opportunity," in evident allusion to just this kind of language, yet it equalizes only false-negative rates while leaving calibration and false-positive rates free to diverge across groups; say whether you think Rawls would recognize this as a faithful implementation of his own idea, or only as a narrow and potentially misleading fragment of it. Second, Rawls insists that his two principles govern the basic structure of society and the distribution of primary goods over the course of a life, not particular transactions or isolated decisions; address whether this restriction means his framework has nothing determinate to say about a local, punitive decision point like a bail hearing, or whether the impossibility result itself gives him grounds to conclude that one of the supposed fairness demands was never really a requirement of justice to begin with.
