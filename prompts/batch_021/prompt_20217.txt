I am drafting a novel built around a single altered afternoon in Sarajevo in June 1914: in my version, the driver never takes the wrong turn that stalled the Archduke's car in front of the gunman, the fatal shot is never fired, and the July Crisis never detonates into a general European war. Before going further, I showed the premise to an old friend who trained as a historian, and she was politely withering about it. She pointed out that E.H. Carr treated this entire mode of "what if" reasoning as little more than a parlor game unworthy of serious history, while Isaiah Berlin, in his essay "Historical Inevitability," seemed to argue something like the opposite: that denying the reality of such open moments comes uncomfortably close to denying that anyone is ever responsible for anything they do. I would like to understand the actual shape of that disagreement rather than simply adopting whichever side happens to flatter my plot.

What I keep running up against is this: it feels intuitively fine to say that if the driver had turned right instead of left, the war might not have started that summer, but the moment you ask what else about 1914 Europe is supposed to stay fixed while you make that one change, the ground gets much less firm. I gather this is a version of what philosophers of conditionals call the cotenability problem, and that something similar shows up, under a different name, in the methodological literature on counterfactuals in political science and diplomatic history: I recall the phrase "minimal-rewrite rule" from something I read, possibly Tetlock and Belkin, describing what makes one proposed rewrite of the past defensible and another merely an arbitrary fantasy. Could you walk me through, in fairly intuitive terms, why this problem resists easy solution, and how it bears on the deeper question of whether a moment like July 1914 is best understood as a genuine hinge or merely the visible tip of deeper, overdetermining causes? I would like enough of a grip on the actual philosophical mechanics to know whether my premise is merely convenient or genuinely defensible.
