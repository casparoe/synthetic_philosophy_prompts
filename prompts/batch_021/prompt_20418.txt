QUESTION: Explain the disagreement between Humean and anti-Humean accounts of laws of nature. Characterize David Lewis's Best System Account, contrast it with David Armstrong's necessitation account, and briefly explain how dispositional essentialism (e.g. Bird's) differs from Armstrong's view even though both usually get filed under "anti-Humean."

STUDENT ANSWER: Hume denied that we ever observe necessary connections between distinct events -- all we get is one thing following another, constant conjunction, never the "cement" that's supposed to bind cause to effect. Contemporary Humeans about laws inherit this: nothing in the world beyond the total, spread-out arrangement of local matters of particular fact (Lewis's "Humean mosaic") actually necessitates anything else, so laws can't be some extra ingredient that makes the regularities happen.

Lewis's Best System Account cashes this out by saying a generalization is a law just when it's a theorem of whichever true deductive system best balances simplicity against strength -- roughly, the system that packs in as much information about the mosaic as possible using the fewest, simplest axioms. This improves on the naive view that a law is just any exceptionless regularity, since plenty of accidental regularities (every gold sphere in the universe happens to be under a mile wide) are exceptionless without being lawlike. Because the whole apparatus is cashed out in terms of the system a suitably rational inquirer would settle on, lawhood on Lewis's view ends up being a mind-dependent status: what counts as a law is fixed by what the best epistemic community would believe, not by how the world is arranged independently of anyone thinking about it. That's the price Humeans pay for giving up necessitation.

Armstrong denies exactly this. Laws, for him, are relations of natural necessitation, N(F,G), between universals -- a further, contingent, second-order state of affairs that, once it obtains, necessitates that all F's are G's. This is why Armstrong thinks his account, unlike Lewis's, can underwrite counterfactuals: it's not just that all F's have so far been G's, but that F's must be G's given N(F,G).

Dispositional essentialists like Bird are anti-Humean in the same broad sense: they too think laws involve real necessity, not mere pattern. The difference from Armstrong is mostly bookkeeping -- instead of locating the necessity in an external relation between two otherwise separate universals, they build it into the identity of the properties themselves, so that being negatively charged just is, partly, being disposed to repel other negative charges. Since Armstrong's necessitation relation is doing extra metaphysical work that the dispositionalist's bare essences don't need, Armstrong's laws end up holding with a stronger, fully metaphysical necessity, while the dispositional essentialist is left with the comparatively weaker natural necessity Armstrong was trying to avoid in the first place -- so, ironically, it's Bird's package that ends up closer to Lewis than Armstrong on the modal status of laws.
