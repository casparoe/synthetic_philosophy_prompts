Question 4. The centipede game is often invoked to illustrate a tension between game-theoretic solution concepts and intuitive judgments about rational play.
(a) [5 pts] Explain the backward induction argument for why rational play in the centipede game involves the first mover ending the game immediately, rather than either player ever passing.
(b) [5 pts] Explain in what sense this conclusion is "paradoxical" -- both in terms of how it compares to laboratory behavior, and in terms of a purely conceptual worry philosophers have raised about the argument itself.
(c) [5 pts] Sketch ONE proposed resolution of the paradox that we discussed in class (e.g. the epistemic, common-knowledge-of-rationality debate, or a reputation/incomplete-information account), and briefly say whether you find it convincing.

And here's the student's answer, copied exactly as written:

(a) In the centipede game, two players alternate decisions over some fixed number of rounds; at each decision node the player to move can either take the pot as it currently stands, ending the game, or pass, which grows the pot for the next round but changes who's "ahead" if the other player then takes. Backward induction reasons from the end of the game backward: at the very last node, since there's no more future to protect, whoever moves prefers to take rather than pass (passing would only benefit the other player, with nothing further for the first player to reclaim). Given that the last mover will definitely take, the second-to-last mover can't benefit from passing either, since they already know what happens right after. Repeating this reasoning back to node 1, the first mover should also just take immediately. So even though both players would collectively end up richer if they cooperated by passing several times, backward induction implies that the only strategy profile consistent with rationality unravels to immediate defection, which is also the unique Nash equilibrium of the game.

(b) This is paradoxical for two different reasons. First, empirically: when the centipede game is actually run in the lab (as in the well-known experiments by McKelvey and Palfrey), subjects overwhelmingly do not defect on the first move -- play typically continues for several rounds before someone finally takes the pot, which flatly contradicts the prediction that rational players defect immediately. Second, and more conceptually, there's a worry, pressed by Aumann, about the argument's own coherence: the backward induction argument requires each player, at every node, to reason about what a rational opponent would do -- but to even reach most of the interior nodes in the tree, some player must already have deviated from what backward induction calls rational. So the argument seems to require reasoning about the rational continuation of a game in which someone has just behaved irrationally, and it's not obvious that's a coherent thing to ask of a "rational" player.

(c) The resolution I find most compelling is the epistemic one associated with the Aumann-Binmore exchange. Binmore's key result is that if there is common knowledge of rationality (CKR) among the players -- each player's strategy is a best response to their beliefs about the other, and this is itself commonly known -- then this formally forces the backward induction outcome, so CKR is doing exactly what it should. Aumann responded by pointing out that CKR is a fragile, all-or-nothing assumption: the moment a single player passes at a node where backward induction says they should take, CKR is falsified for the rest of the game, since it's no longer common knowledge that the players are rational. Once that happens there's no more backward-induction-based prediction to be made about what happens afterward, since the assumption that generated the prediction no longer holds. Weighing these, I think Aumann has the better of the exchange: it explains why cooperation, once it starts breaking down in the lab, breaks down gradually rather than all at once, which a pure CKR-implies-defection story struggles to account for.
