Consider the following maxim: "Whenever assisting a stranger in difficulty would cost me time or resources I would rather spend on myself, and I am confident that no one will think less of me for it, I will walk on and do nothing, on the grounds that other people's troubles are not my responsibility."

This is an exam question from an undergraduate ethics course, together with one student's answer to it. Please grade the answer and provide the specific comments that justify your grade.

Question:
(a) Apply Kant's two-step universalizability procedure (the Formula of Universal Law) to the maxim above, and state what type of duty—perfect or imperfect, to oneself or to others—is at stake, with justification.
(b) The same procedure applied to a maxim of making a false promise in order to secure a loan yields a different verdict. Explain what type of duty is violated in that case and why, and state whether Kant's grounds for this verdict rest on the bad social consequences of universalized lying or on something else.

Student answer:

(a) Kant's procedure has two steps: first, imagine the maxim adopted by everyone as a universal law of nature, and ask whether this can even be conceived without contradiction; second, if it can be conceived, ask whether a rational agent could actually will it. Universalized, our maxim becomes: whenever helping is costly and unobserved, everyone walks on. This is easy enough to conceive—a world of general indifference to others' troubles is not self-defeating the way a world of universal lying is, since indifference does not presuppose any practice that its own universalization would destroy. So the maxim passes the conceivability test. It fails, however, at the level of the will: because we are dependent, finite creatures who will sometimes need the goodwill of others, no one can consistently will a world in which such goodwill is universally withheld, since this would mean willing away, in advance, a kind of help one's own future self may depend on. This is therefore a contradiction in the will, and the duty it generates is an imperfect duty to others: it requires adopting a general policy of beneficence while leaving latitude for judgment about whom to help and how, rather than forbidding the omission on every single occasion.

(b) Making a false promise to secure a loan fails at the first, conceivability step: if everyone borrowed on false promises whenever convenient, lenders would quickly stop believing such promises altogether, credit would dry up, and borrowers and lenders alike would end up worse off than before—which is precisely why Kant regards the maxim as impermissible once universalized. Because the contradiction shows up already at the level of conceivability, this is a perfect duty, admitting no exceptions, whereas the beneficence case above only constrained the will. Since both maxims, in the end, involve making an exception of oneself at other people's expense, however, Kant's considered view is that neither really leaves the kind of latitude he grants to self-regarding duties like talent development; both are, at bottom, perfect duties we owe to others.
