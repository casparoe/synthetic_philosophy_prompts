A friend and I spent much of last night arguing about causation without ever resolving anything. They insisted that giving up necessary connections between cause and effect makes the natural world unintelligible in principle, not merely hard to predict; I kept insisting that you can drop necessity and still have a stable, law-governed universe, provided something else is doing the work of guaranteeing the regularities. Neither of us budged, and it occurred to me afterward that we had basically reinvented the dispute between al-Ghazālī and Averroes (Ibn Rushd) over occasionalism. Write a dialogue between the two of them, staged around al-Ghazālī's example of fire burning cotton from the Tahāfut al-Falāsifa, with Averroes responding as he actually does in the Tahāfut al-Tahāfut. Al-Ghazālī should give his real grounds for denying that fire has any power of its own to produce burning, including why he thinks God's custom, rather than any inherent necessity, is doing the explanatory work, and why this matters for the possibility of miracles; Averroes should press his real objections about what denying such powers does to the intelligibility of natures, definitions, and scientific knowledge, rather than a strawman about induction. Have them genuinely engage each other's strongest moves point by point, and end the dialogue with them at least agreeing on exactly where their disagreement bottoms out, even if neither concedes the argument.
