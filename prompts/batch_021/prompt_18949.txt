At a hiring-committee meeting last spring, two colleagues of mine fell into a disagreement the rest of us never resolved. We were discussing a candidate whose CV listed nine papers, several of which turned out to be the same underlying study split into three "least publishable units." One senior member, Professor K, objected strongly to the practice. Elena pointed out that Professor K had done exactly the same thing early in his own career, a matter of public record, and so had no standing to object. David replied that this was beside the point: Professor K abandoned the practice decades ago and has since written against it, so whatever standing he forfeited through his early conduct, he has since earned back by reforming. Elena answered that reform changes how blameworthy Professor K is now, but not the fact that his padded early CV helped him land the very position he's speaking from; he is still trading on the wrong he is condemning, and objecting to it only after he has already cashed in is exactly the kind of self-exempting move that makes hypocrisy objectionable in the first place. David answered that this proves too much: if benefiting once from a wrong permanently disqualified someone from criticizing it, no reformed person could ever speak against conduct they once engaged in, yet we usually credit former addicts and ex-offenders with more, not less, authority to criticize the practices they have left behind. What should matter, he insisted, is whether Professor K currently relies on or endorses a double standard, not whether he once profited from one.

Please go through this exchange move by move and say who has the better of it at each stage, not just who wins overall. Keep the question of whether Professor K has standing to raise this objection separate from the question of whether the objection itself is correct, and note anything you think both Elena and David are overlooking.
