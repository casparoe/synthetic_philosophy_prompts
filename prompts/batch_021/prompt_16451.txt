I've spent most of the last decade covering science and ideas, with a good chunk of that on the effective-altruism and existential-risk beat. That's presumably why a mid-sized foundation funding long-term-future and existential-risk work recently invited me to join its grants committee as one of two outside 'public members' — people with no financial stake in the outcomes, brought in mainly to stress-test whether the committee's reasoning would hold up once it left the room.

Ahead of our allocations meeting in three weeks, the committee has to rule on an application that has left everyone more confident in their gut reaction than in their ability to defend it, so I've been asked to draft the discussion memo. The applicants aren't proposing a specific intervention; they want roughly a million dollars over two years for a research and advocacy program built on an argument along these lines. Serious cosmological models don't rule out a spatially infinite universe, in which case, on fairly standard assumptions, there exist infinitely many exact or near-exact duplicates of Earth, each causally sealed off from us behind its own cosmological horizon. The applicants argue that by adopting and publicizing a specific cooperative policy — one that any sufficiently similar reasoner elsewhere would, on identical evidence, also be inclined to adopt — the committee can affect, in a purely evidential rather than causal sense, how countably infinitely many of those duplicate civilizations fare. On a standard expected-value calculation, that makes the payoff from funding them literally infinite, and since no finite amount of expected good can outweigh an infinite one, they conclude the grant should take strict priority over everything else in the budget, including our pandemic-preparedness and AI-safety commitments, regardless of how small the committee's credence in the underlying cosmology is. Two committee members find the logic basically airtight and want to fund it as a cheap hedge against an enormous upside; a couple of others want to reject it out of hand as a clever trick not worth the committee's time; I don't think either reaction survives being spelled out in full.

Could you draft that memo for me? I need it to reach an actual recommendation — fund, decline, or fund with specific conditions — on this application, and, since this will not be the last unbounded-value argument to reach this committee, a general rule or procedure it can apply to future proposals of the same shape without simply refusing to engage with them. Please take the applicants' reasoning seriously on its own terms first — including the uncomfortable fact that quietly abandoning expected-value reasoning only when it yields an inconvenient answer is its own kind of inconsistency — before explaining where, if anywhere, you think the argument breaks down. Keep it to two pages or so, something the committee will actually read in one sitting, without flattening the argument just to get there.
