I'm taking a seminar this term that pairs Enlightenment ethics with contemporary moral psychology, and this week we've set Part Two of Franklin's Autobiography — where he lays out his thirteen-virtue scheme and the little book he used to chart his daily lapses — against the situationist critique of character developed by Gilbert Harman and John Doris. Their claim, illustrated by studies like Darley and Batson's well-known Good Samaritan experiment, is that stable, cross-situational virtues of the sort Franklin tracked are largely illusory, and that behavior is driven far more by incidental situational pressures than by settled character traits. I'd like your best reconstruction of how Franklin himself would respond to this challenge, drawing on the specifics of his method: his insistence that virtue must be acquired through deliberate practice rather than mere exhortation (the never-written "Art of Virtue" he says he intended to compose), his decision to work on the virtues one at a time in rotation rather than all thirteen simultaneously, and his own wry admission that even pursuing humility left him newly proud of it. Does his project have the resources to absorb the situationists' evidence, or does it quietly presuppose exactly the kind of stable character they deny exists? Engage with the strongest form of their argument, and be explicit about which of Franklin's specific commitments are doing the work in your answer.
