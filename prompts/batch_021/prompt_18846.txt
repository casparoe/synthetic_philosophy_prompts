I'm a retired entomologist -- spent almost forty years studying leafcutter ants -- and now that I've got time on my hands I've been auditing an intro metaphysics course at the community college, mostly for fun. Last week we hit Peter van Inwagen's "special composition question" and I about spilled my coffee, because its basically the exact question that quietly haunted my whole career without anyone in entomology ever framing it that way: a leafcutter colony behaves in every functional respect like a single organism -- division of labor, homeostasis, something like a collective immune system, even a kind of shared memory laid down in pheromone trails -- so is the colony actually one thing composed of all those ants, or is "the colony" just a handy word for a swarm of seperate ants that never really add up to any further object at all? My instructor sketched three camps: one says composition never happens and only simples exist, full stop; another says any bunch of things whatsoever, no matter how gerrymandered, always adds up to some further object; and van Inwagen's own view says composition happens only when the parts' activity adds up to a "life." I left class with no idea which of these is supposed to be the sober, sensible view and which ones are the crazy ones.

So here's my ask: write me an essay that actually adjudicates the special composition question instead of just cataloguing the positions. Take the three camps above, plus any other serious contender that deserves a hearing, lay out the strongest argument for each -- I gather there's some sort of "vagueness" argument meant to rule out any answer that isn't all-or-nothing, and something about the nihilists owing us an account of what breaks the window if there was never really a baseball there in the first place -- and don't leave me hanging with "reasonable people disagree." Pick a winner and defend it the way you'd defend it to a skeptical colleague. And since it's what started all this, tell me what your answer implies for my ants: was I right to treat a colony as a genuine individual all those years, or was that just a convenient fiction?
