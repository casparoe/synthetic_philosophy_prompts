My father is retiring next month after thirty-six years as an insurance claims adjuster, and his department is throwing him a retirement dinner at which I've been asked to give the toast. He has never pretended the job was more than a job: he took it in his twenties because it paid steadily and let him raise a family, and the warmest word he's ever used for it is "reliable." What he has loved without reservation for just as long is restoring antique clocks in his garage on weekends -- a hobby that earns him nothing and that he would obviously do for free forever.

When I told him I was nervous about writing the toast, he gave me an actual instruction: don't stand up in front of his colleagues and call the job his calling, because everyone who has spent a career doing similar work will hear the falseness in that immediately, but don't make it sound like thirty-six years were just dead time before the clocks either, because he doesn't think that's true and it would be unkind to everyone in the room who has given their working life to the same sort of job. The dinner will be roughly two-thirds coworkers and one-third family and fellow clock enthusiasts, so both the flattering version and the dismissive version of his career will have people present who'd notice if I got it wrong.

This has had me thinking about Moritz Schlick's 1927 essay "On the Meaning of Life" -- an odd little piece from a man mostly known as a hard-nosed logical positivist and a founder of the Vienna Circle -- that has stuck with me since I read it in a philosophy course years ago. Schlick's claim is that an activity is meaningful in proportion to how much it's enjoyed for its own sake, in the moment, rather than performed as a means to some further end; he calls this quality "play," in contrast to "work," which we tolerate only for what it produces and which always invites one more question -- "and what is that for?" -- one step further down the chain. He also ties the capacity for play to youth, which fits my father oddly well, since he seems more like himself, and somehow younger, hunched over a disassembled clock than he ever did commuting to the office. I would like the toast built around this distinction, but I can't quite see how to make it land as a tribute rather than a philosophy lecture. Could you write a five-minute toast that takes Schlick's idea seriously and uses it to say something honest about my father's career and his retirement, without either dressing the job up as something it wasn't or letting that honesty come across as an insult to him or to the coworkers listening?
