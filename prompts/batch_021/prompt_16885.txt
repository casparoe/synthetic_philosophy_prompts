Okay so I'm a phil PhD student working on moral responsibility stuff, and this term I TA'd a seminar that spent two whole sessions on standing to blame, which kind of broke my brain in a good way. We read G.A. Cohen's "Casting the First Stone" essay plus some of the newer responses to it, and I can't stop turning over whether there's actually two different things getting mashed together under the "who are you to talk" complaint. There's the classic hypocrite case: someone who rails against lying constantly but lies just as much themselves, i.e. holds everyone else to a standard they exempt themselves from. But then theres a different case, closer to the old "tu quoque" idea: someone who has done the exact same bad thing as the person they're blaming, but has never actually preached about it one way or the other, so there's no double standard in any strict sense, just shared guilt. Like, I've got one friend who cheated on a partner years ago but has always stayed quiet/nonjudgmental about cheating in general, and another friend who also cheated but is weirdly the most self-righteous person I know about how cheaters are irredeemable. Both would probably get some version of "who are you to talk" if they called out a mutual friend for cheating, but my gut says the complaint against them isn't quite the same complaint.

So here's what I want your take on: is "hypocrisy" (the double-standard thing) and "mere complicity"/tu quoque (shared fault, no double standard) really just two names for one underlying standing-defeater, or are they genuinely separable conditions that can come apart, i.e. can you be hypocritical without being complicit, or complicit without being a hypocrite? And if they are separable, which one is doing more of the actual philosophical work in explaining why compromised blame feels illegitimate - is it fundamentally about fairness/consistency, or fundamentally about the blamer's own guilt regardless of consistency? My advisor thinks I'm overcomplicating a distinction without a difference, so I'd love an actual argument for or against that, not just a survey of the positions.
