Now retired after forty years as an evolutionary biologist, I have only recently begun reading philosophy of science in earnest. I keep returning to the long-running debate between etiological accounts of biological function—in the tradition of Larry Wright—and Robert Cummins's causal-role account. The case I can't stop turning over is exaptation: a trait such as feathers, whose current contribution to flight seems to have little to do with whatever effect it was originally selected for. Please describe the most important implications of this debate for how a biologist should answer the question of what such a trait is "really" for once its current useful effect and its selective history diverge, and whether biologists' routine need to invoke concepts like exaptation—and the further idea that a co-opted trait can subsequently be refined by selection for its new role—suggests that the etiological account is straining to fit how the word "function" actually gets used in practice.
