Since retiring from a career in statistical mechanics, I have been auditing a philosophy of science course, and the instructor - aware of my quantitative background - asked me to help mark a short-answer question on the principle of indifference. I would like an independent, rigorous assessment of one submission before I enter my own marks, so that my grading isn't simply confirming my first impression.

The question set for the students was:

"Bertrand's chord paradox shows that the instruction 'choose a chord of a circle at random' admits several inequivalent formalizations, yielding probabilities of 1/2, 1/3, and 1/4 that the chord is longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle. Explain how E. T. Jaynes proposed to resolve the paradox by imposing invariance requirements on the chord-selection process, state which classical construction his resolution favors and why, and briefly assess whether the result rehabilitates the principle of indifference or instead exposes its limits."

Here is the answer submitted:

"Bertrand's paradox arises because 'a random chord' is ambiguous. Choosing the two endpoints uniformly on the circumference gives P = 1/3; dropping a perpendicular from a uniformly chosen point along a radius gives P = 1/2; and choosing the chord's midpoint uniformly within the disc gives P = 1/4. Since the problem statement never specifies which variable ignorance should be spread evenly over, naive indifference is underdetermined: it can be applied consistently to different variables and still yield conflicting answers. Jaynes's move was to stop asking which variable should be uniform and instead ask which distribution is invariant under the symmetries that any reasonable physical chord-dropping procedure ought to respect. He required the answer not to change if the circle is shifted slightly or rescaled, and, crucially, showed that rotational invariance is what rules out the endpoint and midpoint constructions, since only the radial construction treats every direction alike. Solving the resulting invariance conditions picks out a distribution agreeing with the random-radius method, so the paradox resolves to P = 1/2, matching results Jaynes describes from physical straw-tossing trials. Jaynes therefore does not so much abandon the principle of indifference as vindicate it: once indifference is applied to the correctly, invariantly-selected variable, the earlier disagreement dissolves, and Laplace's original instinct - that ignorance licenses a uniform distribution over some variable - turns out to have been right all along."
