Two people on my team got into it at work last week and I can't stop thinking about it. Our data science lead (Reema) and one of our eng managers (Josh) were arguing over whether we should always run one more experiment before shipping a change. Reema's claim: there's an actual theorem in decision theory (she called it Good's theorem) saying that if you update on evidence rationally and the information itself is free, looking before you decide can never make your expected outcome worse -- worst case it's a wash -- so skipping a cheap test is just leaving value on the table. Josh said that's obviously wrong in practice: he's watched teams talk themselves out of a good call after a noisy result spooked everyone, and one time running a test tipped off another team internally, who copied the idea and shipped first.

Reema's comeback: both of those are the theorem's assumptions failing, not the theorem itself failing -- panicking over noisy data isn't updating rationally, and a test that leaks your plans to a rival team isn't actually free, so neither is a real counterexample.

Josh said fine, maybe it's technically true, but then it's a claim about frictionless ideal agents and useless for telling an actual team what to do, so she can't just invoke "the theorem" like it settles thier roadmap argument.

Reema said it's still useful even so, because it tells you where to put the blame when more info seems to backfire -- a hidden cost, or bad execution -- instead of shrugging that ignorance was somehow better, and that blame is usually findable and fixable.

Josh's last word: "we just executed badly" is basically unfalsifiable after the fact, and if a team predictably mishandles ambiguous results every time for structural reasons that aren't changing, calling that an "execution problem" instead of an "information problem" is a distinction without a practical difference.
