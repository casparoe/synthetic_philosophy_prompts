I record a monthly podcast on philosophy of logic and language, and next week's guest is a philosopher who has built her reputation defending a supervaluationist solution to the sea-battle problem from Aristotle's *De Interpretatione* IX. On her view, a sentence like "there will be a sea battle tomorrow," uttered today, is true just in case it comes out true on every historically possible continuation of the present moment, false just in case it comes out false on all of them, and neither true nor false when the continuations disagree with one another -- even though a classically valid schema like "there will be a sea battle or there will not" stays true throughout, since excluded middle holds on each continuation taken by itself. I've been sitting in on a graduate seminar on tense logic this term, so I already know the elevator-pitch version of the position and would rather not spend the episode re-covering that ground.

Please draft nine or ten interview questions that press on the places specialists actually push back on this view, rather than on generic hand-wringing about fatalism. I'd like at least one question on how she squares a determinately true disjunction with two indeterminate disjuncts -- doesn't that sever the ordinary link between a disjunction's truth and the truth of one of its parts? -- one on why truth-value gaps should be preferred to simply designating one continuation as the "actual" future and keeping bivalence intact (i.e., why not a thin red line instead?); one on whether tomorrow's outcome is allowed to reach backward and retroactively fix what was already true today, once the gap closes; one on why ordinary speakers seem completely at ease flatly asserting future-tense claims like "it will rain tomorrow" if most such claims are, on her account, neither true nor false; and one on what her semantics implies, if anything, for the old theological question of whether an omniscient being can know the truth-value of a claim that doesn't yet have one. For each of these, add a short follow-up that anticipates the reply she's most likely to give and presses further, rather than a question that just lets her restate the framework.
