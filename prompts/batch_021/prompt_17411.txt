During my career as an astrophysicist, I spent a good deal of time thinking about inflationary cosmology and the possibility that the universe extends, causally disconnected, far beyond what any observer could ever see — a genuinely "large world" in more than the everyday sense. Since retiring I have taken up philosophy in a fairly serious way, and a few months ago, while reading through some of the effective altruism and longtermism literature, I came across an idea that ties my old cosmological preoccupations to decision theory: evidential cooperation in large worlds, or ECL.

As I understand it, the argument runs roughly as follows: if the universe, or multiverse, contains a great many agents who reason in ways relevantly similar to us but with whom we can never causally interact, then our own choice to give some weight to the concerns of value systems other than our own is nonetheless evidence about how those causally isolated agents will choose, so that widespread mutual "cooperation" of this kind leaves everyone better off in expectation than universal "defection" would — a prisoner's dilemma played out across the cosmos rather than across a table. This struck me immediately as a grown-up, cosmically scaled descendant of something I remembered from Douglas Hofstadter's old writing on "superrationality": his claim that two sufficiently similar rational agents facing a single, symmetric Prisoner's Dilemma should each reason that whatever is rational for one must be rational for the other, and should therefore both cooperate, even in the total absence of any causal influence over one another's choice.

I would like you to compare evidential cooperation in large worlds with Hofstadter's superrationality in some depth. Where exactly does ECL's justification for cooperation depend on the very same logical structure as superrationality, and where must it go further — for instance, in moving from two agents in a perfectly symmetric, near-identical situation to a vast and diverse population of merely correlated agents who may hold quite different values, and in leaning explicitly on evidential rather than causal decision theory? On balance, would you say ECL is best described as a fairly direct extension of superrationality to a cosmological setting, or does it rest on additional assumptions substantial enough that the two ought to be regarded as importantly different arguments? I would also be curious, in passing, whether you think any of this bears a deeper resemblance to Kant's injunction to act only on maxims one could will to be universal law, or whether that resemblance is merely superficial.
