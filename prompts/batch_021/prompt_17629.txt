Okay so I've spent way too many hours in the Bayesian corner of the internet (started on LessWrong years ago, still lurk on some of the diaspora blogs) and there's this one issue I keep seeing people gesture at without ever actually resolving: the problem of old evidence. You know the setup -- Mercury's perihelion had been precessing in this unexplained way since like the 1850s, everyone agreed it was a real anomaly, and then Einstein's GR predicted the exact excess in 1915 with no fudge factors, and basically everyone (including Einstein himself) treated that as huge confirmation of the theory. Except if you're a strict Bayesian and E is "the perihelion precesses by that amount," then P(E) was already 1 for Einstein before he'd even started on GR, so the whole P(E|H)/P(E) ratio just collapses to 1 and there's no confirmation boost at all, by the actual math. Which seems nuts, because obviously the Mercury result WAS excellent evidence for GR, so something has gone wrong with naive Bayesian confirmation theory here.

I brought this up with some friends from an old reading group and one of them mentioned the Garber/Jeffrey/Niiniluoto move of dropping logical omniscience (so realizing "H entails E" becomes the real new evidence you conditionalize on), another brought up Howson's counterfactual-probability idea instead, and then a third person said both of those only fix the "dynamic," psychological half of the puzzle, and that theres a separate "static" version of the problem that never actually goes away no matter what fix you bolt on. Nobody could agree on which of these (if any) actually rescues Bayesianism, and I don't have the formal background to adjudicate it myself. Could you write me an essay that lays out the main proposed solutions to the problem of old evidence, actually weighs them against the obvious objections to each, and then commits to a real verdict on whether Bayesian confirmation theory can be saved here (versus needing some non-Bayesian patch, or just biting a bullet)? Please don't just land on "reasonable people disagree" -- actually stake out a position and defend it.
