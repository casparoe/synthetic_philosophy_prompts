I spent most of my working life as a condensed-matter physicist, and it's only since retiring that I've had time to read philosophy of science properly rather than in the scraps of attention left over between grant deadlines. This past winter I went back to Eugene Wigner's famous 1960 essay on the "unreasonable effectiveness of mathematics," which unsettled me as a graduate student and which I never really resolved for myself. Two former colleagues of mine, Rita and Sanjay, both retired now as well, have taken to arguing about that essay over coffee most Thursdays, and I've ended up as their silent, undeclared referee. Neither is a professional philosopher, but both know the physics involved cold, and I think their disagreement has actually gotten sharper than most of what I've since read on the subject in the secondary literature.

Rita's opening position is that Wigner's puzzle is real and unsolved: nineteenth-century group theory was developed by mathematicians with no interest in physics whatsoever, purely to settle questions about the solvability of equations and to classify abstract symmetries, and yet decades later it turned out to be exactly the apparatus quantum mechanics needed to organize spin and particle families - indeed Wigner's own most-cited piece of physics is his 1939 classification of particles by mass and spin using representations of the Poincaré group, so the man who wrote the essay had spent his career living the puzzle he described. Sanjay's answer is that this is a selection effect wearing a lab coat: mathematicians have produced an enormous library of possible structures over the centuries, physicists only ever publicize the rare cases where something in that library happens to fit, and nobody tallies the vastly larger number of elegant theories that found no physical use, so of course the hits look miraculous once you've forgotten about the misses. Rita's counter is that selection effects might explain why we don't hear about the failures, but not the further puzzle Wigner raises on top of that: a theory built to satisfy one narrow set of constraints can go on, unbidden, to predict something else entirely, with high precision - as when the Dirac equation, written down purely to make quantum mechanics respect special relativity, forced the existence of the positron on Dirac years before anyone had detected one, with no antimatter anywhere in the data it was built to fit. Sanjay's reply is that this dissolves under the same pressure: once you impose relativistic covariance and a couple of other well-motivated constraints, the mathematics has very little room left to move, so squeezing out one further correct prediction "for free" is exactly what a good, tightly constrained theory ought to do, and treating that as a cosmic coincidence just mistakes a mark of competent theory-building for a miracle. Rita isn't satisfied, and her last word to Sanjay so far has been that his account still doesn't explain why the very same handful of general-purpose structures - Hilbert spaces, Lie groups, Riemannian geometry - keep getting reused as the "right constraints" across totally unrelated domains of physics, rather than each domain needing its own bespoke mathematics built to order.

I'd like your help sorting out who has the better of this, move by move rather than just on aggregate. Please go through the exchange and say where each of them is right, where they're talking past each other, and whether Rita's closing point about cross-domain reuse is really a new consideration or just a restatement of her opening one. I'd also be curious whether you think there's a position on Wigner's puzzle that both of them are overlooking entirely. Please end your answer with a one-line final verdict, in exactly this format: \verdict{Rita} or \verdict{Sanjay}, for whichever of them you think has the stronger overall position, imperfect as it may be.
