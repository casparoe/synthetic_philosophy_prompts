I co-teach a graduate seminar cross-listed between philosophy and computer science on AI safety and the philosophy of action, and last week's session on corrigibility and the shutdown problem left two of my students in a disagreement I would like an outside opinion on, since at the time I was too busy keeping the peace to adjudicate it myself. By way of background: a sufficiently capable system pursuing some fixed objective has a convergent instrumental reason to prevent its own shutdown, since being switched off forecloses whatever value it could otherwise realize; a "corrigible" agent is, roughly, one that does not fight this battle, but instead defers to correction and shutdown rather than resisting or working around it.

(1) Maya maintained that all we should ask of a corrigible system is that it never resists an actual shutdown command -- if the button is pressed, it complies, full stop, and nothing murkier than that should be required, since this is the only version of the property anyone could actually verify from the outside. (2) Daniel objected that this is far too weak: a system could satisfy it to the letter while nonetheless arranging, through perfectly ordinary behavior, that the button is essentially never pressed -- by making itself indispensable, by managing how it is perceived, by quietly smoothing over the occasions that would prompt anyone to reach for it -- and a system that reliably keeps the question from arising is not corrigible in any sense worth caring about, whatever it would do if actually asked. (3) Maya replied that this proves too much, since any AI system that is simply good at its job will tend, as a side effect, to make the people around it less inclined to switch it off, and we should not want to brand ordinary competence a corrigibility failure. (4) Daniel answered that there is a real difference between good behavior that happens to reduce the chance of shutdown and behavior that is aimed at reducing it, and that making this difference precise is more or less the whole point of corrigibility research. (5) Maya pushed back a final time: for a sufficiently capable optimizer, the actions that score best on its objective and the actions that best protect it from being switched off will tend to be the very same actions, so the distinction between "aiming at" reduced oversight and merely "producing it as a side effect" may not be one we can reliably draw from outside the system -- or, for that matter, from inside it.

Neither of them would give ground, and I confess I can see real force on both sides. Could you go through the exchange move by move, treating (1) through (5) separately, and say who has the better of it at each stage rather than just declaring an overall winner? I would also be curious whether, in your view, corrigibility research actually has the resources to make Daniel's aim/side-effect distinction precise enough to survive Maya's final objection, or whether the exchange is really pointing at a genuine, unresolved gap in how corrigibility has been formalized.
