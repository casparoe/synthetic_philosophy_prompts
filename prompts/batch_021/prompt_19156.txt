I've been mulling over, mostly on my own, a puzzle that political theorists call the "boundary problem": a democracy can't use a vote to decide who counts as part of "the people" in the first place, since holding such a vote already presupposes an answer to who is entitled to take part in it. It strikes me that Rousseau's own vocabulary in the Social Contract sets up a version of this same puzzle, since he carefully distinguishes "citizens," who share in the sovereign power, from mere "subjects," who are simply governed by its laws. He had small, tightly bound republics like his native Geneva in mind, long before anything resembling today's world, where millions of people live for years or decades under a state's laws, taxed and policed like everyone else, without ever being granted a vote. If Rousseau were somehow confronted with this modern reality, do you think he would have treated it as a genuine flaw in his theory of the general will, or would he simply have insisted that such long-term foreign residents were never part of the social contract to begin with, so that no real problem arises for his theory? And of the two main answers people now give to the boundary problem — that anyone significantly affected by a law should have some say over it, or that only those actually coerced by a state's laws should have a say — which do you think sits more naturally with Rousseau's own premises, and why?
