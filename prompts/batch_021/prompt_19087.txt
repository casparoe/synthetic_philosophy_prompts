I'm a political theory PhD student working on a dissertation chapter about how early modern skepticism about moral knowledge shaped theories of political authority, and I've become fairly attached to a short fragment from Pascal's Pensées, often cited under the heading "Justice, force." There Pascal writes that justice without force is powerless, that force without justice is tyrannical, and that—since we could not manage to make what is just strong—we have instead arranged things so that whatever is strong simply gets called just. I've been reading this as Pascal's own, more compressed version of a move Hobbes makes at much greater length: because unaided reason cannot settle what justice actually requires, "justice" in political life practically collapses into whatever the dominant power decides to label just, with normative language draped over it after the fact. When I mentioned this reading to my advisor, she said I was "skipping past the argument Pascal is actually making," but we ran out of time before she could elaborate.

What I can't quite square is that elsewhere in the Pensées, Pascal keeps insisting that a real justice (and a real truth more generally) exists, even though our reason, left to itself, cannot get at it—which seems to be a large part of his case for why we need faith at all. So either this is a genuine inconsistency on Pascal's part, asserting in one breath that real justice exists and in the next that it amounts to nothing more than force, or I'm oversimplifying what the justice-force fragment is doing when I read it as a Hobbesian reduction. Since this comparison is going to anchor a fairly important section of my chapter, I'd like to get it right: which is it, and if I'm oversimplifying, what would be a more accurate way of stating Pascal's actual position?
