I'm a novelist currently at work on a book narrated by a former sommelier turned restaurant critic, and in the course of research I've been spending more time at wine dinners than I probably should, with friends who take the subject far more seriously than I do. At the most recent one, two of them — Sofia, who did a stint in sensory science before switching to law, and Dev, who's finishing a psychology PhD — pulled a trick they'd read about somewhere: tinting a good Sauvignon Blanc a deep red with a drop of flavourless food dye, then pouring it for the table without comment. Sure enough, most of us reached for red-wine words — cherry, a bit of tannin, darker fruit — even though several of the same tasters had, twenty minutes earlier, described that exact bottle in terms of citrus and green apple when it was poured with no colouring at all.

Once the trick came out, Sofia and Dev fell into an argument I haven't been able to stop thinking about. Sofia's claim was that the experiment shows taste isn't an insulated sense at all: what the eyes report can reach in and change what the tongue and nose actually deliver to consciousness, so perception can't be sealed off from whatever other information the mind has on hand. Dev pushed back that all the experiment really shows is that people's descriptions shifted, not their experiences — maybe everyone tasted the wine exactly as they had the first time and simply reached for the wrong words once red-wine vocabulary was primed by the colour, which would be a change in judgment or labelling rather than in perception itself. Sofia answered that this move proves too much: if any result can always be redescribed as "just the labels changing," the claim that taste is sealed off from outside influence becomes unfalsifiable — and in any case this wasn't some low-level mixing of two senses, since these are people with years of stored association between colour and flavour vocabulary, so the dye was triggering a genuinely conceptual expectation, not raw cross-sensory noise. Dev's rejoinder was that even granting all of that, there's a real difference between an expectation changing what you attend to or notice in a glass — which nobody disputes — and it changing the actual phenomenal taste itself, and Sofia hadn't shown the second, much stronger thing.

Neither of them would give an inch, and I honestly can't tell who came out ahead. Could you go through the exchange move by move and say who has the better of it at each stage, rather than just declaring an overall winner? I'd also like to know whether either position has a version that survives the other's best objection, and, more generally, what you think the two of them are overlooking — including whether they're simply talking past each other about what would even count as evidence either way.
