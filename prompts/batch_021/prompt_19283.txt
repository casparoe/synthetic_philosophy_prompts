So I'm a software engineer with basically zero philosophy background (one gen-ed ethics class in college, that's it), but I went down a rabbit hole on just war theory last month after an argument with my father-in-law about drone strikes, and ended up writing up some notes trying to connect one piece of it to something I actually know about from my job: computer security. Would love some honest comments before I post this anywhere and embarass myself.

Here's what I've got:

There's this idea called the "moral equality of combatants" (comes from Walzer's Just and Unjust Wars) — basically that soldiers on both sides of a war have an equal right to kill each other on the battlefield regardless of whose cause is actually just, as long as both sides stick to the in bello rules (no targeting civilians, proportionality, etc). The reasoning is that jus ad bellum (was the war itself justified) and jus in bello (was it fought fairly) are totally separate questions, so a soldier can be fighting for a rotten cause and still be doing nothing wrong personally, as long as he plays by the rules.

McMahan's book Killing in War basically torches this. His argument is that the unjust side's soldiers were never morally licensed to kill in the first place, since their targets never did anything to make themselves liable to lethal force. Meanwhile the just side's soldiers ARE licensed, because the unjust combatants made themselves liable just by participating in the unjust threat. So there's a real moral asymmetry hiding underneath, even though the laws of war (and Walzer) treat both sides identically.

What I can't stop chewing on: does any of this map onto state-sponsored hacking? Say an APT group bricks a hospital's network as part of some invasion, versus a defending country's cyber unit that hacks back and fries the attacker's infrastructure. Both sides make the same "we followed proportionality and discrimination" arguments about their ops. If McMahan's right, only one set of hackers is actually in the moral clear, even though they look completely symmetric from the outside (and under international law).

The bit I can't resolve: a lot of state hackers are basically just employees — sometimes conscripted, sometimes just doing a job under a regime where refusing gets you disappeared, not ideologues who chose to serve an unjust threat. That feels like it should lower their liability even inside McMahan's own framework, which almost drags you back toward something like moral equality, at least for that subset of people. Not sure if that's an actual insight or just me re-discovering an objection that's already been beaten to death in the literature.

So — does my Walzer vs McMahan summary actually hold up or am I mangling it somewhere? Does the hacker analogy break down somewhere obvious that i'm just not seeing? And is the coercion point worth anything, or is it a well known dead end?
