So I'm reporting a longer piece on how the rationalist/EA crowd argues for their decision theory commitments, and money pumps keep coming up as the go-to justification for why your preferences have to be transitive — the classic "if you prefer A to B, B to C, and C to A, a bookie can trade you around the loop and drain you of cash forever" story. I've got that part down fine. But last week I was on a call with a philosopher for the piece and she mentioned, almost in passing, that the argument secretly depends on the agent being "naive" about their own future choices, and that a "sophisticated" chooser, or what she called a "resolute" chooser (I think that term's from a guy named McClennen?), could have the exact same cyclical preferences and never get pumped at all. That threw me, because it seems like it kind of pulls the rug out from under the whole argument — if you can dodge the pump just by being cleverer about how you choose over time, does the money pump actually show that intransitivity itself is irrational, or just that naivety is?

Could you give me a fairly granular walkthrough of this? Specifically: what's doing the work, step by step in the trade sequence, that makes a naive agent vulnerable in the first place; what a sophisticated or resolute agent does differently at each of those same steps that lets them keep their cash while supposedly still having the identical underlying preferences; and whether theres some patched-up version of the money pump that gets a fully foresighted agent anyway. I'd also like to get straight what people mean when they contrast "synchronic" and "diachronic" rationality in this context -- is the idea that transitivity is a constraint on your preferences at a single moment, while avoiding money pumps is really more a constraint on consistency of choice across time, and if so, why does that distinction end up mattering for whether the whole argument even works?
