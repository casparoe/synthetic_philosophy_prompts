I am preparing a session on the Vienna Circle for my graduate seminar, and before I stand in front of students I want to pressure-test a conflation I keep meeting in their draft papers, and occasionally in print as well. Otto Neurath's "picture language," laid out most fully in the 1936 pamphlet he wrote in Basic English for maximum accessibility to set down the rules of Isotype -- the pictogram system he built with the artist Gerd Arntz for the Social and Economic Museum in Vienna -- imposed quite specific constraints: greater quantity had to be shown by repeating a unit symbol rather than by enlarging a single one, and he was candid that the system could not depict negation, tense, or logical connectives at all. Students who know that Neurath sat in the Vienna Circle alongside people steeped in the early Wittgenstein tend to assume, on the strength of the shared word alone, that Isotype must be some applied cousin of the picture theory of meaning from the Tractatus, on which a proposition depicts a state of affairs by sharing its logical form with it.

I would like you to test that conflation rather than simply grant my framing of it, if you think the framing is tendentious. Set out what "picture" is actually doing in each account, and say whether Neurath's project is best read as a continuation of Wittgenstein's semantic idea, a repurposing of its vocabulary toward a quite different practical end, or something closer to a rival to it -- bearing in mind how unsentimental Neurath generally was about the mystical residue the Tractatus leaves unsayable at its close, and how selectively the rest of the Vienna Circle tended to draw on Wittgenstein once they had taken what they wanted from him. I am especially keen to know whether the very limitations Neurath owned up to in his picture language, the missing negation and connectives above all, mark a deep structural difference between the two notions of "picture," given that Wittgenstein's picture theory was built precisely to explain how logical form, negation included, gets depicted in the first place.
