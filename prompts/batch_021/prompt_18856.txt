So at a dinner thing last week someone mentioned that her sister won't fly without a couple drinks in her, even though she'll straight-up tell you she knows flying is way safer statistically than the drive to the airport. Two other people at the table got into this whole back-and-forth about what that actually shows about what emotions even are, and I can't stop thinking about it.

(1) Sam said: if she genuinely doesnt believe the flight is dangerous, then calling her fear "about" danger is just wrong -- there's no danger-representing state going on, it's just adrenaline that gets relabeled as fear after the fact.

(2) Leah said no, it's like the Müller-Lyer illusion -- the two lines still look unequal even once you've measured them and know they're the same length -- and fear works the same way: it represents danger without asserting it, which is exactly why you can be scared and know better at the same time.

(3) Sam came back with: that analogy proves too much. The illusion comes from one fixed, low-level visual system that processes line lengths basically the same way in everybody. Fear of flying, fear of spiders, and stage fright come from totally different personal histories and don't share any comparable dedicated mechanism, so "it represents danger" is just charity we're extending to the bodily agitation, not something built into the state itself.

(4) Leah replied that a perception doesn't need its own dedicated organ to have real content -- what makes something count as a perception of danger is its functional role (it's reliably triggered by danger-relevant cues and disposes you to flee or freeze), and we already accept that kind of functional, non-anatomical individuation for stuff like pain or interoception.

Neither of them budged. Can you go through this move by move and say who's got the better of it at each step (they can both be partly right at a given step), then say what, if anything, you think they're both missing overall?
