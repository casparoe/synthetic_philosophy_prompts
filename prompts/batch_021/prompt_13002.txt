Be sure to say exactly what disjunctivism denies, and comment on whether a disjunctivist should treat the case of illusion (e.g., a straight stick that looks bent when half-submerged in water) the same way as a case of total hallucination of a stick that isn't there at all."

And here is the student's answer, reproduced exactly as submitted: "The argument from illusion starts from the fact that objects can perceptually appear to have qualities they don't actually have: a coin viewed at an angle looks elliptical even though it's round, and a stick half-submerged in water looks bent even though it's straight. The Phenomenal Principle says that if something perceptually appears to have a certain quality, then there is something the perceiver is actually aware of that has that quality. Since the coin itself isn't elliptical and the stick itself isn't bent, what the perceiver is aware of in these cases can't be the physical object -- it has to be some further, mind-dependent item, a sense-datum, that really is elliptical or bent. That gives us sense-data in the illusory cases. The argument then generalizes to ordinary veridical perception via the spreading step: because an illusory experience can be subjectively indistinguishable from a veridical one of the same apparent scene, and experiences that are indistinguishable in this way ought to get the same fundamental analysis, the sense-datum conclusion spreads to veridical perception too. So on this picture we are never directly aware of physical objects at all, only of sense-data from which we infer the rest. Disjunctivism, first developed by John McDowell to block Gettier-style counterexamples to the traditional analysis of knowledge as justified true belief, and later extended by Martin and others back into the philosophy of perception proper, resists exactly this spreading step. It denies the Common Kind Assumption -- the assumption that the veridical and non-veridical members of a matching pair must share a fundamental psychological nature -- and holds instead that veridical perception is a distinct kind of state, constituted by the mind-independent object itself, so that nothing about the illusory case can be read into it. On the illusion side, since the stick is really there, the disjunctivist can say the perception itself is accurate and the bend is really just a false belief the viewer forms about the stick's shape; hallucination is the harder case, since there's no object at all, so there the disjunctivist has to fall back on a purely negative, epistemic characterization: an experience indiscriminable from a veridical one, full stop."
