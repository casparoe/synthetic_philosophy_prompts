"Kripke's discussion of Wittgenstein's rule-following considerations centers on a sceptical paradox about meaning. The worry is introduced through the plus/quus example: suppose that in the past I have only ever computed sums involving numbers smaller than 57, and I am now asked for the value of '68 + 57.' I confidently answer '125,' intending to apply the addition function I have always used. But Kripke's sceptic points out that my past usage is equally compatible with my having meant the deviant function quus, where x⊕y equals x+y for the values I have previously computed but equals 5 whenever either argument is 57 or greater. Because our epistemic access to a person's past rule-following is always mediated by a finite sample of responses, no amount of behavioural evidence could settle which function was intended; the paradox is fundamentally an evidential one, since if we could somehow examine every fact about a speaker's history directly, the indeterminacy would presumably disappear. The sceptic's challenge, then, is to identify a fact — behavioural, dispositional, or mental — that could settle which function I mean.

Kripke considers and rejects several candidates, including qualitative mental states and a Platonist appeal to a rule 'grasped' in the mind, on the grounds that any finite rule formulated in the head simply reproduces the interpretive gap at one further remove. He also shows that the most natural response — that meaning plus consists in a disposition to answer with sums — cannot succeed, since dispositions are as finite and error-prone as actual past performance. This rejection of dispositionalism is itself Kripke's sceptical solution: our talk of meaning must instead be grounded in dispositions to respond shared across a community, checked against one another, rather than in facts about isolated individuals. This move echoes Hume on causation: rather than finding a necessary connection in the objects, Hume relocates causal talk in habits of expectation, and Kripke similarly relocates meaning-talk in communal practice instead of individual fact. On this basis, I would say the sceptical solution succeeds: it does not pretend that meaning-facts exist where they do not, but it explains why our practice of ascribing meaning to one another remains legitimate and useful."
