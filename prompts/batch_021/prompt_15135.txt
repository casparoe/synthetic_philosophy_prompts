I spent thirty years as a reactor physicist, much of that time on the safety systems that let us SCRAM a core -- shut the whole reaction down -- within seconds of a bad reading. Since retiring I have been auditing a philosophy department's seminar on AI safety, and its organizers, wanting a concrete opening before a day of theory, have asked me to give ten minutes of opening remarks at their workshop on the 'shutdown problem': why it is so difficult to build an advanced AI system that will reliably let itself be switched off. Half the room will be engineers who assume, as I once did, that this is essentially solved by good interlock design; the other half are decision theorists who know that a sufficiently coherent, goal-directed agent has a standing incentive to resist or manipulate its own shutdown, and that the standard fix for this, so-called utility indifference, has serious problems of its own -- to the point that some now argue genuine corrigibility requires preferences so incomplete that classical rational choice theory forbids them. Could you draft these remarks? They should use my own background honestly, as a real way in rather than false comfort, and should leave the engineers actually understanding why their intuitions do not simply carry over, without letting anyone leave thinking the problem is nearer to solved than it is.
