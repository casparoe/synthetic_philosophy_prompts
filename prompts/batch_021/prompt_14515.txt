I'm a software engineer, and a few months back I was building a small Bayesian localization module for a hobby robotics project when I ran into something that turned out to be a genuine open problem in philosophy rather than a bug in my code. My robot's belief state was a uniform distribution over four map cells, and at one point it received a piece of evidence that wasn't a flat observation but a conditional probability instead — something like "given that you're in the north half, there's a 75% chance you're specifically in the northeast cell rather than the northwest one." When I folded that into the belief state by minimizing KL-divergence relative to the current distribution subject to the new constraint, the update also shifted my credence about the entire southern half, even though the new information was only about how to split the north. A friend with a philosophy background recognized this immediately as a rediscovery of Bas van Fraassen's 1981 "Judy Benjamin problem," and I've been reading around it on and off since, with growing frustration that it doesn't seem to have a clean resolution.

For context, here's the classic version. Judy Benjamin's platoon is dropped into a war-games area split into Blue territory and Red territory, each further divided into a Headquarters area and a Second Company area, giving four equally likely quadrants — Blue-HQ, Blue-2nd, Red-HQ, Red-2nd, each at 1/4 — before any evidence comes in. Judy's own headquarters radios that they can't tell whether she's strayed into Red territory, but that if she has, she's three times more likely to be in their Headquarters area than in the Second Company area, and then the connection is lost. When van Fraassen polled audiences, most people's snap judgment was that Judy's credence in being safely in Blue territory should stay at exactly 1/2, since the message is entirely about the internal Red split and says nothing comparing Red to Blue. But minimizing cross-entropy relative to the uniform prior — the same KL-divergence-based move I'd used, and the standard way of extending Jeffrey conditionalization to this kind of evidence — subject to that constraint instead pushes her credence in Blue up to around 0.53; more strikingly, it can be shown that any reported odds other than 1:1 push it up, regardless of which way those odds point, a pattern van Fraassen himself found suspicious enough to compare to wishful thinking on Judy's part. Yet the flat "always 1/2" answer has its own problem: if the reported odds had gone to infinity — i.e., if headquarters had simply ruled out the Second Company area altogether — ordinary conditionalization would force Judy's credence in Blue up to 2/3, so the invariant answer collapses exactly at the point where the update should be least controversial.

Given all that, I'd like an essay that actually commits to a position, rather than surveying the literature and shrugging. Argue for one of the following, or for something else if you think both are wrong: (a) the "stays at 1/2" intuition is basically correct, and the infinite-odds boundary case needs to be handled some other way than plain conditionalization; (b) cross-entropy/relative-entropy minimization is the right general rule, and its odd, direction-independent shift is simply a correct if counterintuitive fact about rational updating on conditionals; or (c) there's a better rule than either, which you should state and defend. I'd also like you to weigh in on something that has nagged at me specifically as an engineer: machine learning treats KL-divergence minimization as the default, almost automatic way to measure the "closest" update to a distribution, and this puzzle makes me wonder whether that reflex is actually tracking anything philosophically well-motivated, or whether it's just a convenient piece of math we've all agreed to trust. I want a real verdict defended with an argument, not a balanced-sounding non-answer.
