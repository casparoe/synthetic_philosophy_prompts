I co-run my high school's AI-and-philosophy discussion group, and a few weeks ago a friend in the group gave a talk on something called the "Löbian obstacle," which she'd come across while reading old material from MIRI (the Machine Intelligence Research Institute) and LessWrong on AI self-trust. As best I understood her explanation, the problem traces back to Löb's theorem: a sufficiently powerful, consistent formal proof system can never prove, in full generality, the seemingly harmless principle "whatever I can prove is actually true." That's because if it could prove this for every statement, it would, as a special case, be proving its own consistency — and Gödel's second incompleteness theorem says that's only possible for a system that is secretly inconsistent (and an inconsistent system can "prove" absolutely anything, true or false). The upshot for AI, in what's sometimes called the "tiling agents" framework, is that an agent can never fully vouch, in a clean and general way, for a successor that reasons using the very same proof system it does — there's no free logical guarantee that a reasoner just as powerful as you will only reach true conclusions.

Since the talk, I've been reading up on the handful of fixes people have proposed over the years, and I'll admit I'm still fuzzy on how they really stack up against each other. There's "parametric polymorphism," which restructures the proof systems into an indexed hierarchy so that no level has to vouch for one exactly as strong as itself; there's dropping classical proof for calibrated probabilistic belief instead, as in probabilistic reflection or logical induction; and there are "reflective oracles," which dodge the self-reference trap using randomization rather than proof. None of them seem to make the obstacle disappear so much as relocate it, and I can't tell whether that's a sign the obstacle is fundamental or just that nobody's found the right trick yet.

I'm hoping to base my end-of-year research paper for my robotics elective on one of these approaches, so before I commit, I'd like a sense of which ones are actually considered most promising rather than just most famous.
