Me and my friend Priya got into it for like two hours last night about moral luck and neither of us walked away happy, so I want a third opinion. It started with the classic case: two guys drive home equally drunk, equally reckless, same blood alcohol, same road -- one gets home fine, the other hits a kid who steps out from between parked cars. Priya's position was that they're equally blameworthy, full stop, because the only difference between them is luck, not anything they chose, and a decent moral theory can't let something totally outside your control change how blameworthy you are. I agreed with that part. But then I pushed on it: okay, so blame should only track what's "up to you" -- but then isn't the fact that you're even the kind of person who'd drive drunk in the first place also not up to you? That's downstream of your upbringing, your temperament, whatever happened to be in the fridge that night. And if you go back further, the choice to have "one more drink" was itself made by whatever character you had at that moment, which you didn't choose either. Priya said this was a trick and that at some point you have to just stop the regress or nothing is anyone's fault for anything, which she agreed was absurd, but she couldn't tell me where principled the stopping point was supposed to be, other than "somewhere before it gets silly."

I have a vague memory from an undergrad seminar that this is basically the actual structure of Nagel's "Moral Luck" paper -- something about resultant vs. circumstantial vs. constitutive luck (maybe a fourth kind too?) and the worry that once you take the "only judge people for what's in their control" idea seriously, the space of things that are really "yours" shrinks down to almost nothing, which he seems to treat as a genuine paradox rather than something he can neatly solve. I also recall Williams, who I think started this whole debate before Nagel replied to him, has some separate point about a lorry driver and "agent-regret" that's supposed to be relevant but isn't quite the same move. Can you actually walk me through the structure of this argument properly: is the regress from resultant luck to constitutive luck as devastating as it seemed to us last night, and is there a standard, principled way philosophers block it short of just biting the bullet on skepticism about responsibility? I don't want the "there are four types of moral luck, the end" summary I could get off a syllabus -- I want to know whether the actual argument that got Priya stuck holds up, and whether this is a solved problem or still an open wound in the theory of moral responsibility.
