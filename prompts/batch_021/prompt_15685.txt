and here's the student's answer, copied exactly:

"The Oswald/Kennedy pair shows that indicative and subjunctive conditionals with the same antecedent and consequent can diverge in truth value. 'If Oswald didn't kill Kennedy, someone else did' seems true, since we know Kennedy was in fact killed by somebody, but 'If Oswald hadn't killed Kennedy, someone else would have' seems false, since nothing suggests a backup assassin was standing by. Since the two conditionals can come apart like this even holding the antecedent and consequent fixed, Adams concluded indicatives shouldn't be given ordinary truth conditions at all, and should instead be handled by his thesis that the probability of 'if A, C' equals the conditional probability P(C|A). Adams then proved, in his famous 1976 triviality result, that no proposition's probability can track P(C|A) across every probability distribution without collapsing into absurdity, which is exactly why he didn't think indicatives were truth-conditional in the first place. Stalnaker's way of pushing back on this was to offer a possible-worlds semantics, basically the same machinery Lewis uses for counterfactuals: the conditional comes out true just in case the consequent holds at all of the closest worlds where the antecedent holds. That lets Stalnaker keep truth conditions for indicatives, just not ones that satisfy Adams's equation in full generality."
