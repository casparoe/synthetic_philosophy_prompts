I've been sitting in on a philosophy of physics reading group, and this week's session left several of us disagreeing about how two of the field's canonical arguments about the nature of space and spacetime actually relate to one another. The first is Newton's bucket experiment from the Scholium appended to the Definitions at the start of the Principia, together with its companion thought experiment involving two globes. In the bucket case, a spinning bucket of water only develops a concave surface once the water itself starts to rotate, regardless of its motion relative to the bucket; in the globes case, two globes connected by a cord in an otherwise empty universe would develop tension in the cord if the system as a whole is rotating, even though there is no other body for them to rotate relative to. Newton took both examples to show that true rotation cannot be analyzed away as changing relative positions among bodies, and concluded that space itself must be a real, substantival thing relative to which such "true" motions occur.

The second argument is the hole argument, revived by Earman and Norton against substantivalism about the bare spacetime manifold in the setting of general relativity. Because Einstein's field equations are diffeomorphism invariant, one can take any solution and construct a diffeomorphism that acts as the identity everywhere except within some small, matter-free region (the "hole"); dragging the metric along by this map yields a distinct solution that agrees with the original everywhere outside the hole but disagrees inside it. If spacetime points are substantival individuals whose identity is fixed independently of the metric field, this seems to entail a troubling indeterminism, since even a complete specification of matter and geometry everywhere outside the hole fails to fix which of the two solutions obtains within it.

I would like you to compare these two arguments in some depth, rather than simply summarizing each in turn. First, do they really support the same metaphysical conclusion, or is the notion of substantivalism that survives the hole argument importantly different from the notion of absolute space that Newton needed for the bucket argument to succeed? Second, the standard way of blocking the hole argument's indeterminism, often labeled "sophisticated substantivalism," denies that the two diffeomorphic solutions represent distinct physical possibilities in the first place. Is this move a natural refinement of the substantivalist insight behind the bucket argument, or does it concede so much that the resulting view is no longer a recognizable descendant of Newton's own position? Please defend a definite answer rather than simply laying out considerations on both sides.
