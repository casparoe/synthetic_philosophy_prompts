I've been turning over an anecdote a former student told me recently, and I would welcome an outside perspective on it. She took my seminar on philosophy of language a few years back and now works as a project manager at a multinational logistics firm. One of the engineers on her team relocated from abroad about a year ago; his English is fluent and grammatically impeccable, yet he regularly writes things in his status reports that are simply false given the facts on the ground. He describes shipments as "delivered" when they are sitting untouched in a warehouse, or calls a server "offline" while its own monitoring dashboard shows it running normally. Several of his colleagues have quietly started discounting everything he writes, on the assumption that he is careless, or worse, dishonest.

My former student isn't convinced that reaction is fair. She has noticed that his apparent errors are not scattered at random but follow a consistent pattern, which makes her suspect that he may simply be using certain English words in a systematically different way, perhaps carried over from conventions at his previous job, rather than getting the facts wrong. When she tried to settle the matter by asking him directly what he meant, his clarifications turned out to be, of course, just more sentences that themselves stood in need of interpretation, so she didn't feel she had gained any independent foothold on the question. She put it to me this way: is there a principled way to distinguish "he has false beliefs about the shipment and the server" from "he means something different by his words than the rest of the team does," or do the two descriptions collapse into each other once you look closely enough?

I mentioned the case to a couple of colleagues in my department, and we ended up disagreeing about whether this is a determinate question with one correct answer, or a matter where the best one can do is settle on whichever interpretation proves more useful than its rivals. I would be glad to hear how you would advise my former student to make progress on this in practice, and, more importantly, what you take the right general answer to her question to be.
