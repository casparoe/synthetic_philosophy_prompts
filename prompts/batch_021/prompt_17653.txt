In my seminar on promising and obligation this term, we've been contrasting two rival accounts of why promises bind. On one family of views, the obligation is grounded in the trust and expectations that a promise creates in the promisee, whether one explains this, with Hume, as a social convention, or, with Scanlon's "Principle F," as a freestanding moral principle against exploiting the reliance that the promisor's own assurance induced. On David Owens's rival view, the order of explanation runs the other way: promising works by handing the promisee a piece of normative authority over the promisor — a standing to demand performance and to resent its absence — because we have an independent interest in controlling our own normative situation, quite apart from any interest in having our expectations satisfied.

Write a dialogue between a proponent of the trust/expectation view and a proponent of Owens's authority view in which they work through a single case together, each trying to show that it favors their own account and embarrasses the other's. The case: a dying man privately extracts a promise from his daughter that she will scatter his ashes over a particular lake rather than have them interred with the rest of the family. He tells no one else of the request; once he is dead, no one else will ever learn that the promise was made, and nothing about the daughter's reputation or anyone's future trust in her turns on which option she chooses. Is she still bound, and if so, by what, exactly?

Keep both philosophers equally sharp: each should engage the specific point the other has just made rather than simply restate their opening position, and neither should be granted a free final word. End the dialogue at the moment the real crux between them comes into focus, rather than manufacturing a tidy resolution.
