One move I keep running into in rationalist writing on precommitment devices -- Schelling-style precommitment strategies, discussions of akrasia and willpower, and the like -- is the analogy between a constitution and a Ulysses contract. The term shows up, among other places, in psychiatric ethics, where the idea is that a patient with bipolar disorder might, while lucid, sign an agreement authorizing doctors to hospitalize her involuntarily during a future manic episode, overriding whatever objections she raises at the time. The template is Ulysses: foreseeing the Sirens, he has his crew stop their own ears with wax, then has himself bound to the mast with instructions that if he begs to be released once the song starts, they are to tie him tighter and row on regardless. Constitutional theorists borrow this structure to explain things like entrenched rights provisions, judicial review, and supermajority amendment thresholds: the idea being that "We the People," reasoning calmly at the founding, bind our future selves against the mob passions of ordinary politics, exactly as Ulysses bound himself against the Sirens' song.

What I can't work out is why, on this model, a constitution should be amendable at all, let alone as amendable as most real ones are. Ulysses didn't tell his men to leave some slack in the rope in case future-Ulysses came up with a good argument for being untied; the whole device only works because his future pleading is classified in advance as noise, not as evidence to be weighed on its merits. Followed consistently, the logic of the Ulysses contract seems to imply that the most rational constitution is the one that is hardest, ideally impossible, to amend, and that any built-in amendment procedure is really just a confession that the founders didn't trust their own precommitment device. So is the amendability of real-world constitutions basically a bug from the standpoint of precommitment theory, or is there some rationale for it that the Ulysses framing is missing?
