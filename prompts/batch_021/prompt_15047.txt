I'm working on a piece about those AI bird-ID apps like Merlin that can name a species from just a few seconds of song, and for research I tagged along with a friend who's a serious, decades-deep birder on an early morning walk. The app did its usual thing, popping up a species name a beat or two after each call, but my friend kept calling out IDs before it did, with zero apparent effort. When I asked how, he said something that's been stuck in my head since: he swears it's not that he hears the same wall of noise I do and then quickly matches it to a memorized template. He says the calls just sound different to him now — distinct, separated out, almost pre-labeled — in a way they don't for me, standing right next to him hearing what feels like an undifferentiated racket.

That claim is doing a lot of work, and I can't decide if its coherent or just a vivid way of describing something more mundane. "Hearing better" could just mean he's gotten fast and automatic at classifying and attending to sound after the fact, while the raw auditory experience hitting his eardrums is basically identical to mine. Or it could mean something weirder: that all his accumulated bird knowledge has reached backwards and actually changed the raw perceptual experience itself, before any conscious judgment even kicks in, so that two people can end up with genuinely different experiences of the exact same soundwave.

My partner thinks this is obviously just attention and memory dressed up in mystical language, and honestly I'm not sure she's right. Is there a real, principled difference between "knowledge changing what you perceive" and "knowledge changing how fast you interpret or attend to what you already perceive," or is that a distinction without a difference? And is there any way to actually tell which one is happening to my friend, rather than just taking his introspective word for it?
