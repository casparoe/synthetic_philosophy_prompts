Bit of a weird one, but it's been rattling around in my head for a couple weeks now. Some friends from my rationalist meetup (yes, we're the kind of group that's read way too much LessWrong and ACX for our own good) have a running tradition where, before some uncertain event, everyone states their gut probabilities out loud, partly for fun and partly to see who's better calibrated over time.

The most recent round was about a football match with three possible outcomes: the team we all support wins, the other team wins, or it's a draw. I said 55/40/15. One guy, the resident math nerd of the group, pounced on this right away and said that no matter what actually happens on the pitch, he could put together a small set of side bets against my exact numbers so that I end up loosing money regardless of which of the three outcomes occurs -- basically the same trick real bookmakers use to guarantee themselves a profit no matter how the match goes. I did the arithmetic and yeah, my numbers add up to 110%, so that part checks out. But I pushed back and said, ok sure, but nobody is actually going to hand me those bets, I'm not walking into some bookmaker's shop to get picked apart over my football opinions, so why should I care that some hypothetical scheme could exploit me if nobody's ever going to actually run it?

He got kind of exasperated and said I was missing the point -- that the inconsistency is sitting there in my head regardless of whether anyone ever acts on it, and that I do this same kind of thing constantly with all sorts of everyday estimates, it's just normally invisible because nobody bothers working out the exploit. That's the part I can't quite let go of. If the "flaw" never actually costs me anything because no one ever calls it in, is it really a flaw in how I think, or just an artifact of a betting scenario that has nothing to do with my real life? I'd genuinely like to know who's got the better of this argument, not just a "well, both sides have a point" cop-out.
