I'm this year's valedictorian, and I'm struggling with my graduation speech. In our senior ethics elective we spent a week on the puzzle of moral testimony: it seems perfectly fine to believe a fact just because someone trustworthy tells you so, but a lot of philosophers think there's something off about forming a moral belief -- or acting on one -- purely on someone else's say-so, without grasping the reasons yourself, as if what's supposed to matter morally is understanding why something is right, not just landing on the correct answer. The examples in our readings all involved deferring to a wise elder or a professional ethicist. I'm neither of those things -- just another eighteen-year-old who happened to get good grades -- and in three weeks I'm supposed to stand at a podium and, in effect, tell four hundred people how to live. If the pessimists about moral testimony are onto something, that looks like a strange thing for me to be doing, and a stranger thing for anyone to applaud. My ethics teacher will be in the front row and will notice immediately if I wave the problem away; everyone else -- family, cousins, classmates who slept through that unit -- just wants a speech that sends people off feeling something. Could you write it for me? It should run about five minutes, take the problem seriously rather than dodge it, and find some way to move people that doesn't just consist of me handing down moral conclusions on my own unearned authority -- while still working as a real graduation speech and not a seminar on epistemology.
