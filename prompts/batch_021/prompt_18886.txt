I'm taking a seminar this term on early modern political philosophy that pairs Hobbes's Leviathan with several weeks on Pascal's Pensées, which I went into expecting something closer to a devotional writer and was surprised to find him doing real political theory. Last session was built around the cluster of fragments usually grouped under something like "Justice, Force" and what Pascal calls the "reason of effects." The line that anchored most of the discussion was, in our edition's translation, "Justice without force is powerless; force without justice is tyrannical," together with his further move that, since people could never manage to make what is just strong, they settled for making what is strong count as just instead. We also went over the fragment where a river or a couple of degrees of latitude is apparently enough to reverse an entire system of law, and his warning that custom keeps its authority only for as long as nobody asks where it actually came from, since tracing a law back to its real origin destroys it.

After the seminar, two people from my discussion group, Daniel and Sasha, kept arguing about how to read all this over coffee, and I honestly couldn't tell who had the better of it. (1) Daniel's opening claim was that Pascal is basically a debunker: there is no independent fact about justice at all, only power that has successfully gotten itself called just, and the river/latitude fragment proves that Pascal thought "justice" bottoms out entirely in whoever happens to hold force in a given place. (2) Sasha pushed back that Pascal opens the very same fragment by granting that it is right that what is just should be obeyed, so he isn't denying that justice is real; he's making a point about human reason being too corrupted and partial after the Fall to reliably track it, which is a very different, more Jansenist claim than the flat "might makes right" Daniel was attributing to him. (3) Daniel conceded that this might describe what Pascal sincerely believed, but said it makes no practical difference: an epistemic excuse for never trusting your own sense of injustice still tells you, in every actual case, to do exactly what raw power-worship would tell you to do, so the "humility" framing is a distinction without a difference. (4) Sasha answered that it does matter, because Pascal's warning is specifically about the danger of unmasking custom, not a blanket endorsement of any regime whatsoever; he clearly still thinks rulers ought to govern well, and he is only denying that an individual subject's private conviction of injustice is a safe basis for disobedience, since everyone acting on that conviction produces civil war, which he takes to be a worse evil than living under imperfect law. (5) Daniel replied that this concedes his real point: on Sasha's own reading, no regime a subject privately judges unjust could ever be legitimately resisted, since that private judgment is exactly what Pascal tells us to distrust, so the "humility" reading is actually a more effective defense of the status quo than crude might-makes-right, because it now comes with a philosophical reason attached never to act on one's own judgment.

Could you go through Daniel and Sasha's exchange move by move and say who has the stronger argument at each stage, rather than just declaring an overall winner? I'd also be interested in whether you think there is a more defensible way of reading Pascal's actual position on justice and force that both of them are talking past.
