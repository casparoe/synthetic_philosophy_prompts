My older sister, a philosophy PhD student who works on vagueness, is marrying her fiancé — also a philosopher of language — next month, and I've been asked to give the toast. Their whole courtship has revolved around a running, good-natured disagreement about the Sorites paradox: the old puzzle about a heap of sand that supposedly stays a heap no matter how many single grains you remove, until eventually it obviously isn't one. She is an epistemicist, holding that words like 'heap' or 'bald' have perfectly sharp, exact cutoffs that we are simply incapable of ever knowing; he is a supervaluationist, holding that a claim only counts as true if it holds up under every sensible way of making the term precise, so that a borderline case ends up neither true nor false, even though a sentence like 'he is either bald or he isn't' still somehow comes out true overall. Even their friends can't agree on exactly when their bickering turned into flirting, which I suspect is its own small case study in higher-order vagueness. A handful of the wedding guests are philosophers of language who will catch every reference; the rest of my family has no idea any of this exists. Could you write me a five-minute toast, built around one genuine joke about vagueness that works on both levels, that turns their long-running argument into evidence of how well they suit each other, without ever seeming to award either of them the win?
