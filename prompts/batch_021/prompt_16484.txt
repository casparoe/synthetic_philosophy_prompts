Over dinner last week, a friend and I ended up in a long argument about Frankfurt-style cases and what they actually show about the Principle of Alternative Possibilities (PAP). I defended the standard Frankfurtian line on the classic Black-and-Jones case: since Black's device never actually has to intervene, and Jones decides and acts entirely on his own, the mere fact that he could not have done otherwise seems irrelevant to whether he is morally responsible — so much the worse for PAP. My friend refused to concede the point. She argued, first, that some "flicker of freedom" survives even in Black's setup, since Jones is presumably still free to produce or withhold whatever prior sign triggers Black's intervention; and second, more damagingly, that the whole scenario faces a dilemma she attributed to Widerker and Ginet (though I have also seen it credited to Kane): either the prior sign is causally sufficient for Jones's decision, in which case the example quietly presupposes determinism and begs the question against libertarians, or it is not sufficient, in which case Jones could, after all, have decided otherwise. We went in circles for the better part of two hours and never got past this dilemma to the more recent moves on either side — buffer cases, blockage-style scenarios that dispense with the prior sign altogether, the two-path device Mele and Robb use to try to rescue the case, or Fischer's retreat to semi-compatibilism.

I'd like you to pick up roughly where we left off. Please write a dialogue between two philosophers holding our respective positions — one defending the Frankfurtian rejection of PAP, the other defending a refined version of PAP by way of the dilemma defense — and have them actually work through the material we never got to. The PAP-defender should press the dilemma seriously rather than simply restating Black-and-Jones, and the Frankfurtian should have to answer with something sharper than another simple intervener case: for instance, by appealing to a buffer or blockage-style case, to a Mele-and-Robb-style device with two convergent causal paths, or by falling back on Fischer's strategy of insisting that moral responsibility requires only "guidance control" rather than the freedom to do otherwise. I'd like each side to respond to the other's actual arguments rather than talk past one another, with both positions given genuine technical bite rather than one serving as a foil for the other; and if the exchange honestly leads to unresolved disagreement rather than a tidy resolution, that's fine too.
