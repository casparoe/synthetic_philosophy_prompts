I spent the first decade of my career as a nuclear arms-control analyst, and Thomas Schelling's The Strategy of Conflict and Arms and Influence shaped nearly everything I wrote about crisis stability: his account of credible commitment, brinkmanship, and the reciprocal fear of surprise attack was foundational to how I thought. I have recently moved into advising a defense-technology firm on AI systems meant to negotiate and signal on behalf of state actors during crises, and I keep running into a worry that AI-safety researchers call the commitment races problem. Because whichever agent commits first and most irrevocably to a bargaining position tends to lock in the advantage, each side has an incentive to commit as fast as possible, before there has been time to reason the commitment through — risking outcomes worse than either side intended. Schelling never had to consider bargainers that can commit to a stance in milliseconds and cannot easily be talked down afterward. Drawing specifically on his own arguments about the strategic value of narrowing one's own options, the logic of the threat that leaves something to chance, and the instability produced by reciprocal fear of surprise attack, how do you think Schelling would have assessed the commitment races problem? Would he have treated it as merely a faster, more literal version of an instability he already diagnosed and thought could be managed through communication and verification, or would he have concluded that something in his framework simply breaks down once the bargainers are no longer human?
