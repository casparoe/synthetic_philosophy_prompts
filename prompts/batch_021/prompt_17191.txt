I don't have any formal background in philosophy, but for the past year I've spent a good deal of my spare time reading on my own about a question that first occurred to me after a minor car accident I witnessed together with my brother from two slightly different spots on the same street corner: afterward, we could not agree on something as basic as the color of the car under the streetlights, and no amount of comparing notes could talk either of us out of what we were each sure we had seen. That disagreement eventually led me into the philosophy of perception, and in particular into the debate over what's called "cognitive penetration" — the claim that what we already believe or expect can alter the perceptual experience itself, and not merely what we go on to think, attend to, or remember about it. I host a small, self-produced podcast, and next month's guest is a philosopher who defends a strong version of this view: that two observers can, in a substantive sense, see the very same scene differently because of what they already believed beforehand, rather than merely noticing or judging it differently afterward.

I would rather the conversation not consist of her simply restating that thesis alongside her favorite illustrations, so I am hoping you can draft eight to ten pointed interview questions for me. Please include at least one question that presses on the standard objection that every supposed instance of penetrated perception can instead be explained as a shift in eye movements, attention, memory, or response criterion rather than in early visual processing itself, together with a sharp follow-up prepared for however she tries to hold her ground. Please also include a question built around the worry that if a perceptual experience can be shaped by a biased or otherwise unreliable belief, that experience may no longer be able to justify further beliefs the way perception ordinarily does — I would like her pressed on exactly how far she thinks that "downgrading" of justification is meant to spread, and whether it ultimately threatens to make everyday perceptual confidence, including in eyewitness cases like the one that started me on this, look suspect once her premise is granted. Finally, please include one question asking what kind of experimental result would actually persuade her that the view is false, since so much of this debate seems to turn on how people are already inclined to interpret one and the same ambiguous experiment. For each question, add a brief note on the answer she is most likely to give, along with a sharper follow-up keyed to that answer.
