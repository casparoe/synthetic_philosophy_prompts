I've got a CS background and spent a couple years on a distributed systems team, where we used epistemic logic to reason about multi-agent protocols -- modeling what each process "knows" based on its local state, the muddy children puzzle, all that. One thing that just falls out for free in that setup: if a process knows P, it automatically knows that it knows P. I've seen this called the KK principle. It's a totally unremarkable consequence of defining knowledge as a partition over possible states, nobody on that team ever treated it as controversial. So I was pretty confused to find out Timothy Williamson spends a big chunk of a book attacking the KK principle in philosophy, using this "luminosity" argument with margin-for-error cases, like slowly getting colder over the course of a day. If KK just pops out automatically once you define knowledge properly (like we did), isn't Williamson just using a worse defintion of knowledge and then acting suprised it doesn't have nice properties? Why do epistemologists treat this as some deep discovery about knowledge instead of just a matter of how you define things?
