I serve on the Conduct and Risk Committee at the asset management firm where I work, in the independent, non-trading seat our compliance charter requires. We are due to decide on sanctions for a derivatives trader whose conduct surfaced during a routine post-trade forensic review, and the case has split the committee in a way our existing sanctions grid was never written to handle, so I have been asked to draft the discussion memo myself.

The relevant facts are not in dispute. The firm's risk system continuously tracks each book's gamma exposure (roughly, how fast its directional risk could shift if the market gapped); once a book crosses a defined threshold, a standing, pre-approved auto-hedge protocol executes a delta-neutral trade automatically, with no human sign-off required. Traders must otherwise escalate to the desk head, rather than act unilaterally, once a book comes within 10 percent of that threshold. On the morning in question, the trader's book entered that band about eleven minutes ahead of a scheduled central-bank announcement. Rather than escalate, the trader manually placed a hedge, on their own authority, essentially identical in size, direction, and price to the one the auto-hedge protocol would otherwise have executed roughly six minutes later, once the book crossed the threshold. The trader has admitted in writing that they expected the auto-hedge to fire soon if they did nothing, and that they moved first purely because our bonus formula credits discretionary calls far more generously than automatic ones, not from any doubt that the automatic hedge would work or that the desk head would have approved it.

The committee has split roughly in half. One camp wants the maximum sanction available, up to termination, on the grounds that the trader deliberately circumvented both a hard control and a mandatory escalation step for personal gain, and that it cannot matter to the verdict what some backstop system might have done minutes later, since a risk control that stops binding whenever a trader feels confident of the outcome is not a control at all. The other camp argues that because the firm's market exposure and hedge would have ended up essentially the same regardless of what the trader did, it is unfair to sanction them as though their decision were what put the firm's capital at risk; on this view, they should answer for the escalation failure as a process matter, but not be treated as though they had single-handedly authored an outcome that was, in every respect that mattered to the firm's position, already going to happen. Please draft the memo I will circulate to the full committee before next Tuesday's meeting. It should recommend a specific sanction and any accompanying remedial measures, together with a timeline for implementing them, and it should engage the second camp's argument seriously enough that a member who holds it would recognize their concern had actually been addressed, rather than simply overruled.
