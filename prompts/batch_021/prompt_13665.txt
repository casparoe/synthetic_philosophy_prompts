I'm currently taking a seminar on the history of probability and decision theory, and this week's unit was on the St. Petersburg paradox: Nicolas Bernoulli's puzzle about a coin-flip game whose expected monetary payout is infinite even though hardly anyone would pay more than a few coins to play it, and his cousin Daniel Bernoulli's 1738 resolution, published in the proceedings of the Imperial Academy of Sciences in St. Petersburg -- which is in fact where the paradox's name comes from, and has nothing to do with Russian roulette, as I had always vaguely assumed. Bernoulli's move was to replace expected monetary value with what his essay's English translations render as "moral" expectation, as against merely "mathematical" expectation: the expected value of utility, computed under a function in which each additional unit of wealth buys less satisfaction than the last, so that a rational gambler's maximum willingness to pay converges to something modest even though the game's expected monetary payout does not.

During office hours, my professor mentioned, almost as an aside, that after FTX collapsed in 2022, several commentators went back to Sam Bankman-Fried's earlier interview with Tyler Cowen, in which, responding to a hypothetical Cowen posed about a repeated coin flip that would double the world's welfare on a win and destroy it on a loss, SBF argued that a genuine expected-value maximizer acting on behalf of the world's total welfare should keep taking the bet as long as the odds stayed even slightly favorable, and treated the usual appeal to risk aversion as a piece of psychology that makes sense for individuals protecting their own fortunes but has no obvious force for a disinterested maximizer of aggregate welfare. That comparison has stuck with me: a repeated double-or-destroy gamble against a standing chance of total ruin has essentially the same shape as the St. Petersburg game, and Bernoulli built his entire solution around exactly the diminishing-marginal-utility intuition that SBF says shouldn't survive the switch from one's own bank balance to the welfare of everyone.

I'd like your help working out how Daniel Bernoulli himself, reasoning strictly from within his own 1738 argument and not from anything discovered later -- please set aside the Kelly criterion, ergodicity economics, or modern axiomatic expected-utility theory -- would respond to SBF's position. Would he treat SBF's gamble as his own paradox recurring in a new guise, so that some analogous diminishing-returns structure has to be imposed on whatever "welfare" aggregates into, on pain of the same absurdity he was originally trying to avoid? Or does his argument, being explicitly about an individual's diminishing utility for personal wealth, simply have nothing to say about a disinterested maximizer of collective welfare -- so that Bernoulli, working only with the concepts available to him, would have to concede that his fix does not transfer, and that resisting SBF's conclusion requires a genuinely different argument he never made?
