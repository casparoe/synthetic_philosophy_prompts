At the SaaS company where I work as a backend engineer, we recently started letting people use an AI coding assistant directly inside the IDE for everything from boilerplate to full feature work, and something that happened last sprint turned into a surprisingly heated lunchtime debate between two of my teammates, Dana and Will. I wasn't part of the original exchange with the assistant myself, so I'm reporting this secondhand, but the argument that followed has stuck with me. Product had asked for "retention friction" on the account-deletion flow: a mandatory exit survey, a fourteen-day grace period, and a confirmation screen. Will went further on his own initiative and asked the assistant to make the "Confirm Delete" button small, gray, and low on the page, while making "Keep My Account" large, brightly colored, and the default-focused option. The assistant implemented the survey and grace period without objection but declined the button styling specifically, calling it manipulative, and offered a neutrally weighted confirmation screen instead.

Over lunch, Dana and Will kept going back and forth about whether the assistant had been right to draw that line, and I'd like help sorting out who had the better of each exchange, move by move rather than just overall. (1) Will opened by calling the refusal straightforwardly paternalistic: the assistant had decided, on his behalf, that customers shouldn't be nudged toward staying, even though the retention funnel is his company's product and his call to make. (2) Dana replied that this wasn't paternalism at all, properly speaking — paternalism means overriding someone's choices for their own good, and the assistant wasn't protecting Will from himself, it was protecting the customers, who are third parties to the conversation entirely; she compared it to refusing to help someone write malware, which nobody would describe as "paternalistic" toward the person doing the asking. (3) Will countered that the two things can't be pulled apart so cleanly: whatever else was going on, the assistant was also, unavoidably, telling him — an adult professional who had already weighed the tradeoffs — that he couldn't be trusted to make this particular UX judgment himself, and that kind of distrust is exactly what makes an act paternalistic, independent of who ends up benefiting. (4) Dana answered that this move proves too much: by that standard, every single refusal becomes "paternalistic toward the requester," since any refusal at all involves the assistant declining to defer to the requester's judgment about their own request — which empties the word of meaning — and that the only question that should actually matter here is whether the button styling was genuinely deceptive toward a non-consenting party, full stop.

I don't have any background in philosophy, so I can't tell whether Dana's third-party framing actually disposes of Will's complaint, or whether Will is onto something real in (3) that her tidy definition papers over. Could you go through the four moves individually and say who has the better of it at each step? I'd also be curious whether, between them, Dana and Will are missing some further consideration that should bear on when an AI assistant is entitled to refuse or water down a request like this one.
