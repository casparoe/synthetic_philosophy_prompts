I spent my whole career as an experimental physicist, a lot of it on nonlinear dynamics, and now that I'm retired I've been reading moral philosophy for fun. Consequentialism appealed to me right away since it's basically a cost-benefit calculation, but I recently ran into this "cluelessness" worry and it's been bugging me ever since -- its basically the butterfly effect applied to ethics. Take something that seems like a slam-dunk good act, like funding mosquito nets to stop malaria deaths: sure, the direct effect is great, but that also changes who gets born later, shifts population and economic trajectories, etc, and we have no real handle on whether those ripple effects are good or bad on net over the long run. Could you write me a dialogue between two philosophers who both take this stuff seriously -- one arguing that this kind of uncertainty genuinely undermines our confidence that funding the nets is the right call, the other defending the donation -- and have them actually grapple with each others strongest points rather than just talking past one another? Neither should be a pushover, and I don't want the defender to get off easy by just saying "we do our best guess," since that doesn't obviously answer the worry that the unknown effects might be running systematically in one direction rather than just cancelling out.
