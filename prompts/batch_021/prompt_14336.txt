"In 'Epilogue' (1925), Ramsey argues against the idea that the vast size and age of the universe give us any reason to think human life and human concerns are insignificant. (a) Reconstruct the 'argument from cosmic size' that Ramsey has in his sights, and say in your own words what he thinks is wrong with it. (b) Explain what work his image of a 'perspective drawing' (as opposed to a 'scale drawing') of the world is doing in his response. (c) Ramsey is sometimes bracketed together with Russell's 'A Free Man's Worship' as a fellow naturalist trying to salvage some sense of meaning once you get rid of God and cosmic purpose. And here's the student's answer, copied exactly as submitted (typos included):

"(a) The argument Ramsey's responding to says something like: the universe is unimaginably big and old, and human beings and human history are a tiny, brief blip within it, so nothing we do or care about really matters in the grand scheme of things. Ramsey thinks the mistake is treating physical size and duration as if they were relevant to value or importance. On his view the only things that actually have the properties that make something matter -- thinking, feeling, loving, valuing -- are found in us (as far as we know), not in inert matter, no matter how much of it there is. So the argument basically trades on sliding between 'big' in a physical sense and 'big' in an importance sense.

(b) The perspective drawing image is meant to show that seeing yourself and human affairs as central, with the stars shrunk down to almost nothing, isn't some kind of error or self-flattering illusion that a more objective 'scale drawing' view would correct. A perspective drawing is the right way to draw a picture when what you care about is showing what matters to the viewer, and there's no neutral, view-from-nowhere picture that's automatically more correct for that purpose, even though the scale drawing is obviously more accurate for other purposes, like astronomy.

(c) I'd say the pairing is basically right. Both Ramsey and Russell are naturalists who've given up on theism and any cosmic guarantee of meaning, and both end up locating whatever value there is in human relationships, love, and creative or intellectual activity rather than in anything bigger than us. Really they're offering the same basic move: once you accept that the universe is blind and purposeless, the only option left is to defiantly build your sense of meaning on top of what Russell calls an 'unyielding foundation of despair,' just choosing to look toward each other instead of out at the void. This also fits with Ramsey's broader philosophy: since he's famous for arguing that probabilities are just an agent's subjective degrees of belief rather than objective features of the world, its a pretty natural extension of that same anti-objectivist streak to say that the 'vastness' of the universe doesn't carry any objective weight either, and that significance, like probability on his view, is something contributed by the subject rather than read off the world."
