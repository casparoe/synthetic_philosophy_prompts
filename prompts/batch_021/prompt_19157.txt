I've been drafting a short post on evidential cooperation in large worlds (ECL) for the EA Forum. The core idea, going back to Oesterheld's original work on multiverse-wide superrationality (MSR), is that since the universe is probably vast, our decisions are correlated—in the evidential rather than causal sense—with the decisions of the many agents elsewhere who reason as we do, giving us reason to act somewhat cooperatively toward the values of that whole population even though we'll never causally interact with any of them. Before I polish the post further, I'd like a sanity check on its central argument, sketched below.

The case for ECL needs the correlation to survive right at the point of decision: my choosing to cooperate has to be evidence about what "sufficiently similar" agents elsewhere choose. But the decision I'm actually making is a very particular one: whether to act cooperatively having just been talked into it by a long, technical argument about acausal trade with unseen aliens. Agents elsewhere who arrive at cooperation by that exact route are presumably a small, idiosyncratic slice of all possible minds, and likely one that was already unusually cooperative and value-pluralistic to begin with. So the very act of finding the argument persuasive seems to shrink my reference class down toward "people who think like rationalist-forum regulars," rather than the broad diversity of value systems across a large universe that gave the "gains from trade" framing its force in the first place.

The natural patch is to say the relevant similarity is something more abstract, like "disposition to cooperate with unlike values when one takes doing so to be rational," rather than the specific causal history of having read one paper. But I don't think that patch is earned rather than stipulated: plenty of processes could converge on outwardly cooperative behavior without running anything like the same decision procedure — convergent evolution of reciprocity, say, or an unrelated acausal theory that happens to recommend the same action here. If behavioral convergence doesn't secure algorithmic correlation, and algorithmic correlation is what's doing the work, this starts to look less like a proof and more like a hope. This is, I think, most damaging to the third of what Treutlein once called MSR's "three wagers": that our actions carry evidential weight for non-identical agents with different goals, not merely for near-copies of ourselves.

Is this a real problem, one I should be crediting to someone rather than presenting as my own, or am I misunderstanding how "sufficiently similar" is meant to work here? I'd also value pushback on whether the compromise / gains-from-trade framing needs this premise as badly as I think it does, or whether there's a version of ECL that gets by without it.
