A friend and I spent a long drive arguing about a news story: a care-home resident died after a scheduled medication dose was missed one night, and the home was later found liable for her death. I argued that the missed dose really was a cause of her death: had it been given, she would not have died, and that counterfactual dependence of this kind is all causation ever requires, whether what did the causing was an act or a mere absence. My friend, whose sympathies run closer to Phil Dowe's conserved quantity theory than to David Lewis's, insisted that strictly speaking nothing happened that night — there is no physical process running from an absence to an effect — so at best we get "quasi-causation," a courtesy title for responsibility dressed up as physics. His strongest point against me was that the day pharmacist, the resident's estranged son, and countless other people satisfy that very same counterfactual without thereby being causes of her death, so my account must be smuggling in unstated norms about who was expected to act. Neither of us landed a clean blow on the other.

Write a dialogue between two philosophers of causation holding these two positions, in which each presses the other on exactly this difficulty rather than talking past it. Have the process theorist explain what "quasi-causation" really buys her if it is not causation, and have the counterfactualist explain how to block the profusion of equally-qualified non-causes without quietly trading a metaphysical claim for a moral one.
