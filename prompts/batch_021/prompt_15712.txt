A friend who curates contemporary art and I spent last night arguing about a decision now facing the public art commission I sit on. By Friday we must choose between two finalists for a permanent commission at the city's new transit plaza: a large abstract steel installation that our professional jury and every visiting critic call the most inventive submission we've received in years, and a representational bronze group of a mother and child waiting at a bus stop, which several jurors dismiss as sentimental and derivative but which moved most attendees at the public preview to tears. My friend thinks choosing the bronze would be an embarrassing concession to popular taste; I found myself defending it on essentially Tolstoyan grounds — that sincerely and clearly "infecting" ordinary viewers with feeling is the mark of real art, and that technical daring admired mainly by the initiated is closer to what Tolstoy called counterfeit art. Neither of us gave ground. Please draft the memo I will circulate to the commission before Friday's vote: it should recommend the steel piece, the bronze, or some other course of action (such as co-commissioning or a further public comment period), give reasoning other jurors could apply to future decisions, and take seriously the strongest objection to whichever choice you recommend — including the awkward fact that Tolstoy's own criterion, applied consistently, would have condemned much of what we now consider indisputably great art, including a good deal of his own fiction.
