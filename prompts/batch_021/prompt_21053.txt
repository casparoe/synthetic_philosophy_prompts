I'm the student rep on my stats department's grading appeals committee (we set policy for how regrade requests get handled across our big intro courses, not just individual rulings), and there's a recurring argument on our hands that's been bugging me ever since my epistemology seminar spent a week on this stuff about when its rational to believe something just because it's statistically likely, which honestly still kind of messes with my head. Our 400-person intro stats course has all its short-answer questions hand-marked by a rotating pool of TAs, and our own internal audits from the past few years put the per-question error rate at something like 2-3%. Every single term, at least one student makes basically the same move: "this exam had 50 hand-graded questions, so it's overwhelmingly likely that at least one of mine got misgraded, I just can't tell you which one, so I want a regrade of the whole exam, or a couple bonus points to make up for it." Half the committee thinks refusing this is just ignoring math the student is completely right about; the other half points out that the argument never depends on anything specific to their exam, so being consistent would mean we owe literally every student in every large class the same deal, every term, forever. To make it worse, a couple of professors, trying to be upfront about it, now put a line on the grade release saying something like "given the scale of grading in this course, isolated errors are statistically likely, please flag anything that looks off" -- and now students are citing that sentence as basically an admission that backs up their claim.

Could you draft the policy memo I'd bring to our next meeting? I don't want "it's philosophically complicated" as the takeaway -- I need an actual recommendation, with a clear line for what should count as grounds for a regrade versus what shouldn't, and a real response to the strongest version of the students' argument rather than a brush-off. If you can, say something about whether that "errors are statistically likely" line on the grade release is itself a mistake for professors to be making, since that's actually the bit that's splitting the committee even more than the original question.
