So I've been on a bit of a Turchin kick lately (finally got around to reading "End Times" after seeing it recommended in like three different ACX open threads), and it's got me thinking back to a philosophy of history class I took years ago that covered the whole Hempel covering-law-model debate. Hempel's basic claim was that historical explanations, like explanations in physics, ultimately work by subsuming an event under a general law -- even if the law usually has to be left implicit as an "explanation sketch," since the relevant psychological or sociological laws are too messy to state precisely. Danto, Dray, Walsh and the rest of the narrativist crowd pushed back hard on this, and their point wasn't just "history is messy," it was something more structural: that historical explanation works through stuff like colligation (grouping particulars under synthesizing concepts like "the Renaissance" or "the causes of WWI") and narrative judgments about what's relevant to the story being told, not law-subsumption -- and that this isn't a temporary defect to be fixed once the social sciences mature, its baked into what historical explanation even is.

Now here's the thing: cliodynamics looks a lot like exactly the kind of rigorous, quantitative, actually-generates-candidate-laws research program Hempel was hoping for -- secular cycles, structural-demographic theory, elite overproduction driving instability, curve-fit regularities across dozens of agrarian states and empires, not hand-wavy "sketches." A friend of mine who's way more into this stuff than me argued that this basically settles the debate in Hempel's favor, and makes Danto et al. look like they were just complaining about the immaturity of 1960s social science. I'm not so sure, but I can't quite articulate why. Could you make the strongest case that even if we grant all of Turchin's empirical claims, cliodynamics still wouldn't vindicate the covering-law model against the narrativists -- i.e. that it would, if anything, end up illustrating exactly the limits on nomological explanation that Danto/Dray/Walsh were pointing at? I'd love it if you got into the weeds on stuff like the D-N vs I-S distinction, the reference-class problem for applying statistical "laws" to individual cases, and how you'd even individuate something like "the fall of the Roman Republic" as an explanandum in the first place without already doing narrative/colligatory work.
