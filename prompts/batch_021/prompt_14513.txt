I spent thirty-five years as an atmospheric physicist, mostly building and testing general circulation models, before retiring six years ago. Since then I have been slowly working through the philosophy of probability and confirmation theory, partly out of curiosity and partly because I realized I had spent an entire career doing Bayesian updating without ever examining its foundations very closely. A few weeks ago, in a reading group run through my local university's continuing-education program, we spent a whole session on Bas van Fraassen's "Judy Benjamin problem": the parachutist who lands somewhere in a territory split into a Red half and a Blue half, each of which is further divided into an area run by "Headquarters Company" and one run by "Second Company," so that she starts out assigning an equal probability of one in four to each of the four quadrants. She then receives a garbled radio message telling her that, if she is in Red territory, she is three times more likely to be in the Second Company's area than in the Headquarters area - but the message says nothing whatsoever about Blue territory, nor anything that directly compares Red to Blue. The puzzle, as I understood it, is that once you try to build an updating rule that honors this new conditional constraint while otherwise disturbing your prior beliefs as little as possible - for instance, by minimizing relative information, or cross-entropy, with respect to the old distribution - you seem forced to also revise how likely you think it is that she is in Red territory at all, even though nothing in the message spoke to that comparison. This struck me as strange in the way a sign error in a calculation feels strange, and our group spent most of the session disagreeing about whether the moral is that something is wrong with cross-entropy updating specifically, with the very idea of conditionalizing on a conditional, or merely with our intuitions about what "gaining no information about X" ought to mean.

I would like to understand this better than we managed to that evening. Could you lay out what you take to be the most important implications of the Judy Benjamin problem for how one ought to update beliefs on conditional statements more generally - the kind that came up constantly in my own work, whenever a colleague reported something like "if the model is run with the higher aerosol-forcing estimate, then warming by 2050 is much more likely to fall in the upper half of the projected range than the lower half," without saying anything about how probable that forcing estimate itself was? I would be especially grateful if you could address whether the lesson of the problem is that we should be suspicious of "minimal change" updating principles quite generally, or whether there is a narrower lesson specifically about conditionals that leaves the rest of the Bayesian framework intact.
