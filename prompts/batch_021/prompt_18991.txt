I'm a PhD student in cognitive science working on interoception — how accurately people perceive internal bodily states like hunger, heart rate, or temperature. As an informal pilot for a possible side project, I've been logging my own sensations during contrast-therapy sessions (alternating sauna and cold plunge) at a studio near campus, rating every few seconds while I'm in the cold plunge whether I currently "feel cold." What strikes me is that, although I'm obviously fine when I get in and obviously freezing a few minutes later, I can't identify any particular second at which my rating flips from "no" to "yes": the numbers just drift, even though at each individual moment I feel completely certain of whatever answer I'm giving right then.

I mentioned this at dinner to a friend from a philosophy reading group I sit in on, and she gave an argument that has been nagging at me since. Roughly: if there's no sharp cutoff, then for any moment at which I'd confidently say "I know I'm cold right now," there's an adjacent, subjectively indistinguishable moment at which I'd have said exactly the same thing with exactly the same confidence — even though, enough such moments earlier, I plainly wasn't cold yet. So, she said, whatever feeling of certainty accompanies my judgment can't itself be tracking the fact that makes it true, since that same feeling would have shown up just as strongly one imperceptible step too soon. And if my sense of certainty can't do that job right at the boundary, then I have no way of telling, from the inside, whether any given moment of mine is safely clear of the boundary or not. So, she concluded, I never actually know that I know I'm cold — not even right now, mid-shiver, when it feels completely obvious.

I've turned this over for a week without resolving it. It seems obviously true that there's no sharp line, and obviously false that I don't know I'm cold while visibly shivering, but I can't hold both of those at once, and I can't tell which step of hers I'm entitled to reject. Is her argument valid as it stands? If not, exactly where does it fail? And if there's something to it after all, what does that actually show about self-knowledge more generally — something narrow, limited to a strip of genuinely ambiguous moments, or something that should unsettle my confidence in the ordinary, obvious cases too?
