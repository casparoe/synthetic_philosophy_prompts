The question on the exam was: "In no more than one page, explain Nelson Goodman's 'new riddle of induction.' Your answer should (a) (6 points) state how the predicate 'grue' is defined and explain why, given only the emeralds examined so far, the available evidence confirms 'all emeralds are green' and 'all emeralds are grue' equally well; (b) (6 points) explain what the puzzle is supposed to show about Hume's classical problem of induction -- in what sense does grue pose a further difficulty that would remain even if Hume's problem were somehow solved; and (c) (8 points) briefly summarize Goodman's own proposed resolution of the puzzle, and state one significant objection or alternative to it that we discussed in lecture."

Here is the student's answer, reproduced in full:

"Goodman's puzzle starts from a definition: an object counts as grue if it has been examined before some future time t and is green, or if it has not yet been examined before t and is blue. Every emerald anyone has looked at so far has been examined before t and has turned out green, so each of those emeralds is a positive instance of 'all emeralds are green' and, simultaneously, of 'all emeralds are grue,' since being green-and-examined-before-t is exactly what it takes to satisfy 'grue' as well. This means that, using nothing but the standard idea that a general hypothesis is confirmed by its positive instances, the evidence collected so far confirms the grue hypothesis exactly as strongly as it confirms the green hypothesis, even though the two hypotheses disagree completely about emeralds examined for the first time after t: the first predicts they will be green, the second predicts they will be blue.

This is what makes the puzzle a 'new' riddle rather than a restatement of Hume's. Hume asked whether we can ever be rationally justified in extrapolating from observed to unobserved cases at all, given that no amount of past regularity logically guarantees future regularity. Goodman's puzzle assumes that some such extrapolation is legitimate and asks a narrower question: since 'grue' generates a rival extrapolation from exactly the same data, what principled, non-circular reason do we have for projecting 'green' rather than 'grue'? So even a fully convincing answer to Hume, one that told us induction in general is rationally justified, would not by itself tell us which of two equally well-confirmed generalizations to project onto the future -- which shows that the two problems are genuinely distinct.

Goodman's own diagnosis is that 'grue' fails to qualify as a natural kind the way 'green' does: greenness is a real similarity that binds emeralds, frogs, and grass together, whereas grueness is an artificial, disjunctive property spliced together out of green, blue, and an arbitrary date, and only predicates that track a genuine natural kind are eligible to be projected into lawlike generalizations. The main objection raised in lecture was that this just pushes the question back a level, since 'natural kind' is doing a lot of unexplained work here -- one could just as easily have grown up speaking a language in which 'grue' and 'bleen' (things examined before t that are blue, or not yet examined and green) were the primitive color words, in which case 'green' would be the derived, disjunctive-looking predicate instead."
