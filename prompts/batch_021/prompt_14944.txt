I've been falling down a bit of a Wittgenstein rabbit hole this week — I cover science and ideas for a living, and I interviewed a philosopher of religion for a totally unrelated story who mentioned, almost in passing, that the whole "faith doesn't need reasons" thing people attribute to him is a much bigger mess than it sounds. Now I want to turn it into a short piece for the ideas newsletter I write on the side. What follows is more like my rough notes for the opening argument than a polished draft, and full disclosure, I'm working from memory of the Lectures on Religious Belief (the one stitched together from students' notes, not something he sat down and wrote himself for publication), so theres a decent chance I've mangled a detail or two — that's actually part of what I want your help with.

Here's the gist so far: Wittgenstein spends a chunk of these lectures needling a Father O'Hara for talking about religious belief as though it were basically a scientific hypothesis that's well-supported by evidence — Wittgenstein seems to think that's precisely backwards, that treating something like "there will be a Last Judgement" as a claim that could be more or less probable misses what kind of thing religious belief even is. His point isn't "the believer has bad evidence," it's that the disagreement between the believer and the non-believer isn't the same kind of disagreement as two people arguing about a plain fact — he says an "unshakeable" religious belief shows itself in the role it plays in regulating someone's whole life, not in how firmly it's held or how well it's justified. My draft's argument is that if you take this seriously, it ends up being a lot less comforting to religious people than the "fideism" label suggests: it's not just that faith gets a pass from needing evidence, it's that the entire idea of religious claims being true-or-false in the way empirical claims are dissolves — which cuts against confident believers exactly as much as it cuts against confident atheists. I gather Kai Nielsen coined "Wittgensteinian fideism" specifically to attack this kind of view as a cop-out that insulates religion from criticism, and that D.Z. Phillips spent decades arguing that's a bad description of the actual position — I haven't fully worked out who's right about that part.

Could you look this over and tell me whether I'm basically getting Wittgenstein's position right, or whether I'm smushing it together with something more like non-cognitivism or expressivism from ethics in a way the lectures don't really support? I'm also curious whether the "cuts both ways" bit is the genuinely interesting implication here, or whether there's a sharper one I'm missing entirely. My editor wants something punchy at around 900 words, so I don't need a full literature review back, just a gut check on whether the spine of the argument holds up before I flesh it out.
