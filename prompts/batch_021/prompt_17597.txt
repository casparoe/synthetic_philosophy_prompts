I'm at work on a novel whose third act turns on a diocesan historical commission assembling the testimony and documents that will form the positio -- the evidentiary dossier eventually sent to Rome -- in support of the beatification of Sister Agnieszka Radomska, a nun whose convent sheltered the children of parents arrested by the occupying authorities. The cause has advanced for years on witness testimony to her charity and her composure under danger, but a researcher on the commission has just recovered the convent's wartime correspondence, and it shows that when officials came asking after specific children, Sister Agnieszka did not merely stay silent or mislead them with statements that happened to be technically true: she supplied wholly invented cover stories -- false names, false parentage, a fabricated quarantine for scarlet fever to keep inspectors away from the wing where the children were actually hidden -- delivered, by every surviving account, without visible hesitation. Two of the commission's seven consultors now argue that this cannot simply be folded into the record as one more instance of her heroism. The Church's own settled teaching on lying -- running from Augustine's insistence that a deliberate falsehood is never permissible, whatever harm it avoids or good it achieves, through Aquinas, and into the Catechism's own continued teaching that lying is always wrong regardless of the purpose it serves -- seems to make her conduct sinful on its own terms. And if it is, they ask, how can the very same conduct also count as evidence of the heroic virtue that beatification requires?

For the scene built around the commission's closed session, I'd like a draft of the actual memo its chair, a canon lawyer named Father Tomasz Nowak, circulates the night before the vote on whether to forward the cause. He has to recommend one of a small number of live options -- forwarding the cause as currently framed, pausing it pending a formal theological opinion, or reframing the record around a narrower account of what Sister Agnieszka did and why -- and he has to reach that recommendation by actual argument, not by the kind of diplomatic hedging that lets everyone in the room save face. What matters most to me is that the memo take the two dissenting consultors' position seriously on its own terms, including the live possibility that they are simply right and that the tradition they are invoking has the better of this particular case, rather than quietly assuming that some reading of that tradition must exist which lets everyone have the beatification they want.
