Here is the question as set:

"In 'Mr Truman's Degree,' Anscombe argues that Oxford was wrong to award Truman an honorary degree, since he was guilty of murder in ordering the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki. Reconstruct her argument for this conclusion, making clear what role (if any) the doctrine of double effect plays in it, and explain why she takes the case to illustrate a more general problem with consequentialist moral reasoning." (10 points)

And here is one student's answer, verbatim:

"Anscombe's case against honoring Truman turns on the claim that the bombings were murder rather than a legitimate act of war. She accepts that killing civilians can sometimes be permissible under the doctrine of double effect: if an attack on a legitimate military target foreseeably, but unintentionally, kills nearby civilians, and the harm is proportionate to the military advantage gained, this is not murder. Her objection to Hiroshima and Nagasaki is that the numbers of civilian dead were simply too large to be excused this way — however important the target, no proportionality condition could stretch to cover destruction on that scale. That is also why she thinks it is no defense to say that the bombings shortened the war and thereby saved lives overall: the death toll was disproportionate to any such benefit.

She generalizes this into a complaint about consequentialism. A moral theory that lets us weigh a few hundred thousand civilian deaths against a hypothetical, larger number of lives saved by ending the war early is a theory we are never actually in a position to apply responsibly, since we cannot know with any confidence how many lives an invasion of Japan would really have cost. So for Anscombe the deep problem with consequentialist reasoning is at bottom epistemic: it requires a calculation nobody is entitled to make, and Truman's defenders are really just claiming a certainty about the counterfactual that they don't have. Because he acted on that illegitimate certainty, and because the resulting deaths were disproportionate, Anscombe concludes that Truman committed murder and that Oxford should not have honored him."
