I'm preparing the penultimate session of a graduate seminar on the metaphysics of laws of nature, where we move from the familiar Lewis-versus-Armstrong-versus-Maudlin scene-setting into the more technical trouble that afflicts Lewis's own view once objective chance is added to the Humean mosaic. Rereading "Humean Supervenience Debugged" this week, I drafted a short argument that I want to test on you before inflicting it on the students, since I can no longer tell whether it identifies a real cost in Lewis's fix to the undermining problem, or merely restates, in fancier dress, a point the mid-1990s chance literature — Hall, Thau, Loewer, and others — already made more carefully.

Here, compressed, is the argument. On the Best System Analysis, the chance of an outcome at t is whatever the system of axioms that best balances simplicity, strength, and fit with the entire Humean mosaic, past and future, says it is, and the Principal Principle ties rational credence to chance so construed. The "big bad bug" is that a wildly deviant future course of events — one that, were it actual, would be better systematized by different chances, or by outright determinism — is not merely improbable given the current best system; should it occur, it would retroactively falsify the very chance ascription the Principal Principle currently tells us to trust. Lewis's response is to relocate the object of the Principal Principle from categorical, time-indexed chance facts to "history-to-chance" conditionals, restricting admissible evidence so that the relevant conditional chance-facts hold good no matter which continuation of history turns out to be actual. My worry is that this rescues the letter of the Principal Principle only by converting what was advertised as a thoroughly a posteriori, mosaic-tracking notion of chance into something whose truth is secured in advance of, and independently of, the very pattern of outcomes it is supposed to answer to — which looks uncomfortably like the kind of necessary connection between distinct existences that Humean Supervenience exists to forbid. If that diagnosis is right, Lewis does not so much defuse the bug as quietly borrow against the anti-Humean's credit to pay it off.

I would welcome your comments on three things: whether I have reconstructed the undermining problem and Lewis's own reply to it accurately enough to survive contact with students who have read the primary text exactly once; whether the "borrowed credit" diagnosis is actually correct, or whether there is a standard Humean rejoinder — something a New Humean in Loewer's mold would say — that I am not giving enough weight; and, most bluntly, whether the point is original enough to be worth developing into the short paper I have in mind, or whether it is well-trodden ground in the chance literature that I have simply failed to place correctly. I would rather be corrected by you now than by a sharp student in week nine.
