I spent my whole career as an analytical chemist, and now that I'm retired I've been reading my way through the philosophy of language, which is how I ended up down the Kripke rabbit hole on rigid designators. Something's bugging me though. He wants to say "water is H2O" is necessarily true but only knowable a posteriori, fine, but doesn't that mean everyone who used the word "water" for thousands of years before Cavendish and Lavoisier worked out the formula was actually refering to something else, since nobody back then had any concept of H2O in their heads at all? Either the meaning of the word quietly shifted the moment the chemistry got sorted out in the 1780s, or Kripke's whole picture is secretly assuming the very thing the history seems to contradict, namely that "water" meant H2O all along. I don't see a third option here, but everywhere I look this gets treated as settled business. What am I missing?
