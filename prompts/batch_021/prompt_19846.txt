Here is the response I've been asked to grade:

"When Mill published the first edition of the Principles of Political Economy in 1848, he treated the existing framework of private property and market competition largely as background fact, in the manner of the classical economists, and showed little sympathy for contemporary socialist writers. His position had already begun to soften once Harriet Taylor died in 1858, an event Mill later suggested had freed him to publish more openly the radical opinions the two of them had developed together; from that point his statements on socialism grow steadily more favorable, culminating in the unfinished Chapters on Socialism, which appeared in the Fortnightly Review after his own death. Throughout, Mill relies on a distinction he drew early on between the laws of production, which he treats as fixed like laws of physics, and the laws of distribution, which he says are a matter of human institution and can therefore be changed by collective choice. On this basis he argues that the existing distribution of wealth, which rewards inheritance rather than labor, is unjust, and he singles out worker-owned cooperatives descended from the Rochdale pioneers as a promising, voluntary model that avoids the coercion of revolutionary schemes. He is careful to distinguish this position from communism proper, and elsewhere raises worries about incentives and population that such projects would have to answer. But by his final writings, these had become objections to how fast society should move rather than to where it should end up: Mill's considered view is that private ownership of capital could not be permanently justified, so his quarrel with revolutionary socialists was ultimately one of method, not of goal."
