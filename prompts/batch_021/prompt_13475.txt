I cover the "forecasting economy" beat for my outlet — Polymarket, Kalshi, Manifold, the Metaculus tournament crowd — and I'm drafting a longer piece that tries to go one level past the usual "wisdom of crowds" framing that science-and-ideas journalists, myself included, reach for whenever a market calls an election or a ceasefire correctly. The standard justification traces back to Hayek's point that prices aggregate dispersed private information better than any planner could. But while reading around the theoretical side of market microstructure, I ran into something that complicates that story more than I expected: Aumann's 1976 agreement theorem, and the no-trade theorem that Milgrom and Stokey built on top of it, which together seem to say that among Bayesian agents who share a common prior, mere common knowledge that a trade is on offer should be self-undermining — your willingness to sell tells me something, my willingness to buy tells you something, and if we're both rational and know it of each other, we should converge on a price and the market should seize up before any money actually changes hands. That is not remotely how Polymarket's order books behaved during the last election cycle, and I'd rather build the piece around that tension than paper over it the way most popular coverage does.

Here is the paragraph where I try to state the puzzle and push the logic through to a conclusion; I want your comments on it before I build the rest of the piece around its reasoning. "Push the reasoning all the way and you get something uncomfortable: the very liquidity that makes a prediction market useful in the first place — lots of people willing to take either side of a wager — is itself evidence against the market doing the thing we praise it for. If most trading were really driven by private information, Aumann and Milgrom-Stokey's logic says the order book should collapse into a single agreed price the moment it becomes common knowledge that everyone else is still willing to trade. That it doesn't suggests most of that volume isn't information at all; it's priors, risk appetite, hedging, and tribal loyalty dressed up as forecasting. So when a market 'calls' a result correctly, the credit belongs to a thin layer of genuinely informed traders whose bets move the price, not to the crowd generating the volume — and we should stop treating heavy trading as evidence that a market is well-informed. If anything, on this logic, high volume is closer to a warning sign than an endorsement."

I'd like comments in three areas specifically. First, accuracy: have I got what Aumann's theorem and the no-trade result actually establish right, or am I smuggling in a stronger reading than the game theory supports — for instance, by treating "common priors and common knowledge of rationality" as though it describes actual traders rather than a deliberate idealization? Second, the inference: does the closing move, from "the theorem predicts no trade" to "high volume is a warning sign rather than an endorsement," actually follow, or is there a more standard resolution economists reach for — heterogeneous priors, noise traders, something in the Grossman-Stiglitz vein about the returns to information-gathering — that would let real trading volume and real informativeness coexist without the sleight of hand I may be committing? And third, bluntly: is this the kind of tension a general-interest reader finds illuminating rather than pedantic, or should I cut it and default to the safer "wisdom of crowds, with caveats" version editors always prefer? The piece runs about 1,800 words with no footnotes, so if I'm going to hang a section on this, I need to know now whether the logic actually holds up.
