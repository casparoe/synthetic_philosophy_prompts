I'm writing a feature on the "Boltzmann brain" problem in cosmology, and I keep coming back to an irony that rarely makes it into popular accounts: defending his statistical version of the Second Law against the reversibility and recurrence objections, Ludwig Boltzmann himself speculated that our universe might simply be a rare, long-lived fluctuation within a far larger system that otherwise remains at equilibrium. Contemporary cosmologists have pushed that same idea to an extreme he never considered. In models of eternal inflation with a permanent, positive cosmological constant, chance fluctuations would generate isolated, momentarily conscious brains far more often than whole ordered worlds, so that over infinite time such "Boltzmann brains" would overwhelm ordinary observers like us — a conclusion physicists now treat as a strike against those models rather than as a discovery about our own likely condition. Knowing how Boltzmann handled Loschmidt's and Zermelo's challenges to his work, how do you think he would have reacted to seeing his own fluctuation hypothesis carried this far: would he have taken the swarm of Boltzmann brains as vindication of his statistical worldview, or as a reductio revealing that something upstream must be wrong?
