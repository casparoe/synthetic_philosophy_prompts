For my philosophy of language seminar this term we've spent something like three weeks just on the sorites paradox and theories of vagueness (we're working through Williamson's *Vagueness* plus a chunk of the Keefe & Smith *Vagueness: A Reader*), and its honestly broken my brain a little, especially since we've got a paper on it due in a few weeks. Last week two people in the seminar, Mara and Josh, got into an argument after class that they wouldn't let go of over drinks, and I just sat there typing notes on my phone because I couldnt tell who was winning. Mara's been defending epistemicism all semester (the Williamson view that "heap" really does have a razor-sharp cutoff, we just can't ever know where it is), Josh has been team supervaluationist, so this fight had been building for weeks.

Here's roughly how it went, as best I can reconstruct it:

1. Mara: There has to be some exact number of grains where "heap" stops applying, full stop. Our not knowing which number it is doesn't mean there's no fact, it just means our use of "heap" isn't fine-grained enough for anyone to ever track the cutoff. That's not mysticism, that's just admitting language is fallible the way everything else we're ignorant of is.
2. Josh: That's the part I can't buy. Meaning doesn't float free of use, there's nothing else for meaning to be except patterns of use, dispositions to apply the word one way rather than another. If literally nothing in how we use "heap" distinguishes 8,742 grains from 8,743, there's no further fact for our usage to have determined. You're not being fallibilist, you're just insisting on a truth that nothing could even in principle reveal.
3. Mara: Except your own view doesn't get rid of sharp cutoffs, it just relocates them. On supervaluationism there's still a fact about exactly where "definitely a heap" shades into "borderline" -- unless you also want to say that boundary is vague too, in which case you need a "definitely definitely," and then another level after that, forever. So don't act like I'm the only one committed to a hidden sharp line somewhere.
4. Josh: Sure, higher-order vagueness is a real headache, I'm not pretending otherwise. But that's vagueness in our theory of vagueness, all the way up, with no level where I'm forced to plant a flag and say "here, finally, is one exact answer that nothing could ever justify." You don't get to say that: for you there's one determinate cutoff for grains of sand, you've just given up on anyone ever knowing it. An open regress that never bottoms out isn't the same kind of commitment as one hidden fact sitting at the bottom.
