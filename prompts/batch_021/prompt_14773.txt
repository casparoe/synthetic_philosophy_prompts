For the next paper in our metaphysics seminar, take a position on a live controversy surrounding Leibniz's Principle of the Identity of Indiscernibles (PII). In "The Identity of Indiscernibles" (1952), Max Black describes a possible universe containing nothing but two exactly similar spheres, alike in every monadic quality and in exactly the same qualitative relations to the rest of that otherwise empty world; since no monadic predicate and no asymmetric relation distinguishes them, yet there are two rather than one, the case is widely treated as refuting any non-trivial version of PII. Defenders have replied that the spheres remain "weakly discernible": each bears some irreflexive, symmetric relation to the other (such as being a fixed distance from a qualitative duplicate) that nothing bears to itself, which is said to secure their numerical difference without invoking bare particulars or primitive thisness. Critics, including Michael della Rocca, have called this move circular, on the grounds that a relation like being some distance apart already presupposes the very plurality it is meant to explain.

Write an essay that reaches a definite verdict on whether Black's spheres refute PII. Engage seriously with the weak-discernibility defense and the circularity objection to it, and state briefly whether your verdict extends to qualitatively identical quantum particles (such as two electrons in a singlet state) or holds only for macroscopic cases such as Black's spheres. A precise conditional verdict is acceptable ("PII survives for X but not Y"); an even-handed survey that reaches no conclusion is not.
