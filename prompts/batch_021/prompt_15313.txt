I've been mulling over how language manages to meaningfully talk about things that don't exist - take the classic example, "The present King of France is bald." As I understand it, this is exactly the puzzle that Bertrand Russell's theory of descriptions was designed to solve: the sentence secretly claims that there exists a unique present King of France who is bald, and since no such person exists, the whole sentence simply comes out false rather than meaningless. Fair enough. But then it seems to me that its negation, "The present King of France is not bald," would have to be true by the law of excluded middle - and if that is true, then we would be asserting something quite specific about this nonexistent king, namely that he isn't bald, which seems to sneak his existence back in through the back door. So it looks to me like Russell's solution just relocates the original puzzle about empty reference rather than actually solving it. Am I missing something?
