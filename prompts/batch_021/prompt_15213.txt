I began a two-year catechist formation program through my diocese this past fall, and this term's moral theology unit has centered almost entirely on Augustine's two treatises on lying, De Mendacio and the later Contra Mendacium, addressed to a correspondent named Consentius. I'd never actually read them before, only known Augustine's position the way it usually gets flattened into "lying is always wrong," and working through the primary texts has been more unsettling than I expected. There's the story of the bishop Firmus of Thagasta, who protected a fugitive from imperial officers by flatly refusing to disclose his whereabouts rather than lying about them, and held to that even under torture, until the emperor, impressed by his honesty, pardoned the man himself. There's Augustine's insistence that Abraham did not lie to Pharaoh in calling Sarah his "sister," since she genuinely was his half-sister and so no falsehood was actually spoken. And there's Augustine's own ranking of lies by severity, in which even a lie that harms no one and saves someone from death is still, on his account, a sin — just one of the least grave kinds.

I brought this up over dinner last week, which set off an argument between two friends of mine, Owen and Elena, about something a third friend, Mateo, had told us a while back. When his roommate's ex-boyfriend, against whom she has a restraining order, showed up at their door asking if she was home, Mateo told him point-blank, "she's out of town," when in fact she was upstairs asleep. Owen argued that Mateo did something wrong all the same, since Augustine's own cases show a non-lying route was available: Mateo could simply have refused to answer, as Firmus did, or reached for a misleading-but-true statement, the way Augustine reads Abraham. Elena objected that the Firmus comparison doesn't travel — stonewalling officials over an extended interrogation is nothing like getting an agitated, possibly dangerous man off your doorstep in ten seconds, where an outright refusal to answer would itself function as a confirmation. Owen replied that this only motivates the second option, not the lie: Mateo could have said "she's not here right now" or "I haven't seen her all evening," achieved the identical result, and never have asserted, in Augustine's terms, what he believed false in his own heart. Elena's comeback was that this is a distinction without a difference — to the ex, to the roommate, to anyone but Mateo's own conscience, the misleading truth and the flat lie do exactly the same work, so a rule that answers only to what's happening inside Mateo's head, and not to how anyone is actually treated, isn't protecting anyone; it's just bookkeeping for his soul. Owen's last word was that "distinction without a difference" proves too much, since plenty of views we already take seriously, such as the doctrine of double effect, also make wrongness depend partly on what's intended rather than on outward results alone, so Elena owes an independent reason to reject that structure rather than simply asserting it here.

Neither of them gave ground, and I'm really too new to this material to referee it myself. Could you go through the exchange move by move and say who has the better of it at each stage, flagging anywhere either of them seems to have gotten Augustine's actual position wrong rather than simply weighed it differently? I'd also like your own verdict on the two larger questions hanging over the whole argument: whether Mateo's lie comes out as wrong on a fair reading of Augustine, and whether Elena's closing charge — that the entire apparatus of permissible "misleading truths" just relocates the wrong of lying somewhere it can no longer affect how anyone is actually treated — is something Augustine has a real answer to, or a genuine weak point in his view. If there's a consideration bearing on Mateo's case that neither Owen nor Elena thought to raise, I'd like to hear that too.
