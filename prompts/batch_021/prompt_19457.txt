"Explain Kavka's toxin puzzle and what you take it to show about the relationship between forming an intention and having a reason to act on it later. Feel free to bring in related puzzles or proposed solutions from the unit if they're relevant."

Here's the student's answer, verbatim:

"Kavka's toxin puzzle imagines a billionaire who will pay you a million dollars tomorrow morning if, by midnight tonight, you've formed the intention to drink a vial of toxin tomorrow afternoon. The toxin isn't lethal, it'll just make you sick for a day, and the billionaire can tell whether you really intend to drink it (say, via brain scan), so you can't just fake it. Crucially, you get paid as soon as you form the intention, whether or not you actually drink it later, and once the money's in your account you obviously have zero reason left to drink the toxin. So the puzzle is whether you can even manage to form that intention tonight, since intending to X seems to require believing you'll actually X, and you already know tonight that tomorrow-you won't want to. Kavka thinks this shows there are things it's rational to want to intend but irrational, maybe even impossible, to actually intend.

This is basically the same structure as the deterrence paradox from Kavka's earlier paper: a country that wants to rationally deter an attack has to intend to retaliate even though retaliating, if deterrence fails, would serve no purpose and would be irrational at that point. Gauthier's answer to both puzzles is "resolute choice" -- a truly rational agent is one who can stick to a plan formed earlier even after the local reasons that motivated it have evaporated, instead of reoptimizing at every instant. If that's right, both puzzles are less about the limits of rationality than about the limits of a narrow, "reoptimize every moment" picture of what rationality requires."
