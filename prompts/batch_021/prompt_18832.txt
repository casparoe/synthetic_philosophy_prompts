Random philosophy detour from a book I'm drafting, hoping you can help me stress test something before I commit it to paper. I've got a character - a retired, slightly cranky classics teacher - who keeps a beat up copy of Cicero's De Senectute ("On Old Age") on her nightstand, and later in the book she recites its ending to her grandson, who's terrified of dying. You probably know the bit - after all the earlier stuff about Milo of Croton weeping over his withered arms, and the image of ripe fruit dropping off the tree on its own while green fruit has to be torn off - Cato's big closing move is basically: look, either the soul dies along with the body, in which case theres nothing left around to be harmed by death, so theres nothing to fear, OR the soul is immortal, in which case dying is more like a promotion, since you get to go hang out with all the great dead people. He even throws in, if I'm remembering right, that he's happy to be wrong about the immortality part, since no smart-aleck philosopher is going to be alive to mock him for the mistake once he's dead.

I want my classics teacher to genuinely find this argument satisfying, and I want the grandson to land a real hit on it - not just "well that doesn't comfort ME" but something that actually damages the logic. So here's the ask: can you build the strongest case you can that Cato's two horned argument doesn't do the work he thinks it does, because each horn is quietly resting on something he never actually earns? My hunch is that the "soul dies with you" horn is smuggling in a whole theory of what it even takes for something to be bad for a person - like, does someone have to be around to feel a loss for it to count as a loss - and that the "soul survives" horn is smuggling in the assumption that surviving forever is automatically good for you, rather than, i dunno, endless and tedious, or actively worse. Don't just take my rough version of it though - work out the argument properly, and tell me if there's a sharper way to show the whole dichotomy is rigged that I haven't thought of.
