Last weekend a friend and I had a long argument over dinner about a health gadget I bought a few months ago: a blood pressure monitor that I now use every morning instead of getting checked at a clinic. I have no independent way of finding out my actual blood pressure — no cuff-and-stethoscope setup, no scheduled doctor visits — so I described to her a method I had come up with for reassuring myself that the device is trustworthy. Each morning I take the monitor's reading (say, "128/82") and simply accept it as my true blood pressure at that moment. From this it trivially follows that the monitor was accurate that morning, since an "accurate" reading just is one that matches the real number, and I have already treated the two as identical. After doing this daily for a couple of months and getting the same little verdict each time — "accurate again" — I told my friend I had built up a solid inductive case: dozens of mornings, dozens of confirmed-accurate readings, so the monitor must be reliable, no clinic trip required.

My friend saw nothing wrong with this. In her view, every step is individually reasonable: you are entitled to trust a basic reading absent any specific reason for doubt, the inference to "it was accurate this morning" is just a trivial piece of logic, and generalizing from two months of consistent results is exactly how we would assess the reliability of anything else. She also pointed out that this is not so different from how we trust our own eyes or memory in daily life: we never get to independently double-check our senses before relying on them, yet we still treat perception as a genuine source of knowledge. I could not shake the feeling, though, that my "track record" was rigged to succeed no matter what — that it could never have caught the monitor being off by a consistent ten points every single day, precisely because I never checked its output against anything other than itself. Neither of us could say exactly where, if anywhere, the reasoning breaks down, and the argument ended in a draw.

I would like a more considered verdict than either of us reached on our own. Was my discomfort justified, and if so, which specific step in the reasoning fails, given that each one seemed individually sound in isolation? And if my friend has a point instead, does the same maneuver mean that almost any instrument, habit of belief, or even a person's credibility could be "vindicated" purely by using it repeatedly and never cross-checking it against anything external?
