Q: In "Internal and External Reasons," Bernard Williams distinguishes between internal and external reason statements, appealing to the agent's "subjective motivational set" (S). Explain the distinction, then explain why Williams thinks external reason statements are so much harder to make sense of than internal ones. Does his argument show that people who are cruel or indifferent to others have no reason whatsoever to change? (10 pts)

Student answer: For Williams, an internal reason statement ("A has a reason to X") is only true relative to A's S -- the desires, projects and commitments A actually has, or would arrive at through sound deliberation from what's already there. An external reason statement claims A has a reason to X even though nothing in A's S supports it, and it doesn't depend on any such deliberative route. Williams thinks external reasons are incoherent: if telling A that she has such a reason is meant to move her to act, that could only happen if she already had, or could rationally reach, some motivation for it -- but then the reason was internal all along. If no such route exists, it's unclear what the "reason" even consists in. So take someone who genuinely doesn't care about anyone else's welfare: Williams's argument shows that, strictly, there is no reason-statement that's true of him, and since nothing we say to him about how he ought to treat people can be a reason for him, it's strictly meaningless to him. This is basically the same insight behind his motivational internalism: a genuine moral judgement has to motivate the person making it, or it isn't really a moral judgement at all.
