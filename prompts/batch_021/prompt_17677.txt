Question (10 pts): Explain the difference between Wright's etiological account of biological function and Cummins' causal-role account. Then say which of the two accounts has more trouble explaining how a trait can malfunction, and why.

Student answer: Wright's etiological account says the function of a trait is whatever effect explains why that trait is still around today -- a trait has function F if it exists now because past instances of it did F, and doing F is what got it selected for. Cummins' causal-role account is totally different: a part's function is just whatever causal contribution it makes to some capacity of a bigger system we care about explaining, with no reference to the trait's evolutionary history at all. Cummins actually built this account specifically to explain biological traits like organs and circulatory systems, and it only got picked up later by philosophers of mind doing functionalism.

Take the diaphragm as an example: on Wright's view its function is to contract rhythmically, because ancestral diaphragms that did this helped their owners breathe and survive, so the trait got selected for. On Cummins's view, its function is just whatever it currently contributes to the capacity for respiration in the body, full stop, regardless of history.

As for malfunction, I'd say Cummins's account actually handles it more easily than Wright's, since it's only looking at what the part is doing right now -- so whenever the diaphragm stops contributing to breathing properly, we can just say it's malfunctioning. Wright's account has to reach back into a causal history we can't directly observe, which makes it harder to say in the moment whether something counts as malfunctioning or not.
