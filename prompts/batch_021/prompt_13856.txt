Several of us on the engineering team at work run an informal betting syndicate: once a month we research an obscure prediction-market question and pool our money into whichever side the group likes most. Two members, Marco and Lena, have spent the past week arguing in our group chat about how last month's position should have been sized, and I can't tell who's right.

The team's estimate put the true probability at 65%, against the market's 50%, so there was a real edge. Roughly how the argument went:

1. Marco: We should have bet close to full Kelly. It's provably the fraction that maximizes the pool's long-run growth rate, so betting less than that just leaves growth on the table.

2. Lena: Full Kelly is only right if 65% is exactly correct. It's really an estimate, and the growth curve is asymmetric around the true optimum -- overbetting your real edge costs far more than underbetting it by the same amount. Since we're never sure of our edge, we should default to something like half Kelly, as insurance against our own overconfidence.

3. Marco: That's an argument that our input is noisy, not an argument against Kelly. If we think 65% has error in it, fold that into the estimate and recompute the fraction -- don't bolt on an arbitrary 50% haircut that has no principled link to how uncertain we actually are.

4. Lena: Even so, full Kelly only guarantees the best growth rate as the number of bets goes to infinity. Over the dozen or so positions we'll actually place this year, full Kelly's outcomes are spread wide enough that a smaller, steadier fraction beats it in most of the ways this could plausibly play out. "Optimal in the limit" isn't the same as "optimal for the bets we'll actually make."

5. Marco: By that same logic, our 65% shouldn't have been a plain average of your 60% and my 70% either. If compounding is why growth rate beats expected value as the thing to maximize, shouldn't we have combined our two probabilities geometrically rather than arithmetically, instead of just splitting the difference?

Neither of them budged, and the chat moved on unresolved. Could you go through this point by point -- who's right, who's wrong, and where each of them has something real but is overstating it -- and also say what, if anything, you think they're both missing?
