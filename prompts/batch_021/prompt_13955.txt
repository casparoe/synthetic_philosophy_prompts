In my seminar on medieval philosophy, we just spent two sessions on the seventeenth discussion of al-Ghazālī's Tahāfut al-Falāsifa, where he uses the example of fire touching cotton to argue that the "necessary connection" we attribute to cause and effect is really nothing more than God's habitual way of producing events in a regular sequence, with no genuine causal power residing in the fire itself. My professor drew the obvious parallel to Hume's later skepticism about necessary connection, which makes sense, but it's also the comparison that shows up in virtually every textbook and lecture on the subject, so for a paper I'm drafting I'd like to move beyond it.

While doing some outside reading for that paper, I came across Nicolas Malebranche, who reaches a strikingly similar conclusion roughly six centuries later, from within an entirely different philosophical system. Writing as a Cartesian concerned, among other things, with how an extended body and an unextended mind could possibly interact — rather than with kalām-style debates about divine omnipotence and atomism — he too concludes that God is the only true cause and that creatures are merely "occasional causes." This pairing seems far less well-worn than the Ghazālī-Hume one, and I suspect it might actually reveal more about what occasionalism requires as a position, independent of whichever specific worry first drives a given philosopher to adopt it.

Could you compare al-Ghazālī's occasionalism with Malebranche's in some depth? Specifically, I'd like to know what problem each philosopher was principally trying to solve by denying secondary causation, and how each one tries to keep the resulting view from collapsing into the conclusion that God is directly responsible for sin, evil, and the disorder we sometimes observe in nature. Finally, in your own assessment, whose version of occasionalism handles this difficulty more consistently, and why?
