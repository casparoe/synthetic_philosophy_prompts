For the past two years, four friends and I have run a small gaming-commentary channel that started as a lockdown hobby and unexpectedly took off on TikTok and Twitch. We have always split the writing, editing, and on-camera work evenly, and until a few months ago our earnings from ads and sponsorships were roughly equal too. That changed when one of us, Mara, did a solo livestream that went viral: viewers now routinely send her personal tips during her streams, and over the last few months these have added up to several times what the rest of us have earned combined, even though nobody in the group works harder or contributes more than anyone else, by our own honest accounting. When we sat down to decide whether Mara should be required to pool those tips with the rest of us going forward, the argument split the group in a way that felt oddly familiar from a political philosophy seminar I audited years ago.

My friend Priya, who has been rereading Nozick's Anarchy, State, and Utopia since the argument started, says that because every tip was a free gift from a specific viewer to Mara, the resulting distribution is exactly as just as the equal one we started with, and that requiring her to pool the money would just be a more polite way of taking something she is straightforwardly entitled to. My friend Dev, citing G.A. Cohen's response to Nozick, thinks that argument moves too fast: he points out that we built the channel's audience, brand, and algorithmic momentum together, as joint owners of a shared project, so the fact that Mara's tips were individually free does not by itself settle whether the channel's collective assets should keep generating private windfalls for whoever happens to be on camera when the algorithm smiles; a rule requiring pooling, agreed on by all of us in advance, would be no less an exercise of our freedom than a rule permitting unrestricted individual keep. I have since read the actual Wilt Chamberlain passage and Cohen's reply, but I still cannot tell whether Priya and Dev are genuinely disagreeing about justice or merely about what we happened to agree to when we started the channel.

Could you write a dialogue between two people holding these two positions, one arguing in Nozick's entitlement-theory mold and the other pressing Cohen-style objections, in which each has to respond to the other's strongest specific move rather than simply restating their own starting intuition? I would like the entitlement theorist to answer the joint-ownership objection directly rather than just re-narrating the basketball example, and I would like the critic to say plainly what, if anything, is unjust about a particular transfer that both sides agree was free, given that neither of them wants to deny self-ownership. Please do not let either side win by default; I would like to see exactly where their disagreement bites, if it really is a disagreement at all.
