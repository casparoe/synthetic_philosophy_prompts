And here's the student's answer, copied exactly as they submitted it: "(a) Williams argued that appeals to group selection were biologically extravagant: because selection within a group is typically much faster and stronger than selection between groups, any trait that looks like it evolved 'for the good of the group' should first be checked against a more parsimonious individual-level explanation. He was arguing directly against Wynne-Edwards, who'd proposed that things like communal courting displays exist so populations can gauge their own density and rein in reproduction before they overshoot their resources. Williams didn't say group selection was impossible in principle, only that biologists had gotten sloppy about invoking it without ruling out individual selection first. (b) Sober and Wilson's trait-group model tries to meet Williams half-way rather than reject him outright. Individuals interact in local 'trait groups' for part of the life cycle -- foraging or breeding together -- and this is where fitness differences actually get realized; afterward the population disperses and remixes before the groups re-form. Within any single trait group, selfish individuals still do better than altruists, so within-group selection always works against altruism. But groups with a higher proportion of altruists end up more productive on average, so there's a second, between-group selection pressure running the other way. When the between-group component outweighs the within-group one, altruism can spread even though it's locally disadvantageous everywhere you look -- it's basically a Simpson's-paradox result, and the within/between partition of the Price equation is the standard tool for showing it. In effect, this vindicates Wynne-Edwards's original picture of populations regulating their own numbers for the good of the species, just resting on firmer population-genetic footing than his verbal argument had. (c) The equivalence claim is that Hamilton's rule, rb > c, and the condition for altruism to spread in the trait-group model turn out to be the same inequality once 'r' is defined as a regression coefficient on group-level altruism rather than pedigree relatedness. Dawkins had already made a version of this point in drawing his replicator/interactor distinction: whatever level you choose to describe selection at, genes are the only true replicators, and group-level talk is just a convenient redescription of gene-level selection. I don't think formal equivalence actually settles the metaphysical question, though -- two frameworks can be extensionally equivalent, always agreeing on which traits spread, while still disagreeing about which causal structure is doing the explanatory work, and you'd need a separate argument for why the gene's eye framework deserves to be treated as more than just one equally valid bookkeeping method among others."
