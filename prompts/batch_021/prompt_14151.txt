I work on distributed systems, and lately I've been reading about philosophy of time as a side interest. Lamport's classic 1978 paper, "Time, Clocks, and the Ordering of Events in a Distributed System," shows that a distributed system can never recover a single global 'now' across its processes — only a partial ordering fixed by message passing — and it explicitly appeals to special relativity's rejection of absolute simultaneity to justify this, since which events count as simultaneous depends on the observer's reference frame. That seems to settle, as a matter of physics, that the present moment isn't objectively real: it's just an artifact of one's reference frame, which is presumably why physicists prefer to describe reality as a four-dimensional 'block universe' rather than as a series of moments. Given that, I don't see what's actually left for presentists — philosophers who hold that only the present is real — to defend. Is presentism just a position that hasn't caught up with relativity, or is there a serious response to this argument that I'm missing?
