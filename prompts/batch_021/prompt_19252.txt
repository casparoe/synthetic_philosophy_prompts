For my mathematical logic seminar (we're on the unit connecting Gödel/Löb to AI self-modification) we just went over the so-called "Löbian obstacle" — the reason a proof-based agent apparently can't fully trust a successor version of itself even if the successor runs on identical code and axioms. My professor and like half the class kept describing this in pretty loaded language, stuff like "the agent can never really believe its future self is right" or "formal systems have a trust problem," as if Löb's theorem were telling us something deep about trust and rationality in general rather then just being a weird corollary of how provability predicates behave.

Here's my pushback, which I want help turning into an actual argument. Löb's theorem (if T proves Bew(P)→P, then T proves P outright, given the usual derivability conditions) is literally the same machinery that gives you Gödel's second incompleteness theorem as a special case — just plug in P = falsehood and you get "T can't prove Con(T) unless T is inconsistent." Nobody calls that a trust problem, we just call it a fact about relative proof strength. And the standard fix in the tiling agents literature (Yudkowsky and Herreshoff, I think?) is to make each successor use a strictly weaker proof system than its predecessor — which again just sounds to me like a claim about consistency-strength hierarchies, not anything that deserves the word "trust."

So can you write me a tight, well-argued case for the position that the Löbian obstacle is best understood purely as a fact about proof-theoretic/consistency-strength relations between formal theories, and that the popular framing in terms of "self-trust" is a misleading bit of anthropomorphizing that doesnt actually track what the math says? Doesn't need to be super long, but please actually engage with the strongest version of the opposing view (that there's some real epistemic trust-relation being tracked, over and above mere proof strength) instead of strawmanning it — my prof will 100% ask about that objection in section.
