In my metaphysics seminar this term, we've spent the last few weeks working through the simple conditional analysis of dispositional predicates: to say that a glass is fragile, or that a wire is live, is supposed to reduce to something like "if the glass were struck, it would break" or "if the wire were touched by a conductor, current would flow." Two families of counterexamples came up, and I'm still trying to work out how different they really are, philosophically. The first, finkish dispositions (the term comes from C.B. Martin), are cases where the very stimulus that would test for the disposition also changes whether the object has it: a wire wired to a device that cuts the current the instant a conductor touches it remains live at every moment except when the test actually occurs, so the conditional comes out false of a wire that really does have the disposition; wired the other way around, a dead wire hooked to a device that switches the current on the instant it's touched makes the conditional come out true of a wire that isn't live at all. The second, usually called masking or antidote cases, involve no change whatsoever to the object's underlying state: a fragile glass wrapped in enough padding to survive being struck, or a lethal dose of poison co-administered with its antidote, keeps its disposition and causal basis completely intact throughout the test, while something external to the object simply blocks the manifestation from occurring.

Compare finkish and masked dispositions as objections to the conditional analysis. David Lewis's "reformed" conditional analysis tries to rescue the approach from finks by requiring that the object retain, throughout the test, whatever intrinsic causal basis grounds the disposition. Does this fix work just as well against masking cases, or does one of the two phenomena resist repair in a way the other does not? Defend a verdict, and say what it implies about whether dispositions can be analyzed conditionally at all, as opposed to being treated as unanalyzable powers.
