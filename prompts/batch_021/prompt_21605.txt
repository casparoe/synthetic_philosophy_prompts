This term, in my philosophy of science course, we've been working through the literature on structural realism, and I've gotten stuck on Newman's objection to Russell's structuralism in *The Analysis of Matter*. I understand the basic motivation for structural realism itself — for instance, Worrall's point that the mathematical content of Fresnel's theory of light survived, essentially intact, in Maxwell's electromagnetism even though the underlying ontology was abandoned — but Newman's objection is harder for me to get an intuitive handle on. As I understand it, he shows that a claim like "the world has such-and-such structure" is satisfied by almost any sufficiently large domain of objects, more or less automatically, once "having a structure" is spelled out in purely set-theoretic terms, so that the claim ends up carrying no more information than a fact about cardinality. I can follow this line of reasoning step by step, but I don't have a real gut-level sense of why it's supposed to be devastating for epistemic structural realism specifically, rather than a dry technical curiosity that a realist could simply shrug off. Could you explain the intuition behind why the objection actually bites, and then say whether ontic structural realism's strategy of treating relations (rather than structurally-described individuals) as fundamental genuinely escapes the problem, or merely relocates it one level down?
