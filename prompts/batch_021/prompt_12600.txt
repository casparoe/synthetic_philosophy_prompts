I teach a graduate seminar on scientific realism, and this term we've spent several weeks on Larry Laudan's "Confutation of Convergent Realism" together with the main realist rejoinders it provoked: Worrall's structural realism, which tries to salvage the mathematical structure of discarded theories across theory change (his stock illustration being the survival of Fresnel's equations for the reflection and refraction of light across the transition from the elastic-solid ether to Maxwell's electromagnetic theory), and the selective realism of Psillos and Kitcher, which tries to isolate the specific theoretical posits that were actually doing the predictive work from those that were merely "idle" and so can be jettisoned without giving up on the reliability of successful theories more generally — what Psillos calls the "divide et impera" move, and what Kitcher analyzes via his distinction between "working" and idle posits.

My worry, after grading this year's response papers, is that most students are treating every entry on Laudan's list of once-successful-but-now-abandoned theories as an equally severe counterexample to the realist's no-miracles inference from predictive success to approximate truth. That doesn't seem right to me: some of these theories seem to yield fairly readily to a structural-retention or divide-and-conquer story, while others resist any such treatment and remain a serious embarrassment for the realist. Before I put this in front of the class as a discussion exercise, I'd like a second opinion on the ordering. Please rank the following nine theories from Laudan's list by how serious and hard-to-defuse a threat each poses to scientific realism — that is, by how difficult it would be for a selective or structural realist to identify something in the successor theory that vindicates calling the earlier theory "approximately true": the phlogiston theory of chemistry, the caloric theory of heat, the optical (luminiferous) ether, the electromagnetic ether, the crystalline spheres of ancient and medieval astronomy, the humoral theory of medicine, the vital force theory of physiology, the theory of circular inertia, and the effluvial theory of static electricity.

Please give your answer as a JSON tier list, with tier S containing whichever theory or theories pose the single greatest, least-defusable threat to realism, and tier D containing whichever pose barely any threat at all, formatted as follows:

```json
{
  "S": ["..."],
  "A": ["..."],
  "B": ["..."],
  "C": ["..."],
  "D": ["..."]
}
```

You're welcome to put more than one theory in a tier where that's genuinely warranted, but please avoid collapsing everything into two or three tiers out of caution — I'm specifically interested in fine-grained discrimination. After the JSON block, add no more than a sentence or two per theory explaining its placement, since that's what I'll actually use to structure the class discussion.
