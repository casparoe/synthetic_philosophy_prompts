Over the past year I finished an MA in philosophy with a thesis chapter that touched, among other things, on David Lewis's Principal Principle: the claim that a rational agent's credence in an event should equal whatever the objective chance of that event turns out to be, once we set aside "inadmissible" evidence such as direct foreknowledge of the outcome itself. At the time this felt like one abstract puzzle among many. It stopped feeling abstract a few months ago, when I took a job as a pricing analyst at a reinsurance firm, where I'm now expected to treat the outputs of our catastrophe and mortality models -- "there is a 2.3% chance this policy triggers a claim this year" -- as numbers my own beliefs and recommendations should simply track, exactly as the Principal Principle prescribes. It's a strange experience to have a philosophy seminar suddenly show up in your job description.

What's nagging me is a tension I haven't been able to resolve on my own. Lewis named his own overall metaphysics "Humean supervenience" specifically because he wanted chances to be nothing more than whatever falls out of the best systematization of the entire pattern of events in the world -- the fittest trade-off of simplicity and fit with the actual frequencies -- with no hidden dispositions or powers added on top, very much in the spirit of Hume's denial of any real necessary connection between cause and effect. Yet Hume has his own explicit discussion of chance in Book I of the Treatise, where he treats "chance" as essentially a name for our ignorance of causes, and treats a graded belief as a habit produced in us by the observed frequency of past resembling cases, rather than as something reason obliges us to hold. That looks much closer to a purely descriptive, custom-driven story than to Lewis's principle, which is explicitly normative: it says what your credence rationally ought to be, on pain of a kind of incoherence if it comes apart from the chance.

So here is what I would like help thinking through, treating Hume as a real interlocutor rather than just the source of a modern label: if he were presented with Lewis's Principal Principle and the best-system picture of chance behind it, would he have recognized it as the natural, even inevitable, completion of his own account of probability and causation, or would he have resisted it -- and on what specific grounds drawn from his actual arguments, rather than the shorthand "Humean"? I would especially value your view on whether his discussion of "the probability of chances" already contains the seeds of something like a frequency- or best-system-style analysis, and on whether his broader commitments about custom, sentiment, and the limits of reason would lead him to deny that there is any such thing as a credence we rationally "ought" to have at all, as opposed to one we simply find ourselves with.
