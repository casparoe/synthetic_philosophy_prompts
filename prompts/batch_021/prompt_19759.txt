I spent most of my working life as a cosmologist, and since retiring a few years ago I have been trying to fill in the philosophical gaps around ideas I only ever used informally on the job -- chief among them the anthropic principle, which Brandon Carter proposed in the 1970s to explain why we find ourselves in a universe hospitable to observers. What I had not appreciated until recently is that Carter also floated a much stranger piece of self-locating reasoning, later developed by John Leslie and formalised by Nick Bostrom, known as the Doomsday Argument. The idea is that if you treat your own birth rank as a random draw from the set of all humans who will ever live, then discovering that you fall somewhere around the hundred-billionth mark is more probable under hypotheses on which the total human population stays comparatively modest than under hypotheses on which it runs into the trillions -- so elementary Bayesian updating on your birth rank alone should shift your credence toward an earlier rather than a later end for our species.

A reading group of fellow retirees I belong to has been going back and forth on this for weeks, and what none of us can resolve is which of Bostrom's two competing assumptions is correct: the Self-Sampling Assumption (SSA), which treats you as a random sample from your "reference class" and delivers the doomsday shift, or the rival Self-Indication Assumption (SIA), which additionally weights each hypothesis by how many observers like you it contains to begin with, and which appears to cancel that shift out entirely -- at the price of licensing the notorious "Presumptuous Philosopher," who claims he can settle a live dispute between two cosmological theories from his armchair, purely because the theory positing more observers is thereby more probable. Rather than simply tell me which one is correct, I would like you to write a dialogue between two equally sharp philosophers holding these two positions -- one a convinced defender of SSA and the Doomsday Argument, the other an equally convinced defender of SIA -- who happen to run into one another in front of the live population counter in a science museum's exhibit on demography, and end up arguing the matter out on the spot. I would like the SIA defender to press the reference-class problem, and something like the "Adam and Eve" cases Bostrom uses to probe SSA, where self-locating reasoning alone seems to license conclusions about ordinary physical events; and I would like the SSA defender to press the Presumptuous Philosopher in return. Each should respond to the specific objection just raised rather than simply restating their opening view, and neither should concede or fall silent by the end -- I am more interested in seeing exactly where each side is forced to bite a bullet than in a tidy resolution.
