So I've been implementing a distributed consensus thing at work (Raft-adjacent, don't ask) and a teammate linked me "Reasoning About Knowledge" by Fagin, Halpern, Moses and Vardi as background reading for why we keep talking about processes "knowing" things about each other's state. I ended up way deeper in that book than I meant to, specifically the chapter on the "problem of logical omniscience" — the fact that if you model knowledge with possible worlds (an agent knows P iff P is true in every world it can't currently rule out), you get the weird side effect that every agent automatically knows every tautology and every logical consequence of whatever it knows, instantly, for free, no computation required. Which is obviously nuts for anything resource-bounded, whether that's a human brain or a process running on actual hardware with a clock speed.

Once I started pulling on that thread I found theres a whole zoo of proposed fixes: impossible-worlds (non-normal-worlds) semantics, awareness logic, algorithmic knowledge, "step logics" for resource-bounded reasoning, justification logic, plain old syntactic/sentential models of belief, and even this "logical induction" framework out of MIRI that I'd vaguely run into before from AI safety blog posts. As someone with zero formal philosphy training I have no feel for which of these are considered genuinely promising by people who actually work in epistemic logic/formal epistemology versus which are more like "historically important first attempt, mostly superseded now."
