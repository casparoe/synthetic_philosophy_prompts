Over the past year, a few friends and I have become fairly interested in prediction markets such as Polymarket and Kalshi (mostly through play-money versions, since some of us are still minors), and that sent me down a rabbit hole into the game-theoretic side of trading. I kept encountering the claim that if everyone in a market is rational, trades only on private information, and starts from the same prior beliefs, then essentially no trading motivated by that private information should occur at all: the mere fact that someone else is willing to take the other side of your bet is itself evidence you ought to update on, and once you do, whatever expected gain made the trade look attractive should evaporate. (Obviously people still trade for other reasons, like hedging or differing risk tolerances, but set those aside.) As far as I can tell, this is essentially Aumann's agreement theorem — the result that two Bayesians with a common prior cannot knowingly agree to disagree — combined with the no-trade theorems that economists such as Milgrom and Stokey later built on top of it. A friend of mine calls this the "Groucho Marx theorem," after the joke about refusing to join any club willing to have him as a member, though I have no idea whether that is an actual name for it or just something people repeat online.

Separately, I am writing my IB Extended Essay on a topic somewhere in the neighborhood of behavioral versus classical finance, and my supervisor assigned me the chapter of Keynes's General Theory in which he compares professional stock-picking to a newspaper contest, where the prize goes not to whoever picks the objectively prettiest face but to whoever best guesses which faces the other entrants will guess are prettiest, along with the surrounding discussion of "animal spirits" as what actually drives most investment decisions, rather than any careful weighing of probabilities.

Since Keynes died in 1946, decades before Aumann, Milgrom, or Stokey wrote any of this down formally, I would like your best reconstruction of how he would have reacted if someone had explained the no-trade theorem to him. Would he have conceded that the argument is essentially correct for the idealized rational traders it describes, while insisting it is irrelevant to real markets, which run on exactly the kind of recursive second-guessing and "animal spirits" that rule out anything like a shared prior? Or is there a way in which the theorem actually vindicates something Keynes already believed, given how much of his account of speculation depends on people trading precisely because they are not reasoning the way the theorem requires? I would rather have an answer grounded in what Keynes specifically argued about expectation and speculation than a generic guess at what "some skeptical economist" might say.
