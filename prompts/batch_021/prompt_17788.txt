Now that I am retired from a career in observational cosmology, I have taken up philosophy in earnest, and I would like an informed outside check on an argument I have drafted for my local reading group. It concerns the disagreement between the Self-Sampling Assumption (SSA) and the Self-Indication Assumption (SIA) in anthropic reasoning, and a parallel I keep noticing between two literatures that rarely cite one another.

Bostrom's Presumptuous Philosopher is designed as an objection to SIA: told that theories T1 and T2 are empirically equivalent except that T2 entails vastly more observers, SIA permits the philosopher to announce, in advance of any experiment, that T2 is overwhelmingly more probable, simply by counting hypothetical heads. The natural reaction is that this cannot be right: physical theories should be settled by data, not by population-weighted priors over how many observers each theory implies.

Yet the measure problem in eternal inflation appears to license an almost identical move, run in the opposite direction, and it is treated as entirely respectable. Faced with several candidate measures for regularizing the infinite populations of observers that eternal inflation generates, cosmologists routinely reject measures on which the "youngest" possible observers, or Boltzmann brains condensing out of vacuum fluctuations, vastly outnumber observers with a history like ours — precisely because that would make us atypical. No new observation is required; a measure is disfavored purely by what it implies about the relative numbers of observers, which is exactly the move that renders the Presumptuous Philosopher absurd, only run with SSA-flavored typicality in place of SIA-flavored "more observers, more credence."

My tentative diagnosis is that the two cases are not genuinely parallel: the Boltzmann brain argument is a self-undermining, or cognitive-instability, argument about a single theory's internal consistency — if a theory predicts that observers with exactly your evidence are overwhelmingly likely to have delusive, unreliable faculties, it defeats the very grounds on which you accepted it — rather than a straightforward comparison of credences across rival theories individuated by observer population. That would explain why it escapes the "why bother experimenting" absurdity that sinks the Presumptuous Philosopher. I am not, however, fully confident that I have not simply reverse-engineered a distinction to protect the conclusion I wanted from the outset.

I would welcome your judgment on whether this diagnosis holds up, where the disanalogy is strongest or weakest, and whether there is existing work that already makes, or refutes, this comparison that I ought to read before taking the argument any further.
