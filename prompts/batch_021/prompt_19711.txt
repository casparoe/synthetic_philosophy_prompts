So I teach a graduate seminar on formal epistemology this term, and last week's session on anthropic reasoning turned into a proper argument between two of my students, Anya and Theo, that's been bugging me since. Quick context in case it's useful: the self-indication assumption (SIA) says you should reason as if you're a random sample from the set of all *possible* observers, which in effect means a theory predicting more observers gets a boost in probability proportional to how many more it predicts. The self-sampling assumption (SSA) says instead you should reason as if you're sampled only from the observers who *actually* exist within your reference class. The classic case against SIA is Bostrom's "presumptuous philosopher": two cosmological theories fit all the data equally well, but one implies the universe contains vastly more observers than the other, and just as physicists are about to run a decisive experiment, a philosopher strolls in and announces the experiment's pointless, because SIA alone already makes the high-population theory a near-certainty.

Here's roughly how it went. (1) Anya said this is basically a reductio of SIA -- it can't be right that a philosopher gets to outrank an actual experiment without leaving their armchair, just by knowing how populous each theory says the universe is. (2) Theo said fine, but that's a bit rich coming from someone who presumably prefers SSA, since SSA is exactly what generates the Doomsday Argument, which lets you shift your credence about how soon humanity goes extinct purely from your own birth rank, no new facts about asteroids or pandemics required. If armchair reasoning overriding empirical matters is disqualifying, he said, SSA should go too. (3) Anya answered that the cases aren't actually parallel: the presumptuous philosopher's shift can be made arbitrarily large just by inflating the population ratio between the two theories, so in principle it swamps any finite amount of experimental evidence, which is what makes it a reductio. The Doomsday shift, by contrast, is comparatively modest and doesn't have that swamping structure. (4) Theo replied that this is a difference of degree dressed up as one of kind: compare a "doom soon" hypothesis on which only 200 billion humans ever live to a "galactic colonization" one on which 200 quadrillion humans ever live -- your own birth rank favors "doom soon" by a Bayes factor equal to that very same population ratio, a factor that also blows up without bound as you make the second hypothesis more populous. So SSA can swamp arbitrarily strong object-level evidence too, if you pick your comparison hypotheses right, and Anya's bounded-vs-unbounded line doesn't actually seperate the two views.
