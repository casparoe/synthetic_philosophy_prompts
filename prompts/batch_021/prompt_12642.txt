I spent thirty-six years as an analytical chemist running combustion and emissions laboratories, first for two engine manufacturers and then for an independent testing house, before retiring four years ago. Since then I've been working through the metaphysics of science with a local reading group, and we recently spent several sessions on the literature on dispositions: the simple thought that a fragile glass just is something that would break if struck, the trouble that thought runs into once you meet C. B. Martin's "electro-fink" (a device that makes a dead wire live for exactly as long as it takes a conductor to touch it), David Lewis's attempted repair by restricting the analysis to the object's intrinsic properties held fixed just before the stimulus, and the further, structurally different counterexamples that Mark Johnston and Alexander Bird raised under the headings of "masking" and "antidotes," where the disposition's basis survives untouched but something else quietly intervenes between basis and manifestation. It took me embarrassingly long to notice that none of this was abstract to me: a certification test is exactly an attempt to fix "the appropriate conditions" under which a manufactured thing's disposition to perform, or to pollute, is supposed to reveal itself, and much of what I did for a living amounted to trying to keep that test honest.

That resemblance has become concrete rather than merely edifying. I now sit on the technical panel that advises our national vehicle type-approval authority on contested certifications, and a case before us is, on inspection, a fink or a mask wearing an engineering disguise -- the panel is genuinely divided on which. A manufacturer's engine control software appears to recognise the load-and-speed trace of our standard certification cycle and, only while that specific trace is under way, increases the urea-injection rate and retards injection timing so that measured NOx clears the legal limit; under every real-world driving trace our staff have since reproduced on the road, the same hardware reverts to a reduced urea-dosing map and emits roughly three times the certified figure. One member, representing industry, argues that the regulation defines compliance as "would emit no more than X under the specified test," full stop; that the vehicle straightforwardly satisfies this; that demanding robustness against every clever way of detecting a test is an unfalsifiable, regress-prone standard no finite protocol could ever meet; and that voiding a certification the manufacturer earned under the rules as written, rather than the rules we wish we had written, amounts to retroactive rule-making. Two other members want to void the certification and refer the matter for penalties immediately, treating the industry argument as bad-faith rationalisation rather than answering it.

I have to circulate a short written opinion before the panel's vote in three weeks. Could you draft it? I would like it to: (1) say plainly how you would classify the pending case -- compliant, non-compliant, or compliant-pending-further-testing -- and why; (2) propose a specific redrafting of how the standard defines "compliance" going forward, with a realistic timeline for phasing it in, that does not simply relocate the same gaming problem one level up; and (3) engage seriously with the industry member's regress worry rather than waving it away, explaining, if you think it ultimately fails, exactly where it fails. Where it matters to your recommendation whether this case is best understood as finkish or as masked/antidoted, please say so and say why -- I suspect the panel's two camps are each half-consciously gripping one horn of exactly that distinction.
