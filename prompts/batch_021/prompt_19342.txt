A friend and I spent much of last night arguing about the St. Petersburg paradox, until she raised its more vicious cousin: Nover and Hájek's Pasadena game, in which a coin is flipped until it first lands heads and the payoffs alternate in sign and grow without bound. She used it to insist that expected-utility theory faces a much deeper problem than merely assigning a gamble infinite value. I understand that the trouble traces to the payoff series being only conditionally, rather than absolutely, convergent, so that reordering the outcomes changes the sum — but when I tried explaining to her why that particular mathematical fact is what does the philosophical damage, I ended up just invoking the Riemann rearrangement theorem as a black box. Could you make that connection genuinely intuitive, and explain why most commentators treat the outright nonexistence of the expectation as a worse verdict for decision theory than St. Petersburg's merely-infinite one? I'd also like your own take on the deeper disagreement it exposed between us: whether the right moral is that expected-utility theory is incomplete as a normative theory of choice, or that the Pasadena game was never a legitimate gamble to begin with.
