I was moderating a philosophy-of-science panel at a festival last month, and two of the other panelists ended up arguing about Popper and Darwin over drinks afterward, long after the actual panel had wrapped up. (1) Owen, a philosopher, said Popper was dead right the first time around, when he called natural selection a "metaphysical research programme" rather than real science: "survival of the fittest" is a tautology, since fitness just means differential survival/reproduction, so the "law" reduces to "those who survive, survive" and forbids nothing. (2) Simone, an evolutionary biologist, said that's not how fitness actually works -- biologists tie it to independently measured traits (beak depth, running speed, camoflague pattern) fixed before anyone looks at outcomes, so "this trait raises survival odds in this environment" is a genuine, falsifiable claim that sometimes turns out false. (3) Owen said fine, maybe for textbook cases like finch beaks, but most of the time biologists can't specify the trait in advance -- they look at who survived, find whatever those individuals have in common, and call THAT the fit trait after the fact, which just smuggles the circularity back in. (4) Simone said that's a complaint about lazy methodology in particular studies, not about the theory itself, and anyway Popper recanted the tautology charge a few years later and admitted natural selection is testable after all -- so leaning on "Popper said" now means citing a position its own author abandoned. (5) Owen's comeback was that a recantation doesn't settle anything philosophically -- Popper could've been right the first time and just caved to pressure from furious biologists, so the argument should stand or fall on its own merits, not on what Popper said later.

They never landed anywhere and I honestly lost the thread of who was winning. Can you go through moves (1) through (5) and say who's got the better of it at each one -- not just an overall verdict -- and then flag anything you think both of them are missing?
