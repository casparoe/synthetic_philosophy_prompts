I spent most of my career as a behavioral ecologist studying risk-sensitive foraging: the well-documented tendency of animals to depart from simple expected-value maximization when choosing between a fixed and a variable food source, becoming risk-prone when their expected intake falls short of what they need to survive the day and risk-averse once it comfortably exceeds that threshold. Since retiring, I have been working my way through the decision theory literature, and I recently finished Lara Buchak's 2013 book *Risk and Rationality*, in which she develops risk-weighted expected utility (REU) theory as an explicitly normative account of choice under risk: on her view, a rational agent may weight a gamble's outcomes according to their rank among the possible outcomes rather than according to their bare probabilities, by means of a "risk function" that captures an attitude toward risk wholly independent of the curvature of the agent's utility function. What struck me, coming from my own field, is how closely this formal device resembles John Quiggin's older rank-dependent utility (RDU) theory from 1982, which, as I understand it, was originally introduced to accommodate observed departures from expected-utility maximization, such as the Allais paradox, rather than to vindicate such departures as rational.

I would appreciate your help thinking through the relationship between these two frameworks. Could you compare Buchak's REU theory with Quiggin's RDU theory, focusing on what, beyond their shared machinery of rank-dependent probability weighting, actually distinguishes them as theories? More specifically, does Buchak's normative reinterpretation of this machinery succeed in showing that such rank-dependent risk attitudes are genuinely rationally permissible, or does the fact that the very same formal apparatus was first developed to model anomalous or biased choice behavior count as evidence against treating it as a theory of rationality at all? Please justify whichever position you defend.
