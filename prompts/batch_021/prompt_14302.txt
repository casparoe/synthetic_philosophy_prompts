I write a column on science and ideas for a general-interest outlet, and I am drafting a piece on causation by omission: the puzzle of whether failing to do something can be a genuine cause of what follows, rather than just grounds for blame. Before I file it, I would like an outside read from someone who knows the literature. Below is the current draft. I would particularly value comments on three things: whether I have represented the two rival solutions accurately and fairly; whether the closing move is a legitimate philosophical inference or a stretch; and whether the central example carries enough weight for a reader with no background in philosophy.

Draft: "The Trouble With Not Doing Anything"

Last summer, a neighbor who had agreed to look after a community garden plot went away for three weeks and forgot to arrange for anyone to water it. The tomatoes died, and nobody hesitates to say her negligence killed them. But look closely at what that claim requires. Thousands of other people also failed to water those tomatoes that month — everyone in the building next door, every stranger on the subway, the mayor, me. Each of those non-waterings satisfies exactly the counterfactual test we would use to convict the neighbor: had they watered the plants, the plants would have lived. So why does her omission count as a cause of the tomatoes' death, and not theirs?

Philosophers sometimes call this the problem of "profligate" causation by omission, and it is not merely a parlor trick — it sits underneath real disputes about negligence and safety regulation. One influential answer says an omission counts as a cause only when the agent had some prior duty or expectation to act: the neighbor had taken responsibility for the garden, so her failure is causally loaded in a way the mayor's is not. This is roughly how the common law has long handled the question, refusing to recognize any general duty to rescue, so that a bystander who watches a stranger drown owes no causal explanation while a lifeguard who does the same becomes a cause of the death (several continental European legal systems, by contrast, criminalize failing to assist). Critics, including skeptics about omissive causation like Helen Beebee, object that this gets the order of explanation backwards: we want to say the neighbor is blameworthy because her omission caused the tomatoes to die, but if "caused" already presupposes a judgment about duty, causation is just wearing the clothes of moral responsibility rather than grounding it.

A newer line of work tries to avoid moralizing causation so directly. Building on formal, counterfactual models of causation, philosophers including Joseph Halpern and Christopher Hitchcock argue that an omission counts as a cause only relative to a background of "normal" or default conditions: the neighbor's watering the plants was the expected course of events, given that she had taken the job on, whereas a stranger's watering them was never a live possibility worth tracking. That looks cleaner until you ask what makes a course of events "normal" — a matter of statistics, or of what people are supposed to do. Hitchcock and Knobe's experimental work suggests ordinary causal judgments trade on exactly that ambiguity: people are likelier to call something a cause when it also violates a norm, moral or otherwise. If so, the second solution may simply relocate the moralizing rather than remove it.

If that is right, the profligate-omissions puzzle is not a special defect of omission cases. It suggests our judgments about ordinary, "positive" causes are quietly normative too, and we only notice it with omissions because there the background conditions are too numerous to take for granted.

That's the draft as it stands. I'd welcome your take on the three points above, and on anything else that seems off.
