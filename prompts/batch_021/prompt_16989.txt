Ok so this happened at journal club and I still can't decide who won. We're both behavioral-ecology PhDs, and the argument was between two people in my cohort about whether "countersignaling" — like a tenured prof who dresses like he rolled out of bed, or old money wearing totally unmarked clothes — is even compatible with the handicap principle, or whether it's secretly a different mechanism wearing the same name.

Mara's opening move: if Zahavi's whole point is that cost is what enforces honesty, then a signal that costs the high-quality sender basically nothing (the famous prof looses zero status by looking scruffy) can't be doing the honesty-guaranteeing work handicaps are supposed to do. Call it whatever you like, it's not a handicap.

Deniz's reply: that conflates the cost of the signal with the cost of faking it. In Grafen-style ESS versions of the principle, what has to be expensive is a low type mimicking the signal, not the honest performance itself — a nobody who dresses shabby just reads as a nobody, so the relevant cost gets paid off the equilibrium path, same as healthy peacocks arguably paying very little marginal cost for their tails at the ESS.

Mara's comeback: fine, but "costly to fake, for reasons I can specify after the fact" applies to literally any behavior that happens to correlate with status, so the principle stops predicting anything and just relabels whatever equilibrium we already believed in.

Deniz's last word: not any behavior — only ones where you can independently show a low type would get pooled with genuinely low quality if they tried the same move. That's basically the countersignaling story economists tell (Feltovich/Harbaugh/To, if I've got the cite right), and it's a real structural claim, not a relabeling.
