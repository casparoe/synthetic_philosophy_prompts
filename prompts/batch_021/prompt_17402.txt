I'm one of four students on the rules committee for my school's Computer Science Society, and after our fall tournament we're stuck on a dispute none of us feels equipped to settle. For the past two years the tournament has just been a standard iterated Prisoner's Dilemma bot-off, but this year, after a couple of us read about a corner of game theory sometimes called 'program equilibrium' or 'open-source game theory' — the idea that letting two players inspect each other's source code before acting can support cooperation that classical game theory says is irrational — we redesigned it as a single-shot format in which each bot's complete source is shown to its opponent before either one has to output Cooperate or Defect.

Here is the complication. Two of our strongest competitors, who are close friends and wrote their entries together, each submitted a bot that cooperates if and only if the opponent's source code is identical to its own once comments and whitespace are stripped out, and defects otherwise. Because their two files matched, the bots cooperated with each other every time and defected against the rest of the field, which was enough to lock up first and second place overall. A third competitor has filed a complaint arguing that this is collusion dressed up as strategy — no different from two players signaling under the table — since the 'cooperation' tracks friendship rather than any reasoning about the opponent. The two accused students respond that they broke no written rule, exchanged no information once the round started, and simply used the format's own mechanic, mutual code visibility, exactly as it was meant to be used. They also point to a fourth competitor, a stranger to both of them, whose bot instead cooperates only when it can verify from the opponent's code — using a small proof search one of them says was inspired by something called Löb's theorem, from a paper on 'robust cooperation' — that the opponent would cooperate with it specifically; that bot ended up cooperating with several strangers over the course of the tournament, and nobody has objected to it.

Our advisor wants the committee's ruling in writing before Friday's awards ceremony, since a small prize fund rides on the final standings. Could you draft that memo for me? It should actually rule on the complaint — uphold it, dismiss it, or land somewhere in between, including any effect on the standings — and it should propose a workable rule for next year's format that draws a principled line between legitimate uses of code transparency and collusion. Please take the accused students' defense seriously rather than simply assuming that two friends cooperating must be cheating; if there is a genuine difference between what they did and what the fourth bot did, I'd like the memo to say plainly what it is and why it should matter for the ruling.
