I spent my career as a comparative ethologist, working mostly on problem-solving and social learning in corvids and cephalopods, and since retiring a few years ago I have been filling gaps in my education by reading through the history of moral philosophy. That background makes Kant's confident claim that animals simply lack any relevant capacity for judgment feel a little dated, even if understandable for his time, and it eventually brought me to his scattered remarks on animals in the "Doctrine of Virtue" section of the Metaphysics of Morals and in the student notes collected as the Lectures on Ethics. From there, on the recommendation of a friend in my philosophy reading group, I moved on to Christine Korsgaard's book Fellow Creatures, which tries to use recognizably Kantian machinery to reach a conclusion quite different from Kant's own.

As I understand it, Kant denies that we can have any direct duties toward animals, on the grounds that they are not rational and therefore not "ends in themselves"; our duties "regarding" them are merely indirect duties to humanity, justified by the thought that gratuitous cruelty (his stock example is having a dog shot once it is too old to be of use) dulls the very sympathy we need in order to treat other human beings decently, while something like gratitude toward a long-serving animal is worth cultivating for the sake of our own character rather than the animal's. Korsgaard wants to preserve Kant's basic derivation of why rational beings count as ends in themselves - roughly, that a being whose own nature makes its good normatively binding on it thereby has unconditional worth - but she argues that this derivation, honestly generalized, does not actually require rationality at all: any creature that pursues ends "tethered" to its own nature, for whom things can genuinely go better or worse, has a good of its own in the relevant sense, and so should count as an end in itself with a claim on our direct concern rather than merely our indirect one.

What I would like from you is a close comparison of these two positions rather than a general survey of "Kant and animals." Specifically: does Korsgaard's tethered-ends argument amount to a legitimate extension of Kant's own premises about the ground of unconditional worth, or does it have to quietly discard the very feature - rational autonomy, the capacity to step back and endorse or reject one's ends - that was doing the philosophical work in Kant's original route to the Formula of Humanity? Put differently, is she completing Kant's argument or replacing it with a broadly sentimentalist, interest-based theory dressed in Kantian vocabulary? I would be grateful for an actual verdict, with reasons, rather than just a balanced summary of both sides.
