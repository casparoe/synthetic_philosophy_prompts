Marcus, my closest friend and statistical conscience for three decades, died last month, and I have been asked to deliver his eulogy at a memorial service that allots about eight minutes to personal remembrances. The request arrives at an odd moment for me: I spent thirty-five years running a cell-biology lab, publishing p-values for most of that time without ever pausing to ask what they meant philosophically, and that reckoning has only caught up with me in retirement, through a philosophy-of-science reading group I joined somewhat on a whim and which recently spent a whole term on frequentist versus Bayesian inference and the replication crisis.

Marcus was a Neyman-Pearson man to his marrow, and he watched the last fifteen years of methodological reform in the biomedical and social sciences with a very specific irritation -- not at preregistration, registered reports, or larger sample sizes, all of which he championed as perfectly good frequentist practice, but at the way each of these fixes got retold, at conference after conference, as a vindication of Bayesian philosophy. 'They kept the arithmetic and stole the credit,' he liked to say. Two of his own doctoral students became committed Bayesians along the way, and neither relationship with him ever entirely recovered, which he regretted far more than he ever let on. Not long before he died, he told me, half joking, that I was not to let his funeral become the occasion on which 'the Bayesians finally won' -- but he was equally firm that I was not to turn his eulogy into a defense of significance testing either.

The room at the service will hold, in roughly equal measure, statisticians who fought this exact fight with him for years, working scientists like me who used his methods for decades without ever examining their foundations, and family members who know only that he was rigorous, stubborn, and kind. Could you write that eulogy for me? It needs to be faithful to Marcus's actual, considered position -- not a caricature of either side of this dispute, and not a suggestion that the matter has been settled in his favor -- while still functioning as a real eulogy: specific and warm enough that the people in the room who have never heard of a p-value have something to hold on to.
