Explain what 'dilation' refers to in this setting, illustrating with a simple example. Why has dilation struck some philosophers as a problem for the imprecise-probabilities framework, and how might a defender of the framework respond?"

Student's answer: "An imprecise probability model replaces a single credence function with a whole set of probability functions consistent with the agent's evidence — a 'credal set.' For any event A this lets us talk about a lower probability P_*(A) and an upper probability P^*(A); when they're equal your opinion is sharp, and when they diverge that gap is supposed to represent genuine indecision rather than just a fact you happen not to know.

Dilation, an idea Walley illustrates with a pair of coins, is the counterintuitive result that conditioning on new evidence can widen this gap instead of closing it. Say coin 1 is known to be fair, and coin 2 is also fair, but you're told nothing about how the two are correlated — for all you know they could be perfectly matched, perfectly opposed, or anything in between. Unconditionally your credence that coin 1 lands heads is a precise 0.5, since that's true no matter what the correlation is. But once you condition on how coin 2 landed, the answer for coin 1 depends entirely on which correlation is the true one, so your credal set for coin 1 dilates all the way out to [0,1].

This is unsettling because we normally assume free evidence can only help you, never hurt you, and dilation seems to show an imprecise agent should sometimes rather not look — which is really just the Ellsberg paradox's ambiguity aversion showing up again, this time in updating instead of choice. Defenders reply that each distribution in the set is still being conditioned in the ordinary Bayesian way, so nothing incoherent is happening distribution-by-distribution; the appearance of paradox only arises if you additionally assume rationality requires evidence to sharpen belief, which they deny."
