"Good's theorem says that for a Bayesian decision-maker, the expected utility of running a cost-free experiment before choosing an action is always at least as great as the expected utility of choosing an action without running it. Formally, if V(no info) = max_a Σ_θ P(θ)U(a,θ) is the value of acting on your prior alone, and V(info) = Σ_x P(x) max_a Σ_θ P(θ|x)U(a,θ) is the value of observing an experiment's outcome x and then acting optimally given the resulting posterior, Good's theorem says V(info) ≥ V(no info). This is because "best achievable expected utility given a posterior" is a concave function of the posterior distribution, so by Jensen's inequality, averaging over the possible posteriors (which average back out to exactly the prior, by the law of total probability) can only help you relative to just using the prior straightaway — experimenting for free literally cannot lose you expected value on average.

One consequence people don't always appreciate is that a rational Bayesian should always want more free evidence before acting, full stop: Blackwell's theorem tells us that any two experiments can be ranked by informativeness, and every decision-maker, whatever their prior and utility function, prefers the more informative of two experiments. So Good's result basically falls out of Blackwell's theorem once you compare a given experiment against the totally uninformative "null" experiment.

That said, this doesn't mean you should always go empirically looking for information even when its free — information can carry its own hedonic cost. Someone deciding whether to take a free test for an untreatable genetic disease might rationally decline, since learning the result can't change any action available to them but might make them miserable if the news is bad. This isn't a real counterexample to Good's theorem though, since the proof assumes utility is a function of the action taken and the true state alone, not of what you believe. Once dread at learning bad news gets folded into "utility," finding out is itself now an action with its own payoff, and the theorem doesn't straightforwardly apply to that expanded problem anymore."
