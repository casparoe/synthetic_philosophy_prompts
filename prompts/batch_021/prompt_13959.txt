I have spent the last few weeks working on a feature about why fact-checking so often fails to change the minds of people who hold obviously false beliefs, and interviewing epistemologists for it kept pointing me back to the same slim, posthumously published book: Wittgenstein's *On Certainty*. The idea, as it was explained to me, is that some of what we take for granted — Wittgenstein's own examples are homely ones, like the fact that the earth has existed for a long time, though people working in this literature now also reach for things like "I am not a disembodied brain in a vat" — function as "hinges": propositions we do not hold on the basis of evidence and cannot really investigate or doubt, because they are, in the book's most-quoted line, "like hinges on which [our doubts] turn" (§341). What I could not get a straight answer on, and what turns out to be genuinely contested among the specialists I spoke to, is a seemingly basic question: are these hinge commitments beliefs — just permanently ungrounded, unevidenced ones — or are they something categorically different, a kind of certainty that is shown in what we do rather than represented in a proposition we assent to? Duncan Pritchard and Danièle Moyal-Sharrock are usually credited with the second, "non-doxastic" view; other commentators, Annalisa Coliva prominent among them, insist hinges remain beliefs in every sense that matters, just ones that happen to be groundless.

For a sidebar to the piece, I would rather stake out a position than just narrate the dispute, so I am hoping you can help me pressure-test one side of it. Please argue, as rigorously as you can, for the non-doxastic reading: that our relation to hinge propositions is not belief, however groundless, but a more primitive kind of certainty that is not a propositional attitude at all. I would like the argument to confront its two hardest problems head-on rather than route around them. First, if hinges are not beliefs, you owe an account of how something non-doxastic can still do the job hinges are supposed to do — fixing in advance which ordinary propositions even count as candidates for doubt or evidence — without smuggling belief back in through the side door. Second, you will need to deal with the passages in *On Certainty* where Wittgenstein himself seems perfectly comfortable describing these bedrock commitments in terms of an inherited "world-picture" that we come to hold, which sounds for all the world like a description of believing something.
