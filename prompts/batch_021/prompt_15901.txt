I'm drafting a short paper on whether algorithmic information theory secures the objectivity often claimed for it as a formalization of Occam's razor, and I'd like your comments before I develop the argument further. Here are my current notes:

(1) Solomonoff induction and the associated notion of Kolmogorov complexity are often presented as finally putting Occam's razor on a rigorous footing: "simplicity" is cashed out as the length of the shortest program that generates a hypothesis or data string on some fixed universal (Turing) machine, and induction becomes a matter of assigning higher prior weight to hypotheses with shorter programs.

(2) The standard answer to the obvious worry — that complexity is relative to a choice of machine or programming language — is the invariance theorem: for any two universal machines A and B there is a constant c, depending only on A and B and not on the string x, such that |K_A(x) - K_B(x)| <= c for every x. This is usually glossed as showing that machine-relativity is a bounded nuisance rather than a fatal problem for objectivity.

(3) My worry is that the theorem bounds the disagreement only by a constant that itself depends on which pair of machines you compare, and that constant has no independent upper bound. Given any hypothesis H that looks complex on some "natural" machine, one can always build a second universal machine that treats a single reserved codeword as shorthand for H, so that H's description drops to essentially one bit, while every other string's shortest description grows by at most one bit (to preserve a prefix-free code). That is enough, in general, to reverse the complexity ranking of H against a rival hypothesis H' of otherwise comparable, everyday size. So for exactly the case that matters for real theory choice — two specific, human-sized rival hypotheses, rather than the asymptotic behavior of complexity as string length goes to infinity — there need be no machine-independent fact about which one is simpler.

(4) If that's right, the machine-relativity of Kolmogorov complexity is not a new difficulty created by formalizing Occam's razor; it is Goodman's grue problem wearing computational dress. Insisting on a single "privileged" or "natural" reference machine to block the construction in (3) just reintroduces, through the choice of machine, the very appeal to naturalness or entrenchment that the algorithmic approach was supposed to make unnecessary.

I'd welcome your comments — in particular, whether I have the invariance theorem and the machine-swapping construction in (3) right, whether the Goodman analogy in (4) is doing real philosophical work or is merely superficial, whether there are standard replies from the Solomonoff-induction literature I need to address before taking this further, and whether this point has already been made prominently enough elsewhere that I should be citing it rather than presenting it as my own.
