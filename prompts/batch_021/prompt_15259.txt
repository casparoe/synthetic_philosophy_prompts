I'm drafting a chapter of a novel narrated by a former student of Hannah Arendt's, now herself an aging political theorist, who spends the chapter sorting through her late teacher's papers and ends up mentally reconstructing the lecture Arendt never lived to give: the one on truth, lying, and politics in an age of synthetic media. I want that imagined lecture to read as something Arendt herself could plausibly have written, not as a magazine think-piece that borrows her name and the word "Orwellian" and calls it a day — so before I draft the scene, I need to work out, on the merits, what she would actually have argued.

Here is the specific difficulty I keep running into. In "Truth and Politics" (1967) and "Lying in Politics" (1971), Arendt treats the traditional political lie as one that conceals a particular secret while leaving the surrounding world of established fact intact; the modern, PR-driven lying she diagnosed in the Pentagon Papers, by contrast, tries to substitute one coherent, internally consistent picture for reality itself — and she seems to think this kind of large-scale image-making is inherently effortful and ultimately self-undermining, since the fabricators must keep patching and extending their story against a reality, and a community of witnesses, that keeps reasserting itself. What I want to know is whether that self-limiting mechanism, on her own premises, survives contact with generative AI, which lets a political actor manufacture unlimited, mutually consistent fake video, audio, and documents at essentially no cost, removing the very expense of coherence she seems to be counting on. Would she treat this as simply the Pentagon Papers problem taken to its logical extreme, or as something closer to the condition she describes decades earlier in The Origins of Totalitarianism, in which people lose the capacity to distinguish fact from fiction altogether rather than being handed a rival but still checkable set of facts? And what would she make of the further wrinkle that much of today's actual disinformation doesn't even bother with the coherence she assumed a convincing lie needs, and instead floods people with mutually contradictory stories precisely in order to exhaust their confidence that the truth is knowable at all?

Please write out the substantive core of that lecture — or working notes toward it, whichever lets you argue more rigorously — in a voice that could plausibly be hers, but don't let the ventriloquism outrun the argument. I want an actual, defended position, built out of her real vocabulary and premises rather than generic media-literacy commentary, and I would much rather you mark the point at which her framework runs out and any further extrapolation becomes speculative than paper over that point with a confident-sounding non-answer.
