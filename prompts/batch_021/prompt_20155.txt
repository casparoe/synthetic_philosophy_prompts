Early in my career as a novelist, a well-established writer I barely knew agreed to read an unsolicited draft of my first book. She saw promise in it, spent the better part of a year helping me revise the manuscript, and personally vouched for me to her own agent — a favor that probably made the difference between that manuscript's languishing in a drawer and its eventually being published. She asked for nothing in return except a pledge: once I was in a position to do for someone else what she had done for me, I would. She mentioned, half in jest, that she had been burned before by protégés who made similar promises in the heat of gratitude and quietly let them lapse the moment they no longer needed her, so she had gotten fairly good at telling who actually meant it. I gave her my word, and at the time I meant it completely.

That was nine years ago. My fourth novel comes out this spring, and I now get unsolicited manuscripts from strangers with some regularity. I notice I keep finding reasons not to engage with them seriously — I am busy, the industry has changed, I already showed good faith by meaning the promise sincerely back when it counted. When I described all this to a friend recently, she pointed out, not unkindly, that this reasoning would have made it perfectly sensible for me to promise insincerely in the first place, which unsettled me more than I expected, since I know for a fact that I was not being insincere at the time.

I would like help thinking this through properly rather than just sitting with a vague sense of guilt about it. Was my promise really only ever a means of getting me through that vulnerable early moment, in which case honoring it now serves no further purpose and can reasonably be retired, or is there something wrong with that way of framing what it actually means to make a sincere promise in the first place?
