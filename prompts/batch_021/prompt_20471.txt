I'm finishing a longread on the strange afterlife of art forgeries for a magazine that covers science and ideas, and I keep getting stuck on the same philosophical splinter. The case that started it was Han van Meegeren, the Dutch forger whose fake Vermeers were so convincing that one was sold to Hermann Göring during the war; when the Allies later charged him with treason for selling a national treasure to the enemy, his only way out was to confess to the forgery and then paint a new "Vermeer" under guard to prove he could do it. What strikes me isn't the trial so much as what happened to the paintings afterward: nothing about their pigment, brushwork, or craquelure changed the instant they were exposed as fakes, yet they instantly lost most of their market value, and by several contemporary accounts, some of the same connoisseurs who had once praised them as masterpieces now claimed to see their crudeness plainly. Nelson Goodman pointed to cases like this to argue that "autographic" arts such as painting differ in kind from "allographic" ones like music, since a painting's identity is bound up with the actual history of its production in a way a musical work's isn't; Walter Benjamin, coming at it from another angle, wrote about a work's "aura" dissipating even when a copy is materially indistinguishable from the original.

I'd like you to write me a short story (roughly 800–1,200 words) that dramatizes this puzzle without simply retelling the van Meegeren story. You could set it in a near-future conservation lab where some new imaging process can produce a physically undetectable replica of a painting, or in a small provincial museum where a beloved local treasure's authenticity comes under sudden question, or somewhere else entirely — the setting, characters, and plot are yours to invent. What matters to me is that the story earns its emotional weight from the specific tension Goodman and Benjamin are gesturing at, namely that learning an object is a copy can transform how it is experienced and valued even though nothing physically detectable about it has changed. I'd rather you let that tension sit unresolved at the end than have a character step in and neatly explain it away.
