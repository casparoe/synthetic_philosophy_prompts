In my political theory seminar, we've spent the last few weeks on Locke's account of political obligation in the Second Treatise, especially his distinction between tacit and express consent: on his view, merely residing in a territory, holding property there, or even just travelling its roads binds a person to obey its laws, but this tacit consent alone does not make someone a full member of the political society — that requires express consent. This got me thinking about a live debate today: several jurisdictions now grant long-term non-citizen residents the vote in at least some elections, on the reasoning that people who live under a government's laws and contribute to its upkeep deserve some say in making them. Reasoning strictly from Locke's own premises, rather than from present-day politics, would he have endorsed extending the franchise to such residents, or does his tacit/express consent distinction actually give principled grounds for excluding them? Does this case expose any tension within his theory of political obligation itself?
