So this happened at a town meeting I only went to because there was also a water-rate hike on the agenda, and it ended up eating the whole night. Our town has this extreme haunted-attraction type place out past the fairgrounds — I don't even know its real name, everyone just calls it "the Cellar" — where adults pay to go through hours of mock-abduction, restraint, and general terror and humiliation stuff. It's fully consensual: huge waivers, a safeword that apparently actually works (a coworker's done it twice), medics on-site, nobody's been seriously hurt in the ten-ish years it's been open. Its permit came up for renewal and the room was packed with people wanting it pulled, not because anyone's been hurt against their will but because, as one guy put it, "no decent town should have that going on inside it." Two people I know, Mara (a 2nd-year law student) and her uncle Walt (on the zoning board for something like 20 years), kept going at each other about it in the parking lot afterward, and neither one struck me as obviously wrong, which is why I wrote down as much as I could remember.

Mara opened with: nobody's coerced and nobody outside those walls is affected, so this is exactly what the harm principle exists for — keeping the law out of stuff that's really a private-morality question, not a public one. Walt said that's too clean: a town isn't just a pile of separately-harmed-or-not individuals, it's held together by a shared sense of decency, and that shared sense is worth protecting for its own sake, same as the town protects its own existence against anything that threatens it outright — let enough "well, nobody's technically hurt" exceptions through and eventually there's no shared "town" left, morally speaking. Mara shot back: okay, but where's the evidence one haunt is going to unravel the social fabric? By that logic any strongly-felt majority prejudice could get written into permanent law, including ones we'd now call bigoted — you need some way to sort the town's actual considered morality from "enough people are grossed out," and being grossed out can't do that sorting on its own. Walt's answer surprised me: he said demanding people's convictions be "considered" and "reasoned" before they count is its own kind of gatekeeping, that most people don't need a philosophy class to know something's degrading when they see it, and that if morality only counts once it clears some intellectual bar, you've quietly written most people's actual moral sense out of the room. Mara came back with: the town isn't even consistent about this — the bar downtown runs "final gulp" drinking contests that've sent people to the ER, and nobody's coming for that license — which suggests the Cellar's just an easy, weird-looking target, not evidence of any real principle. Walt's last word was that uneven enforcement doesn't kill a principle; society gets serious about one thing before it gets around to another all the time, and that lag doesn't show the underlying worry about the Cellar is mistaken.
