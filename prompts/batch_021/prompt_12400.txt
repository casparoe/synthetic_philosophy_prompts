I convene a small, biweekly reading group on formal ethics and social choice theory with a handful of my graduate students, and we spent our last two sessions working line by line through Harsanyi's 1955 aggregation theorem: the result that if every individual's preferences over lotteries satisfy the von Neumann-Morgenstern axioms, if the social ranking over those lotteries satisfies them as well, and if the two agree whenever every individual is indifferent between two prospects, then the social utility function must be a constant plus a weighted sum of the individual utility functions. Afterward, two of my students, Marcus and Elif, fell into an extended disagreement over coffee about what the theorem actually establishes, one that ran long enough that we never got back to the applications I had planned for that day. I've reconstructed their exchange as faithfully as I can below; both were arguing in good faith, and neither budged.

(1) Marcus said the theorem is close to a proof of utilitarianism for anyone who accepts expected-utility rationality: you don't get to keep the von Neumann-Morgenstern axioms for individuals and for society, plus a bare unanimity condition, and still reject some form of weighted-sum aggregation -- maximin and other social welfare functions that aren't additively separable across people are simply off the table for anyone who accepts the premises. (2) Elif objected that this oversells the result: the individual utility functions in the proof are von Neumann-Morgenstern functions, pinned down only up to each person's own arbitrary risk-scaling, not to anything that measures how well their life is actually going, and -- as Sen pointed out -- the specific weights the proof extracts change if you rescale any individual's utility function, which you are always free to do without changing that person's actual preferences over gambles. Real utilitarian weights shouldn't be an artifact of which representative function you happened to write down, so what the theorem hands you is a fact about risk attitudes wearing the costume of welfare. (3) Marcus replied that this is a complaint about the label 'utilitarianism,' not about the force of the constraint: call u_i whatever you like, the theorem still forces the social ranking to be some affine combination of the individual rankings, and that already rules out plenty of positions people actually hold. (4) Elif came back with a case she said doesn't depend on any interpretive dispute at all: take two candidates with a perfectly symmetric claim on one scholarship, equal in every respect the theorem could care about, so that they get equal weight. Run that through the affine form the theorem delivers, and giving the scholarship to A outright, giving it to B outright, and flipping a coin to decide are all exactly tied. But almost nobody actually thinks that: the coin flip is the only one of the three that gives each candidate a fair shot instead of picking a winner by fiat, and a theorem whose own conclusion can't tell 'flip fairly' apart from 'arbitrarily favor A' has lost something real about fairness. (5) Marcus answered that nothing is lost if the case is told honestly: if the candidates genuinely care about the fairness of the procedure and not only about who wins, that preference belongs in their von Neumann-Morgenstern utility functions from the outset, at which point they are no longer indifferent among the three options and the tie disappears -- you don't get a real counterexample by quietly changing what's in the utility function partway through the example. (6) Elif answered that this move saves the theorem by emptying it: if any ethical consideration can always be relocated into 'what the utility function really contains' whenever it's needed, then Pareto indifference was never the thin premise it was advertised as, and the theorem has been rendered compatible with anything -- which is not the same as having been defended.
