I'm teaching a graduate seminar on philosophy of language this term, and we just spent two weeks on Davidson's radical interpretation stuff, mainly "Radical Interpretation" (1973) and "Belief and the Basis of Meaning," with a bit of Quine's indeterminacy of translation before that as background. Every single year I hit the same wall getting this to land: the second I mention "figuring out what someone means when you share no language with them at all," everyone's mind jumps straight to Arrival-style alien linguistics or code-breaking, and they miss what's actually weird and specific about Davidson's version of the problem. You literally can't solve for what someone means without simultaneously solving for what they believe (and vice versa), and the only way in is to assume up front that the speaker is mostly rational and mostly right about the world (the "principle of charity"), then let meaning and belief settle out together from that starting assumption. It's not translation in Quine's sense, where you're matching an unknown tongue onto bits of a language you already have -- its interpretation from absolute scratch, with nothing held fixed except your own charity toward the speaker.

Anyway, I want to try assigning some fiction alongside the readings this year instead of just another lecture with the same two diagrams on the whiteboard. Could you write me a short story (1000-1500 words is fine) that actually dramatizes this specific bind, rather than being a generic first-contact-and-decode-the-language story? Setting's up to you -- a field linguist embedded with an isolated community, a first-contact scenario, someone trying to work out whether some new kind of machine intelligence is even saying anything at all, whatever grabs you -- but I want the plot to hinge on the interpreter having to make an uncomfortable, consequential call about whether to attribute a strange or false belief to the speaker versus revise their whole guess about what the speaker's words mean, so the charity principle is actually doing dramatic work instead of sitting in the background as flavor. If you can find a natural way to work in Davidson's later idea of "triangulation" -- that meaning requires two creatures responding to a shared world and to each other, not just one clever interpreter alone with a notebook -- even better, though don't force it if it doesn't fit.
