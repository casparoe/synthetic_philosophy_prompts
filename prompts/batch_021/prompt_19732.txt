Over the past year I've become deeply involved with a parish-based coalition that advocates for immigrants facing deportation, and the experience has forced me to think much harder than I used to about the relationship between my faith and my political speech. Several of the more theologically serious people I've met through this work are drawn to the "postliberal" or integralist critique of liberalism associated with writers like Adrian Vermeule and Patrick Deneen, who argue that the kind of self-restraint Rawls asks of religious citizens is either incoherent or a quiet way of disqualifying religious reasoning from politics altogether. The timing feels almost pointed, because this semester I'm also taking a seminar that pairs Political Liberalism with Rawls's own undergraduate thesis on sin and faith — the one he wrote as a young man who had once intended to enter the ministry, and which wasn't published until 2009, long after he had already built his mature, resolutely secular apparatus of public reason. I can't stop thinking about the distance between that devout young author and the philosopher who spent his later career trying to keep comprehensive doctrines at arm's length from politics.

What I'd like from you is your best reconstruction of how Rawls himself might have responded to developments that mostly postdate his death in 2002. I'm especially interested in whether he would have counted today's integralists and Christian nationalists as advancing a "reasonable" comprehensive doctrine entitled to a hearing within public political culture, or whether their explicit rejection of liberal neutrality would itself disqualify them on his own terms; in how someone in my position should apply the "proviso" and the wide view of public political culture when the whole point of the organizing is to move policy using frankly scriptural language, and whether Rawls's own appeal to the abolitionists and to Martin Luther King Jr. really licenses that or instead trades on a disanalogy; and in whether he would have read the recent run of Supreme Court decisions expanding religious exemptions as confirmation that an overlapping consensus was doing its work, or as a sign that the consensus was already coming apart. I'd rather you work through the actual mechanics of his view — the proviso, the burdens of judgment, overlapping consensus — than rest on generic claims about church and state, and I'd be genuinely interested if you think his own religious biography pulls against the theory he spent his career building.
