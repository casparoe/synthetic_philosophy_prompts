For the past few months, a few colleagues and I have been running an informal prediction-market and calibration-training group at work, partly inspired by the idea — which I understand traces back to Robert Aumann's "agreement theorem" — that two people who trust each other's rationality and share the same priors shouldn't be able to knowingly keep disagreeing about a probability estimate once they've each stated what they believe. That sent me down a rabbit hole into the philosophical literature on "peer disagreement," where I gather the two main camps are conciliationism (roughly, that discovering an epistemic equal disagrees with you should move your credence substantially toward theirs) and steadfastness (roughly, that you can sometimes be justified in holding firm anyway). From what I can tell, people like David Christensen and Adam Elga are associated with the conciliationist side and Thomas Kelly with pushing back against it, though I'm sure I'm fuzzy on some of the finer distinctions between their positions, since I'm coming at this with zero formal training.

What's been bothering me is a wrinkle I think I picked up from something by Elga: conciliationism is itself just another philosophical claim that plenty of smart, well-informed epistemologists reject, and those epistemologists seem, by any reasonable standard, to be the conciliationist's epistemic peers on the question of how to handle disagreement in general. So if I actually accept conciliationism, and I notice that it is itself the object of exactly the kind of sustained, informed disagreement it tells me to worry about, doesn't the view instruct me to lower my own confidence in it? That feels like either a fatal, self-consuming flaw or a strangely appropriate case of the theory eating its own tail, and I haven't been able to work out which on my own.

Could you write a rigorous, opinionated argument for the position that conciliationism is self-undermining in a way that isn't rescued by the obvious fixes? I'd specifically like you to engage with at least one serious escape route a conciliationist might reach for — for instance, some principled distinction between disagreements over first-order factual matters and disagreements over fundamental epistemic methodology, or a distinction between one's credence in a general policy and how one should act on it in a particular case — and show concretely why that move does or doesn't save the theory. Please end on an actual verdict rather than leaving the puzzle open; "the objection succeeds, but only establishes something a sensible fallibilist should have granted anyway" is a fine place to land, as long as you argue for it rather than just narrating both sides.
