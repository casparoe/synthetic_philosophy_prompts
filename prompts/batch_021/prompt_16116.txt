For a dissertation chapter on early modern theories of causal power, I keep returning to a question I haven't seen addressed in the literature. Hume holds that our idea of causal power reduces entirely to the constant conjunction of event types plus the mind's habitual expectation that like will follow like; strictly speaking, we never perceive any "power" or "necessary connexion" residing in objects themselves. Twentieth-century philosophers, in the tradition of Ryle, tried to vindicate this same reductive spirit for dispositions generally, analyzing a claim like "this vase is fragile" as meaning simply that the vase would break if struck – but that conditional analysis famously foundered on finks and masks. A dead wire rigged to a device that instantly energizes it the moment a conductor touches it (a fink, of the sort first described by C. B. Martin) makes "if touched, it would shock you" true of a wire that isn't otherwise live, while a fragile vase packed in styrofoam remains genuinely fragile even though it wouldn't break if dropped (a mask). Given Hume's actual commitments in the Treatise and first Enquiry, how should he respond to these cases: do they show that causal and dispositional talk can never be fully cashed out in terms of actual and counterfactual regularities, pushing him toward exactly the sort of mind-independent power he spent so much effort resisting, or is there a recognizably Humean way of accommodating them? I would also value your view on whether "New Hume" readers such as Galen Strawson, who take Hume to be a closet realist about hidden causal powers, are better placed to handle finks and masks than the traditional regularity reading is.
