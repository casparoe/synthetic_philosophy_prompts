I spent my career as a field ecologist, and in retirement I have been slowly working through decision theory, where Herbert Simon's notion of satisficing keeps coming up. Two people in the philosophy discussion group I attend got into a running disagreement about it that I could not settle myself. (1) Marta opened by claiming that satisficing is not a genuinely distinct rational strategy: once you price in the cost of continued search or deliberation, stopping at the first option that clears some threshold is exactly what expected-utility maximization recommends, so 'satisficing' is just optimizing under a fuller accounting of costs. (2) Dennis objected that this erases a distinction Simon himself drew between procedural and substantive rationality: a genuine cost-aware maximizer recomputes, at every step, whether the marginal value of further search still exceeds its cost, while a satisficer instead follows a standing aspiration level and stops once it is met, without rerunning that calculation each time -- so the two rules can issue different verdicts even when search is costly. (3) Marta replied that any aspiration level worth calling 'rational' must itself be updated as circumstances change, and once that is granted, the updating rule is doing exactly the optimizing that Dennis wants to deny is happening. (4) Dennis's comeback was that demanding an optimal updating rule only relocates the problem: working out the best way to revise a threshold is itself a costly computation, and insisting it be solved perfectly generates an infinite regress of meta-level optimizing that a genuinely bounded agent cannot carry out -- so a non-optimizing stopping rule has to enter somewhere.

Could you adjudicate this move by move? Please give each of the four a score from -2 (clearly mistaken) to +2 (clearly right), briefly justify each score, and then say whether Marta and Dennis are, between them, overlooking some more basic point about satisficing and bounded rationality.
