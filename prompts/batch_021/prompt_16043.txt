I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and next month I am interviewing an evolutionary biologist who has spent her career defending and extending Amotz Zahavi's handicap principle, from animal ornaments to costly religious ritual in humans. Draft eight to ten interview questions that push past a bare restatement of the idea that costly signals are honest because only high-quality individuals can afford them. I want at least one question on how Alan Grafen's formal evolutionarily-stable-strategy models are supposed to have rescued the principle from the objections raised by theoretical biologists like John Maynard Smith, who found Zahavi's original verbal argument logically dubious; one on whether the cost of a signal such as the peacock's tail is best understood as a strategic cost that itself enforces honesty, or merely an efficacy cost needed to make an already-reliable signal detectable; and one on how she would empirically distinguish her account of such an ornament from a Fisherian runaway process, which can produce the same exaggeration with no connection to underlying quality. For each, include a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give, and close with a question about what observation would actually count as evidence against the handicap principle.
