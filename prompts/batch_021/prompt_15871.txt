Here's the setup: you draw a random chord on a circle and ask what's the probability it's longer than the side of the equilateral triangle inscribed in that circle. Sounds like a well-defined question, right? Bertrand (1889) points out three totally natural ways to generate a "random" chord that all sound equally reasonable, and they give three different answers:

1. Pick two random points on the circumference and connect them. P(chord longer than triangle side) = 1/3.
2. Pick a random radius, then a random point along it (uniform between center and edge), and draw the chord through that point perpendicular to the radius. P = 1/2.
3. Pick a random point uniformly inside the disk and let that be the chord's midpoint. P = 1/4.

Same phrase, "pick a random chord," three different, individually-consistent procedures, three different numbers. That's the paradox, and its basically the sharpest illustration there is of why the principle of indifference (the Laplace-y idea that if you're totally ignorant between options you should spread probability evenly over them) is broken as stated — "evenly" secretly depends on which variable you're being uniform in, and "I don't know anything" never tells you which variable that should be.

The fix I find most satisfying is Jaynes'. His move is basically: the problem only looks unsolvable because we haven't used all the info that's actually given — we know it's a circle, and nothing pins down that circle's exact size or position, so whatever distribution we use has to be invariant to sliding or resizing the circle, or it's smuggling in info we don't have. He runs the maximum entropy principle over that invariance requirement and it spits out exactly one of the three methods (the "random radius" one, giving 1/2) as the only self-consistent choice. Kind of a beautiful move, turning "I don't know" into an actual constraint instead of a shrug.

The bit I keep chewing on: this is exactly the situation every time someone reaches for an "uninformative" or "objective" prior in Bayesian ML, like using a Jeffreys prior because you want something that doesn't privilege one parametrization of your model. Same move as Jaynes' — instead of asking "what distribution reflects my ignorance," you ask "what distribution is invariant to how I happened to write the problem down." Which also means "I have no prior info" isn't really a coherent state you can just encode as a flat prior and walk away from — you always need some extra structural assumption to get a unique answer. Bertrand's chord included.
