Last night, a friend and I argued for well over an hour about a case in the spirit of Taurek's "Should the Numbers Count?": suppose you have a single dose of a life-saving drug that can either save one stranger outright or be divided to save each of five other strangers instead, but not both. My friend maintained that one obviously ought to save the five, since doing otherwise would mean treating four additional people's survival as counting for nothing. I found myself defending Taurek's far stranger conclusion: that because each person's death is a complete loss to that person alone, and losses to distinct people do not sum into one larger loss the way a single person's five lost fingers would, one is permitted, and perhaps even required, to give both sides an equal chance, for instance by flipping a coin, rather than automatically handing the drug to the larger group. Neither of us managed to land a decisive blow on the other's position.

Could you write a dialogue between a committed Taurekian and an equally sharp aggregationist, in which each presses the other on their hardest case rather than simply restating slogans? I would like the aggregationist to force the Taurekian to say what they would do at a very lopsided ratio, such as one person against a thousand, and I would like the Taurekian to make the aggregationist explain exactly what licenses treating many people's separate losses as a single larger loss to be weighed against a smaller one. Feel free to draw on the kind of objections Parfit raises in his reply "Innumerate Ethics," or on related worries about the separateness of persons, but make sure both characters genuinely engage with each other's specific arguments instead of talking past one another.
