I'm a retired physicist who's gotten into philosophy of science lately, and I keep running into the Tycho Brahe vs Copernicus setup trotted out as the classic case of underdetermination of theory by evidence -- before decent telescopes, Tycho's model (earth sits still, sun goes around it, but the other planets circle the sun) fit the naked-eye data just as well as full Copernican heliocentrism. That always bugged me a bit as a scientist though, because the two models don't actually make identical predictions forever: a genuinely moving earth should eventually show up in stellar parallax and aberation, and, if its spinning too, in stuff like a Foucault pendulum -- its just that 17th century instruments weren't anywhere near good enough to catch any of that yet. So argue for the position that the Tycho/Copernicus case is really just "transient," evidence-poor underdetermination dressed up in the textbooks as the strong, permanent, in-principle kind that Duhem and Quine actually had in mind, and that philosophers of science ought to stop reaching for it as their go-to illustration of real underdetermination.
