So I spent about thirty-five years as a behavioural ecologist – mostly contest behaviour in red deer and fiddler crabs – and now that I'm retired I've somehow drifted from Sapolsky's determinism book, through Dennett, into these rationalist/AI-safety blogs, which is a strange place for an old zoologist to wash up of an evening. The thing that's been stuck in my head for a couple weeks now is what people over there call the "commitment races problem." As I understand it (and correct me if I've mangled it), the worry is this: if you're bargaining with another sufficiently sophisticated agent, and you both know that whoever binds themself to a strategy first gets the upper hand – that's basically just Schelling's old point about the value of commitment – then each of you has an incentive to lock yourself in as early and as irrevocably as possible, before you've had time to actually think the situation through or learn much about the other side. Since both sides reason this way, you get a race to commit blind and fast, which runs exactly backwards to the usual advice that more information and more deliberation make for better decisions.

What I'd like from you is one single, fully worked-out example of that exact dynamic – it doesn't need to involve AI at all, a pair of firms, two states, or even, given my own background, two animals sizing each other up in a contest would suit me fine – where you show, step by step, how each side's decision to commit early is individually the locally rational move, and yet the two commitments together lock in an outcome that's worse for both of them than if they'd simply stayed flexible a while longer. Then use that same example to pin down what's actually doing the work: what has to be true – about credibility, about the impossibility of backing down once committed, about each side seeing (or half-seeing) the other's move coming – for this to count as a genuine commitment race, and not just an arms race or a game of chicken with a fancier hat on. My neighbour, who's some kind of retired economist, insists there's no real difference and it's all just chicken; I don't beleive that's right, but I can't yet say precisely why.
