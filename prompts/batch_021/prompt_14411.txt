I never studied philosophy in school, but ever since I got recruited onto my county's new "diversion panel" for protest-related arrests, I can't stop turning it over in my head. The District Attorney's office set up a small volunteer panel, pulled from the same pool as jury duty, to review low-level charges connected to political protests -- trespassing, blocking traffic, that sort of thing -- and recommend whether they should be dropped, reduced to a small fine, or sent back into the regular court system. The pitch we got at orientation was that people engaged in genuine civil disobedience shouldn't necessarily be handled like ordinary criminals, but nobody ever wrote down what "genuine" is supposed to mean, so every meeting turns into a fight. My husband thinks I'm overanalyzing what's basically a stack of trespassing tickets, but the longer I sit with it the more it feels like the panel is quietly deciding something much bigger than that. I looked up "civil disobedience" one night hoping for a clean checklist and found a hundred years of philosophers arguing with each other instead, which was interesting but didn't tell me what to actually do this Tuesday.

The case on our docket: a dozen people opposed to a new asphalt plant going up next to an elementary school locked themselves to the plant's gate at 5 a.m., blocking delivery trucks for about six hours until police cut them free with bolt cutters. Nobody resisted once they were freed, nobody was violent, and the organizers say they'd spent two years filing public comments and petitions before doing this. Half the panel wants to call this textbook civil disobedience and recommend dismissal, since it has the marks people usually point to: open, nonviolent, a last resort, no attempt to flee arrest. The other half argues that quietly waiting to be arrested, the way people famously did at lunch counters, is a different act altogether from locking yourself to a gate so that police need hours and bolt cutters just to reach you -- the first accepts the law's authority even while breaking it, the second just resists being stopped, and stretching "civil disobedience" to cover both empties the term of whatever made it deserve special treatment in the first place. Underneath that, there's a question nobody on the panel wants to say out loud: a couple of us admitted we'd feel entirely different about a clinic blockade by protesters on the opposite side of the abortion debate using the identical tactics, which makes me suspect our sympathy runs toward the cause and just borrows the language of civil disobedience to dress itself up.

Could you write the short memo I'll bring to Tuesday's meeting? I need an actual recommendation for what to do with this batch of charges, not just a list of considerations, plus some standing criteria the panel could point to next time regardless of whose cause is involved -- and please take the losing side's best argument seriously rather than waving it off, since that's the half of the room I'll actually have to convince.
