In the centipede game, backward induction implies that a fully rational player must defect ("take") at the very first move, forfeiting the much larger joint payoff available from even a little further mutual cooperation — a prescription famously at odds with both ordinary intuition and experimental behavior. Robert Aumann's epistemic game theory seemed to vindicate this prescription: he proved that common knowledge of rationality alone, without any auxiliary appeal to trembling hands or bounded reasoning, is sufficient to force the backward-induction outcome. Critics such as Ken Binmore and Cristina Bicchieri have replied that Aumann's proof only goes through by assuming that players can coherently go on believing in an opponent's future rationality even at nodes which, on the theory's own terms, a rational opponent should never allow the game to reach — a difficulty already anticipated by Philip Pettit and Robert Sugden's earlier "backward induction paradox." Write an essay that engages with this specific dispute over counterfactual reasoning and common knowledge off the equilibrium path, rather than settling for the intuitive appeal of cooperation, and that defends a definite verdict on whether backward induction is genuinely forced on ideally rational agents in finite games of perfect information such as the centipede game. A qualified verdict is acceptable — for instance, one that distinguishes what a fully demanding notion of common knowledge of rationality requires from what is merely permissible for more realistically idealized agents — but a purely descriptive summary of the dispute is not.
