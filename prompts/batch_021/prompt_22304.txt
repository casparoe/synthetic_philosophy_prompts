Over dinner last week, a friend and I ended up in a long argument about whether Plato was really as austere about bodily pleasure as he is usually taken to be. My friend's position, built mainly on the Republic and the Phaedo, was that for Plato the entire task of philosophy is to train reason to master or transcend appetite, so that anything designed to loosen self-control — communal drinking chief among them — ought to count as straightforwardly bad on his own premises. I countered with the discussion of wine-parties in Book I of the Laws, where the Athenian Stranger appears to defend carefully regulated drinking bouts as a genuine training-ground for self-mastery, on analogy with the way facing danger trains courage. We never quite settled how that argument is supposed to work, and going back to Jowett's translation this morning left me more puzzled than before — especially the passage below, which comes right after the Stranger has proposed that such gatherings need "a sober man and a wise" appointed as "master of the revels"; earlier in the same discussion, he has already secured Cleinias's agreement that wine "heighten[s] and increase[s] pleasures and pains, and passions and loves" precisely by making perception, memory, and prudence "desert" the drinker.

"Now suppose such associations to be framed in the best way possible in states, and that some one blames the very fact of their existence — he may very likely be right. But if he blames a practice which he only sees very much mismanaged, he shows in the first place that he is not aware of the mismanagement, and also not aware that everything done in this way will turn out to be wrong, because done without the superintendence of a sober ruler. Do you not see that a drunken pilot or a drunken ruler of any sort will ruin ship, chariot, army…"

What I can't pin down is exactly how much weight the pilot-and-ruler analogy is meant to carry. Read one way, it is a fairly modest, almost administrative point: a badly run institution reflects on its management, not on the institution as such, so that the Spartan and Cretan policy of banning wine outright discards something that could in principle be salvaged by better supervision. Read more ambitiously, though, the Stranger seems to be claiming that a properly supervised symposium could reliably serve as a genuine test and training exercise for self-control, on a par with how a controlled brush with danger trains courage. If the second, more ambitious reading is the intended one, doesn't his own earlier concession about wine — that its whole effect is to inflame the passions precisely by disabling perception, memory, and prudence — work against him? A single sober president seems like thin protection once you grant that the entire point of the exercise is to strip everyone else of the very faculties that would keep the evening from getting out of hand. I would be grateful for help sorting out what the argument actually needs to establish in order to succeed, and whether this stretch of the text gives the Stranger the resources to meet that worry, or whether my friend's more austere picture of Plato on pleasure survives the Laws passage better than I initially gave it credit for.
