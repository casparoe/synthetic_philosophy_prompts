I've been reporting on wild-animal-welfare stuff lately, and it actually sparked a real argument at dinner between two friends of mine, Dana and Leo. We'd been on a hike and passed a half-eaten rabbit, and Dana said: this is basically what nature looks like most of the time -- huge amounts of suffering -- and if suffering is bad no matter who or what causes it, we at least owe it to these animals to look into ways of reducing it, like wildlife vaccines or fertility control for the species that boom-and-bust the hardest. Leo said ecosystems are tightly coupled feedback loops, so tweaking one variable (predation, infant mortality) usually just relocates the suffering to disease or starvation later, and brought up the cane toad disaster in Australia as proof we're bad at this. Dana said fine, thats an argument for caution, not for treating hands-off as some default virtue -- we already intervene on wild populations constantly for other reasons (rabies bait drops, contraceptive darts for deer) and nobody calls that hubris. Leo said those are narrow, local fixes, nothing like being committed in principle to reducing predation as such, which really just means wanting to re-engineer the whole food web, and that how often the small interventions backfire should make us way more nervous about that giant one. Neither of them budged and honestly I can't tell whos winning which point. Can you go through the exchange move by move, say who's right about what (not just who wins overall), and flag anything you think both of them are missing?
