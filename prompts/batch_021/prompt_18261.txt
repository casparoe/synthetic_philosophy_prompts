I've spent enough years reading rationalist blogs and forums — LessWrong chief among them — that Newcomb's Problem is, by now, thoroughly familiar territory: one-boxing, two-boxing, causal versus evidential decision theory, and the newer functional-decision-theory attempts (with their timeless- and updateless-decision-theory ancestors) to dissolve the whole dispute are all old friends. Lately, though, a small philosophy reading group I belong to has been working through Leibniz's *Discourse on Metaphysics*, and one thread of it kept nagging at me in a way that felt oddly familiar. Leibniz argues that the "complete concept" of an individual substance — his own example is Alexander the Great — already contains, from eternity, absolutely everything true of that person, including free actions still to come, so that God, surveying the concept alone, can read off everything Alexander (or any of us) will ever do. A few sections later, though, Leibniz insists this doesn't make our choices necessary the way geometric truths are: they are certain, but only "hypothetically" so, grounded in God's free choice of this world as the best among infinitely many possible ones, and knowable only through what he calls an infinite analysis that no finite mind could ever carry out. The standard illustration of this part of the doctrine — Leibniz's own, if I remember right — is Caesar's decision to cross the Rubicon: contained in his concept and foreknowable from eternity, yet, on Leibniz's account, never necessary.

It struck a few of us that this is really a theological ancestor of Newcomb's Problem: some entity whose "prediction" of your choice is, for all practical purposes, infallible, arrived at by means that don't run through your choice causing anything. One member of the group insisted that Leibniz's apparatus commits him to one-boxing, since on his view the correlation between foreknowledge and action reflects your own nature rather than a mere coincidence that clever dominance reasoning is entitled to exploit. Someone else thought Leibniz would refuse to engage with the thought experiment at all, on the grounds that his notion of contingency requires exactly the kind of "could have gone otherwise, holding fixed everything the predictor knows" that Newcomb's setup seems to rule out by stipulation. We went back and forth for the better part of an hour without resolving it.

Leibniz obviously never encountered anything resembling Newcomb's Problem, so I am not asking you to dig up a quote — I would like your own reconstruction instead. Reasoning from his actual doctrines of complete concepts, hypothetical versus absolute necessity, compossibility, and infinite analysis, what would Leibniz most plausibly say if confronted with the standard modern setup (an opaque box, a predictor with a long track record of accuracy, the usual thousand-dollar and million-dollar stakes)? Would his framework commit him to one-boxing or two-boxing, or does it undercut the question itself — and either way, how would he defend that verdict using his own metaphysical resources rather than the ones decision theorists have since invented?
