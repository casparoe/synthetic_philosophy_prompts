So this week my epistemic logic seminar spent basically the whole session on Fitch's paradox and I haven't been able to stop poking at it since (I should honestly be writing my own seminar paper instead of bugging you with this but whatever). Quick version in case it's useful context: if you buy the knowability thesis -- every truth is at least possible to know -- plus two pretty modest-sounding logical principles, that knowledge distributes over conjunction and that knowledge entails truth, you can derive that every truth is already known, which is obviously absurd. Fitch apparently just noted this almost in passing in his 1963 paper and moved on, and it only became a big deal decades later once people realized its basically a bomb planted under Dummett-style semantic anti-realism, since anti-realists are the ones who actually need the knowability thesis to be true.

What's been bugging me is the standard anti-realist comeback: restrict the knowability thesis so it only covers what Tennant calls "Cartesian" propositions -- roughly, truths that aren't guaranteed to be unknowable just by their logical form, so something like "p is true but nobody knows it" gets excluded while ordinary garden-variety unknown truths don't. My advisors take is that this is basically gerrymandering the thesis to dodge the counterexample, and that if you have to restrict it like that you've already conceded the realist's point. I keep going back and forth on whether that's fair every time I reread the exchange, so I'd rather just see two people who actually know what they're doing argue it out in front of me.

Don't let either of them turn into a mouthpiece who just restates their opening move -- they should actually respond to what the other one just said, concede points when they have to, and honestly I'd love it if it ended without a clean knockout, since I don't think this debate actually has one.
