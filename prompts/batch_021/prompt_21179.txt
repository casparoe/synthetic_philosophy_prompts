I've spent a long time reading LessWrong, Astral Codex Ten, and assorted MIRI technical reports, and a recent re-read of "Tiling Agents for Self-Modifying AI, and the Löbian Obstacle" sent me back into an old disagreement with a friend from my local rationalist meetup, this time about Gödel's second incompleteness theorem rather than about tiling agents as such. The theorem tells us that Peano Arithmetic cannot prove its own consistency, on the assumption that PA is in fact consistent, and that ZFC, being considerably stronger, is in exactly the same position with respect to itself. My friend's argument, which I've never quite managed to talk myself out of, is that this should count as at least mild Bayesian evidence that PA and ZFC are inconsistent after all: a formal system that could vouch for its own soundness would obviously be more trustworthy than one that structurally cannot, so the inability to do so ought to cost a healthy axiom system a few basis points of credence, in roughly the way a suspect's failure to produce an alibi should nudge a rational juror toward "guilty," even though it's nowhere near proof. It strikes me as slightly odd that working mathematicians treat the consistency of PA and ZFC as close to certain when, by their own best theorem, these systems are structurally incapable of the one thing that would settle the question directly.

Given that this seems basically right to me, I'm hoping you can help me put a number on it. I recall that MIRI's work on logical induction was specifically concerned with assigning coherent, evolving probabilities to arithmetical statements, including consistency claims, as a reasoner accumulates computational evidence over time, so it seems like that framework, or something in its spirit, ought to tell us how many basis points of credence PA and ZFC should each lose on account of their Gödelian inability to prove their own consistency, over and above whatever credence they separately lose from the more familiar consideration that no one has found a contradiction yet, but one might still turn up tomorrow. Is there a principled way to quantify that specific penalty?
