And here's the student's answer, also verbatim: "Hegel's target here is basically Kant's essay on perpetual peace, though what he's objecting to is subtler than 'Kant wanted world government and Hegel didn't.' Kant's proposed federation of free states was meant to be voluntary rather than a coercive world-state sitting over its members, precisely so it wouldn't collapse into the kind of despotism Kant worried a single global sovereign would produce. Hegel's point is that this voluntariness is exactly the problem: a league that depends on being freely entered and freely respected is, in Hegelian terms, just a treaty, and a treaty for Hegel is always at the mercy of the particular will of the states party to it, since nothing like a domestic court's authority actually stands over them to enforce it. So the Kantian proposal can guarantee peace only by ceasing to be a mere league of sovereign states -- i.e. by becoming a coercive super-state, which would just relocate the problem rather than solve it, since you'd then have to ask what stops a plurality of such super-states from facing exactly the same difficulty one level up. Because states remain genuinely sovereign -- meaning, for Hegel, having no earthly superior -- unresolved disputes of right between them have no third party to appeal to, and can only be decided by the states' own force; whichever side prevails is 'right' only in the retrospective sense that world history, as the 'world's court of judgement,' vindicates it after the fact, not because some antecedent tribunal ruled on the case in advance.

Hegel then adds a further, less obvious claim: that permanent peace wouldn't even be good, not just unreachable, because a people that never has to close ranks around the state starts treating civil society's ordinary business -- private property, contracts, the pursuit of individual advantage -- as though these were the highest things there are. War interrupts this by making the state call on individuals to risk exactly the goods, property and physical safety, that had come to seem absolute, and in doing so shows that those goods were never more than 'ideal,' i.e. subordinate moments within the ethical whole, rather than ultimate ends that the state exists to serve, which is the instrumental picture of the state Hegel is pushing back against in thinkers like Locke.

Because Hegel thinks this whole business of dealing with other states has to issue from a single, undivided act of will rather than from deliberation among many, he assigns the actual decision to declare war to the legislature acting through the Estates, on the same participatory model he uses for approving taxation: it's one more place where the universal has to pass through the particular wills of the people's representatives before becoming actual. And because no power stands over sovereigns to enforce anything, war for Hegel isn't bound by rules like safe conduct for envoys or the sparing of civilians in the way domestic law binds a citizen -- those are courtesies, not obligations."
