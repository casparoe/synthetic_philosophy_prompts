While reporting a piece on rational choice theory, I had dinner with two philosopher friends whose conversation turned into a real argument about something that had happened at a whisky tasting the weekend before. One of them, Mara, had made a sequence of trades with another guest at the tasting: she paid $8 to swap her pour of a single malt for a blend, $10 more to swap that blend for a bourbon, and $12 more to swap the bourbon back for a pour of the single malt she'd started with -- ending the night $30 poorer and holding the same whisky she began with.

When Mara told the story, her friend Theo called it a textbook 'money pump': no stable ranking of the three whiskies can rationalize all three trades, so her preferences must have cycled, and cyclic preferences are exploitable by anyone patient enough to walk you around the loop. Mara said that's too quick -- palates drift over a long evening of tasting, and three comparisons made an hour apart aren't the same as one incoherent judgment held all at once. Theo pointed out that all three trades happened within about twenty minutes at the same table, with the same three pours in front of her the entire time, and that she'd admitted afterward that a direct, no-trading comparison between the single malt and the bourbon would always favor the bourbon -- which flatly contradicts what her own third trade required. Mara's answer was that catching a pattern like that in real time isn't something anyone does perfectly: a version of her with a sharper memory but identical tastes would have refused the third trade rather than pay $12 to land back where she started, so the failure was one of attention, not of preference.

Neither of them would budge, and I didn't feel qualified to settle it myself. Could you go through the exchange move by move, say who has the better of each point, and flag anything you think both of them are missing?
