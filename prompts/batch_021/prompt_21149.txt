For my AP Research paper this year, I've built the whole project around a probability puzzle called the Judy Benjamin problem, and before I write the rest of the paper I want feedback on whether the core argument actually holds up. Here is the section that lays out the puzzle itself:

Judy Benjamin is a soldier dropped into a region split evenly between Blue Army territory (her own side) and Red Army territory, each of which is further split evenly into a "Headquarters Company Area" and a "Second Company Area." Before she hears anything, she should spread her credence 1/4 over each of the four combinations. Then a radio message gets through: she's told that if she has actually wandered into Red territory, it's three times more likely she's in their Headquarters Area than their Second Company Area, and then the radio dies.

Intuitively, that message is entirely about how Red territory breaks down internally. It never compares Red to Blue, and it says nothing about the Headquarters/Second split inside Blue. So my first instinct was that her credence in Red versus Blue should stay locked at 1/2 each, with only her split within Red moving, from 1/4-1/4 to 1/8-3/8.

That is not what happens if she updates by picking the new distribution that satisfies the 3:1 constraint while staying as close as possible, in the relative-entropy (KL-divergence) sense, to her prior — the same quantity minimized as a loss function when training a classifier, just applied here to a whole belief state instead of a model's output layer. This has been the standard resolution since van Fraassen introduced the example in 1981, and running the optimization gives approximately P(Red, Second) ≈ 0.117, P(Red, Headquarters) ≈ 0.351, and P(Blue) ≈ 0.532. Her credence in being in friendly territory rises above 1/2 from a message that never mentioned Blue at all. It gets stranger still: the same thing happens for any asymmetric split you plug in instead of 3:1, and as the split is pushed to its extreme, P(Blue) creeps toward 2/3 — exactly what ordinary conditionalization gives you if "overwhelmingly likely Headquarters" is simply treated as "definitely not Second."

My best attempt at explaining why: this kind of updating is a global operation on the whole distribution, not a local edit to one corner of it. Forcing a lopsided 3:1 split inside Red is a costlier, lower-entropy state to be in, and the update offsets some of that cost by shifting credence toward Blue, the one region the evidence left free to stay perfectly even. Uneven news anywhere in the space has to be "paid for" somewhere, and the bill doesn't fall only on the region the news was actually about. I'm not confident that's a real explanation rather than a restatement of the math, and I'm even less sure the "Red and Blue must stay at 1/2 each" intuition survives once the extreme-split case lines up with ordinary conditionalization.

Could you go through this and tell me:
1. Whether my diagnosis in the paragraph above actually explains the shift, or just redescribes it;
2. Whether the extreme-split argument is good enough reason to give up the "irrelevant information shouldn't move Red versus Blue" intuition, or whether that intuition can be defended some other way;
3. Whether there are alternative ways of updating on conditional information, besides relative-entropy minimization, that avoid this shift, and if so, what they give up in exchange;
4. Whether anything in the numbers or the setup above is actually wrong.

I have to defend this paper in front of a panel next month, so I'd rather you find the holes now.
