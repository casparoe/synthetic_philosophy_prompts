I'm running a graduate seminar this term on Humean approaches to laws and chance, and this week we worked through the undermining problem for Lewis's Principal Principle -- the "big bad bug" from his 1994 "Humean Supervenience Debugged" paper. Quick recap for context: if chance is supposed to supervene on the whole Humean mosaic, including stuff that hasn't happened yet, then there are "undermining futures" that are compatible with the chance of A being x right now but which, were they to come about, would make it the case that the chance had actually been something else all along. That seems to wreck deference to chance pretty badly, since full information about such a future should rationally push your credence away from x even while you also know, by hypothesis, that the chance is x. There's a whole cottage industry of fixes for this at this point, starting with Lewis's own New Principle (a move that, apparently, Ned Hall and Michael Thau independently landed on around the same time) and running up through later attempts to patch that further, or to just ditch Humean supervenience about chance entirely.

My students can recite each of these moves fine but have zero feel for which ones anyone still actually takes seriously vs. which are more like dead-end curiosities that get a footnote and nothing else -- and honestly, its been a few years since I taught this material closely enough to be confident of my own ranking.
