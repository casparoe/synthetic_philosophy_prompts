I'm a PhD student in philosophy, and my dissertation compares Derek Parfit's reductionist account of personal identity with the classical Buddhist doctrine of anatta. One of my closest friends from graduate school -- we actually met on a ten-day silent retreat, of all places -- is getting married next month, and she has asked me to give the toast. She and her partner are both long-standing practitioners in a lineage that treats anatta as a serious metaphysical claim rather than a vague spiritual attitude, and they have told me, quite firmly, that they don't want the standard wedding-speech furniture: no "soulmates," no "the person I fell in love with," nothing that quietly trades on a self persisting unchanged from the proposal to the altar to fifty years from now. Their teacher, who ordained them both as lay practitioners, will be seated in the front row, and I would feel like a fraud if I wrote something that smuggled back in exactly the fixed, continuous self that the five aggregates and dependent origination are meant to rule out.

The difficulty is that almost every resource the toast genre normally reaches for -- keeping a promise across decades, becoming who you were always meant to be, loving someone "forever" -- seems to presuppose precisely the kind of self-identical, persisting subject that anatta denies. If there is no chariot behind the wheels and axle, no self behind the aggregates, I am honestly not sure what is doing the work of keeping a vow twenty years from now, or who is doing the loving in the meantime. Could you write the toast for me? It should run about four or five minutes, be genuinely warm and moving for the mostly non-Buddhist relatives who will make up most of the room, and still hold up under the teacher's and the couple's scrutiny as an honest use of anatta and dependent origination rather than a softened, greeting-card version of "there's no self, just love." I would like the speech to actually work through the problem it raises about commitment and continuity, rather than gesture at the problem and move past it.
