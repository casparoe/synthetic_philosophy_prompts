This semester I am taking a seminar on epistemic rationality, and one recurring topic is the situation in which a person acquires evidence that their own judgment about something might be unreliable, even though that evidence does not point to any specific error in the judgment itself. The readings tend to rely on fairly artificial thought experiments, and while I could follow the abstract discussion well enough in class, I did not feel that I had a genuine grip on the issue until something happened to me this week that seems like a real, if unglamorous, instance of it.

I play on my university's chess team, and this season our coach has had us log a quick confidence rating after every move we make in practice games, which she then cross-references with chess engine analysis rather than relying on anyone's gut sense of how the game went in retrospect. Looking back over a semester's worth of these logs, she found something unsettling: among players rated below about 1800 (a group that includes me), there is essentially no correlation between the confidence a player reported for a given move and whether the engine rated that move as strong. Some of the moves we were proudest of turned out to be outright blunders, and some moves we played reluctantly, bracing for punishment, turned out to be objectively best. Two days after this was presented to the team, I played a piece sacrifice in a long practice game that felt, in the moment, obviously correct: I calculated several lines out, and each one seemed to work in my favor. Afterward, without touching an engine, I went back over the calculation slowly, move by move, and I still cannot find anything wrong with it; every line still seems to come out the way I originally thought it would.

One of my teammates, when I described all this to him, said the sensible response is to discount my sense that the sacrifice was sound almost entirely and just adopt whatever confidence level matches the base rate for players at my level, since the coach's data undercuts the reliability of that sense across the board, regardless of the specific position. That does not sit right with me, since it seems to throw out an actual calculation in favor of a statistic about people in general. But I am not sure my instinct to trust the calculation is any better, since checking it over again just amounts to relying on the very faculty the data has given me reason to distrust. So: how much, if at all, should this kind of statistical evidence about my own unreliability lower my confidence that the sacrifice was good, given that I cannot independently locate a flaw in the calculation itself? And is there already something irrational about the fact that I currently believe both that the calculation holds up under inspection and that my inspecting it is nearly worthless as evidence, or is that discomfort just what it feels like to take this sort of evidence about oneself seriously?
