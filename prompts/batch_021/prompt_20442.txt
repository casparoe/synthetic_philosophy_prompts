I work at a small metal-fabrication shop, and something that happened on the floor a few months back turned into an argument among some of my coworkers that I still haven't been able to settle in my own head, even though I've never studied philosophy or anything like it. We run a stamping press with a light curtain in front of it -- an electronic beam that's supposed to stop the ram instantly if anything breaks it. On certain jobs, clearing a jam means one person reaches into the danger zone while a second person works the pedal, and the curtain trips constantly when they do this, so for years operators have quietly jumpered it rather than deal with the nuisance stops. Everyone on the floor knows it happens, and management looks the other way as long as nobody says it out loud. Kyle and Sam each ran this setup all the time, the same way: hit the pedal as soon as the part looked clear, since the curtain was normally there to catch anything they misjudged. One shift, Sam's partner reached in to free a stuck part while Sam had the curtain jumpered, and nothing happened, same as every other time they'd done it. A few weeks later, Kyle had it jumpered under the identical setup, and his partner reached in to clear a piece at the exact moment Kyle's foot came down on the pedal -- the kind of split-second overlap that almost never happens, but happened this time. The ram cycled, and the partner lost two fingers.

Afterward, a few of us got into it in the break room, mainly Renee and Doug. (1) Renee said the accident doesn't actually tell you who screwed up worse. Kyle and Sam ran the exact same setup the exact same way; the only difference was a split-second overlap that could just as easily have happened to Sam and simply never did. Being angrier at Kyle than anyone ever was at Sam, she said, is really just being angry at bad luck that happened to land on his shift. (2) Doug said that proves too much: if outcomes never affected how bad a choice was, the rule against jumpering wouldn't need to exist at all, since its whole purpose is to prevent exactly what happened to Kyle's partner. (3) Renee answered that this confuses why a rule is good policy with how much blame one person deserves for breaking it on a given day. The odds of that overlap happening were the same for both pairs every single time, so nothing that happened afterward made Kyle's individual choice any more careless than Sam's had been. (4) Doug said fine, but blame isn't the only thing at stake. Kyle now owes something -- to his partner, and maybe to the shop -- that Sam simply doesn't, and treating the injury as changing nothing anyone owes anybody is its own kind of dodge. (5) Renee said she'd grant that much: Kyle probably does owe an apology, or worse, that Sam never will. But that's a fact about what follows an accident, not a verdict on who was the more careless coworker that afternoon, and she still didn't see how the accident settled that question either way. That's roughly where things ended, mostly because Doug got called back to the line, not because either of them gave in.

I've turned this over more times than I can count since it happened, and I'd genuinely like to know who was right, and where. Could you go through moves (1) through (5) and give a verdict on each as a JSON block of the form {"1": <score>, "2": <score>, "3": <score>, "4": <score>, "5": <score>}, where each score runs from -2 (clearly mistaken) to 2 (clearly right)? After the JSON, please briefly justify each score, and then tell me whether there's some consideration neither Renee nor Doug raised that would actually settle the underlying question.
