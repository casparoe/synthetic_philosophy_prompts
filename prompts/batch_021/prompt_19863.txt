For nearly three decades I worked as an astronomer surveying near-Earth objects, so I spent a good part of my career thinking about how often the Earth gets struck by asteroids and comets large enough to do serious damage. Since retiring I have joined a small reading group that works through books on the history and philosophy of science, and lately we have been arguing over a question that the book we are currently reading raises only in passing, but that I have not been able to let go of.

The book estimates the probability of a civilization-ending impact, or of a supereruption on the scale of Toba, by counting how many such events show up in the geological and historical record and dividing by the span of time that record covers. One of the other members, a former statistician, insists this is exactly the right method, give or take the usual worries about small samples: it is simply base-rate estimation from observed frequency, the same procedure most of us used constantly in our own work. Another member, who is finishing a philosophy degree decades after the rest of us finished our science ones, objects that the method is quietly rigged, because the record we are counting from can only ever be a record in which no such event has actually finished us off — if one had, there would be no one left to compile the tally. On her view, our very presence as observers doing the counting is itself indirect evidence that the truly dangerous events are under-represented in the record relative to their real frequency, so the naive count understates the risk, perhaps badly. The statistician answers that this cannot be right, because the same move would let you wave away any inconvenient absence of evidence for anything at all, on the grounds that you would not be around to see it if the worst had already happened.

I keep going back and forth on who has the better of this. Could you help me work out whether the philosophy student's objection is actually sound, and, if it is, what a defensible way of estimating the risk of these rare, potentially terminal events would have to look like once the objection is taken into account?
