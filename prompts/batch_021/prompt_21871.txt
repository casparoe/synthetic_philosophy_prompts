My grandfather died a week and a half ago at eighty-seven, and I've been asked to give his eulogy this coming weekend. He spent his career as a professor of classical Ash'ari theology (kalam), and the center of his intellectual life, as far as I can tell from the marked-up copy of al-Ghazālī's *The Incoherence of the Philosophers* I found on his nightstand and the lecture notes I've been sorting through since, was Ghazālī's argument that, strictly speaking, fire does not burn cotton: that God alone directly causes the burning, moment by moment, and that fire and cotton are merely the occasion for it, with the whole reliable pattern we call a law of nature being nothing more than God's consistent habit, which He could suspend at any moment without contradiction. I gather this general position has a name, "occasionalism," though knowing the label hasn't helped me much with getting the substance right. He used to visibly wince when people said things like "his heart finally gave out" or "the cancer took him," because to him that phrasing smuggled in exactly the picture of the world he had spent forty years arguing against: things possessing their own causal power and operating on their own, rather than a God who is personally and freshly authoring every event at every instant. He told my mother more than once, entirely seriously, that if anyone at his funeral said the cancer had "taken" him, he would consider the whole eulogy a failure no matter how many nice things were said around it.

The funeral audience is going to be an odd mix: several of his former graduate students and colleagues from the theology department, who will notice immediately if I flatten his actual view into something vaguer like "everything happens for a reason," and then most of the room, including my own coworkers and cousins, who have no background in any of this and just need the next twenty minutes to feel honest and comforting rather than confusing. I'm an engineer with no philosophy training myself, and I'm anxious about two things at once: getting the metaphysics precise enough that his former students don't wince the way he used to, while also not making him sound, to everyone else, like his life's choices were just a puppet show God was running through him. He apparently cared a great deal about there still being some real sense in which his actions were genuinely his own -- which I gather is a whole separate piece of the theory that I don't want to accidentally erase in trying to get the causation part right.

Could you write the eulogy for me? I'd like it to run about five minutes, work the fire-and-cotton example in somewhere without turning the room into a seminar, and end in a way that actually comforts people rather than one that is merely doctrinally correct.
