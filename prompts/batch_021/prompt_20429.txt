I'm a novelist who jots down real arguments I overhear — better material than anything I could invent. One, from a recent dinner party, has stayed with me. (1) My friend Daniel described backing out of a promise to help our friend Mara move apartments: a major work opportunity had come up for the same Saturday, and Mara had already taken the day off for the move. He sent her considerably more money than movers would have cost, arguing that this settled things, since she ended up at least as well off as if he'd shown up. (2) Another friend, Lauren, said he'd still broken his promise: a promise gives someone a claim to that particular performance, not to whatever sum the promisor decides is equivalent, so no transfer, however generous, discharges it. (3) Daniel replied that this proves too much, since people routinely make amends for broken promises; if no substitute ever counted, promises could never be repaired, and if Mara has nothing left to complain about, there's no wrong left to explain. (4) Lauren answered that making amends presupposes that a wrong occurred; it doesn't show that none did, and Daniel doesn't get to unilaterally set the terms of what counts as 'just as good.' Neither side gave ground, and I still can't settle it. Could you go through the exchange move by move, saying who has the better of it at each point, and flagging anything about why promises bind that neither Daniel nor Lauren considered?
