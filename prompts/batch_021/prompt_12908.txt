I'm reworking the free will unit for my moral responsibility seminar this term and want a second opinion on something before I lock in the lecture slides. For years I've pitched Galen Strawson's Basic Argument to students as basically hard determinism with extra steps: you act the way you do because of how you are, and you didn't ultimately choose to be that way (character's shaped by genes, upbringing, culture, whatever), so real, desert-entailing moral responsibility is off the table. A colleague from cognitive science who sat in on the lecture last week told me afterward that this framing is a bit off, since Strawson is pretty insistent the argument doesn't actually depend on determinism being true -- apparently its supposed to bite just as hard even if theres some genuine indeterminism (quantum noise or whatever) feeding into how our neural wiring and characters got formed.

That surprised me, honestly, because my instinct runs the other way: if part of what made me who I am was undetermined rather than fixed in advance by prior causes, shouldn't that carve out at least a little room for the kind of self-originating agency the libertarian is after, and blunt the regress at least somewhat? So who's right here, me or my colleague -- and if it's her, can you walk me through, in Strawson's own terms and not just a generic appeal to luck, exactly why throwing indeterminacy into the mix doesn't loosen the argument's grip at all? Bonus points if you can also say a word about why this is supposed to distinguish his "pessimism" from garden variety hard determinism, since I suspect that's the exact distinction I've been flattening for my students.
