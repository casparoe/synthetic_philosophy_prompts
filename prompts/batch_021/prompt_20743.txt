I've been reporting a piece on whether fiction can make us more virtuous—the news hook is a recent wave of op-eds debating whether violent, morally ambiguous prestige dramas are dulling or sharpening viewers' real-world empathy—and the deeper I get into the background reading, the more it looks like a rerun of a specific eighteenth-century quarrel I'd like to understand properly before I use it as my frame. In 1757, d'Alembert's Encyclopédie article on Geneva praised the city's pastors as tolerant, almost philosophical men and went on to suggest that Geneva's old Calvinist refusal to license a theatre was an unnecessary austerity. Rousseau, a Genevan by birth, answered at length with the Lettre à d'Alembert sur les spectacles, which turns out to be much more than a defence of local custom: it argues that the pity theatre arouses is a kind of counterfeit virtue, letting an audience discharge its moral feeling cheaply on people who don't exist and so excusing it from the costlier business of helping people who do. He even extends the point to comedy, arguing that Molière's Misanthrope teaches audiences to laugh at Alceste's disgust with a corrupt society rather than share it, training them toward accommodation rather than resistance. Diderot had for years been Rousseau's closest friend before their painful falling-out at almost this exact moment, and he was developing his own theory of sentimental drama at almost exactly the same time; so far as I can tell, he never answered Rousseau's argument directly in print. Yet a couple of decades later, in the Paradoxe sur le comédien, he more or less granted Rousseau's premise that a great actor feels nothing genuine on stage, while still insisting that watching the performance could train an audience's real capacity for fellow feeling.

For the piece, I'd rather see the two of them actually contend with each other than have me summarize both sides in my own voice. Could you write a substantial dialogue between Rousseau and Diderot, set loosely after their falling-out, in which Diderot is allowed to draw on the fuller account of acting and spectatorship he only worked out later in the Paradoxe? I'd like Rousseau to spell out concretely why he thinks tragic and comic pity are corrosive rather than harmless, including the point about the Misanthrope, and I'd like Diderot to explain how sentimental theatre could still cultivate genuine moral sensibility even on his own terms, given what he concedes about what the actor onstage does and doesn't feel. Most importantly, please have them genuinely engage each other's specific moves—conceding a point where it's due, pressing where the other is weakest—rather than each delivering a self-contained speech.
