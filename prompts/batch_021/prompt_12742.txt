Over dinner last night, a friend and I argued at length about the Pasadena game, a St.-Petersburg-style wager introduced by Nover and Hájek: a fair coin is tossed repeatedly until it first lands heads, and if this happens on toss n, the payoff is (−1)^(n+1) · 2^n/n. Because the resulting series converges only conditionally rather than absolutely (it sums to ln 2 when the terms are taken in their natural order, while the sum of the absolute values of those same terms diverges), the Riemann rearrangement theorem guarantees that reordering the very same outcomes can make the game's "expected value" equal any real number whatsoever, or diverge to infinity, or oscillate without ever settling. My friend maintained that this is no paradox at all: a normative decision theory owes no verdict on every conceivable gamble, so the sensible response is simply to declare the Pasadena game's expectation undefined and move on, the way we'd shrug off any other ill-posed request. I argued that this concedes too much, since every probability and payoff in the game is individually well-specified and finite, so a theory that falls silent here has a substantive gap rather than a harmless idealization. Please make the strongest case for my side of the argument: that games like this expose a genuine defect in expected utility theory as a normative theory of rational choice, and that the "undefined, so ignore it" response fails to contain the damage.
