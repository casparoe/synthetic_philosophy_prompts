So I'm running a philosophy of math reading group this term with a handful of grad students, and we're currently slogging through constructive set theory. Last week we hit Diaconescu's theorem (some books credit it to Goodman and Myhill instead, depending which corner of the literature you're in), and it's been bugging me ever since. The result is that if you have a constructive set theory with full, unrestricted separation plus the full axiom of choice, you can derive excluded middle for an arbitrary proposition P -- even though excluded middle isn't assumed anywhere else in intuitionistic logic.

The proof is short enough to fit on an index card: let A = {x in {0,1} : x=0 or P} and B = {x in {0,1} : x=1 or P}, both of which are inhabited no matter whether P is true, false, or totally undetermined. Choice hands you a selector -- elements f(A) in A and f(B) in B -- and then a case-split on whether f(A) equals f(B) or not (perfectly kosher constructively, since equality on a two-element set is decidable) ends up settling P or not-P outright. Everyone in the group could verify every single line, but when I asked "ok, but why does this actually work, what's the deeper story here," I mostly got blank stares. It's like watching a magic trick where you saw every move and still can't find the hidden pocket.

Can you give me a genuinely intuitive, gut-level explanation of what's going on -- not just a re-walkthrough of the steps, which we've already got? Specifically I'm curious why unrestricted separation plus choice, together, manages to pull this off, whether theres some clean way to say what the "hidden classical content" of choice actually amounts to (given how innocent an existence principle it looks on its face), and whether the BHK reading of disjunction is really doing the heavy lifting here. Half of me suspects the honest answer is something like "choice functions have to be genuinely decisive in a way that outruns what a constructive existence proof can license," but I'd like to hear that actually argued for rather than just asserted.
