I've been chasing a story for a health-and-science piece about the fight over updating the Uniform Determination of Death Act, and it keeps dragging me into an argument that none of my sources ever actually finish — they just restate their own side and move on. The orthodox view, the one basically every hospital protocol runs on, is that whole-brain death (irreversible loss of function of the entire brain, brainstem included) really is death, because the brain is supposedly what integrates the body into one living organism rather then a bag of separately-ticking organs. Then there's the Alan Shewmon camp, who point to his case reports of "brain-dead" bodies that kept doing all sorts of integrated things for months or years on a ventilator: fighting infections, healing wounds, growing, even gestating a pregnancy to live birth in a couple of documented cases. That seems to wreck the premise that you need a functioning brain for bodily integration, which is basically the whole argument for treating brain death as real death. I keep thinking about the Jahi McMath case as the ugly practical version of this fight: declared dead in 2013, kept on a ventilator for years by her family, and then given a second, official death certificate when she died again in 2018.

Instead of just telling me who's right, could you write me a dialogue between two equally sharp people holding these positions — an ICU physician defending the whole-brain standard, and a neurologist arguing that only circulatory death is the real thing and "brain death" is a useful fiction invented for transplant purposes? I want genuine back-and-forth, each one pushing on the other's strongest point rather than restating their own: why the integration rationale is (or isn't) supposed to survive Shewmon's cases, whether "permanent" cessation is different from "irreversible" cessation in these debates, and what's actually at stake given that the transplant system runs on the dead donor rule. Bonus points if they end up arguing about whether their disagreement is even a biological one, or just a dispute over where to draw a useful legal line.
