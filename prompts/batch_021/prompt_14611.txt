I've been working on a piece about the cultural history of dieting, and Nietzsche keeps coming up in ways that go well beyond the usual Übermensch quotations people reach for. In "Ecce Homo," he insists that nutrition, place, climate, and choice of recreation matter more, philosophically, than any question of God, soul, sin, or virtue, and he traces years of his own migraines and stomach trouble to having simply eaten and lived wrong for too long; he mocks heavy, starchy national cuisines for producing sluggish minds, warns against alcohol, and treats his own painstaking dietary trial-and-error as a precondition for getting any work done at all. Elsewhere, in "The Case of Wagner" and "Nietzsche contra Wagner," he reads the aging Wagner's turn toward Christian pity, chastity, and sympathy for vegetarianism as decadence dressed up as spiritual advance. Given all that, how do you think Nietzsche would have judged GLP-1 drugs like Ozempic, which chemically quiet appetite and "food noise" rather than requiring the diner to build restraint or discriminating taste through practice? Would he count this, on his own terms, as the same kind of intelligent physiological housekeeping he praised in himself, or as one more narcotic by which an exhausted modern will dodges the self-overcoming he prized? Ground your answer in specific moves in his thought rather than the general idea of the will to power.
