Most of my thinking about epistemology has grown out of arguing with friends and family about politics and religion, and trying to work out how much it should actually move me when someone I respect just flatly disagrees. Reading around on my own, I found that people who study this divide roughly into conciliationists, who hold that discovering a genuine epistemic peer disagrees with you is itself strong reason to shift your credence toward theirs -- in the limiting case, splitting the difference -- and steadfasters, who hold that you can sometimes be justified in holding your ground even once you know of the disagreement. What I can't get past is a worry that the conciliationist position eats itself. Conciliationism is a substantive, contested thesis in epistemology, and plenty of thoughtful, informed people, including professional epistemologists who are clearly each other's peers, reject it in favor of steadfastness. So a consistent conciliationist, on registering this very disagreement, seems committed by the view's own logic to lowering confidence in conciliationism -- perhaps low enough to no longer be entitled to tell a steadfaster, in some unrelated dispute, that the bare fact of disagreement requires conciliating. Either the view carves out some special exemption for its own master principle, which looks like special pleading, or it has to accept that even its staunchest defenders shouldn't be very confident in it, which amounts to conceding the debate. I'd like to see this actually fought out rather than summarized. Please write a dialogue between a conciliationist and a steadfaster in which the steadfaster presses exactly this self-undermining objection, the conciliationist offers the strongest available reply, and the steadfaster doesn't fold but pushes back on that specific reply, so that the exchange runs several rounds deep, with each side responding to what the other has just said rather than restating an opening position. Neither character should be easier to argue for than the other.
