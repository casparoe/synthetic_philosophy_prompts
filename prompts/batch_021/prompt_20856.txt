I am a fourth-year PhD candidate in clinical psychology, and my dissertation examines expectancy and placebo effects in chronic pain management, which means that professionally I spend most of my time being suspicious of the idea that believing something will make it so, and designing controls precisely because patients' hopes so reliably distort their own reports of how well a treatment is working. So it's a little humbling that a decision in my own life has lately taken on exactly the shape of the thing I study.

I'm training for a marathon this spring with a qualifying time I need to hit to get into a race I've wanted to run for a decade. My last several long runs have put me right on the edge of that cutoff: my coach, extrapolating from my best recent tempo work, thinks I have a real shot if I commit fully to goal pace from the gun, while a training partner who is a far better statistician than I am ran the same splits through a more sober model and puts my odds of holding that pace for 26.2 miles closer to two in five -- and thinks that committing to goal pace, rather than starting conservatively and adjusting by feel, is asking to blow up somewhere in the last 10K. The awkward part is that I can't wait for better evidence before deciding how to run the race: I have to commit to a strategy at the start line, and by every account from runners I trust, it matters enormously -- in a way that is not just about performing confidence for its own sake -- whether I have actually managed to believe I can hold the pace. Doubt in the later miles is supposedly one of the more reliable ways to guarantee you slow down; real conviction is at least part of what lets people hold on once it starts to hurt.

So here is my question, and I would rather have your actual reasoning than a pep talk: is it defensible for me to work myself into genuinely believing I can hit this time, on the grounds that the belief itself is partly what would make it true, or is that just the same motivated reasoning I would flag in about five seconds if a patient told me they had decided to trust an unproven treatment because believing in it felt like it was working? I keep going back and forth, and I would like an outside, non-runner's perspective on whether there is a principled difference between those two cases, and if so, what it is.
