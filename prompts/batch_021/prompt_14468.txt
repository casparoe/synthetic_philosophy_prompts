I'm a retired experimental physicist (spent about thirty-five years doing condensed matter stuff before I hung up my lab coat), and since I retired a few years ago I've fallen into reading philosophy, which was never remotely my field before. Mostly I just read on my own, plus the odd zoom call with an old grad school buddy who went the philosophy route instead of physics. Lately we've been chewing on Thomas Hobbes, and not the "nasty, brutish and short" stuff everybody already knows -- I got hooked on his weirdly intense relationship with geometry instead. There's that story (Aubrey tells it in Brief Lives, if I'm remembering right) about Hobbes stumbling on Euclid's 47th proposition in some gentleman's library when he was already about forty, refusing to believe it, and working backward proposition by proposition until he was hooked on geometry for life. Then decades later, thoroughly convinced of his own genius at it, he decides he's squared the circle, publishes the "proof" in De Corpore, and spends something like the next twenty five years, right up until he died, refusing to admit that John Wallis and basically every competent mathematician in Europe had thoroughly demolished it. I picked up a book called Squaring the Circle by a guy named Jesseph about the whole saga, and honestly the parallels to certain people whose grant applications I used to have to referee were uncomfortably strong.

Here's what I can't settle with my friend though. Hobbes didn't want geometry just for its own sake -- he wanted Leviathan and his whole civil philosophy to be demonstrated "more geometrico," built up step by step from definitions with the same certainty as a proof, which is a big part of why he thought we should just accept his conclusions about sovereignty and the state of nature rather than keep arguing with them. But it turns out (again, correct me if I've got this garbled) that his own idea of what a proper geometric definition even is was pretty idiosyncratic -- he thought you had to define a figure by the motion that generates it, a circle as the path swept out by a line rotating round a fixed point, all tied up with his materialist picture of bodies and motion. My friend says the mathematical fiasco is just an embarassing sideshow with nothing to say about the political philosophy. I'm not so sure -- if the guy's basic conception of what counts as a rigorous geometric demonstration was already off, and he was constitutionally incapable of accepting correction from people who plainly knew better, that seems like it could actually infect the very method he's leaning on to sell us Leviathan.

Could you write me an essay that actually settles this, at least to your own satisfaction? Lay out the strongest version of both sides -- that the mathematical failure is a seperate, personal blind spot versus that it exposes something rotten in his whole geometrical approach to civil philosophy -- but don't leave it hanging the way my friend and I have. Come down on one side, or on some precise conditional version of one side, and defend it properly.
