The question was: "In her Discourse on Happiness, du Châtelet argues that certain things are necessary for the happiness of 'a person like her.' Describe the role she thinks passions and illusions should play in a happy life, and explain why she thinks the love of study is especially important for women's happiness in particular. (10 points)"

And here's the student's answer, verbatim:

"Du Châtelet starts from the idea that happiness is something we have to work at rather than leave up to chance, and that a person in her position needs a specific set of things to be happy: freedom from prejudice, good health, virtue, and the right relationship to passion and illusion. On illusions, she thinks we need them, and that we shouldn't go around exposing every pleasant illusion we happen to have (about love, about our own importance) just because a colder, more philosophical look would show them to be false -- someone who insists on totally clear-eyed truth all the time will end up miserable, so a happy person has to be willing to be a little self-deceived. On passions, she basically sides with the Stoics: passions are what unsettle us and keep us from real tranquility, so the goal is to use reason to gradually master and extinguish them, since its the person who has trained herself out of strong passion who fortune can't touch. Finally, on study, she argues that it matters most for men, since men are the ones who can convert learning into public glory and reputation, so cultivating a love of study gives a man's ambition somewhere to go even in periods when public honors aren't available to him."
