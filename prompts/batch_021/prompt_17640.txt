I'm planning to write my IB Extended Essay in philosophy on the problem of material constitution, and while narrowing down my topic I've been reading around the classic puzzle of the statue and the clay: a sculptor shapes a lump of clay into a statue, so that, for as long as the statue exists, it and the lump seem to share every single particle and occupy exactly the same place at exactly the same time — yet they don't seem to be simply one and the same object, since the lump could presumably survive being squashed back into a ball while the statue could not.

From my reading, two of the most fully worked-out rival solutions are the "constitution" view associated with people like David Wiggins and Lynne Rudder Baker, on which the statue and the lump are two numerically distinct objects that merely coincide, related by a special relation of constitution rather than identity, and the four-dimensionalist or "temporal parts" view associated with David Lewis and Ted Sider, on which ordinary objects are extended through time the way they are through space, so that the statue and the lump are really two different, merely overlapping aggregates of momentary stages — no more mysterious, on this view, than two roads that share a common stretch of pavement.

What I haven't been able to get a good feel for, from the secondary literature alone, is how a defender of each view would actually respond to the other's strongest pressure points rather than just talking past them — especially on two issues I keep running into. The first is what's sometimes called the "grounding problem": if the statue and the lump are made of exactly the same matter, arranged in exactly the same way, at every moment they coexist, what could possibly make it true that they have different persistence conditions? The second is the modal version of the puzzle, where the very same statue could have been sculpted from slightly different clay, which seems to push the four-dimensionalist toward supplementing temporal parts with something like Lewis's counterpart theory. Could you write me a dialogue between a committed constitution theorist and a committed four-dimensionalist, imagined as standing together in a sculptor's studio in front of a freshly finished clay statue and arguing about whether they are looking at one object or two? I would like both characters to be equally sharp, engaging directly with each other's objections — including the two above — rather than one side being an easy target, so that the dialogue actually reaches a genuine point of disagreement instead of a tidy resolution.
