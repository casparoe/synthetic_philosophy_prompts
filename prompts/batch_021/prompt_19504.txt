The question set was: "In De Interpretatione 9, Aristotle uses the example of a future sea battle to resist a fatalist conclusion he takes to threaten us as soon as we assume that every statement about the future is already true or false. (a) Reconstruct the fatalist argument in your own words. (b) Explain Aristotle's own way out of it, being precise about exactly which classical logical principle he preserves and which he gives up. (c) Relate his solution to one of the modern branching-time treatments of future contingents discussed in seminar (Peircean semantics, Ockhamism, or supervaluationism), and state the standard difficulty facing that treatment."

Here is the answer I have been asked to mark:

"Aristotle worries that if it is already true today that there will be a sea battle tomorrow, then the battle's occurrence was fixed from the start, so no one involved could have done otherwise; the same reasoning would apply to any future event whatsoever, so nothing would ever be genuinely contingent — everything would happen of necessity. What makes this troubling is that the conclusion seems to follow from logic alone, without appeal to any causal determinism in nature.

Aristotle's response is to deny that future contingent statements are true or false at all before the relevant event is settled: 'there will be a sea battle tomorrow' and 'there will not be a sea battle tomorrow' are both, at present, neither true nor false. Since he holds that a disjunction is only as good as its disjuncts, he takes this to show that the law of excluded middle itself fails for statements about the future, even though it continues to hold for statements about the present and the past. In effect, he trades classical two-valued logic for a three-valued logic with an 'indeterminate' value for future contingents — a move Łukasiewicz would later make formally precise.

This is close to what supervaluationists say today. On the supervaluationist picture, a future-tensed sentence is true just when it comes out true on every possible continuation ('history') running through the present moment, false when it comes out false on all of them, and neither true nor false when the continuations disagree. Since 'there will be a sea battle tomorrow' is true on some continuations and false on others, it is truth-valueless, exactly as Aristotle wanted. Critics call this the 'thin red line' problem: because supervaluationism treats every continuation as equally real, it cannot explain our strong sense that one of them, and not the others, is how things are actually going to turn out."
