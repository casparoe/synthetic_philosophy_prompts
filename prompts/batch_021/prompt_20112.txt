I'm nearing the end of a novel set in a near-future American city-state that broke away from the federal union after a protracted constitutional crisis and refounded itself as an epistocracy: a standing Board of Franchise administers a demanding civics-and-statistics examination, and only those who pass may vote or hold office. The emotional centerpiece of the book is a scene between two former law-school roommates, estranged for a decade, who run into each other at the funeral of a mutual friend — Dana Prentiss, who helped design the examination and now sits on the Board, and Marcus Cole, who failed it on his third and final attempt and has since become the reluctant public face of a movement to restore universal suffrage. I want their conversation afterward, over the reception's bad coffee, to escalate from stiff pleasantries into a real argument, and I'd like your help drafting it.

Dana shouldn't simply assert that the informed ought to rule; she should make the strongest version of the case, drawing on the sort of argument associated with Jason Brennan's work, that most citizens are either disengaged "hobbits" or tribal, score-keeping "hooligans" rather than the dispassionate, well-calibrated "vulcans" that classical democratic theory quietly assumes turnout will produce, and that Mill's old, half-abandoned proposal to weight votes by demonstrated competence deserves a serious, empirically accountable revival rather than reflexive dismissal. Marcus shouldn't just appeal to fairness in the abstract; he should press her on the hardest problems for any such scheme in practice — who writes and grades the exam, and on whose authority; whether "competence" can be measured by any instrument that doesn't quietly re-launder existing advantages of class, race, or schooling under a more flattering name; and whether democratic equality is something citizens are owed regardless of how the aggregate result compares to what a panel of experts would have chosen. I'd like the two of them to actually answer each other's best points rather than deliver parallel speeches, and for the scene to end without either side clearly winning the argument. Could you draft this as a dialogue of roughly 700–900 words, in prose with minimal stage direction, that reads like two people who once loved each other's minds and are quietly dismayed at how far apart they've drifted?
