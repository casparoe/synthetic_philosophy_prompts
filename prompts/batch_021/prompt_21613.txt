I have no formal training in philosophy, but I've spent a good deal of time on my own working through Leibniz's claim that God surveyed every possible world in his understanding and chose to actualize only the single best one, leaving every rejected alternative behind as nothing more than an idea that never receives actual existence. What's been nagging at me lately is how this compares to Hugh Everett's many-worlds interpretation of quantum mechanics, where every physically possible outcome of every measurement actually occurs in some branch, with nothing resembling a selection of "the best." I'd like your best-supported guess at how Leibniz, reasoning strictly from his own principles rather than the mere coincidence of the phrase "many worlds," would have judged Everett's picture. Would the fact that the universal wavefunction evolves with strict, unbroken determinism, with no fundamental indeterminism and no arbitrary collapse, have struck him as finally honoring the principle of sufficient reason better than the standard collapse picture ever could? Or would the sheer proliferation of equally real branches have violated his principle that only the single best world gets to be actual? I'd also like to know whether his notion of compossibility gives him any principled way of denying that Everett's branches even count as distinct possible worlds in his own technical sense, given that they share one common history right up to the moment they split. Please work through this tension rather than settling for whichever answer sounds tidiest.
