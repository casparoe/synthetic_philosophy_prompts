I have no academic background in philosophy, but I have spent a good deal of time working through Popper's writings on my own — particularly Conjectures and Refutations and Objective Knowledge — and I have become stuck on his notion of verisimilitude: the idea that a theory can be false and yet closer to the truth than some other false theory. I have since learned that Popper's own official definition of this, which ranks one theory as more verisimilar than another only if its class of true consequences is a superset of the other's and its class of false consequences is a subset of the other's, was shown by Tichý and Miller in 1974 to make it essentially impossible for one false theory ever to count as more verisimilar than another false theory. Rather than accept this on faith, I would like the difficulty made concrete: please construct two false theories about some ordinary domain of your own choosing — not the toy weather-forecast example that gets recycled in most treatments of this — such that almost anyone would judge one closer to the truth than the other, and then work through Popper's definition explicitly to show why it cannot deliver that verdict. Finally, I would appreciate your honest assessment of whether the later similarity-based repairs to verisimilitude, such as those of Niiniluoto or Oddie, actually solve this problem or merely relocate it.
