I'm currently taking a philosophy seminar on risk and rationality, and an experience from a few weeks ago has stayed with me. I had signed up for a paid decision-making study run by the economics department, and near the start of the session I was offered a choice between (a) a guaranteed $12, or (b) a coin flip paying $28 on heads and nothing on tails. I chose the guaranteed $12, even though the average payout of (b) works out to $14, and I'm fairly confident I'd choose the same way if offered the choice again. When I described the episode to a friend who's majoring in economics, she argued that my choice can't be explained by the usual reason people prefer sure things over gambles -- namely, that an additional dollar matters less to you the more of it you already have -- since $12 and $28 are both trivial sums for either of us and wouldn't change anything about our lives either way. On her view, this means I simply reasoned badly: I gave up money in expectation for no good reason. I don't feel that I made a mistake, but I can't quite say why her argument fails, if it does. Was I being irrational, or is there a legitimate way of defending a preference like mine that her argument overlooks?
