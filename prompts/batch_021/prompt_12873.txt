Last night a friend and I got into a long, slightly too intense argument over drinks about whether advanced AI systems should be built to reason explicitly as utilitarians. My friend's position was straightforward: if maximizing aggregate welfare really is the correct standard of rightness, then any sufficiently rational and well-informed agent — including an AI system more capable and less self-interested than either of us — should simply reason that way, and hard-coded deontological side-constraints (rules against lying, manipulating users, and so forth) are best understood as patches for our own cognitive and motivational limitations, not as anything a good enough utilitarian reasoner would actually need. I pushed back with something I half-remembered from Parfit about consequentialism being 'self-effacing': the idea that the utilitarian calculus itself might recommend that agents not consciously run it, on the grounds that agents who go around explicitly maximizing expected aggregate welfare are unusually prone to certain predictable disasters — manipulation, motivated reasoning about consequences, corrigibility failures, and so on. My friend's counter was that this just relocates the problem: if the correct theory ends up recommending that agents act like rule-following deontologists, in what sense is it still 'utilitarian,' except as a label for whatever process generated the rules in the first place? Neither of us had a good answer, and we ran out of time well before we ran out of disagreement.

I'd like a careful explanation of what's actually going on here, since I don't think either of us was arguing at full strength. Specifically: what precisely distinguishes a theory's being self-effacing from its being merely self-defeating, in Parfit's sense of that term? How does the self-effacing move relate to the more familiar two-level picture, i.e., Hare's distinction between intuitive and critical thinking, and to the older worry, which I believe goes back to Sidgwick, about whether utilitarianism ought to be kept esoteric — is there a real difference between these three ideas, or is 'self-effacing consequentialism' just old wine in a new bottle (I want to say the derisive label for the two-level view is something like 'Government House utilitarianism,' though I'm honestly not certain I'm attributing that phrase correctly)? And, more importantly for the argument my friend and I were actually having: does the self-effacing move rescue 'an ideal AI would just be a utilitarian' from the objection, or does my friend have a real point that a theory which recommends its own concealment or replacement has thereby stopped functioning as an action-guiding theory at all? If the latter, does that mean the choice between training a system on explicit hard constraints (something like Constitutional AI) and training it on outcome-based reward signals is actually a disagreement about normative ethics, or does that choice turn out to be closer to orthogonal to it?
