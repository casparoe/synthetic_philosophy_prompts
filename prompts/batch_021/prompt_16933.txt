I'm taking a seminar this term called "Political Obligation and Civil Disobedience," and after a few weeks on Rawls's account of when civil disobedience can be justified, we've moved back to the more basic question of whether ordinary citizens have any general obligation to obey the law at all. This week's readings were A. John Simmons's survey and rejection of the standard justifications for political obligation—consent, gratitude, fair play, and natural duty—together with Robert Paul Wolff's In Defense of Anarchism. Simmons works through each theory in turn and argues, I thought fairly persuasively, that none of them succeeds in grounding a general duty to obey the law even for citizens of a reasonably just, democratic state; Wolff reaches a similar anarchist conclusion by a different route, from an argument about moral autonomy.

My study group got stuck on something afterward that I now have to write a short response paper on, and I haven't been able to resolve it on my own. If the best attempts to justify a general obligation to obey the law really do all fail, it seems to follow that "philosophical anarchism" is correct, and that in turn seems to mean no citizen of any actual state is ever genuinely bound to obey its laws. So shouldn't we conclude that a person is morally free to break any law she judges sufficiently unwise or inconvenient for herself, given that, by Simmons's and Wolff's own arguments, she was never under a real obligation to follow it in the first place? A couple of people in the group objected that this is a much stronger claim than the "philosophical anarchism" that Simmons and Wolff actually defend, but no one could quite explain why the inference should fail once their premises are granted. Could you help me work out where, if anywhere, this reasoning goes wrong, and what someone like Simmons would actually say about how a citizen is entitled to act, given his own conclusions about the state's authority?
