I have no academic background in philosophy, but a few months ago a comment under a YouTube video sent me down a rabbit hole of blog posts and Stanford Encyclopedia articles on free will, and I can't stop thinking about Harry Frankfurt's example of Jones and the neuroscientist Black. As I understand it, Black has wired up Jones's brain so that if Jones ever showed signs of deciding not to do the thing Black wants, Black would step in and force it; but Jones goes ahead and decides on his own, so Black never actually has to intervene. Frankfurt's point is that Jones seems fully responsible even though, because of Black, he couldn't have done otherwise, which is supposed to be fatal to the old idea that you're only responsible for what you could have avoided doing.

What I haven't been able to sort out is how serious the pushback to this actually gets. The first reply I found was the "flicker of freedom" move — the idea that Jones still retains some thin alternative, like being able to produce whatever warning sign would have tipped Black off, even though he can't avoid the final outcome. But then I found a separate and apparently much tougher objection (the names Widerker and Ginet keep coming up) that turns on a dilemma about that warning sign itself: either the sign guarantees in advance what Jones will do, in which case the whole scenario has quietly assumed determinism and hasn't told us anything about a genuinely free, undetermined choice; or the sign doesn't guarantee it, in which case Jones could really have done otherwise all the way up to the moment he acted, and the example never succeeded in removing the alternative possibility in the first place. I can't tell whether a convinced Frankfurtian has anything better to say to this than they do to the flicker objection, or whether it's actually a much harder problem for them.

Instead of another summary, I'd like to watch two people who each fully understand the stakes actually argue it out, with neither one written as the pushover. Could you write me a dialogue — somewhere around eight to twelve exchanges — between someone convinced by Frankfurt that alternative possibilities are irrelevant to responsibility and someone defending the alternative-possibilities principle who specifically presses the dilemma about the warning sign rather than resting on the flicker-of-freedom move? I want each line to respond to the actual content of the previous one (for example, if the defender presses the two horns of the dilemma, the Frankfurtian needs to say something specific about whether the sign is meant to be deterministic, rather than just reasserting that Jones is responsible), and it's fine if the two of them never reach agreement by the end.
