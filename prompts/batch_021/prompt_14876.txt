**Question (20 points total).** In Part I of Section X of *An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding*, Hume argues that "no testimony is sufficient to establish a miracle, unless the testimony be of such a kind, that its falsehood would be more miraculous, than the fact, which it endeavours to establish." In Part II he adds four independent reasons why no miracle report in human history has actually satisfied that standard.

(a) [8 pts] Reconstruct the reasoning behind the Part I maxim: why can a miracle report, at best, usually only balance the evidence rather than outweigh it?
(b) [8 pts] Summarize Hume's four reasons from Part II.
(c) [4 pts] Some commentators take Hume's overall conclusion to be that miracles are metaphysically impossible. Is that an accurate reading of what he is actually arguing?

**Student answer.**

Hume's argument rests on his general theory of belief in matters of fact: such belief is always proportioned to the evidence, and evidence is nothing but our past experience of how reliably one kind of event has been found to accompany or follow another. Testimony is itself just one more species of evidence, trusted only because experience has shown us that testimony of that general sort tends to correspond to fact.

(a) A law of nature, on this view, is simply the record of our most uniform and unvarying experience, and a miracle is defined as a violation of such a law. So whenever a witness reports a miracle, two bodies of evidence pull in opposite directions: the "proof" (Hume's term for an empirical argument strong enough to leave no room for doubt) supplied by the uniform experience underlying the law, and the comparatively weaker assurance supplied by testimony in general. Because the law rests on exceptionless experience while testimony rests merely on the generally-reliable-but-fallible experience of how often witnesses turn out to be right, the case against the miracle starts out stronger than the case for it. That is why Hume's maxim sets such a demanding bar: only if supposing the testimony false would itself violate our experience even more than the reported miracle does could the testimony actually tip the balance. Part I doesn't yet claim that this bar is never met in practice — that is Part II's job — it only establishes how demanding the bar is: in general a miracle report can at best fight the proof from experience to a draw, leaving a weak residual assurance rather than outright belief.

(b) Part II argues that no actual case has ever come close to clearing that bar, for four reasons. First, no miracle is backed by a sufficient number of witnesses of unimpeachable education, integrity, and reputation, reporting events public enough that fraud would surely have been detected. Second, human beings take a natural, agreeable pleasure in surprise and wonder, which by itself gives people a motive to originate and spread miracle stories even without any intent to deceive. Third, such reports are concentrated among ignorant and "barbarous" peoples, and civilized nations that have inherited them got them from exactly such sources — the pattern of the reports tracks credulity rather than divine activity. Fourth, the miracles of rival religions are set against one another, since each is invoked to certify a different, mutually exclusive religious system; taken together, this fourth point is really Hume's proof that no historical religion's claims can be true, since the miracle testimony of every faith is cancelled out by the equally strong miracle testimony of all its rivals.

(c) I'd say yes — this really is Hume's conclusion, not just a loose gloss on it. If a law of nature just is an exceptionless regularity, then an event that violates it isn't merely wildly improbable, it is impossible, and that is the deeper reason no accumulation of testimony could ever be sufficient: you cannot out-argue a logical impossibility with more witnesses. This also fits Hume's broader empiricist skepticism, elsewhere in the Enquiry, about necessary connection — in both places he denies that the lawlikeness we project onto nature is something we ever actually discover in it.
