I work in the scheduling office of a regional distribution company, and a few weeks ago two of my coworkers got into an argument over lunch that I still haven't been able to settle in my own head. Last fall we rolled out new order-picking software in stages: our three smaller centers received it in September, while the two flagship centers that handle most of our volume didn't get it until November, right as the holiday surge began. Meg, who works on the data side, went back through the numbers and pointed out that average pick time per order fell at every single center once it had the software, substantially at the small centers and more modestly at the flagship ones. Owen, from operations, said that's beside the point, because the company-wide average pick time for the whole quarter actually rose slightly compared with the quarter before, so by the one number the regional office actually looks at, the rollout was a wash at best.

Meg answered that Owen's company-wide figure is exactly the kind of thing that misleads people: because the holiday surge meant a much larger share of that quarter's total orders came out of the two flagship centers, which are inherently slower per order than the small ones, the blended average can rise even while every individual center's own rate falls. She said she'd never heard a name for this until a friend mentioned Simpson's paradox, but that once you see the mechanism, it's obviously what happened here, so the honest comparison is center by center, not the combined total. Owen granted that this kind of reversal is a real statistical phenomenon in general, but said Meg couldn't just invoke the name of a paradox and call the matter settled. The centers weren't handed the software at random: the small ones went first as low-stakes pilots, while the flagship centers got it last, exactly when their own volume and staffing were about to be most strained by the season. So, in his view, whatever improvement showed up there is tangled up with everything else that changes every November, and comparing center by center doesn't untangle that -- it just relocates the same problem one level down. Meg's rejoinder was that his company-wide figure compares the very same two time periods and is therefore just as exposed to that seasonal tangle as her center-level numbers are, so he has no special standing to trust his number over hers. Neither of them budged, and although I've never studied statistics formally, the argument has nagged at me ever since; the little reading I've since done on my own about Simpson's paradox has only made me less sure who's actually right. Could you go through their exchange and say, at each step, who has the better of the argument -- not just who wins overall -- and whether there's some more basic issue about what this kind of data can and can't show that both of them are missing?
