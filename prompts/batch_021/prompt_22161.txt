I've been slowly working through Alan Turing's original 1950 paper on machine intelligence, and one part caught me off guard: he treats telepathy as a genuine complication for his imitation game, not as something to be laughed off. He worries that a human contestant with real extrasensory powers might unintentionally tip off the interrogator, and he even suggests running the whole test inside a "telepathy-proof room" to rule this out. Strikingly, he says outright that the statistical evidence for telepathy available in his own day, drawn from card-guessing experiments, struck him as overwhelming rather than as something to dismiss.

Decades later, the psychologist Daryl Bem published a series of experiments, using entirely standard methods and passing peer review at a major journal, claiming that people's choices could be nudged by events that hadn't happened yet. Other labs couldn't reproduce his results, and the episode is now widely credited as one of the things that pushed psychology toward pre-registering studies and tightening up its statistics.

Given how much weight Turing put on the statistical case for ESP in his own time, how do you think he would have reacted to the Bem affair, both to the original claims and to how they later fell apart? Would it have changed his views on how much evidential weight statistics alone can carry, or on how seriously he thought ESP-related worries deserved to be taken in his test?
