I'm taking a seminar this term on early modern philosophy of science, and this week's unit paired Hobbes's remarks in *De Corpore* on the peculiar certainty of geometrical knowledge with excerpts from Douglas Jesseph's *Squaring the Circle: The War Between Hobbes and Wallis*. Our professor sketched the basic shape of the fight — Hobbes insisting for the rest of his life that he had squared the circle (and duplicated the cube, and trisected the angle), and Wallis, the Savilian Professor of Geometry at Oxford, publishing rebuttal after rebuttal for decades — but we moved on to Leviathan before really explaining why Wallis's objections were supposed to settle the matter, and it has been bothering me since.

Here is my worry. Squaring the circle by compass and straightedge was not actually proven impossible until Ferdinand von Lindemann's 1882 proof that pi is transcendental, more than two centuries after Hobbes and Wallis were writing. If the impossibility of the task itself was not established until the nineteenth century, it seems to follow that nobody in the 1650s or 1660s could have had a genuine mathematical refutation of any particular attempt at quadrature — at most they could have had strong suspicions dressed up as demonstrations. That suggests to me that Wallis's "victory" in the eyes of their contemporaries was really institutional authority winning out — Wallis held an Oxford chair and belonged to the circles that became the Royal Society, and Hobbes had neither — rather than Hobbes actually having been caught in a checkable mathematical error. A friend of mine in the math department agreed when I described it this way, on the grounds that you cannot convict someone of botching an impossible task if nobody yet knows the task is impossible. Is that basically the right way to understand why the dispute went the way it did, or could seventeenth-century mathematicians adjudicate a specific proposed construction as flawed without first needing the general, much later impossibility result?
