So I've been working on a longer piece about split-brain patients — the people who had their corpus callosum severed (mostly decades ago, to treat severe epilepsy) — and I fell down a bit of a rabbit hole. Everyone knows the classic Sperry/Gazzaniga story: cut the connection between the hemispheres and you basically end up with two seperate streams of consciousness running around in one skull, one verbal and dominant, one mute but still perceiving and acting on its own. It's in every intro psych textbook and half the consciousness documentaries on Netflix. But then I found this 2017 paper out of Yair Pinto's group, published in Brain, that argues almost the opposite: that the patients they tested showed a single, unified consciousness the whole time, and that severing the callosum mainly creates a bottleneck on transferring information between the hemispheres rather than literally splitting the subject of experience in two.

Now I'm stuck on which reading actually holds up, for the piece I'm writing. Could you write me an essay that lays out the strongest case for the classic "two minds/two streams" interpretation versus this newer "one mind, degraded integration" interpretation -- feel free to pull in stuff like Tim Bayne's "switch model" or Nagel's old puzzle about how it might not be a clean one-mind-or-two situation at all, if that's useful -- and then actually commits to an answer about what split-brain cases really show about the unity of consciousness? I don't want a "well, both sides have a point, more research is needed" cop-out ending; I want an actual verdict, defended properly, the way I'd have to defend a claim if my editor made me put it in the nut graf.
