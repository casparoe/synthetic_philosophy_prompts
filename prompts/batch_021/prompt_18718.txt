My labmate and I run a tiny philosophy podcast on the side of our PhDs (mostly started as an excuse to email people whose papers were kicking our ass in the proseminar), and next month's guest is a philosopher of language who's written a whole book defending the view that propositions aren't structured complexes of objects and properties glued together by some relation, but are actually a kind of act -- specifically, the act of predicating a property of something. Her whole pitch is that this is what actually solves the old unity-of-the-proposition problem: a proposition hangs together because an act is intrinsically unified, not because it's "ingredients" get stuck together by extra relational glue. Whenever someone presses her on cases where nobody could ever have performed the relevant act -- dinosaurs, say, roaming around before there were minds to predicate anything of them -- she has this move she always makes: "it's the type of act doing the work, not the token, and types don't need to be instantiated to be well defined." It shuts the question down every time and I want to design questions that make that harder to reach for.

I'm actually writing my second-year paper on exactly this literature, so I dont want eight softball questions where she just restates the act theory again. Can you draft me 8-10 interview questions? I want at least one that pushes on whether the act theory actually solves Bradley's regress or just relocates it one level down (what unifies the act itself, if not some further relating), one on whether the type/token move is doing real metaphysical work or is just a new name for the problem, one that asks her to compare her view honestly to Frege's unsaturated concepts (isn't this basically Frege psychologized, and if so what do you gain over him by paying the cost of talking about acts instead of concepts), and one on whether she's quietly resurrecting Russell's multiple relation theory of judgment and therefore inherits Wittgenstein's old objection that it can't fix the logical form of what's judged -- so it can't distinguish judging that Abelard loves Heloise from judging that Heloise loves Abelard, let alone from nonsense. For each of those four, give a short follow-up keyed to the deflection she's most likely to reach for, and order the sequence so the "it's a type, not a token" move gets progressively harder for her to lean on -- ending on a question about what would actually count as evidence against her view, as opposed to just more research left to be done.
