In the Seventeenth Discussion of The Incoherence of the Philosophers, al-Ghazālī denies that fire's contact with cotton necessitates burning. On his view, the true agent of burning is God; the correlation we observe between fire and burning reflects nothing more than God's habitual custom (al-ʿāda) rather than any necessary connection, and God can suspend that custom at will, as when Abraham emerged unharmed from the furnace. Interpreters disagree about what this actually commits al-Ghazālī to: some read him as a thoroughgoing occasionalist who denies created things any real causal powers whatsoever, others as making only the more modest claim that a necessary connection between cause and effect cannot be rationally demonstrated, which would leave room for genuine secondary causes sustained by God. Averroes, in his rebuttal, objected that stripping things of real natures and causal powers destroys the very possibility of scientific knowledge and turns nature's order into an arbitrary sequence of unrelated divine fiats. Write an essay that first stakes out and defends a reading of what al-Ghazālī's position actually is, and then, on that reading, evaluates whether his occasionalism succeeds in securing divine omnipotence and the possibility of miracles without either destabilizing the natural order that scientific and practical reasoning depend on, or making God the direct author of every human sin. Weigh the strongest considerations on both sides, but don't end on a noncommittal note: defend a definite verdict.
