So for my seminar on theories of punishment we just spent a week on this weird little David Lewis paper called "Do We Believe in Penal Substitution?" -- yes, THAT David Lewis, the modal realism guy, apparently he wrote this barely-seven-page detour into the Christian doctrine of the Atonement and it's honestly one of the most fun readings we've had all term. His basic move: we're pretty sure a murderer's devoted friend can't just serve his prison sentence for him (the state would never allow it), and punishment isn't really like a debt, because with a debt the creditor just needs to not lose money and doesn't care who pays, whereas with punishment it specifically has to be the guilty person who suffers -- there's no "creditor" to satisfy. But then he points out that we totally do let a friend pay someone's fine for them, no questions asked, even when the fine is a serious one that functions just like any other punishment -- so apparently we're secretly fine with penal substitution in at least one case, which he thinks means the Christians who explain the cross that way aren't as confused as we like to pretend, since the rest of us are just as inconsistant.

Anyway, our philosophy club is hosting a visiting philosopher of religion in a couple weeks who defends the penal substitution theory of the Atonement and apparently cites this exact Lewis paper as one of her strongest cards, and im the one who got roped into drafting questions for the audience Q&A. I don't want anything softball -- I want questions that actually force her to deal with the hard parts of the argument instead of just waving at the fines example and moving on. Could you write me like 8-10 interview questions, each with a quick follow-up guessing at the deflection she'd probably reach for? I definitely want one on his point that substitution would only make sense if punishment served a "compensatory" function (like his example where the burglar basically has to work off his sentence as the victim's slave, or a version where the victim gets to enjoy watching the hanging) and why that's nothing like how real criminal punishment actually works, one that presses the "two-mindedness" charge directly -- does she actually bite the bullet that a murderer's mother should be allowed to serve his prison term, and if not, what's different about the fine case -- and one on whether the "too impractical to actually stop" explanation that Lewis is pretty skeptical of might secretly be the true story after all. If you can work it in, I'd also love a question on whether she's over-reading Lewis's pretty tentative, hedged conclusion (he's careful to say the tu quoque isn't itself a defense of the doctrine) into something closer to a full endorsement than he intended.
