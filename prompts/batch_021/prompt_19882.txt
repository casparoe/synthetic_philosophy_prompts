I sit on my university's Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee, and a protocol up for renewal this cycle has split the room in a way our usual welfare-based discussion hasn't been able to settle. The proposal comes from a pain-research lab that wants to use a nerve-injury model in mice to study chronic neuropathic pain, in which analgesics must be withheld or minimized for several weeks after surgery because they would mask the very pain signaling the study is designed to measure; humane endpoints and environmental enrichment are offered as the only mitigation. The investigator has satisfied every item on our usual checklist under the 3Rs, and several members are ready to approve on that basis alone. But one member, a philosopher, objects that this checklist quietly presupposes a view of what we owe animals that has never actually been argued for: on Kant's own account, animals are neither rational nor self-conscious, so we have no direct duties to them at all — our only duty "regarding" animals is an indirect duty to ourselves, not to coarsen our own character through habitual cruelty, which would make the committee's real job the oversight of researchers' training and disposition rather than the weighing of the mouse's pain as such. Another member, invoking Christine Korsgaard's Kantian argument in Fellow Creatures that any being with a good of its own thereby counts as an end in itself owed direct moral consideration, thinks this indirect-duty reading badly misconstrues what Kant's framework actually commits us to, and wants the protocol rejected or substantially narrowed until the animals' own stake in the outcome is given independent weight, not merely factored in via staff conduct.

Could you draft the decision memo I will circulate before next week's meeting? It should recommend one concrete outcome — approval, rejection, or approval subject to specific, enforceable conditions — and justify it in terms the investigator could actually respond to point by point, without quietly substituting a utilitarian pain calculus for the Kantian disagreement that is actually driving the split. Please close with a short paragraph honestly stating what would have to be true for the losing side of that disagreement to have the better argument, so the committee understands what our decision is actually trading away, not just what was decided.
