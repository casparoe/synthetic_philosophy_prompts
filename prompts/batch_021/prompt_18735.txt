So this argument happened at my old job and I've been chewing on who was actually right ever since. We'd just migrated a pretty small internal app to Kubernetes, and two coworkers got into it at lunch about whether that decision made any sense. (1) Jake said it was basically herd behavior wearing an engineering costume -- he'd asked around and nobody who pushed for k8s could give him a reason beyond "this is what everyone uses now," so calling it a considered technical decision was generous. (2) Nisha pushed back with something she'd read about information cascades: once enough people ahead of you have made the same choice, it can be totally rational to set aside your own weak private read and just go with what the crowd's actions reveal, since their choices carry information even when nobody ever states it out loud -- so "no one could articulate a reason" doesn't prove irrationality, it just means the reasoning got done upstream and folded into the action itself. (3) Jake replied that this only holds if the earliest movers actually had good independent signal to begin with, and if you trace k8s adoption back far enough the "early movers" are Google-scale companies with totally different constraints plus a ton of conference-talk hype -- so the cascade never had an informative seed, its noise all the way down, and everyone since has just been updating on updating on nothing. (4) Nisha said fine, but by that same theory you basically can't tell from the outside whether a cascade is hollow like that or genuinely seeded by good signal, so Jake can't be that confident either -- and she also granted some of it is probably not informational herding at all but the "nobody gets fired for choosing kubernetes" kind of career-covering herding, though that's still each engineer behaving sensibly given the incentives they're under, so if there's a mistake here it's on the company for how it rewards decisions, not on individual judgement.
