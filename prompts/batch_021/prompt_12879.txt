I spent nearly forty years as an experimental physicist, and retirement has left me free to read philosophy in earnest for the first time. Lately I have been working through the literature on rational agency, in particular the "coherence arguments" that keep surfacing in discussions of AI risk: the claim that any agent whose preferences violate the axioms behind the expected-utility representation theorem — by being cyclic, say, or merely incomplete — can be exploited through a sequence of trades (a "money pump") that leaves it strictly worse off by its own lights, and that this exploitability is what forces any sufficiently capable or self-improving agent toward behaving, in the limit, like an expected-utility maximizer with respect to some utility function. Two objections trouble me. First, the representation theorem seems almost too cheap to satisfy: a rock, or an agent that simply never acts, trivially counts as maximizing a constant utility function, so bare coherence looks compatible with having no interesting goals at all; the alarming further conclusions about power-seeking or resource acquisition seem to need substantive premises about the content of the utility function, not merely its existence. Second, the dynamic money-pump arguments seem to presuppose that the agent cannot simply foresee the trap and decline that particular sequence of trades, or hold preferences over entire plans rather than over each option taken in isolation; if it can do either, cyclic or incomplete preferences need not be exploitable in practice. Write an essay assessing whether coherence arguments succeed in showing that sufficiently advanced or rational agents must converge on something well approximated by expected-utility maximization, engaging with both objections above, and close with a definite verdict rather than a mere survey of considerations.
