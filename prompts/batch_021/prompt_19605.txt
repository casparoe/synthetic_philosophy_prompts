For my graduate seminar on the epistemology of disagreement, I want a case built around Aumann's theorem: two Bayesians with a common prior cannot knowingly agree to disagree once their posteriors on some proposition are common knowledge between them. Write a dialogue between two colleagues debriefing after a forecasting tournament who, after fully disclosing their evidence to each other, discover that their credences for the same proposition still diverge — a fact now common knowledge between them. One argues that this sort of persistent, fully-informed disagreement is itself a mark of irrationality, traceable to a hidden mistake, undisclosed evidence, or a prior that should never have been held. The other rejects the common prior assumption (sometimes called the Harsanyi doctrine) outright, insisting that two equally rational Bayesians can simply start from different priors, making their disagreement stable and blameless. Have each press the other on where a "rational" prior is supposed to come from if not from evidence, and on whether the common prior assumption is a genuine requirement of rationality or an unearned extra posit — without letting either side win by default.
