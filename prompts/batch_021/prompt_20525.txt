I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, but I've recently been reading about attempts to naturalize mental content — that is, to explain in purely biological terms how a thought or perceptual state comes to be "about" something in the world. One such approach, associated with the philosopher Ruth Millikan and generally called teleosemantics, holds that a mental state's content is fixed by the biological function it was selected for: a frog's snapping reflex represents "fly" because natural selection shaped it to fire in the presence of flies, and the reflex misrepresents when it fires at a passing BB pellet instead. A well-known objection is that a trait's selection history often cannot settle on a single determinate content this way, since several candidate properties may have been perfectly correlated throughout the relevant evolutionary history: was the frog's reflex really selected to track flies specifically, or merely small, dark, moving objects, given that in the frog's ancestral environment the two came to the same thing? I'd like you to construct an original example of this indeterminacy problem, drawn from a different organism, behavior, or even an engineered system rather than the standard frog case, and to walk through exactly where and why the indeterminacy arises in it. Finally, say whether you think teleosemantics has any principled way of resolving the problem, or whether it is fatal to the theory's ambition of fixing determinate content.
