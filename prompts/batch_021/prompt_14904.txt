So I'm TAing a history of political thought seminar this semester and we just wrapped up the unit on Aquinas and tyranny — De Regimine Principum, the bit in his Sentences commentary (book II, dist. 44, I think it's q.2 a.2 but don't quote me on the exact article) about whether killing a tyrant counts as sinful sedition, plus the parallel passage in the Summa (II-II q.42, on sedition). What's been bugging a couple of my students, and honestly me too, is how the standard undergrad gloss — "Aquinas was basically fine with tyrannicide" — flattens a position that's actually pretty hedged. He's clearly nervous about private individuals just unilaterally deciding some ruler counts as a tyrant and acting on it, as opposed to the community or some public authority doing the deposing, and there's also that distinction (formalized more by guys like Bartolus later, but at least implicit in Aquinas) between a tyrant by defect of title — basically a usurper — versus a legitimate ruler who becomes tyrannical in how he actually exercises power.

For a discussion section exercise I want to spring on them something totally anachronistic: would Aquinas think a modern democratic state's intelligence service is justified in drone-striking a foreign dictator (think a Gaddafi-type figure, or hypothetically someone like Kim Jong-un) rather then invading the country or just doing sanctions. Obviously drone warfare, the Westphalian state system, and international law on non-intervention are all things he never had to reckon with, so "well he was ok with killing tyrants so sure" feels too easy to me. I want something that actually engages his framework: does a foreign agency like the CIA count as the kind of "public authority" he thinks removal requires, does the usurper vs. tyrant-by-conduct distinction even make sense applied to something like a hereditary dictatorship, and does his common-good/natural-law apparatus actually cash out into a determinate verdict here or does it just run out of road at some point. Would love your reconstruction of this, staying as close as you can to the actual texts and distinctions rather than the pop-summary version of "Thomistic tyrannicide."
