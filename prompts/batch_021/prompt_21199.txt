I'm assembling a collection of short stories about scientific partnerships that outlast the marriages that started them. For the title piece, I want two paleontologists — long divorced, still occasional co-authors — reunited by a small, exquisitely preserved feathered theropod that neither of their labs can agree how to interpret. She holds what I gather philosophers call the etiological, or 'selected effects,' view: a structure's function is whatever effect it was selected for, full stop, so a filament that wasn't selected for insulation, display, or flight has no function yet on this particular animal, however useful it looks. He holds the rival, Cummins-style causal-role view: a structure's function is simply whatever it currently contributes to the systems around it, selection history be damned, and he thinks her position leaves her unable to say what the filament is for in the present tense — a gap that, for this particular animal, might never close, since it is extinct and no further selective history is coming. I want their old, unresolved argument about the fossil to double as the argument, still unresolved, about whether a person is what they were raised to be or simply whatever they currently do. Write the story. Keep the biology exact, and don't let either the marriage or the philosophy resolve into a lesson.
