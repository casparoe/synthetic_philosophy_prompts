"Good's theorem (I. J. Good, 1967) is often summarized as showing that 'the expected value of (cost-free) information is never negative' for a Bayesian decision-maker. (a) State the theorem more precisely, including the condition under which equality, rather than strict inequality, holds. (b) Good presented this result as a vindication of Carnap's 'principle of total evidence.' Explain in your own words what work the theorem is doing here, and what (if anything) it shows about the rationality of gathering evidence before forming beliefs or acting. (c) Give one real-world example that illustrates the theorem, and one example that might look like a counterexample at first; explain why the apparent counterexample doesn't actually refute the theorem."

and here's her answer:

"(a) Good's theorem says that for an agent maximizing expected utility, the expected utility of getting some extra piece of evidence for free and then choosing an action in light of it is always at least as high as the expected utility of just picking the best action right now without it. If you have a prior over states of the world and an experiment S correlated with the state, then E[ max_a E[u(a,θ) | S] ] ≥ max_a E[u(a,θ)], with equality exactly when there's no possible outcome of S that would lead you to choose a different action than the one you'd have chosen anyway. So free evidence can never make you worse off in expectation, and makes you strictly better off unless it's practically irrelevant to what you'd do.

(b) Carnap's principle of total evidence says that when you assign a credence you should conditionalize on all the evidence available to you, not just some subset of it. Good's contribution is showing this isn't just a brute methodological rule imposed from outside decision theory - it actually falls out of expected utility maximization itself. If you're already committed to being an expected-utility maximizer, you don't need a separate principle telling you to use all your evidence, because ignoring evidence (or refusing to gather more of it when it's free) can only ever hurt your expected outcomes, never help. So the theorem gives an internal, decision-theoretic justification for a norm that otherwise looks like a purely epistemic add-on - and since using more evidence can only bring your credences closer into line with the truth, it also explains why the norm of total evidence is truth-conducive and not just prudent.

(c) A nice example is a hospital deciding whether to run an extra diagnostic test before starting treatment. Even when the base rate of the disease is low, Good's theorem tells us that running the test and conditioning treatment on the result is always at least as good in expectation as treating (or not) based on the base rate alone, which is why it's standard practice to run every available test before committing to a treatment plan. A case that looks like a counterexample: a patient gets a test, it comes back a false positive, and they end up having unnecessary surgery they wouldn't have had otherwise - so gathering the extra information made things worse, not better, in that instance. This doesn't actually violate the theorem though, because the theorem is about expected value calculated in advance of seeing the result, not about what happens in any particular case - averaged over all the ways the test could come back, weighted by how likely each is, ordering the test is still at least as good as not ordering it, even though this specific realization went badly."
