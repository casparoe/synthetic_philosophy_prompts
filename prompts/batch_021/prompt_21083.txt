For a piece on how AI-alignment researchers have quietly imported ideas from moral philosophy, I've been struck by the resemblance between Eliezer Yudkowsky's proposal that a superintelligence be built to pursue humanity's "coherent extrapolated volition" (CEV) — roughly, what humanity would want if it knew more, reasoned more clearly, and had resolved much of its internal disagreement — and the older philosophical tradition of idealized-preference or full-information accounts of a person's good, defended by philosophers such as Richard Brandt and Peter Railton. Please argue that CEV inherits the central weakness critics such as David Sobel pressed against those accounts: idealizing a preference-holder enough to strip away ignorance and inconsistency tends also to strip away whatever made the resulting preferences recognizably that person's — or, in CEV's case, humanity's — rather than some other, merely hypothetical agent's. Since CEV must also locate convergence across many different people's extrapolated preferences rather than idealizing a single individual, please weigh in on whether this interpersonal step makes the problem worse or instead offers some way of dissolving it.
