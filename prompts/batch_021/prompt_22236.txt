I'm a philosophy and economics double major, and this semester I'm taking a seminar on formal social choice theory. A few weeks ago we spent a full class period working through a proof of Arrow's impossibility theorem, and I came away feeling like I finally understood why designing a fair voting procedure is so hard once people have different rankings over more than two options. Then, almost immediately afterward, something happened on a committee I sit on outside of class that felt related but not quite the same, and I still haven't worked out whether it's really a version of the same problem or a genuinely different one.

I help run a small student-funded grant that gives a handful of classmates money for summer research projects. This cycle, our three-person panel — two other students and I — had to evaluate a proposal that, according to our charter, can only be funded if the panel judges it to be both (a) feasible to complete within a ten-week summer, and (b) likely to produce a result worth writing up. I thought the project was feasible and would produce a good result, so I voted to fund it. One co-panelist thought it was feasible but wouldn't produce anything worth writing up, and voted no. The other thought the timeline was unrealistic, but that the underlying idea was strong enough to produce an excellent result if it were somehow pulled off, and also voted no. Tallying our final yes-or-no votes, I was outvoted two to one. But when I mentioned this afterward to a friend from my seminar, she pointed out that if you look at the two requirements separately instead of looking at our final votes, a majority of us actually agreed the project was feasible, and a majority also agreed it would produce a good result if completed — two different majorities, but majorities all the same — so criterion by criterion, both of the conditions our charter demands were satisfied by a majority of the panel. Our bylaws simply say the panel decides "by majority vote," and nobody had ever noticed before that voting on the individual requirements and voting on the final decision could come apart like this.

So which of these two ways of counting our votes is the right one, with the bylaws simply needing to be rewritten to say so? Or is this actually a disguised version of the same difficulty Arrow's theorem points to, so that no procedure could avoid this kind of clash in general? I'd like to understand whether this is a recognized problem with some established resolution, or whether a committee in our position is really just stuck picking a convention and living with what follows from it.
