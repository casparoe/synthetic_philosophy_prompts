I was on assignment covering a public panel about whether AI-assisted formal verification (Lean and its relatives) is making mathematical proof more trustworthy, and after the session two of the panelists — a proof theorist and a set theorist — kept arguing about a more basic question over dinner. I took notes but don't have the technical background to referee it myself.

(1) The proof theorist opened by saying that none of us can really know that Peano Arithmetic, let alone ZFC, is consistent: Gödel's second incompleteness theorem shows that any consistent theory strong enough to formalize arithmetic cannot prove its own consistency, so whatever confidence mathematicians place in the axioms isn't licensed by proof, just by the habit of not yet having hit a contradiction.

(2) The set theorist replied that this overstates things. Gentzen already showed, in 1936, that PA's consistency follows from accepting transfinite induction up to the ordinal ε₀, a principle most people find at least as evident as PA's own axioms. And, she added, nobody has managed to derive a contradiction from PA in the decades since, despite real incentive to try — witness Edward Nelson's widely discussed 2011 claim to have done exactly that, which other logicians soon showed rested on an error.

(3) The proof theorist answered that the unblemished track record proves less than it seems to. If PA were secretly inconsistent, the shortest derivation of a contradiction from its axioms could be absurdly long, so "nobody has found one" is exactly what we would expect to see whether or not PA is consistent. If anything, he said, Nelson's case shows that even serious logicians can convince themselves, for a while, that they've found precisely such a derivation.

(4) The set theorist countered with what she called the real point: Gödel's theorem doesn't just make Con(PA) unprovable within PA, it entails that an actual proof of Con(PA) inside PA would itself show PA is inconsistent. So the silence he was treating as inconclusive is, in a consistent theory, exactly what we should expect, and even hope for; wanting PA to certify itself is wanting the wrong thing.

(5) The proof theorist granted that this was a nice point about what a consistent PA would look like from the inside, but said it does nothing to show PA actually is consistent, and that Gentzen's proof doesn't really escape the problem either — it just relocates our trust onto transfinite induction up to ε₀, formalized in a system that itself has to be believed, in a way that echoes how Hilbert's original hope for an absolutely certain, finitary consistency proof for all of mathematics didn't survive Gödel's theorems in the first place.

Neither of them conceded the larger point, and I couldn't tell who had the better of it. Could you go through the exchange turn by turn, say who has the stronger argument at each stage, and note anything you think both of them are missing?
