I'm an undergraduate philosophy major and one of two student representatives on my university's Honor Council. This term I'm also taking a seminar on social epistemology, where we just spent two weeks on the Condorcet Jury Theorem: the result that, given independent voters who are each individually more likely than not to be correct, a majority vote converges on the right answer with near certainty as more voters are added, but that the very same logic runs in reverse — converging on the wrong answer with near certainty — if the voters are individually worse than a coin flip.

This has turned out to be directly relevant to council business. Over the past year, a growing share of our cases has turned partly on AI-writing-detection scores, and one finding was overturned on appeal after the accused student, a non-native English speaker, showed through her drafts and an interview that the flagged passages were entirely her own. Partly in response, the steering committee will soon vote on a proposal to enlarge our hearing panels from three members to seven, possibly also requiring five of the seven votes, rather than a simple majority, to sustain a finding against a student. The proposal's backers, including a friend of mine on the committee, appeal to a straightforward wisdom-of-crowds intuition: more reviewers, fewer mistakes. Some of us are unconvinced, since every panelist receives the same short training on how to weigh the detection score, so their judgments may be far from independent — and if that training biases panelists toward over-trusting the score for certain writing styles, individual competence on exactly the hardest cases could already be below chance, in which case that same theorem implies a larger panel would make wrongful findings more certain, not less.

I have been asked to circulate a short memo before the vote, and I would like your help drafting it. It should state a clear recommendation on the panel-size proposal, and on the voting-threshold question if you think it bears on your reasoning, and it should explain that recommendation in terms the steering committee could actually evaluate — taking the wisdom-of-crowds intuition seriously rather than dismissing it out of hand. If your recommendation depends on procedural changes, whether to training, to how votes are recorded, or to anything else, please spell those out concretely enough that the committee could act on them at the same meeting.
