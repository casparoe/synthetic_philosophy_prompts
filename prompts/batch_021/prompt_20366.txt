I'm a PhD student in financial economics, and for my job-market paper I've been trying to model trading volume around scheduled disclosure events, which has sent me back to the no-trade literature I only half-absorbed during my first-year theory sequence. This term, our department's formal epistemology reading group spent two sessions on Aumann's agreement theorem and its extension by Milgrom and Stokey to full-blown trade: the result that if an allocation is ex ante Pareto optimal under a common prior, and it is common knowledge that a proposed reallocation is one every party would (weakly) accept given their private signal, then that reallocation has to be a null trade, because the very fact that everyone is willing to trade is itself information that should already have been priced in. Everyone in the room agreed the argument was basically airtight given its premises, and everyone agreed that real markets generate enormous volume that looks nothing like what the theorem predicts. Where we split was over which premise to blame. Half the room wanted to keep the common-prior assumption intact and explain volume away as liquidity trading, portfolio rebalancing, and noise traders providing camouflage, in the spirit of Grossman and Stiglitz. The other half wanted to keep every trader fully rational and fully informed of everyone else's information, and instead reject the idea that rational agents must share a prior to begin with, so that a good deal of real speculative volume — the spikes around earnings announcements that seem too large to be mere liquidity trades, for instance — reflects genuine, non-defective differences in how people interpret the very same public information.

I'd like you to write a dialogue between two of the participants from that session, who run into each other afterward at the coffee cart outside the seminar room. Dr. Okafor holds the first view: she thinks that "agreeing to disagree" about priors is not a coherent resting point for fully rational agents who both know they are trying to track the same underlying truth, so any residual disagreement not attributable to differences in information is really a symptom of bounded rationality or noise somewhere in the system, and she wants the no-trade theorem's logic preserved more or less intact for sophisticated, well-informed traders. Dr. Lindqvist holds the second: she thinks the common-prior assumption is doing all the work in generating the "paradox" in the first place, that treating priors as themselves answerable to shared evidence quietly conflates priors with posteriors, and that a fully Bayesian, fully rational agent can simply start from a different place than another without either of them being at fault. I want real engagement rather than a draw: have Dr. Okafor press Lindqvist on whether "rational but irreducibly different priors" is doing any genuine explanatory work, as opposed to just relabeling disagreement as acceptable; and have Lindqvist press Okafor on why liquidity- and noise-trader stories should be expected to track the actual empirical timing of volume around information events as well as they would need to. Let the two of them end the conversation having each stated, in their own words, what kind of evidence would actually change their mind. I am not looking for a tidy resolution, just a clear picture of exactly where the disagreement bites.
