One question on the final was: "In Knowledge and Its Limits, Williamson argues that no non-trivial condition is luminous, using an example of a subject's feeling cold over the course of a gradually warming morning. Reconstruct the margin-for-error argument in your own words, and explain what Williamson takes it to show about the KK principle — the claim that knowing P entails being in a position to know that you know P. Does the argument leave any room for conditions to remain luminous, and if so, which ones?" It's worth 20 of the 100 points on the final.

Here's the answer, blind-graded so I only have it as "Student 11":

"Williamson's target is the luminosity thesis — the idea that whenever a condition obtains, the subject is automatically in a position to know that it obtains. He thinks this fails for every condition except trivial ones, and argues for it with an example of someone standing outside while the morning gradually warms up, from freezing at dawn to comfortable by late morning, in changes too small at any given step for introspection to register. The crucial premise is a margin-for-error principle: since introspection isn't perfectly reliable, knowing that you feel cold at a given moment requires that you still feel cold at every moment close enough to be indiscriminable from it, because otherwise your belief could too easily have turned out false. That principle can be chained all the way down the series, from the last moment, when you're clearly not cold, back to the first, when you're clearly freezing, and the chain ends in contradiction. So it turns out you never strictly know, at any single point, that you feel cold — not even at the moments where there's no doubt that you do. Since the condition is satisfied without the subject being in a position to know it, luminosity fails there, and Williamson thinks the same kind of argument generalizes to nearly any condition we might have assumed was self-intimating.

I think this fits with his broader anti-realism about vagueness — 'cold' behaves like a sorites predicate here, and the moral is that there's no sharp fact about the exact instant the morning stopped being cold, just a fuzzy stretch where our concepts run out. It also undoes the KK principle, although in a slightly different shape than I expected going in: Williamson's point ends up being that even when you don't know something, you aren't thereby guaranteed to know that you don't know it either, since your own ignorance can land in the same kind of borderline territory as anything else. The one loophole is trivial conditions, like logical truths, which hold at every case and so give the margin-for-error argument nothing to grip on — those can stay luminous."
