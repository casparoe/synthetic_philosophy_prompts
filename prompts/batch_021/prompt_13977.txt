Standing to blame stopped being an abstract question for me a few months ago, when I raised a concern about a manager's favoritism and was told I had no room to talk, since I had benefited from similar favoritism earlier in my career. I've been mulling that over ever since, which is probably why an argument I overheard between two friends, Priya and Marcus, at dinner last week has stuck with me.

The subject was a mutual friend, Simon, who had taken to "ghosting" people after a few dates rather than simply telling them he wasn't interested. Renata called him out on this at a party, and here is how Priya and Marcus's subsequent argument went:

(1) Priya said Renata had no standing to criticize Simon, since Priya happened to know that Renata had ghosted at least three people herself in the past year.
(2) Marcus said that was beside the point: even if Renata is a hypocrite, ghosting is still wrong, and pointing out her hypocrisy doesn't make Simon's behavior any less bad -- Priya was attacking the messenger rather than answering the message.
(3) Priya replied that blaming someone is not just asserting a true proposition -- it implicitly claims the authority to hold them to a standard, and a hypocrite forfeits that authority by exempting herself from the very standard she invokes. So the content of Renata's complaint can be correct while Renata is nonetheless the wrong person to be voicing it.
(4) Marcus answered that this cannot be right as a general principle, since almost everyone has, at some point, failed to live up to a standard they still enforce on others; if any past lapse disqualified a person from blaming, hardly anyone would ever retain standing to blame anyone. So Priya's view needs some further distinction -- recency, repentance, or similarity of offense, perhaps -- that she has not supplied.
