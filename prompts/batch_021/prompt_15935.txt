Here's the question I set: "Augustine denies that there is ever a permissible mendacium officiosum -- a 'lie of kindness,' e.g. one told to save an innocent person's life. Reconstruct the argument that gets him to this conclusion, and explain how his position handles the apparent counterexamples of the Hebrew midwives (Exodus 1) and Rahab (Joshua 2), both of whom lied and were seemingly rewarded by God for it. Does his handling of those cases sit comfortably with his official view that lying, without exception, is always sinful?"

And here's the answer, reproduced exactly as submitted, typos included:

"Augustine's view is that a lie is defined not by whether what you say is objectively false, but by whether theres a mismatch between what's in your heart -- what you actually believe -- and what you assert, combined with an intention to decieve. So someone who sincerely but mistakenly asserts a falsehood isn't lying at all, and, more surprisingly, someone who deceives through a statement that's technically true could still count as lying in Augustine's sense. Given this, Augustine still thinks every lie, no matter how well-intentioned, is sinful. He's explicit that even the 'best' kind of lie -- one that harms nobody and is told only to save an innocent life -- remains wrong, because for Augustine, as later for Kant, the prohibition on lying has to be universalizable: if we let ourselves lie whenever the stakes are high enough, the whole practice of assertion collapses, since no one could trust anyone else's word anymore. That's why Augustine can't just carve out an exception for 'good' lies -- doing so would undermine the very possibility of truthful speech as a reliable practice.

This creates an obvious problem for him with Exodus 1, where the midwives lie to Pharaoh about killing the Hebrew infants, and Joshua 2, where Rahab lies to protect the Israelite spies, since in both cases scripture seems to say God rewarded them for it. Augustine's response is that God was rewarding their underlying mercy and fear of God, not the lies themselves -- the lying is a separate, regrettable act that happens to accompany a praiseworthy motive, rather than being retroactively excused by it. But notice that Augustine also ranks the lie-to-save-a-life as the least blameworthy type of lie on his scale of gravity, with only jesting lies ranking as milder still. So he's already conceding, in effect, that this kind of lie is basically fine in practice, even if he won't quite say so outright -- which suggests his official absolutism is less a live commitment than a kind of rhetorical hard line he draws for pedagogical reasons."
