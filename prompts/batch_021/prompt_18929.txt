I'm a fourth-year philosophy PhD student working on practical rationality — my dissertation has a chapter on resolute choice that started life as a term paper on Kavka's toxin puzzle — and this year, as part of my program's clinical ethics practicum, I've been sitting in on my teaching hospital's ethics committee as a non-voting student member. A case came up this week that the committee has asked me to help draft an opinion on, and it keeps striking me as the toxin puzzle wearing a hospital gown, though I'm honestly not sure the resemblance is doing real philosophical work rather than just relabeling the difficulty.

The patient is a woman in her early thirties with a long history of bipolar I disorder. Three years ago, after two manic episodes in which she refused all treatment — one ending in a serious car accident, the other in a bankrupting spending spree — she worked with her outpatient psychiatrist and a hospital attorney, while stable and by every available measure competent, to execute what our program calls a "Ulysses clause": a directive stating that if she is ever hospitalized for mania and refuses medication, the team should treat her over her objection, and that no refusal she offers in the moment should count as revoking it, since she expected her future manic self to construct a plausible-sounding reason to refuse. She has reaffirmed the document at every annual visit since, most recently four months ago. She is now four days into her first manic episode since signing it, refusing her mood stabilizer and asking to leave against medical advice. The complication is that she is not obviously incapacitated: on today's assessment she states her diagnosis accurately, cites her own history of bad outcomes off medication, and gives a coherent, if manic-flavored, reason for refusing now — that the medication would blunt her before she finishes a project she has become consumed by this week. The resident thinks she clears the ordinary bar for capacity; the attending thinks the content of her reasoning is itself evidence the bar shouldn't be cleared. Her outpatient psychiatrist, on the call, argues that honoring the refusal defeats the entire point of the exercise: a Ulysses clause that only ever binds a patient who turns out to be incapacitated anyway was never doing any independent work. Other members worry that overriding a patient who passes a standard capacity interview, on the strength of something she signed at thirty-one, comes uncomfortably close to saying she forfeited any future say over this particular decision for the rest of her life. Hospital counsel says that if we want to pursue involuntary treatment, we need to file for an emergency hearing by tomorrow afternoon; otherwise the applicable hold statute requires releasing her. About a dozen other patients in our program have signed similar clauses, so whatever we decide will function as precedent whether or not we call it that.

Could you draft the short written opinion I'll circulate before tomorrow's meeting? It should give a concrete recommendation for the case with that deadline attached, plus a separate recommendation on whether the program should keep offering this no-in-episode-revocation version of the clause going forward, or require some real-time override mechanism instead. I'd like it to take her psychiatrist's objection seriously — that a self-binding commitment nobody's future self can ever actually invoke isn't really a commitment, which does sound like Kavka's own difficulty about rationally intending now to do something your later self will have every apparent reason not to do — without collapsing into the view that a capacitated adult's contemporaneous refusal simply stops counting once she has signed a form. And if you think the toxin-puzzle framing is actually the wrong tool for this case rather than merely an imperfect one, I'd genuinely like to hear why.
