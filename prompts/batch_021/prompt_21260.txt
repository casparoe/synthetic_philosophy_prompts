I've been tinkering with a little side project modeling what nodes in a distributed consensus protocol "know" at each round (partly inspired by the muddy children puzzle and stuff like the Two Generals Problem), and that sent me down a rabbit hole into epistemic logic over the weekend. I kept running into this thing philosophers call the "problem of logical omniscience" -- apparently in the standard possible-worlds (Kripke-style) semantics for knowledge, any agent that knows a couple of basic facts automatically also knows every tautology and every logical consequence of those facts, instantly and for free, which is obviously not how any real reasoner (human or program) actually works.

Thing is, I don't really get why this is treated as such a deep, unsolved puzzle. In model checking we deal with state explosion constantly and nobody writes papers agonizing over it like its some profound mystery about the nature of "checking" -- you just bound the search, use symbolic methods, and move on with your life. So why can't we do the same move here: just cap how many inference steps the modeled agent gets per round, treat anything past that as "not known yet," and call logical omniscience basically solved -- an artifact of sloppy modeling, not a real puzzle? I brought this up with a friend who actually has a philosophy degree and she got weirdly cagey, just insisting its way more subtle than that without really explaining why. Feels like maybe people are dressing up a straightforward implementation detail as a Deep Problem. Am I missing something real here, or is my engineer instinct basically right on this one?
