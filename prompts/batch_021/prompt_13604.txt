I'm a philosophy PhD student working in formal epistemology, and I'm currently revising the dissertation chapter that deals with chance-credence norms. Last week I gave a work-in-progress talk on David Lewis's Principal Principle that went worse than I'd hoped: I could state the 1980 principle cleanly enough -- your credence in A, given that the chance of A is x plus whatever else you know that's admissible, should equal x -- and I could gesture at the trouble Lewis got into once he insisted, as a good Humean, that chances themselves supervene on the entire history of the world, future included. But when a committee member pressed me on why this actually breaks the Principle rather than just making objective chance awkward to live with, I realized I'd been reciting vocabulary -- "undermining futures," the "big bad bug," the "New Principle" -- without being able to explain the mechanism to someone who wasn't already sold on it.

I'd like your help getting a genuinely intuitive, non-formal grip on three things, ideally anchored to a toy example (a repeatedly tossed coin whose bias is fixed by a best-system analysis works fine) rather than a restatement of the machinery. First, why does the mere possibility of futures that would undermine a chance theory generate an actual inconsistency for the original Principal Principle, as opposed to just making present chance hard to know from the inside? Second, in the fix Lewis adopts in "Humean Supervenience Debugged" (1994) -- conditionalizing credence on the whole chance theory T that entails ch(A) = x, rather than on the bare value x -- what exactly is doing the work of blocking the derivation of the bug, and why doesn't the same undermining worry just reappear one level up, aimed at our credence in T itself? Third, I'd value your own assessment: is the New Principle a genuine solution, or does it just relocate the admissibility problem onto the question of which complete theories we could ever be in a position to have non-undermined credence in?
