For a paper I am writing on diachronic norms of credence, I have been trying to pin down a persistent difficulty with van Fraassen's Reflection Principle. The principle requires that an agent's current credence in a proposition A, conditional on the supposition that her credence in A at some later time will equal r, itself equal r — a constraint van Fraassen defends by extending the diachronic Dutch Book argument standardly used to motivate conditionalization. Read without qualification, however, the principle seems to demand deference to future credences even when the agent can already foresee that the shift between now and later will stem from something like forgetting evidence, rather than from any evidentially warranted change of mind, and van Fraassen accordingly restricts Reflection to "rational" changes of opinion. What I have not been able to work out is why this restriction is so difficult to spell out precisely: what exactly goes wrong if "rational change of opinion" is simply cashed out as "change by conditionalization on new evidence", and is there a principled way of restricting Reflection that leaves it doing genuine normative work beyond what conditionalization already secures, without quietly presupposing an answer to the question of which future selves deserve deference?
