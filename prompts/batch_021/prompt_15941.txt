A friend and I spent a long train ride arguing about Skolem's Paradox without settling anything, and the same fight broke out again that night, more sharply, between two other people at dinner. I've reconstructed their exchange below and would like your move-by-move assessment, not a single verdict.

(1) Ines said the paradox dissolves once you're careful: if ZFC is consistent, the downward Löwenheim–Skolem theorem gives it a countable model, and some set inside that model counts as uncountable only because no element of the model encodes a bijection between it and the natural numbers. Uncountability itself isn't relative, she said; the model just lacks a witness that exists outside it.

(2) Daniel said "exists outside it" is the whole problem: whether that bijection exists depends on which background universe you check in, and any universe you name is, by the same theorem, itself just another countable model relative to some further one. There's no level at which the bijection is absolutely missing rather than merely not-yet-found -- which, he said, is why Skolem himself concluded that cardinality is only ever a relative notion.

(3) Ines replied that this just redescribes ordinary non-categoricity as though it were a discovery about meaning: no first-order theory forces every model onto the cumulative hierarchy, but that doesn't make "uncountable" indeterminate there. This, she said, is the extra step Putnam later took to wring a general anti-realist conclusion out of the same fact -- and even most people sympathetic to Skolem think that step overreaches.

(4) Daniel said she couldn't have it both ways: she gets to posit "the cumulative hierarchy, the real one" as settling things while calling his regress illegitimate, when our only grip on that structure is through descriptions that countable models satisfy equally well. Realism might still win in the end, but she hadn't earned it here any more than he'd earned his skepticism.

Neither gave any ground. Please go through the four moves and say who has the better of it at each one -- a tie is a fine answer where that's genuinely warranted -- and let me know whether there's an assumption neither of them ever defends that the whole disagreement actually turns on.
