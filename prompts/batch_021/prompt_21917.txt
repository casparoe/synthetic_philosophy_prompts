I'm a PhD student in philosophy finishing a dissertation chapter on Leibniz's philosophy of mind, and I've run into a question the secondary literature doesn't really address. Leibniz pulls in two directions on mechanism: his Mill Argument (Monadology §17) denies that any arrangement of moving parts could ever add up to perception, yet his lifelong project of a characteristica universalis and calculus ratiocinator treats correct reasoning itself as something that could, in principle, be carried out mechanically. He also held that every necessary truth is resolvable by a finite analysis into an identity, whereas only contingent truths require the infinite analysis available to God alone. Now suppose Leibniz were shown Gödel's incompleteness theorems and the Lucas–Penrose argument built on them: roughly, that because a mathematician can recognize the truth of a Gödel sentence that no consistent formal system encoding her own reasoning could prove, her mind cannot simply be that system. Would Leibniz's own doctrines push him toward Lucas and Penrose's anti-mechanism, or against it? I'm especially interested in whether Gödel's theorem is actually more of a problem for his claim that necessary truths admit finite proof than it is support for his resistance to mechanized perception. Please reason from Leibniz's own specific commitments rather than simply rehearsing the standard modern debate.
