I write fantasy, and back when I started my current series (three books deep now, sadly bleeding into a fourth) I made my editor promise me something. I've got a bad habit of caving on consequences - killing off a character and then finding some clever loophole a book later because test readers cried too hard or I got attached myself. So before we'd even finished outlining book two, I told her flat out: whatever happens, don't let me undo Kestrel's death, no matter how good my excuse sounds at the time. We wrote it into the series bible together, and even built in an escape hatch - the rule could only be changed if we both agreed on it after sitting with the idea for a month, specifically so neither of us could get talked into something in the heat of an argument.

Well, I'm most of the way through book three now, and I've found a way to bring her back that genuinely doesn't feel like a cheat to me - it resolves a thread from book one that I don't think I even understood the implications of when I wrote it. I did the whole process properly too: sat on it a full month, made my case in writing, gave her time to think it over. She still says no. Her argument is that this is precisely the kind of situation the rule was built for, that of course it feels justified from in here, thats what makes it dangerous, and that the whole point of requiring both of us to sign off was so present-me doesn't get to be the sole judge of when an exception applies. Which is fair, except - dont writers get to grow, and see things their past self couldn't have? At some point doesn't sticking to the original rule just become an excuse to never change anything, since "no" is automatically the default outcome whenever we disagree?

I brought this up with some writer friends at a conference last month expecting a quick answer and instead got an hour of arguing that went nowhere, so I'm genuinely stuck on it. Was my past self right to set things up so my present self doesn't get a vote of her own here, or is there some point where a rule like this stops deserving to bind you, even after you've gone through its own process to challenge it properly? And is it a bug or a feature that, whenever we're deadlocked, the tie automatically goes to the rule instead of to whoever wants the change?
