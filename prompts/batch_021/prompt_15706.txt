For the past year I have been teaching myself decision theory in my spare time, working through papers and lecture notes rather than any formal coursework, since I never studied philosophy at university. A friend who is doing the same thing and I have taken to setting each other short practice questions, and I would like a rigorous outside opinion on how to grade the answer she sent back this week, since neither of us has any formal training to fall back on.

The question I set her was: "Explain the 'Death in Damascus' scenario from decision theory in your own words. Which theory of rational choice was it originally constructed to cause trouble for, and what exactly goes wrong for that theory in this case? Finally, does the rival theory escape completely unscathed, or has that been disputed as well?"

Here is her answer, verbatim:

"Death in Damascus asks you to imagine a man who learns that Death is coming for him. Death is a perfect predictor of where people will be, so it makes no difference whether the man stays in Damascus or flees to Aleppo: Death has already set out for wherever the man will end up. Gibbard and Harper introduced this case as a counterexample to causal decision theory. CDT tells an agent to act on the causal upshot of each option, but since nothing the man does can causally affect where Death already is, CDT treats staying and fleeing as equivalent and can't give a firm recommendation. Worse, as the man deliberates, updating on whichever option he currently favors keeps shifting which act looks better, so CDT can never settle on a choice that remains best after that updating -- there is no ratifiable option. Evidential decision theory does better here, since it tracks whichever act counts as the best news given all the agent's evidence, including evidence about his own state of mind, so it converges on one stable answer instead of flip-flopping forever. In that sense, Death in Damascus is really just Newcomb's Problem again: the same dominance reasoning that tells a Newcomb agent to take both boxes regardless of what the predictor did tells our man to simply flee regardless of where Death is, which is exactly the stable verdict causal decision theory is supposed to deliver but, on its own, fails to reach here."
