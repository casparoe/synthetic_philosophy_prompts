I've been working through Wittgenstein's On Certainty for a seminar and I think I've basically cracked what he's doing, except my professor says I've got it backwards, and I don't fully get why. My reading is that Wittgenstein is doing a more sophisticated version of foundationalism: instead of Descartes cogito or "clear and distinct" ideas as the bedrock, the bedrock is ordinary stuff like "I have two hands" or "the earth has existed for a long time" -- his hinge propositions. These are supposed to be the things we're MOST certain of, certain beyond any possible justification, and that's exactly why they can function as the foundation everything else -- our doubting, reasoning, inquiring -- gets built on top of and ultimately justified by, like premises sitting at the bottom of the chain. That sounds like textbook foundationalism to me: a layer of privileged, maximally justified basic beliefs that ground and support all the rest.

So when my professor insists that hinge epistemology is actually a rejection of foundationalism rather than a fancier version of it, I get stuck -- if hinges aren't playing the role of justified foundations, what are they even doing in the structure of knowledge? Is this just people bickering over what counts as "foundationalism," or is there a real, substantive difference in how Wittgenstein thinks certainty works that I'm not seeing? Would appreciate a sanity check before I build my whole term paper around this and embarrass myself in front of the seminar.
