I'm taking a bioethics seminar this semester, and while renewing my driver's license last week I hesitated for the first time over the organ donor checkbox, which I'd always ticked without a second thought. We had just discussed how a growing share of deceased organ donation now proceeds through "donation after circulatory death" rather than a declaration of brain death: life support is withdrawn, and once the heart stops, clinicians typically wait only a few minutes — anywhere from two to five, in the protocols I've come across — before pronouncing death and beginning to recover organs. That window strikes me as uncomfortably short, given that circulation is sometimes restored after considerably longer gaps in other medical contexts, such as resuscitation following cardiac arrest. If it isn't clear that the heart's stopping is irreversible at that point, I don't see how the person can already count as dead rather than simply dying and not yet resuscitated. Is there a good answer to this worry, or does donation after circulatory death actually rest on a thinner notion of death than the one used for brain-dead donors? I'd like to work out what I should think before I check that box again.
