As a science journalist, I often have to explain to readers why a once-dominant theory—phlogiston, the caloric theory of heat, the luminiferous ether—was eventually abandoned, without leaving the impression that today's leading theories are just as disposable. I've been reading John Worrall's structural realism, which holds that what actually survives revolutionary theory change is mathematical structure rather than ontology (his standard example is Fresnel's equations for light persisting, reinterpreted, inside Maxwell's electromagnetic theory, even after physicists discarded the elastic-solid ether they were derived from). But I've also come across the older Newman objection to Russell-style structuralism, which holds that merely preserving "structure" across theory change is a nearly empty achievement unless the realist says more—partly why philosophers such as Ladyman and French push toward a more radical ontic structural realism instead of Worrall's epistemic version. Please describe the most important implications of this debate for how I should responsibly narrate scientific progress and theory change to a general readership: should I still call an abandoned theory simply "wrong," or should I foreground whichever structure persisted through the revolution, and does the choice between epistemic and ontic structural realism actually change what I owe my readers?
