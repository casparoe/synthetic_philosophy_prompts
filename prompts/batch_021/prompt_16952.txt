I am plotting a novel in which the residents of a small mill town have long had their own name for a cluster of illnesses they attribute to the local water supply, years before any doctor, epidemiologist, or regulator is willing to take the pattern seriously. Before I let this premise anchor the book's central conflict, I want to be sure I am drawing on the philosophy honestly rather than merely borrowing its vocabulary. Miranda Fricker's concept of hermeneutical injustice describes a disadvantage arising from a genuine gap in a society's collective interpretive resources; her own illustrative case is a woman enduring sexual harassment at a time when, on her account, no one -- including the women themselves -- yet possessed the concept needed to name it. Kristie Dotson's related notion of contributory injustice instead covers cases where the relevant concept already exists and functions perfectly well within the marginalized group's own epistemic community, but is refused uptake by a dominant group unwilling to draw on interpretive resources it did not itself originate. Write an essay that lays out this distinction with precision, weighs the strongest considerations for treating these as genuinely different kinds of epistemic injustice against the considerations for treating contributory injustice as merely a variant of the hermeneutical kind, and closes by defending a clear verdict on which concept better fits a case like the one I've sketched, where what is missing is not vocabulary but recognition.
