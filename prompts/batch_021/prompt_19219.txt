In a graduate seminar on philosophy of mind, after working through Clark and Chalmers's "The Extended Mind," we spent a session testing their four-part criterion for extended cognition -- that a resource be constantly available and habitually invoked, easily accessed, automatically endorsed once retrieved, and the product of a prior conscious endorsement -- against a case the original paper never considers: someone who works through hard personal decisions by typing the competing considerations into a large language model, reading its reply, and revising their view accordingly, and who does this so habitually that they rarely settle anything important without it. Two people in the seminar, Renata and Oskar, disagreed sharply about whether this counts as genuine extended cognition on a par with Otto's notebook, and kept arguing well after the session ended; neither convinced the other, and the seminar leader declined to settle it on the spot. I've tried to reconstruct the exchange faithfully below.

(1) Renata argued that the case satisfies all four conditions -- the model is constantly at hand and habitually consulted, its output is retrieved as easily as opening an app, the user accepts what it says without the scrutiny they'd give a stranger's advice, and their eventual view is continuous with what they endorsed earlier in the same exchange -- so by the parity principle it should count as an extension of reasoning, exactly as the notebook extends Otto's memory. (2) Oskar objected that there is a structural difference the four conditions were never designed to register: Otto retrieves the very content he once wrote down, whereas the model generates a fresh response each time from training and computations the user neither authored nor previously endorsed, which makes this look less like accessing a stored belief of one's own and more like consulting another agent's judgment -- something he doubts Clark and Chalmers's own examples were ever meant to cover. (3) Renata replied that "who authored the content" appears nowhere among the actual four conditions, so Oskar is quietly importing a fifth requirement to protect his intuition, and that if this requirement were legitimate it would also disqualify cases Clark and Chalmers clearly do want to count, such as a practiced, unreflective reliance on a slide rule or a printed multiplication table, both of which embody procedures the user never personally devised. (4) Oskar answered that he was not invoking authorship for its own sake but pointing out that it undermines reliability specifically, one of the actual four conditions: Otto's notebook returns the same address every time he opens it to that page, whereas asking a model the same question twice can return different, sometimes conflicting, answers, and a coupling that unstable cannot be "trusted" the way the notebook is -- which, he added, is really just what Adams and Aizawa call the coupling-constitution fallacy showing up again, mistaking something being causally hooked up to cognition for its being constitutive of it.

Could you go through the exchange move by move, from (1) to (4), and say who has the better of each specific point rather than just declaring an overall winner? I would also be interested in whether there is some consideration both of them are overlooking that bears on how the underlying question ought to be settled.
