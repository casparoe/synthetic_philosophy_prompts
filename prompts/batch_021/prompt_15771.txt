The question everyone had to answer was: "Using Philippa Foot's doctrine of double effect, determine whether flipping the switch is morally permissible in Judith Jarvis Thomson's 'loop' variant of the trolley problem, in which the only reason diverting the runaway trolley away from the five people on the main track actually saves them is that a single very large man standing on the loop track will be killed when the trolley strikes him, his body stopping the trolley before it can come back around onto the main line. Explain what philosophers generally take the loop case to show about the doctrine of double effect's ability to distinguish the ordinary switch case from the footbridge case."

I was assigned my classmate's response to grade, and before I write any comments on their paper I'd like a second opinion, since I don't want to shortchange them if it turns out I'm the one who's confused about the loop case. Here is their answer, word for word: "The doctrine of double effect, first defended by Philippa Foot, says that it can be permissible to cause a harm as a side effect of achieving a good result, even though it would be wrong to cause that same harm as a means to the result. Foot uses this to explain the standard trolley cases: in the switch case, the bystander merely redirects the trolley away from the five, so the one man's death is a foreseen but unintended side effect of saving them, which makes flipping the switch permissible. In the footbridge case, the agent would have to push the large man onto the tracks and use his body as an instrument to physically stop the trolley, making his death a necessary means to saving the five, which the doctrine forbids. This is why most people judge the switch case permissible but the footbridge case impermissible, even though the same number of people die and are saved in both. Thomson's loop case is structurally identical to the ordinary switch case, because the agent performs exactly the same action in both: flipping a switch to send the trolley onto a side track instead of the main one. Since the doctrine of double effect cares about the type of act being performed rather than the layout of the tracks, flipping the switch in the loop case should be permissible for exactly the same reason as in the ordinary case: the large man's death is a foreseen side effect of redirecting the trolley, not a means to saving the five. The fact that the side track loops back around is just a detail about the geography of the tracks and has no more moral significance than the shape of the tracks in the original case. So the loop case actually confirms that the doctrine of double effect can cleanly separate the switch case from the footbridge case, since in both the switch case and the loop case the harm is merely foreseen, while only in the footbridge case is the harm an intended means."
