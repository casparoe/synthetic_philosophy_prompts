I spent most of my working life as a field ecologist studying sexual selection in birds, and since retiring I have been reading my way through the philosophical literature on trust and communication, where Amotz Zahavi's handicap principle keeps being invoked as something like a general theory of honesty. As I understood it from my own field, and as it is generally presented in what I have been reading (including Brian Skyrms's work on the evolution of signaling), the peacock's tail and the gazelle's stotting display in front of a predator count as honest signals precisely because they are so costly to grow or perform that a lower-quality animal could never sustain them convincingly; it is the sheer expense of the display that is supposed to rule out bluffing. Writers on human affairs seem to reach for exactly the same reasoning when they discuss things like tattooed credentials among criminals, initiation rites demanded by fraternities and gangs, or extravagant wedding spending as honest signals of underlying quality or commitment, on the assumption that someone of lower quality simply could not sustain the same outlay.

If I have understood Zahavi correctly, it seems to follow that, for any trait we might want signaled, a more expensive version of a display should always be at least as trustworthy an indicator of that trait as a cheaper one, purely in virtue of costing more. Since this appears to license reading raw extravagance, in animals and humans alike, as evidence of underlying quality, I would like to know whether that is in fact the correct lesson to draw from the handicap principle and, if the logic really generalizes this cleanly, why so many economists continue to treat costly status displays such as conspicuous consumption as mere waste rather than as a valuable source of information about the people who engage in them.
