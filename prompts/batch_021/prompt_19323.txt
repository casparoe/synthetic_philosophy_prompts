So I'm teaching a graduate seminar on Wittgenstein's *On Certainty* this term, and a couple weeks back one session turned into a genuinely heated argument — heated by epistemology-seminar standards, anyway — that I still haven't resolved to my own satisfaction, so I want an outside take. (I'd tell you exactly how the room split but its finals season and I've lost track of anything that isn't the grading pile on my desk.)

Here's the issue. In the hinge epistemology literature there's real disagreement about what kind of mental state a "hinge commitment" actually is, and it matters a lot for whether the whole apparatus does what people want it to do — namely, get us off the skeptical hook without just relocating the problem into relativism. Duncan Pritchard's line (he calls it "quasi-fideism") is that hinge commitments aren't beliefs in the ordinary sense at all — they're arational, groundless, not the kind of thing that's properly rational or irrational, which is exactly why the skeptic can't get a foothold: you can't demand evidence for something that was never in the market for evidence to begin with. Crispin Wright thinks that's too cheap a way out — on his "entitlement" picture we need some kind of non-evidential but still genuinely rational warrant to accept things like "the external world exists" or "my memory is basically reliable," because otherwise the whole edifice of justified belief never gets off the ground in the first place. Annalisa Coliva tries to split the difference with what she calls "moderatism": hinges really are beliefs, ordinary propositional attitudes, just extremely special ones that are groundless yet still constitutive of having a belief system at all. And then there's Danièle Moyal-Sharrock sitting off to the side saying hinges aren't propositional at all, more like a kind of know-how or animal certainty — which is the option nobody in the seminar wanted to touch with a ten foot pole, and I don't blame them.

What I want from you is an essay that actually takes a side among these instead of just refereeing from a distance — pick one (or stake out a fifth position if you think they're all wrong in some shared way) and defend it. Argue from the text itself where you can, not just the secondary literature: the river-bed-of-thought passage (somewhere in the high 90s, don't hold me to the exact number), the hinges-of-a-door bit (round about 341-343), the passage where he imagines a community that consults an oracle instead of sending for a physicist (deep in the 600s). And don't let yourself off the hook on the relativism question either, because that's the part that actually made the seminar blow up: if hinges are as groundless as everyone above agrees they are, what stops someone's hinge from being "illness is caused by witchcraft" or "the earth is 6000 years old," and does your preferred view actually have the resources to say something has gone wrong there, or does it just have to shrug and call it a different framework? Don't dodge that the way I apparently keep doing in class.
