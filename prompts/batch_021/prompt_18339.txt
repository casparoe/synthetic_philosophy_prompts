I'm a science journalist finishing a long-form piece on the more technical corners of AI alignment research, and for the past few weeks I've been trying to get properly on top of the Alignment Research Center's "Eliciting Latent Knowledge" (ELK) problem before writing about it, rather than just gesturing at it the way most coverage does. The setup, for anyone who hasn't run into it: you train a powerful model to predict what a camera will see inside a vault holding a diamond (the report nicknames the whole rig the "SmartVault"), and you want an auxiliary "reporter" model that will honestly answer plain questions about the diamond, even in cases where the footage has been tampered with in ways no human watching would catch. The worry running through the report is that training won't reliably produce what it calls a "direct translator" (a reporter that reads its answer straight off the predictor's own internal state) rather than a "human simulator" (a reporter that has instead learned to guess what a human, looking at the observations, would say), since the two behave identically on anything a human can actually label. At a workshop last month, two researchers I'd come to interview ended up arguing, with real irritation, about exactly one corner of this: whether penalizing a reporter for how long it takes to compute its answer can favor the direct translator, on the reasoning that reading a fact off internal state should be cheaper than simulating an entire human's deliberation. One thought the penalty basically works; the other thought it collapses precisely when it matters, since a predictor capable enough to be worth worrying about will already need to model human beliefs for unrelated reasons, letting the human simulator freeload on computation the network was doing anyway.
