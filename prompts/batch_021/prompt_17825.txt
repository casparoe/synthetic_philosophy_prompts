Last weekend a close friend of mine came over for dinner, and while the two of us were occupied in the kitchen, my cat and my dog were left alone in the living room. When we came through afterward, my late grandmother's ceramic plant pot was in pieces on the floor, soil scattered across the rug. Neither of us had seen what happened, but we agreed on the relevant background facts: no one else was in the apartment, the window was shut, and my dog, though generally placid, only ever bothers the houseplants when my cat has already knocked something down and provoked him into a chase.

On this basis, I said, "well, if the cat didn't knock it over, the dog did," which my friend accepted without hesitation. Emboldened, I then claimed that this meant if the cat hadn't knocked it over—say, if she had been at the groomer's all afternoon and therefore could not possibly have been responsible—the dog would still have ended up knocking it over on his own. My friend rejected this outright, insisting that the second claim does not follow from the first at all, and that if anything the dog was probably innocent in that imagined scenario. I maintained that the two claims are really just one and the same claim about the cat and the dog, merely dressed up in different tenses, so that if one is true the other must be too. What should have been a two-minute conversation turned into nearly an hour of increasingly pedantic argument; we even recruited a third friend by text to break the tie, and nobody ended up budging.

Thinking it over since then, I suspect my friend's resistance was tracking something real rather than simple stubbornness, though I cannot work out what. Could you help me think this through? Are "if the cat didn't knock it over, the dog did" and "if the cat hadn't knocked it over, the dog would have" really making the same claim in different grammatical dress, or is there a substantive difference in what they assert? If they can genuinely come apart in truth value, I would like to understand exactly why—what it is about these two sentences that allows one to be true while the other is false—and, ideally, who had the better of the argument that night. More generally, I would welcome some guidance on how to recognize, in future disagreements like this one, which of two such superficially similar "if... then" statements I should actually be relying on when something practical, like whether the dog can be trusted alone with the plants from now on, depends on the answer.
