I've recently been rereading Nick Bostrom's "The Fable of the Dragon-Tyrant" alongside some of the older Overcoming Bias and LessWrong writing on why death should be treated as an urgent problem rather than a fact of life to be shrugged off, and it struck me that almost all of it quietly assumes a deprivationist answer to Epicurus's argument — death is bad for you not because it's some positive experience of harm, but because it deprives you of goods you would otherwise have had — without grappling much with how much trouble that answer runs into once you get past the opening exchange. In particular, deprivationism has to face the "timing puzzle": if your death harms you, there seems to be no time at which you actually bear the harm, since you aren't deprived of anything while you're alive, and once you're dead there's no "you" left to be the subject of any deprivation at all. The proposed fixes I've seen — locating the harm earlier, at the moment of death, spread timelessly across your life, or nowhere determinate — have always struck me as a bit ad hoc, though deprivationists seem willing to accept one or another of them as the price of resisting Epicurus.

A separate but related refinement to deprivationism is Jeff McMahan's time-relative interest account, on which how bad your death is for you scales with how psychologically connected you are, at the time you die, to the future self or selves whose goods you thereby lose. Part of its appeal is that it seems to vindicate the common intuition that a healthy young adult's death is worse than a newborn's, even though, on a naive tally of life-years lost, the infant loses more: the infant is only weakly connected psychologically to its future selves, so it has correspondingly weak time-relative interests in continuing to live, while the young adult's connections to the near future are robust.

I'd like you to argue for the less comfortable position: that a consistent deprivationist should reject the time-relative interest account and simply accept, against the intuition it was built to save, that the infant's death is at least as bad as the young adult's — arguably worse, if we're just counting years of life lost. Since deprivationists already have to bite a bullet on timing to survive Epicurus in the first place, part of your case should be that swallowing this further bullet is cheaper than it looks, once we're careful about which of our intuitions here are actually tracking the badness of death for the one who dies, and which are tracking something else (our own grief, say, or the psychological continuity we project onto the infant on its behalf). You should also bring out the costs of the rival strategy: scaling badness by psychological connectedness commits you to some awkward views about how bad we should think amnesia, severe dissociation, or advanced dementia are, given that these erode psychological connectedness without killing anyone. Please steelman McMahan's view rather than arguing against a simplified version of it.
