In 1956, Oxford University decided to confer an honorary degree on former U.S. President Harry Truman. G.E.M. Anscombe, then a fellow of Somerville College, responded with a pamphlet, "Mr. Truman's Degree," arguing that the university had no business honoring a man she considered guilty of mass murder: in ordering the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki to force Japan's surrender, she argued, Truman had made the deaths of civilians the very means of victory, and no calculation of lives thereby saved could redeem an act of intentionally killing the innocent. Anscombe was no pacifist; she accepted that killing enemy combatants in a just war could be justified. Her objection was narrower, and in her view non-negotiable: deliberately targeting noncombatants is murder, not an unfortunate side effect of otherwise legitimate warfare, whatever the strategic case for doing so. The protest did not stop Oxford from awarding the degree, but the episode fed directly into her use of the doctrine of double effect and is often read as one spur behind her later, more famous indictment of consequentialism in "Modern Moral Philosophy."

Write an essay evaluating whether Anscombe's absolutist verdict on Truman is defensible. Engage seriously with the strongest considerations on each side: the double-effect distinction between intending civilian deaths as a means to victory and merely foreseeing them as a side effect of otherwise legitimate military action; the historical question of whether the bombings were really aimed at civilian populations as such, as opposed to military and industrial targets with heavy foreseeable civilian casualties; and the standard consequentialist argument that a land invasion of Japan would have cost still more lives, quite possibly including more Japanese civilian lives, than the bombings did. Do not end on the fence: defend a specific verdict, and use it to say something about whether absolutist constraints of the kind Anscombe defends have any legitimate place in the ethics of war, as against a consequentialist weighing of costs and benefits.
