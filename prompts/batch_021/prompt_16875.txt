I'm currently taking an upper-level seminar on philosophy of mind that has spent the last several weeks on naturalistic theories of mental content, and for the seminar's final project each of us has to script a mock podcast interview with a philosopher defending one such theory. I've chosen an etiological teleosemantic theory of content in the tradition of Ruth Millikan's biosemantics: the view that what a mental state represents is fixed by the "proper function" it was selected -- by natural selection, or in some derivative way -- to perform, rather than by any current causal, informational, or resemblance relation it bears to the world. My interviewee is an invented defender of this exact position rather than Millikan herself, so please don't assume any biographical facts about her; I just want the questions to track the real commitments of the view.

Could you draft eight to ten interview questions? I'd like at least one on the disjunction/indeterminacy problem: since flies and small, dark, moving things were coextensive across most of the relevant selective history, natural selection could not have selected for tracking the former rather than the latter, so it's unclear what makes a frog's snapping mechanism represent flies determinately rather than that disjunctive or more general property. Please add a follow-up that presses on whether an appeal to "Normal conditions" genuinely resolves this indeterminacy or just relocates it one level up. I'd also like a question on the Swampman case -- a being who springs into existence when lightning strikes a swamp, a molecule-for-molecule duplicate of a normal human being but with no selection history whatsoever -- asking directly whether the guest is willing to say such a being has no beliefs or desires at all, with a follow-up on what should follow for cognitive-scientific practice if two physical duplicates can differ in mental content purely in virtue of their causal histories. And I'd like one question pressing the charge that historical content is explanatorily idle, since a state's current causal and functional role seems to do all the work in explaining behavior while the selection history that supposedly fixes its content plays no discernible role -- with a follow-up asking the guest for a concrete case where the historical story actually earns its keep.

Beyond those three required questions, please add further questions of your own on related pressure points from the course -- for instance, how "derived" proper functions are meant to extend the theory to representations fixed by learning within a single lifetime rather than by natural selection, or whether the view has anything plausible to say about abstract, non-perceptual concepts that were never themselves objects of selection. As with the first three, keep every question tied to a specific technical commitment of the theory rather than something she could answer by simply restating the slogan that content is proper function.
