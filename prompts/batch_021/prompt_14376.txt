Henry Sidgwick co-founded the Society for Psychical Research in 1882 and served as its first president, spending much of his last two decades investigating mediums, telepathy, and reported apparitions — including a large statistical survey, the "Census of Hallucinations," meant to test whether people's waking hallucinations of a particular person clustered around that person's death more often than chance would predict. This was not a hobby kept apart from his ethics. In the famously bleak concluding chapter of The Methods of Ethics, Sidgwick argues that his own careful reconstruction of common-sense morality leaves practical reason with an unresolved "dualism": impartial benevolence and rational egoism can genuinely conflict, and nothing in his analysis guarantees that duty and self-interest coincide short of the universe itself being morally governed in a way that brings the two into line — for instance, through some form of personal survival after death. By his own account, psychical research was a serious attempt to find empirical evidence for exactly that possibility, once his inherited faith could no longer supply it. He died without having found it, leaving him, by most accounts, genuinely disappointed rather than settled.

Write an essay addressing whether Sidgwick's psychical research was a rational and methodologically consistent extension of the position he stakes out in the Methods of Ethics, or whether it betrays some deeper confusion — about what evidence could actually settle the dualism, about what testimony-based inquiry can establish, or about something else you take to matter more. Engage the strongest version of Sidgwick's own reasoning before arguing against it, and end with a definite verdict rather than a balanced non-answer.
