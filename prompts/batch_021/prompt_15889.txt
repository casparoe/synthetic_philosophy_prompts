I'm a novelist currently drafting a subplot involving two former graduate-school housemates who, fifteen years on, still can't get through a dinner without reviving the same argument. I lifted the scene almost verbatim from an actual dinner party I attended recently — hosted by one of the two people involved — and before I fictionalize it for the book, I'd like a second opinion on who was actually getting the better of the exchange, since my own attempt to referee in the moment was laughed out of the room. The argument was set off by an article one of them had read that week about a proposal to fold several disability benefits into a single unconditional cash payment, but it very quickly turned into a much older disagreement about what equality is supposed to be equality of in the first place.

Renata, who does policy work for an insurer, opened by defending the cash-payment idea in principle: (1) the state should equalize resources, not outcomes, by giving everyone the same starting bundle — or its cash equivalent — and then holding them responsible for what they make of it; anything more ambitious, calibrated to how satisfied or well-off people end up feeling, either rewards chronic dissatisfaction or forces the state into impossible judgments about whose suffering counts as real, so a flat, resource-based metric is the only option that is both administratively honest and respectful of people's own choices. Marcus, who teaches political philosophy, said (2) this gets the disability case exactly backwards: handing a wheelchair user and an able-bodied person the same cash payment doesn't equalize anything real between them, since a large share of hers has to go on equipment, care, and accessible housing just to reach the baseline he starts at for free; calling that formally equal transfer "fair" just relabels an unchosen inequality in life-prospects and calls it a day. Renata answered that (3) this attacks a cruder version of resource-egalitarianism than the one she actually holds: the considered view doesn't stop at handing out identical bundles, it corrects for exactly this by asking what a prudent person, ignorant of her own future circumstances but aware of the population-wide odds of various disabilities, would have paid for insurance against them, and then taxes everyone that hypothetical premium and uses the proceeds to compensate whoever actually ends up disabled — up to what an average prudent person would have insured for, not an open-ended amount pegged to how bad anyone happens to feel. That, she said, lets resource-equality absorb brute bad luck without collapsing into the welfare-chasing she'd rejected a minute earlier. Marcus wasn't having it: (4) how much insurance a rational chooser would buy against blindness or paralysis already depends on facts about how much those conditions would set back the kinds of lives and projects people care about, which is exactly the sort of welfare-laden fact Renata's view was supposed to let the state avoid pricing; and since nobody actually bought this insurance, the premium is just whatever the theorist stipulates an average, prudent person would have paid, which quietly smuggles in a judgment about whose risk-aversion and life-plans count as the reasonable baseline — the very judgment Renata's official position was designed to let the state avoid making.

Neither of them budged, and by dessert they'd agreed only to disagree, again. Could you go through this move by move and adjudicate it properly, rather than just handing one of them the overall win?
