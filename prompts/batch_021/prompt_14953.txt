Discussions of the Lucas–Penrose argument against mechanism in my philosophy of mind seminar tend to stop at Lucas's original 1961 claim that a mind can "see" the truth of a formal system's Gödel sentence in a way the system itself cannot. I'd like an essay that instead engages with Penrose's more careful reformulation in Shadows of the Mind, which requires only that the relevant formal system be one whose soundness could in principle be known, rather than one that can prove its own consistency. Assess whether this reformulation genuinely escapes the classic objection pressed against Lucas, notably by Putnam: that any claim to know a given system is sound already presupposes exactly the assurance of that system's own consistency which Gödel's second incompleteness theorem shows the system itself cannot supply. Or has Penrose simply relocated the original difficulty one level up? Also address Benacerraf's distinct objection: that even a fully successful argument of this form would show only that no formal system we can identify and verify captures the mind, leaving it open that we are some machine whose program we can never specify or certify. Weigh these considerations carefully, but don't conclude on the fence — state and defend a specific verdict on whether any version of the Gödelian argument succeeds in refuting mechanism.
