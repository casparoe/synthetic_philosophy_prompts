The question, worth 20 points, was:

"Explain the Self-Sampling Assumption (SSA) and the Self-Indication Assumption (SIA). Using the presumptuous philosopher thought experiment, explain which of the two assumptions it is meant to embarrass, and why the other assumption is standardly thought to escape the same embarrassment. Then explain how the Doomsday argument bears on this comparison, and evaluate whether, on balance, one assumption should be preferred to the other."

One student answered as follows: "The Self-Sampling Assumption (SSA) says that I should reason as though I were randomly drawn from the set of all actual observers who fall within my reference class — for instance, every human who has ever lived, if the reference class is set at that level. The Self-Indication Assumption (SIA) instead says that I should weight competing hypotheses about the world by how many observers like me each one predicts: hypotheses on which many such observers exist are, other things equal, more probable, simply because my existing at all is more likely under those hypotheses. The presumptuous philosopher case is aimed at SIA. Two cosmological theories, T1 and T2, are empirically identical except that T2 implies a trillion trillion times more observers than T1. Physicists are about to run an experiment to decide between them, but a philosopher who reasons via SIA can announce beforehand that T2 is a trillion trillion times more probable, simply because they are far more likely to exist at all if T2 is true. This lets a purely armchair fact about population size pre-empt an actual experiment — which is the absurdity the case is meant to bring out. SSA escapes this particular embarrassment because, on Bostrom's setup, the evidence I actually have — that I am a physicist about to run the test — is just as likely to occur under T1 as under T2; my existing doesn't discriminate between the two theories, so SSA gives the philosopher nothing to work with. SSA has its own notorious cost, though. Applied to my rank in the sequence of humans who will ever live, it generates the Doomsday argument: a low rank is far more surprising if the total human population turns out to be huge than if it is small, so finding myself this early in the sequence should push my credence toward hypotheses on which humanity goes extinct relatively soon. This is where SIA is usually said to earn its keep. Because SIA rewards hypotheses with larger populations, it directly counteracts the SSA penalty against a large future population, and on the standard calculation the two effects exactly cancel. In other words, SIA solves the Doomsday problem by showing that humanity's prospects are actually better than Carter's argument suggests. Given that SIA can neutralize SSA's most notorious bullet in this way, while the presumptuous philosopher is a comparatively contrived, exotic scenario, I would say SIA comes out ahead overall."
