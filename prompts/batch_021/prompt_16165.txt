I'm several chapters into a novel narrated by a woman recovering from a callosotomy — the "split-brain" surgery once used, before better anticonvulsants existed, as a last resort against catastrophic epilepsy — and I would rather not build the recovery chapters on the cartoon version of that syndrome that circulates in popular science writing. At dinner last week I put the question to two friends I thought might help me think it through: Naomi, who has a doctorate in cognitive neuroscience, and Felix, who did a philosophy MA and treats every dinner as an occasion to relitigate the mind-body problem. What I had hoped would be five minutes of fact-checking took up most of the evening, and since I found both of them persuasive in the moment, I would like a more careful adjudication than I could manage at the table.

Naomi opened with the classic Sperry and Gazzaniga picture: flash an image to a split-brain patient's left visual field, and her left hemisphere — which does the talking in most people — will insist it saw nothing, while her left hand, driven by the right hemisphere, correctly picks the matching object off a tray anyway. (1) Since the two halves can hold incompatible "beliefs" about the same moment and act on them independently, she concluded that a callosotomy leaves two separate subjects of experience sharing one skull, and that my narrator should be written as someone who has, in some real sense, become two people. (2) Felix answered with a point he credited to Thomas Nagel: dissociated reports and dissociated actions don't, by themselves, tell you how many subjects of experience are in the room, and our ordinary habit of counting minds in whole numbers may simply have no clean application to a case this strange — so treating "two" as the sober conclusion the data force on us already claims more than the evidence shows. (3) Naomi granted that the number can't be read straight off behavior, but answered that the disconnected right hemisphere doesn't act like a reflex arc: it follows novel multi-step instructions, corrects its own mistakes, and visibly resists when the left hand is steered somewhere it doesn't want to go — and if anything else on earth behaved that way, we would credit it with a point of view without a second thought, so refusing to here, just because it happens to share a head with someone we already call a person, is a double standard rather than caution. (4) Felix's reply was empirical: he mentioned a study, led by a researcher named Pinto, in which split-brain patients could still reliably indicate whether something had appeared somewhere in their visual field at all — though not what it was, or exactly where — no matter which hand answered. If bare awareness that a stimulus occurred can still cross a severed corpus callosum, he said, the tidy picture of two fully sealed-off streams of consciousness, which is what the "two people" conclusion actually needs, may be less a discovery about split brains in general than an artifact of a handful of surgically unusual patients studied decades ago.

Could you go through this move by move — Naomi's opening claim, Felix's Nagel-inspired reply, Naomi's rejoinder about behavior, and Felix's empirical complication — and say which of them has the better of it at each stage, rather than simply declaring an overall winner? I would especially like to know where, if anywhere, either of them leans on an inference that doesn't actually hold, and whether there is a more defensible view of split-brain consciousness that their argument, as it actually went, never quite reaches. Please close with a one-line verdict in the form \verdict{Naomi}, \verdict{Felix}, or \verdict{Neither}, for whichever of them comes closer to being right overall — this matters to me for more than idle curiosity, since a novel is exactly the sort of place where one vivid wrong idea can outlive a hundred careful correct ones.
