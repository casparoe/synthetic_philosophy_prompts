I've spent a long time following the decision-theory threads on LessWrong and Overcoming Bias, where Piccione and Rubinstein's "absent-minded driver" keeps coming up. He is a driver who cannot tell whether he has already passed the first of two indistinguishable highway intersections, and so must use the same probability of exiting rather than continuing at both of them. Committing in advance to continuing with probability 2/3 at each intersection maximizes his expected payoff — what Aumann, Hart, and Perry later called the "planning-optimal" strategy. But the reasoning Piccione and Rubinstein give for what he should do once he is actually at an intersection, and has updated his belief about which one it is, leads him to a different rule that pays strictly less. This is one of the standard examples cited for why updateless decision theory has caught on in those circles: an agent who updates mid-decision can apparently end up worse off than one who just commits to his ex ante optimal plan and never revisits it. But if updating on the evidence available to you can provably leave you worse off than not updating, doesn't that show ordinary Bayesian conditionalization is simply the wrong norm to follow whenever new information might arise in the course of carrying out a decision, rather than an oddity confined to this one puzzle?
