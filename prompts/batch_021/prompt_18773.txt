I am a site reliability engineer, and part of my job involves estimating the probability of rare, high-severity failure modes—for example, a slow data-corruption bug that goes undetected for weeks—so that we can size our incident-response budget appropriately. Our three most senior engineers' informal estimates for the same failure mode often differ by an order of magnitude, and forcing that disagreement into a single "best guess" number before computing expected cost has started to feel epistemically dishonest. I have since come across imprecise probability theory, where uncertainty is represented as a whole set of probability distributions (a credal set) rather than one sharp number, along with decision rules such as Γ-maximin and E-admissibility that operate directly on such sets instead of collapsing them first. Please describe the most important implications of taking this framework seriously, rather than continuing to collapse disagreement into a single precise credence, for how a team like mine should structure and act on risk estimates—and, if possible, explain why conditioning on new evidence can sometimes widen rather than narrow a credal set (the "dilation" phenomenon), since that seems like it could catch us out in practice.
