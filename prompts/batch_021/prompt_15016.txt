I've served for six years on my town's volunteer fire department's internal conduct committee, a group of seven senior members who hear complaints about behavior on calls, separate from the chief's normal chain of command. We have a case that has split the committee badly, and I'm the one who has to write the recommendation memo we'll circulate before next Tuesday's meeting, so I'd like help thinking it through properly rather than just papering over the disagreement.

Here's what happened. One of our most respected members, a lieutenant with fifteen years in, grabbed a probationary member by the coat and screamed in his face at the scene of a bad multi-car accident, in front of the victims and a small crowd of onlookers, over what was by all accounts a minor equipment mistake. People who were there said it was frightening and humiliating, and the probationary member has told our recruitment officer he's thinking about quitting over it. Under ordinary circumstances this would be a straightforward case for our discipline policy. But three weeks later, before we'd even scheduled the hearing, the lieutenant was diagnosed by a sleep specialist with severe obstructive sleep apnea, the kind that, going by his test results, had likely been starving his brain of real sleep for well over a year. The doctor's letter states plainly that chronic sleep deprivation of that severity is well documented to cause exactly the irritability and loss of self-control he showed that night. It has also come out, now that people feel safe saying so, that several members had noticed him nodding off during briefings and snapping at people for months, but nobody flagged it because the department has an unspoken rule that you don't complain about being tired, and he himself brushed off his wife twice last year when she pushed him to get checked out.

The committee has split into three camps. One group wants to treat this as essentially a medical event rather than a discipline matter: mandate treatment and a fitness-for-duty evaluation, no formal sanction, on the grounds that it wasn't really him acting. A second group thinks that's letting him off too easily: he had a year of warning signs and chose the department's toughen-up culture over getting help, so the diagnosis shouldn't erase what he's responsible for, especially since going easy on him now would tell everyone they can behave badly and then produce a diagnosis afterward. A third group keeps pointing out that whatever we decide about the lieutenant, we still haven't addressed what the probationary member is owed, and our written conduct policy has a "discipline" track and a separate "fitness for duty" track with nothing to say about cases landing on both. I've never studied philosophy -- I run a small HVAC business -- but the argument bothered me enough that I dug up a library book on blame and free will, and the one thing I took from it is a distinction between excusing what someone did and deciding the person isn't really a fit target for blame at all, which sounds close to our split, except I can't tell whether that distinction actually settles anything for us. Could you draft the short memo I'll circulate before Tuesday? It should come down clearly on one course of action for both the lieutenant and the probationary member, with a timeline. And it should take the "it wasn't really him" camp seriously on its own terms, rather than dismissing it with the one-line reply that explaining his behavior isn't the same as excusing it -- I don't think that line actually settles our case.
