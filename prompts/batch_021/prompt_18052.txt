In my seminar on convention and the philosophy of social norms, we have just finished David Lewis's *Convention*, where he models signaling systems -- including, he suggests, natural language -- as conventions sustained by common knowledge among rational agents in a coordination game; his own illustrative case is the lantern signal arranged between Paul Revere and the sexton of the Old North Church ("one if by land, two if by sea"). This week's reading pairs that account with Brian Skyrms's evolutionary treatment of the very same kind of signaling game, developed especially in his book *Signals: Evolution, Learning, and Information*, in which a conventional, arbitrary mapping from signals to states of the world emerges instead through replicator dynamics in a population of agents who need not have any beliefs, let alone common knowledge or rationality. I would like you to compare these two explanations of how a signaling convention becomes established. Specifically, is Lewis's apparatus of common knowledge and mutual expectation doing indispensable explanatory work that the evolutionary story cannot reproduce, or does Skyrms's model show that the very same conventions can be fully accounted for without any of that cognitive machinery? If you think something is genuinely lost in translating Lewis's picture into Skyrms's, please say precisely what it is.
