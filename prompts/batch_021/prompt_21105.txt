I don't have any formal background in philosophy -- I work as an electrician -- but I do a lot of reading on my own time, and a while back I fell into an old internet rabbit hole about "Roko's Basilisk." From what I can piece together, it started as a post on LessWrong, an online forum built around rationality and AI risk, arguing that a future superintelligent AI, precisely because it wants to come into existence as early and as powerfully as possible, would have reason to punish anyone who understood the argument but didn't help build it -- and that the mere possibility of that punishment is supposed to give people today a reason to help now, even though the AI doesn't exist yet and might never. Eliezer Yudkowsky, the site's founder, was apparently alarmed enough by it that he deleted the original post and discouraged discussion of the whole idea for years, on the theory that simply knowing about the argument puts you at risk. The more I read, the more it struck me as Pascal's wager with the serial numbers filed off: both ask you to weigh a small, everyday cost against a possibly enormous downside imposed by an agent whose existence you can't verify, and to let the sheer size of the stakes settle the matter regardless of how unlikely the agent seems.

Since Pascal essentially invented this style of reasoning, I'd love to know what you think he would actually make of the Basilisk if it were explained to him in his own terms. Would he recognize it as just his own wager in new clothes and accept it for the same reasons he accepted his, or does something in his own thought give him a principled way to resist it? Two things about the original wager seem relevant to me. First, Pascal never argued for betting on some generic higher power; from what I understand, the wager sits inside a much longer case, made elsewhere in the Pensées, for why Christianity in particular deserved belief over rival religions -- which looks like his answer, prepared in advance, to the complaint that identical reasoning would support any number of incompatible gods. The Basilisk runs into exactly that problem again, since nothing rules out other, equally speculative future AIs with opposite and mutually exclusive demands. Second, the God of the wager is supposed to be a loving one, and Pascal has quite a lot to say elsewhere about that God choosing to remain partly hidden rather than compelling belief through overwhelming force or fear -- which sits awkwardly next to a "friendly" superintelligence that is somehow meant to prove its benevolence by threatening people. Would that tension give Pascal grounds to be suspicious of the Basilisk's premises, and not just its arithmetic? I'd like an actual reconstruction of how he would reason through this, not just a verdict at the end.
