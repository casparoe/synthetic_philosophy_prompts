I've been on one of my periodic rationalist-blogosphere deep dives, this time into old posts about "acausal" threats and multi-agent AI safety (I think a lot of it traces back to Center on Long-Term Risk-adjacent writing, though bits of it got cross-posted to LessWrong and the alignment forum too, so at this point I've lost track of where I originally read what). One idea that keeps coming up, if I'm summarizing it right, is the "surrogate goal" trick as a defense against this kind of threat: since agents that can predict each other's behavior without any causal contact at all (across branches of the multiverse, or just by simulating each other's source code) can in principle threaten each other the same way blackmailers or extortionists do, the standard advice is to precommit to never giving in to threats against the stuff you actually value, since a rational threatener only bothers threatening you if they expect it to pay off. The twist with surrogate goals is that, since that kind of precommitment is hard to make fully credible (and doesn't help retroactively if a threat lands anyway), you instead invent some fake, low-stakes "surrogate" goal, loudly commit to caving to threats against *that* one instead, and hope real threats get redirected onto the decoy, since thats the only place a threat against you would actually work.

I get the one-paragraph pitch, but I don't think I understand the mechanics well enough to defend it against objections, which is embarassing given how long i've been reading this stuff. Like, what stops a clever threatener from just bundling the threat so it hits the real goal and the surrogate at once, or ignoring the real/surrogate distinction entirely if they're not fully convinced you'd really let the real goal burn? Does the whole scheme secretly depend on the threatener being a very specific kind of rational, profit-motivated agent, in a way that falls apart against something spiteful, or against something trying to build a reputation for following through on threats rather than maximizing expected payoff? And is there some regress problem lurking, where your commitment to defend the surrogate goal just becomes a new real goal that's itself vulnerable to the exact same trick? Would love a walkthrough of the actual mechanism in more depth than the usual one-paragraph gloss, plus the main ways it's supposed to break down.
