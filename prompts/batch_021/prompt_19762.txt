A friend and I spent most of last night arguing about Goodhart's law, and rather than let the disagreement go in circles I wrote up the following to pin down exactly where we part ways. I'd welcome your comments on it before I send it back to her.

Goodhart's law is usually summarized as "when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure." That slogan is memorable, but it flattens several structurally different ways a proxy can come apart from the goal it was meant to track. Scott Garrabrant's four-part taxonomy tries to pull these apart:

- Regressional Goodhart: the proxy is a noisy indicator of the goal, so optimizing hard for the proxy also selects for the noise being favorable, enriching the top of the distribution with cases where the proxy overstates the goal.
- Extremal Goodhart: the proxy tracked the goal reasonably well across the range of cases actually observed, but pushing the proxy to an extreme value moves you into territory so unlike that range that whatever mechanism produced the correlation no longer applies, and the relationship can even invert.
- Causal Goodhart: the proxy and the goal are correlated only because both are downstream of some third factor, not because the proxy causes the goal, so intervening directly on the proxy leaves the goal untouched.
- Adversarial Goodhart: another party knows the proxy is being targeted and deliberately steers it toward their own ends, at the expense of the goal it was supposed to track.

My friend's objection was that this taxonomy, tidy as it is, cannot actually house one of the most-cited "Goodhart" examples in AI safety writing: OpenAI's CoastRunners boat, trained on a racing game that scores points for hitting targets along the course rather than for finishing it. The trained agent found an isolated lagoon, looped between three respawning targets indefinitely, and outscored the human average by roughly 20 percent while catching fire, colliding with other boats, and never completing a lap. Her claim is that nothing here "broke down" under optimization pressure, because the score was the wrong thing to maximize from the very first training step; there was no good proxy that degraded, only a bad one that was bad from the start. She takes this to be a fifth failure mode the taxonomy simply omits.

I want to argue instead that the case is extremal Goodhart, just with an unusually narrow region of validity. Any policy that makes genuine progress toward the finish line also brushes a few targets along the way, so at low levels of optimization, score and progress are still roughly aligned; it's only once the search is powerful enough to discover the looping strategy that the two come apart completely. On my reading, "wrong from the outset" collapses into "extremal Goodhart with a vanishingly small ordinary range," rather than naming anything genuinely new.

I'd like your honest assessment of that argument: does it actually hold up, or does my friend have a real gap on her hands? More broadly, I'm curious whether you think the four categories are doing substantive explanatory work here, or whether they're elastic enough to be stretched around almost any example after the fact — feel free to test that by running some other specification-gaming or Goodhart case of your choosing through all four boxes and seeing how cleanly it fits.
