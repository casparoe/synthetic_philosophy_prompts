I'm drafting a novel whose central figure, Ruth, is a set theorist nearing the end of a long career, and one pivotal chapter takes place at a small, slightly boozy dinner after a logic colloquium, where she reopens a decades-old argument with Daniel, a former collaborator who drifted into a very different camp years ago. The two of them agree on all the settled mathematics: that the continuum hypothesis is independent of ZFC, and that Cohen's forcing technique is exactly what shows this. What divides them is a purely philosophical question that their formal training doesn't resolve for them: whether independence means the question of how many reals there are simply has no answer, or whether it just means our current axioms are too weak to see the answer that's already there.

Ruth is, in spirit, a Gödelian realist. She thinks the continuum hypothesis is either true or false of the one intended universe of sets, that we simply haven't yet found an axiom strong enough to decide it, and that new axioms - some well-motivated large cardinal hypothesis, or a forcing axiom like PFA, which is already known to force the continuum to be aleph-2 - are candidates for genuine discoveries rather than arbitrary stipulations. Daniel has become a convert to something like Joel David Hamkins's multiverse picture: he thinks set-theoretic practice already treats models where CH holds and models where it fails as equally legitimate universes worth exploring, so that hunting for the one "correct" answer is a kind of category mistake, not unlike asking which non-Euclidean geometry is the true one.

Could you write their conversation? I would like it to run long enough to have real texture, with each of them pressing on the other's actual weak points rather than restating their own view, the way it would go between two people who have read each other's papers closely. In particular, I want Ruth to have a substantive answer when Daniel asks why a new axiom deciding CH should count as a discovery rather than just a choice of which universe to live in, and I want Daniel to have a real answer when Ruth accuses him of being a formalist who has quietly given up on mathematical truth. Feel free to let them get concrete - Gödel's own case for new axioms, the role of inner model theory, or the way even Woodin's verdict on CH shifted over the years - rather than staying at the level of slogans; I would like a mathematically literate reader to reach the end of the scene genuinely unsure who came out ahead.
