I'm working on a longer piece about this wave of "free will skepticism" pop-science books (Sapolsky's *Determined*, etc) and got steered toward Galen Strawson's Basic Argument as basically the cleanest philosophical version of the "you can't really be responsible because you didnt ultimately make yourself" move. Digging into where that idea comes from, I kept running into the same Nietzsche paragraph cited as a kind of ancestor to it — this is from Beyond Good and Evil, section 21, Kaufmann's translation:

"The causa sui is the best self-contradiction that has been conceived so far, it is a sort of rape and perversion of logic; but the extravagant pride of man has managed to entangle itself profoundly and frightfully with just this nonsense. The desire for 'freedom of the will' in the superlative, metaphysical sense, which still holds sway, unfortunately, in the minds of the half-educated, the desire to bear the entire and ultimate responsibility for one's actions oneself, and to absolve God, the world, ancestors, chance, and society involved therein, is nothing less than the desire to be precisely this causa sui and, with more than Baron Munchhausen's audacity, to pull oneself up into existence by the hair, out of the swamps of nothingness."

I read this about five times on the train this morning and get the general vibe — something like, really deserving blame or credit would require having caused yourself, which is absurd — but I can't actually reconstruct it as an argument, sentence by sentence. Like, why is causa sui specifically a "self-contradiction" and not just a weird or unfamiliar notion, and what is the Munchhausen image doing there beyond being a good insult, is it doing real logical work? I mentioned it to a friend whos doing a philosophy PhD and all she offered was "oh, the causa sui regress," then went back to her coffee, so that didn't get me very far. What I actually want is for someone to walk through the logic of this passage properly, and then tell me how closely it maps onto Strawson's regress (you do what you do because of how you are, so being truly responsible would require having made yourself that way, which requires already existing to do the making, and so on back). Is Nietzsche basically running the same argument a century before Strawson does, or is he doing something looser and more psychological/rhetorical that just happens to land in the same place?
