I'm taking a seminar on comparative ethics this term, and this week's discussion pairs Roger Ames and Henry Rosemont's account of Confucian role ethics with Aristotelian virtue ethics. I follow the headline claim—that role ethics treats relations themselves, rather than an antecedently individuated self, as ethically basic—but I'm having trouble seeing how this actually bites in practice rather than staying at the level of slogans.

Could you write a dialogue between a proponent of Confucian role ethics and an Aristotelian virtue ethicist that works the disagreement through the Analects 13.18 case of the son who conceals his father's sheep-stealing rather than reporting it? The role ethicist should explain why terms like zhi ("uprightness") and ren only acquire determinate content within a specific relation—here, father and son—rather than naming fixed traits of an individual considered apart from any relation. The virtue ethicist should press the "critical distance" worry in response: if a person just is the roles they occupy, what standpoint permits them to judge that a given role or relationship, including an oppressive or corrupt one, ought to be reformed rather than simply performed well? Each side should respond to the other's actual argument rather than simply restate their own, and the dialogue shouldn't resolve in a clean victory for either.
