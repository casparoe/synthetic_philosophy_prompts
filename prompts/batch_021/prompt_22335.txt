My college roommate Nick is getting married in a few weeks, and he's asked me to give the best man's toast. I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, but Nick did his PhD on free will and moral responsibility, and he came out the other side a committed hard determinist -- or "hard incompatibilist," which he insists is the more precise term -- who will cheerfully explain, at length, that people are really just deterministic systems running a program nobody wrote, so nobody strictly "deserves" praise, blame, gratitude, or resentment in the way we normally assume. A couple of weeks ago, half-joking but not entirely, he told me that if I stand up and thank everyone or praise his fiancée Elena in the usual sentimental way, I'll be making him a hypocrite about his own life's work. He mentioned that his last dissertation chapter was essentially an argument with P.F. Strawson's "Freedom and Resentment," and when I tried reading it myself to prepare, I got as far as the part where Strawson separates actually holding someone responsible from what he calls the "objective attitude" -- treating a person as a case to be managed rather than someone you reason and feel things with -- before I gave up and found a summary instead.

The wedding is small, around sixty people, and the room splits neatly in two: Nick's philosophy department, who will immediately notice if the toast is either naive about his position or just a cheap "gotcha, you're secretly a compatibilist" bit, and both families plus Elena's friends, who mostly want three or four warm, funny minutes about two people they love. Elena, for her part, finds the entire debate faintly absurd and is marrying him regardless, which seems like a detail worth using.

Could you write that toast for me? I'd like something meant to be read aloud in about four minutes that takes Nick's view seriously on its own terms, rather than either refuting it or quietly working around it, and that still comes across as completely sincere gratitude and admiration rather than a philosophy bit at his expense. My understanding, secondhand, is that Strawson's own answer to people like Nick was roughly that our gratitude, resentment, and love don't actually have to wait on the free-will debate being settled one way or another -- and I'd like the toast to lean on something like that, lightly, without turning into a lecture on compatibilism. One line that lands as a genuine joke for the philosophers and a genuine joke for everyone else would be the dream.
