I'm taking a history of economic thought seminar this semester and we just spent two weeks on the bit in Aristotle's Politics (Book I) where he seperates household management (oikonomia) from the broader art of wealth-getting (chrematistike), and argues that one branch of the latter - trading money for money's own sake, especially charging interest - is "unnatural." His reasoning is basically that currency is a conventional stand-in for real goods, so it shouldn't be treated like it can reproduce on its own; apparently tokos, the Greek word he uses for interest, literally means something like "offspring," which I thought was a pretty good detail for a philosophy prof to drop on a Tuesday morning. His bigger point is that natural wealth-getting has a built in stopping point (you only need so much food and so many sandals), while this other kind of exchange has none, since the target becomes the token itself rather then whatever it's supposed to stand for.

This turned into a whole argument with two people from my study group afterward about what Aristotle would say about "yield farming" and "liquidity mining" in DeFi - basically the crypto setups where you lock your coins into some protocol and it hands you back more coins over time, with no goods, services, or even a person on the other side of the transaction anywhere in sight, just contracts generating tokens out of other tokens (the industry really does use words like "farming," "yield," and "harvest," so either total coincidence or someone in that world has a philosophy degree they're not using well). One guy in my group figures Aristotle would take one look and call it usury with extra steps. The other thinks that's too quick, since Aristotle's whole objection was about one person extracting an unearned "birth" from a loan they made to another person, and there's no lender, no borrower, and arguably no relationship at all in a lot of these automated setups - so maybe his framework just doesn't have anything left to grab onto.

Can you actually work through this rather than giving me a "who can say what a dead guy would think" shrug? I want you to take Aristotle's actual premises about the natural limits of household wealth and the function of currency seriously, and tell me whether they extend cleanly to this kind of leaderless, personless money-generating-money, or whether something about the setup breaks the argument in a way he never had to consider.
