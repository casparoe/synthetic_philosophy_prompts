I spent thirty-eight years as an astrophysicist, much of it doing stellar spectroscopy, and only took up philosophy in earnest after retiring, when a running argument at a dinner party — about whether David Lewis's modal realism was just a philosopher's way of dressing up the multiverse — sent me and three former colleagues into a year-long reading group. We now have what I think is a reasonably firm grip on the disagreement between Lewis's counterpart theory, on which no individual exists in more than one possible world and a claim like "x could have been F" is made true by some qualitatively similar but numerically distinct counterpart of x, and the rival view, transworld identity, on which the very same particular literally turns up in more than one world. We have also read Kripke's two stock objections from Naming and Necessity often enough to recite them on demand: that on the counterpart analysis, "Humphrey could have won the election" ends up being a claim about somebody else's victory that the real Humphrey could not be expected to care about; and, from the essentiality of origin, that Elizabeth II could not have been born to different parents, even though someone qualitatively indistinguishable from her might have been.

Both examples have become so familiar to the group that I suspect we are now just pattern-matching rather than actually testing our grip on the theories. What I would like from you is a genuinely new example of the same underlying disagreement — one drawn, if at all possible, from territory I actually know something about, physics or chemistry or astronomy, rather than British monarchs and hypothetical elections — in which counterpart theory and transworld identity issue different verdicts about a specific de re modal claim concerning some particular physical object or event. Please set out the scenario, state plainly what each theory says about it, and tell me whether thinking it through leaves you more sympathetic to Lewis's deflationary picture or to the idea that the object in question really does have some further, primitive fact of identity tying it to the very same thing in that other world.
