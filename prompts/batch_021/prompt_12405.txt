Over the past few months, since my mother's cancer went from "manageable" to "terminal" almost overnight, I've found myself returning to philosophy books I haven't opened since college, looking for something sturdier than the consolations people usually offer. Our hospital chaplain, of all people, mentioned in passing that certain old theologians had argued we already live in "the best of all possible worlds" — clearly bracing for the eye-roll everyone gives that phrase, since I mostly knew it as the punchline of Voltaire's Candide. That sent me back to Leibniz's own Theodicy rather than the parody, because I wanted to see the actual argument before deciding whether to find it comforting or obscene.

I got through the preface well enough, but I've been stuck for about a week on the section in Part I of the Theodicy (§22, in the standard Huggard translation) where Leibniz explains how God can be said merely to "permit" evil rather than will it outright. As best I can reconstruct his argument, he splits the divine will into two "degrees." An antecedent will considers every possible good entirely on its own terms, apart from all the others, and inclines God toward it simply because it is good — presumably including, among countless other things, my mother's recovery. A consequent will is the one that actually gets carried out; it emerges, he says, from all of these separate antecedent inclinations toward good, and away from evil, being combined, in something like the way component forces combine in mechanics into a single resultant motion — one that isn't identical to any of the contributing tendencies but reflects all of them as well as they can jointly be reflected. The line I keep rereading is his claim that any one of these antecedent inclinations is "efficacious of itself (per se)... [yet] does not pass into final exercise (ad summum conatum)" unless something stronger opposes it, as though absent any opposition it would simply prevail on its own.

What I can't work out is what this "antecedent will" actually contributes, if it never itself produces anything and is always overridden by whatever the consequent total turns out to be. Is it just scholastic vocabulary dressed up to make it sound as though God almost intervened, or is Leibniz committed to something more substantive in calling it genuinely efficacious? And the mechanics comparison bothers me for a related reason: a resultant force in physics isn't wise, deliberate, or chosen — it's simply whatever the vectors add up to — so if the consequent will toward the best is a "resultant" in that same sense, doesn't that make God's toleration of any given evil sound less like a considered judgment and more like an automatic by-product of arithmetic, which seems like the opposite of the deliberate goodness the rest of the book keeps insisting on? I'd like to actually understand what this passage is doing in Leibniz's larger argument — what the antecedent/consequent distinction buys him, and whether the mechanical analogy is meant to explain the choice of the best or quietly explains it away — before I decide how much weight, if any, to put on the thought that whatever my mother is going through was somehow required by the best possible arrangement of things.
