I don't have any formal training in biology or philosophy, but I've spent a good deal of time on my own puzzling over animal signaling, and one question keeps nagging at me. Darwin was candid, including in his own correspondence, about how much the peacock's tail troubled him: sexual selection let him explain why peahens might favor such an extravagant ornament, but its sheer cost and impracticality sat awkwardly alongside the rest of his theory, and by his own admission it was among the aspects of his work that gave him the most difficulty. It was not until nearly a century after his death that Amotz Zahavi proposed the handicap principle, the idea that a trait's very costliness is what makes it a reliable signal of underlying quality, since only a genuinely superior individual could afford to bear the expense, a claim later given firmer footing through Alan Grafen's game-theoretic work. Reasoning from how Darwin actually argued in his own writing and letters, what is your best-supported guess as to whether he would have welcomed Zahavi's solution as resolving the very problem that troubled him, or whether there is something in its underlying logic that would have sat poorly with his own way of thinking?
