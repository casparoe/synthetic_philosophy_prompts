I'm a novelist, though I started out in a philosophy PhD before I bailed after two years to go write fiction instead (no regrets, but I kept enough of it to be dangerous at parties). My old officemate from that program, Priya, just defended her dissertation on Sartre's account of love and the Other in Being and Nothingness, and she's marrying Diego, a high school chemistry teacher who has never read a word of continental philosophy and is thrilled about that fact. They've asked me to give the wedding toast, which feels like being handed a grenade with the pin half out, because about half the guests are Priya's cohort -- people who will absolutely clock it if I flub the sadism/masochism "reefs" bit or flatten "the look" into a Hallmark card -- and the other half is Diego's family, lovely people who are expecting the standard soulmates, written-in-the-stars wedding toast.

Here's the actual problem though: Priya has told me, only half-joking, that if I get up there and say these two were destined for eachother or found their other half, she'll never forgive me, because that's bad faith in the most literal Sartrean sense -- it denies that she and Diego are two radically free people who have to keep choosing each other, again and again, with nothing in human nature or the universe backing that choice up or guaranteeing its still true tomorrow. So I need a genuinely warm, funny, five-minute toast that takes Sartre's stuff on freedom and bad faith seriously enough that nobody from her cohort can accuse me of dumbing it down, but that still lands for people who've never heard the phrase "for-itself" in their lives and just want to be moved. Extra credit if you can slip in one line that's secretly for the philosophy table -- something that nods at the "reefs" Sartre thinks love keeps nearly wrecking itself on -- without anyone else in the room noticing it's a joke and not just a nice metaphor.
