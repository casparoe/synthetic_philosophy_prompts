So I've been kinda stuck on this old Zhuangzi story for weeks now, found it in a philosophy anthology and honestly can't remember which translation. Right before he dies, his students want to give him this huge fancy funeral, and he tells them:

"I shall have heaven and earth for my coffin and shell; the sun and moon for my two round symbols of jade; the stars and constellations for my pearls and jewels; and all things assisting as the mourners. Will not the provisions for my burial be complete? What could you add to them?"

Then the students say they're worried crows and kites will eat his body, and he comes back with:

"Above the ground, it will be food for the crows and kites; below it, it will be food for the mole-crickets and ants. Taking from the one to give to the other — why should you have a preference between them?"

I get the whole "don't be scared of death, its natural" vibe from the first bit, but that last line about crows vs bugs is what's really getting me. Why does that specific comparison matter so much to him? Is he just making a point about how random our preferences are, or is there something bigger about his whole deal with the self and nature that this line is supposed to sum up? Would love if someone could break down what's actually going on in that last line.
