I've been stuck on the "Death in Damascus" case from decision theory lately (Gibbard and Harper's nastier cousin of Newcomb's problem), and a friend from my formal epistemology reading group and I spent an embarassingly long time arguing past each other about it, so now I want to see it actually argued out properly by two people who know what they're doing. Quick recap in case it's useful: a man in Damascus is told by Death that Death is coming for him. Death is a perfect predictor of where the man will end up. Overnight the man can stay put or flee to Aleppo; he dies if he's in the same city as Death and survives otherwise. Because Death predicts so well, the second the man provisionally settles on a plan -- say "I'll flee to Aleppo" -- updating on that plan tells him Death will be in Aleppo too, which makes fleeing look like the losing move and staying look better, but settling on staying flips the same way in reverse. So neither pure plan is what decision theorists call ratifiable: there's no stable fixed point among the non-randomized options. Gibbard and Harper's own proposed fix is that the man should flip a coin, since a genuinely randomized 50/50 plan is apparently the one thing that doesn't unravel like this.

Write me a dialogue, maybe 800-1200 words, between two causal decision theorists standing with the man at the city gate. Both fully accept the causal framework and both agree that neither pure act is ratifiable -- I don't want a strawman on either side, they should be equally sharp -- but they draw opposite morals from that shared premise. One thinks the unratifiability result is exactly why the man is now rationally required to go randomize before he does anything else. The other thinks demanding ratifiability here quietly smuggles in the very "update your credence on the choice you haven't made yet" move that causal decision theory is supposed to rule out, and that it's perfectly fine for the theory to just output a tie between staying and fleeing with no further requirement tacked on -- and, they'd add, if Death is really a perfect predictor, wouldn't she predict the coin flip too, so what does the randomizing actually buy him? I want genuine back-and-forth where each one presses on the other's specific move rather than just restating their own view.
