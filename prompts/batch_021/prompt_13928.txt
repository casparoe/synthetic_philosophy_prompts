And here is my friend's answer, word for word:

"Fitch's paradox starts from two claims that look individually very plausible. The knowability principle (KP) says every truth is at least possibly known: for any p, if p is true then it's possible that someone knows p. The non-omniscience thesis (NonO) says we aren't omniscient: there's at least one true proposition nobody actually knows, i.e. ∃p(p ∧ ¬Kp).

To see that KP and NonO can't both be true, take the particular unknown truth NonO guarantees and call it p, so p ∧ ¬Kp is true. Since KP applies to every proposition, it applies to this conjunction too, so it must be possible for someone to know the whole conjunction: ◇K(p ∧ ¬Kp). But knowledge distributes over conjunction, so anyone who knew (p ∧ ¬Kp) would know p and would know that ¬Kp, i.e., know that nobody knows p. Since knowledge is factive, knowing that nobody knows p guarantees that nobody actually knows p. So anyone who knew (p ∧ ¬Kp) would have to both know p and not know p — a contradiction. Because this reasoning never depended on which p we picked, it's a necessary truth that (p ∧ ¬Kp) can't be known by anyone, which directly contradicts the ◇K(p ∧ ¬Kp) we got from KP a moment earlier. So KP and NonO are jointly inconsistent.

What the proof ultimately establishes is that KP, combined with the obvious fact that we aren't omniscient, is inconsistent, so holding onto KP forces the absurd conclusion that every truth is already known by someone. The reason this happens is that p itself — not some derivative or exotic proposition, but the very unknown truth we started with — turns out to be unknowable given KP: since assuming p is both true and unknown leads straight to contradiction, p can never, even in principle, be known once KP is on the table. That's exactly why the argument is so damaging for verificationists and semantic anti-realists in the tradition associated with Dummett, who want to equate truth with idealized verifiability: a principle that looked like a modest, nearly analytic commitment of their own view turns out to be flatly inconsistent with our evident non-omniscience.

Historically, the proof has a strange origin. Fitch didn't come up with it himself; he received it anonymously in 1945 from a referee reviewing a paper of his on the definition of value — a referee later revealed to be Bertrand Russell — and he didn't publish it until 1963, tucked inside that same paper on value theory rather than presented as a standalone result. It was mostly ignored until W. D. Hart resurfaced it in 1979, arguing that it deserved far more attention as a piece of logic than it had received, after which philosophers like Mackie and Routley picked it up as a serious problem for anti-realist theories of truth."
