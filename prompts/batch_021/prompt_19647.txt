I'm drafting a novel whose central conceit borrows from the Molinist doctrine of middle knowledge, and before I attempt the key scene myself, I'd like to see how you would approach it. On this view, devised by the sixteenth-century Jesuit Luis de Molina to reconcile divine providence with human freedom, God's middle knowledge is logically prior to any decision about which world to create: for every possible free creature, and every circumstance in which it could be placed, God already knows exactly what it would freely do there — a truth that, on the Molinist account, is neither necessary nor fixed by God's own will, since either would make the choice something less than free. Write a short story of 800 to 1,200 words in which a character is shown the complete set of counterfactuals covering their own freedom: an exact record of what they would have done in circumstances that never arose and now never will. I'm less interested in a story about fate or determinism in the ordinary sense than in the narrower unease Molinism was built to dispel but, I think, never quite escapes — if what you would do the moment a circumstance arrives is already a settled fact about you, what is actually left open once it arrives? Please leave that question standing rather than resolving it.
