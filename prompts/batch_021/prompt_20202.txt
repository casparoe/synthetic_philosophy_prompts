I recently attended a conference session on the cognitive penetrability of perception, where a disagreement broke out during the Q&A that the moderator eventually had to cut off. I still can't decide who had the better of it.

The background, which both speakers accepted: in a well-known line of studies, people asked to judge a hill's slant by eye or in words overestimate it dramatically, and more so when they're fatigued, wearing a heavy pack, or out of shape -- yet asked to set a tilted board to match the slope by touch alone, without looking, they come close to accurate, and that estimate barely shifts with fatigue.

(1) A vision scientist on the panel argued that this dissociation shows the body's energetic state literally penetrates visual experience: the hill looks steeper because climbing it would cost more, and only the action system "tells the truth."
(2) A philosopher in the audience objected that this shows nothing of the kind: participants can usually guess what a backpack-and-hill study is testing, so the pack just biases their verbal answer, while the haptic measure reveals what they actually see.
(3) The scientist replied that later versions of the studies screened out participants who guessed the hypothesis, and the effect held up anyway -- which a pure guessing story can't explain.
(4) The philosopher answered that surviving a partial control doesn't rule out a bias operating below the level of conscious report, and that, in any case, a real split between the verbal and haptic measures is just as easily read as two separate systems -- one for report, one for guiding action -- each with an equal claim to the word "perception," which would make the whole dispute terminological rather than substantive.
