I'm several drafts into a novel set inside a biogerontology lab, and over dinner with two people who have been advising me on the science and the ethics of it, the conversation turned into a genuine argument I haven't been able to shake since. Renata, a biogerontologist who studies cellular senescence, argued that aging itself deserves to be classified as a disease rather than treated as mere background to the diseases it causes. Owen, a philosopher of medicine who has been checking my chapters for accuracy, pushed back hard, and neither gave an inch. I'd like an outside read on the exchange, since my own verdict keeps flipping.

Reconstructed as closely as I can manage:

(1) Renata: Aging is a single, cumulative process of molecular and cellular damage that raises mortality risk and erodes nearly every physiological function in the body. Something with that profile counts as a disease under any reasonable functional definition; that medicine has never called it one is an accident of history, not a fact about biology.

(2) Owen: On the standard naturalist account in philosophy of medicine — Boorse's, in the jargon — disease is statistically subnormal functioning relative to what's typical for members of your age and sex within the species. Aging happens to everyone who lives long enough, so it cannot be "subnormal" relative to a reference class in which it sets the very baseline for normal. Calling it a disease isn't a discovery about the body; it's a value judgment wearing a lab coat.

(3) Renata: That argument proves too much. Osteoporosis, cataracts, and atherosclerosis are all extremely common with age too, and become close to universal among the very old, yet nobody treats their prevalence as a reason to stop calling them diseases. If high prevalence within an age bracket doesn't disqualify those conditions, it can't be what disqualifies aging either.

(4) Owen: Those conditions still admit exceptions — some ninety-year-olds have dense bones and clean arteries — so they remain statistically subnormal even inside a narrowly age-matched reference class. Aging itself has no such exceptions. Narrow the reference class enough to exclude every aging-related effect and there's no one left inside it: you've defined the aging cohort out of existence rather than found a baseline to measure it against. That's a genuine disanalogy, not a technicality.

(5) Renata: Then the whole account is hostage to how finely we agree to slice reference classes, which is a judgment call dressed up as a biological fact. Biogerontologists increasingly doubt there is a principled line between "normal aging" and "age-related disease" anyway, since both trace back to the same handful of underlying damage processes. A distinction that keeps dissolving on contact with the biology isn't tracking a natural kind.

At that point they both turned to me, and I had nothing useful to offer. Could you go through the exchange move by move, (1) through (5), and say who has the better of it at each step, rather than just naming an overall winner? I'm especially interested in whether Owen's move in (4) actually survives the objection in (3), whether (5) contributes a genuinely new argument or just restates (1), and whether there's a consideration neither of them raises that would help settle this.
