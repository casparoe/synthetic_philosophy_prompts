In the Leibniz seminar I am taking this term, we have just worked through the Discourse on Metaphysics alongside selections from the Theodicy, and something about his moral philosophy strikes me as inconsistent in a way our secondary reading has not addressed. In §13 of the Discourse, Leibniz concedes that the complete concept of an individual substance — Adam, say, or Julius Caesar — already contains every act that substance will ever perform, and that God actualizes that concept only because the entire world-series to which it belongs is the best possible one. Arnauld's reaction to this seems exactly right to me: if my whole biography, sins included, was fixed the instant God preferred "this" concept of me over some ever-so-slightly different one, then I could never have done otherwise, no matter how the story gets dressed up with words like "spontaneity" or "contingency." So is Leibniz's talk of sin, desert, and divine punishment in the Theodicy anything more than moralizing language layered over a necessitarianism no different in substance from the one he attacks in Hobbes and Spinoza?
