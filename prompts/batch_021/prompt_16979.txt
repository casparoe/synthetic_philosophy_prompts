The question, worth 20 points, read as follows: "Aquinas is sometimes summarized as having endorsed tyrannicide. Assess this characterization. In your answer, explain how Aquinas's account of tyranny relates to the Aristotelian classification of regimes he inherits; explain the distinction he draws between a ruler who has seized power without proper title and one who holds power legitimately but exercises it unjustly, and what he takes to follow from this distinction for the permissibility of resisting each; and explain the grounds on which, in Book I of De Regimine Principum, he counsels caution against private individuals taking it upon themselves to remove a tyrant."

The student wrote: "Aquinas's account of tyranny is best understood against the Aristotelian classification of regimes that he inherits and largely accepts. On this scheme, government by one, by a few, or by the many can each be either correct, when it is directed at the common good, or corrupt, when it is directed instead at the private advantage of the rulers: kingship corrupts into tyranny, aristocracy into oligarchy, and polity into democracy. Because kingship is the best of the correct regimes, its corruption, tyranny, is for Aquinas the worst of the deviant ones; concentrating power in a single self-interested ruler does more damage than the corresponding failures of the many-ruled or few-ruled regimes, since there are fewer checks on one man's will.

Within tyranny itself, Aquinas separates two cases that call for different responses. A ruler who has seized power by force or fraud, without any title conferred by the community or ratified by a legitimate superior, is a tyrant by defect of title: since he never possessed genuine authority, acting against him does not upset any legitimate order and is accordingly not sedition. Indeed, in his commentary on the Sentences Aquinas is willing to go further and treat the killing of such a usurper as licit, invoking the Old Testament story of Ehud's killing of Eglon, the Moabite oppressor of Israel, as a scriptural example of tyrannicide carried out with divine approval. The second case is a ruler who holds power legitimately but comes to exercise it tyrannically. Here Aquinas is markedly more cautious. In Book I of De Regimine Principum, he warns that private persons who take it upon themselves to remove such a ruler risk provoking worse cruelty if they fail and, if they succeed, risk teaching would-be usurpers that the charge of 'tyranny' is a usable pretext for seizing power for themselves. The safer and properly ordered remedy, he argues, lies with public authority: whichever body had the right to install the ruler retains the right to restrain or depose him, and where the ruler is answerable to some higher power, the appeal should go there instead; only where there is no higher power at all does Aquinas fall back on the more otherworldly remedy of prayer, since God, who permits tyranny as a punishment for a people's sins, can also convert or remove the tyrant.

Since Aquinas restricts legitimate action against an entrenched tyrant to public authority rather than private initiative, his considered view leaves the community, as distinct from its formal institutions, with no legitimate avenue against such a ruler beyond enduring him or praying for his conversion or removal. This is a considerably narrower position than the blanket endorsement of tyrannicide sometimes attributed to him, and it suggests that the popular summary needs to be qualified rather than simply accepted."
