I spent forty years working in condensed-matter physics; since retiring a couple of years ago I have taken up philosophy of science in earnest. One idea I grew fond of during my career was Popper's suggestion that a theory can be strictly false and yet "closer to the truth" than the one it replaced — that the free-electron model of metals, say, marked a genuine step nearer the truth, and not merely a more convenient fiction. I have since learned that Popper's own attempt to make this precise, via his qualitative definition of verisimilitude in terms of a theory's true and false logical consequences, was shown by Tichý and Miller in 1974 to entail that no false theory can ever have more verisimilitude than another false theory — which seems to leave the notion useless for comparing any two real, imperfect scientific theories. Please describe the most important implications of this technical failure for whether scientists are still entitled to describe theory change as progress toward the truth, and for how much of that intuitive idea survives in the verisimilitude literature that followed.
