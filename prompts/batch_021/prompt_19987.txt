So I'm taking a legal history seminar this semester and we just spent two sessions on this huge rift in the antebellum abolitionist movement over how to read the U.S. Constitution, and I honestly cant stop chewing on it. Garrison and Wendell Phillips took the position that the document was pro-slavery through and through, pointing to the three-fifths clause, the fugitive slave clause, and the 1808 slave-trade clause as proof, and famously called it "a covenant with death, and an agreement with hell" -- which is why Garrison literally burned a copy of it at a rally. Douglass started out agreeing with all this (he says so himself in his later autobiographies), but around 1850-51 he broke with Garrison after getting won over by Gerrit Smith and Lysander Spooner's arguments, and spent the rest of his career insisting that the Constitution's actual text, read on its own terms and apart from what individual framers privately intended, contains no explicit sanction of slavery at all. His 1860 Glasgow speech is basically the definitive version of this case, where he calls the document, rightly interpreted, a "GLORIOUS LIBERTY DOCUMENT."

My professor basically presented this as Douglass simply being right and Garrison being a purist who let his rhetoric run away from him, but a few of us in the study group afterward were pushing back on that a bit. Douglass's reading leans alot on background natural-law purposes and what the Constitution "ought" to mean given its preamble, rather than on the plain text or what people at the time actually understood it to permit -- which sounds a lot more like a living-constitutionalist move than a textualist one, even though Douglass usually gets framed as the more rigorous close-reader of the two. Garrison's case, whatever you think of the disunionist tactics that came with it (refusing to vote, wanting the free states to just secede), doesn't require you to read past the compromises that are, pretty undeniably, baked into the document.

Can you write me an essay that actually wrestles with this instead of splitting the difference? Lay out the strongest version of both the Garrisonian "pro-slavery compact" reading and the Douglass/Spooner "anti-slavery document" reading of the pre-Reconstruction Constitution, and then commit to an answer: on the interpretive merits alone, bracketing which position had the better real-world political consequences, which reading do you actually think holds up, and why?
