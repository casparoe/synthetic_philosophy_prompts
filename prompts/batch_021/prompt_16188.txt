Question (10 pts): The COMPAS/ProPublica dispute is often summarized as "both sides were technically right, they were just using different fairness metrics." (a) Name the two metrics at stake and say which side invoked which. (b) State the formal result explaining why COMPAS couldn't satisfy both at once, including exactly what has to be true about the two groups for the impossibility to actually kick in. (c) Give one thing this result does NOT show.

Student's answer: (a) ProPublica's investigation found that among defendants who did NOT reoffend, Black defendants were flagged "high risk" way more often than white defendants, and among those who DID reoffend, white defendants were more often flagged "low risk" -- so COMPAS violated equalized odds. Northpointe's response was that COMPAS was calibrated: within each risk decile, Black and white defendants actually reoffended at close to the same rate, so the score meant the same thing regardless of race. (b) Kleinberg/Mullainathan/Raghavan (and separately Chouldechova) proved this wasn't a coincidence: whenever the two groups have different base rates for the outcome, no risk score can have equal accuracy across groups AND equal false positive/negative rates at the same time, unless its a perfect predictor. Since Black and white defendants in the COMPAS data have different rearrest base rates, ProPublica and Northpointe were basically always going to find different things, no matter how the tool got tuned. (c) It doesn't show COMPAS is actually a good tool to use for bail/sentencing decisions -- thats a separate question about whether recidivism-risk prediction should factor into those decisions at all. It also shows that the "which metric is fair" debate is basically arbitrary, since you literally can't satisfy every definition at once -- there's no principled way to say Northpointe or ProPublica had the more "correct" notion of fairness, it just comes down to which one you personally care about more.
