The question, worth 25 of the exam's 100 points, read:

"Reconstruct Kim's causal exclusion argument against non-reductive physicalism in premise-conclusion form, being careful to distinguish the causal closure principle from the exclusion (no-systematic-overdetermination) principle. Then explain Stephen Yablo's determinable/determinate response to the argument, and assess whether it succeeds in securing the causal relevance of mental properties for the non-reductive physicalist, or whether it merely relocates rather than solves the exclusion problem."

Here is the student's answer, transcribed exactly as submitted:

"Kim's argument can be reconstructed as follows. (1) Distinctness: non-reductive physicalism holds that mental properties, although realized by physical properties, are not identical with them -- this is what separates the view from type identity theory. (2) Causal closure: every physical event that has a cause at a time has a sufficient physical cause at that time; physics never needs to appeal outside itself to explain a physical occurrence. (3) Exclusion: a single event cannot have two distinct sufficient causes at the same time unless it is a genuine case of overdetermination, and mental-to-physical causation is not plausibly like that. (4) Mental causal efficacy: mental properties really do cause physical effects (behavior, further mental states, and so on) -- this is the pretheoretical assumption non-reductive physicalism is trying to preserve. From (1)-(3), Kim derives that whenever a mental property M appears to cause a physical effect P*, its physical realizer is already sufficient for P*; given (3), M cannot also independently cause P* without collapsing into overdetermination, so M's causal role is screened off unless M just is a physical property -- which (1) forbids. Non-reductive physicalism is therefore caught in a dilemma: keep distinctness and lose causal efficacy (epiphenomenalism), or keep efficacy and lose distinctness (reduction).

Yablo's response tries to defuse this by denying that mental and physical properties are ever really rivals for the same causal job. He borrows the determinable/determinate relation familiar from properties like color -- scarlet is a determinate of the determinable red -- and applies it to the mind-body case by treating the neurophysiological realizer as the determinable and the mental property as its determinate, so that being in this particular pain state is simply one specific way of satisfying the broader physical description. Because a determinate is not an independent cause competing with its own determinable, invoking both is not overdetermination, and (3) is simply never triggered: the mental and the physical were never two sufficient causes of the same effect in the first place, only one cause described at two levels of grain. If this is right, Yablo's strategy succeeds by weakening the causal closure principle itself, since it shows that some physical effects have their most proportionate cause at the mental rather than the physical level, so physical closure no longer holds without remainder. I'd stop short of saying Yablo fully escapes Kim's challenge, though, since showing that mental and physical properties don't compete in the way two independent sufficient causes would is a different achievement from showing that the mental determinable, rather than its physical determinate, is genuinely the more proportionate cause -- and it's really the second, stronger claim that Kim's challenge requires."
