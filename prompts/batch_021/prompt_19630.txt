I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and I've been asked to toast Dana Kessler at her retirement dinner. Dana has been our standards editor for twenty-eight years, and she enforced, without exception, a genuine knowledge norm of assertion: not "reasonably believed," not "well justified," not even "true, so far as we can tell" -- if a reporter couldn't be said to know a claim, it didn't run. Newsroom lore holds that she once spiked a story her own reporter had gotten exactly right, because the source had, as she put it, "gotten it right by accident" -- about as close to a live Gettier case as a newsroom will ever produce -- and a wire service scooped it two days later. I got tapped for the toast because my beat means I'm the one person in the room who talks to philosophers for a living, and roughly half the tables actually are philosophers, since Dana spent her last decade co-teaching a seminar on assertion and the news with the philosophy department. She has asked, in writing, not to be toasted as though she was always right -- only as though the rule was. Write a five-minute toast built around the Gettier anecdote: it should read as affection, not a lecture, to the newsroom half of the room, while not flattening, for the philosophers, what a knowledge norm actually costs against the truth-norm and belief-norm alternatives.
