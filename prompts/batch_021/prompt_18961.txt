I've been researching Thomas Hobbes's mathematics for a piece on how deep philosophical commitments can generate equally deep, stubborn errors. The case in point is Hobbes's decades-long insistence that he had squared the circle, a claim John Wallis spent a quarter-century publicly demolishing without ever extracting a concession. Behind the error lies a specific epistemology: Hobbes held that geometry alone yields genuine certainty because we ourselves construct the figures whose properties we then prove — his "maker's knowledge" argument — and he wanted civil philosophy to rest on the same footing, since a commonwealth, too, is something built rather than discovered. Please compare this "maker's knowledge" argument with the more general, much later idea of mathematical constructivism, as in Brouwer's intuitionism: where do the two positions genuinely converge, and where does the comparison break down?
