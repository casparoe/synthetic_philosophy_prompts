Ever since I first came across the old paradox of the heap — remove one grain of sand from a heap and it is surely still a heap, yet repeat this enough times and you are left with a single grain that is obviously not one — I have found myself unable to let it go. Having no formal training in philosophy, I have spent a good deal of my spare time over the last year reading around the subject on my own, and I eventually discovered that philosophers of language have developed several rival technical solutions to this puzzle, of which two seem to be the most discussed: epistemicism, associated above all with Timothy Williamson, and supervaluationism, associated with Kit Fine and defended at length by writers like Rosanna Keefe. I understand the basic shape of both positions now, but the more I read, the more it seems to me that each one purchases its solution at a cost its proponents tend to understate, and I would like a genuinely rigorous outside opinion rather than another encyclopedia-style summary.

As I understand epistemicism, it holds that predicates like "heap," "bald," and "tall" really do have an exact, razor-sharp cutoff — some precise number of grains, or a specific hair, or a specific millimeter — except that competent speakers can never know where that cutoff falls, because Williamson ties this ignorance to a general "margin for error" requirement on knowledge: our discriminatory and linguistic practices are simply not fine-grained enough to track a boundary that thin. What troubles me about this view is that nothing in how we actually use the word "heap" seems to single out grain number 4,127 rather than 4,128, so I do not see where such an exact fact could come from in the first place. Supervaluationism, by contrast, holds that a vague predicate comes with a whole range of equally legitimate ways of sharpening it into something precise, and that a sentence counts as true only if it comes out true on every one of those sharpenings — so "this is a heap or it is not" remains true, since that holds under any sharpening you pick, even though, for a genuinely borderline pile, neither disjunct is by itself true or false. What troubles me about this view is the problem of higher-order vagueness that I keep running into in my reading: if there is a blurry zone between the clear heaps and the borderline cases, then surely the edges of that blurry zone are just as vague as the edges of "heap" itself, so stacking up "definitely" operators on top of one another seems only to relocate the original problem rather than dissolve it.

Could you write me an essay that lays out the strongest version of each position, takes seriously the objections I have raised against them (correcting me if I have misstated either position), and then actually comes down on one side, or specifies clearly the conditions under which one view should be preferred over the other? I would much rather receive a real, defended verdict, even a heavily qualified one, than a conclusion that simply leaves both options standing as equally reasonable.
