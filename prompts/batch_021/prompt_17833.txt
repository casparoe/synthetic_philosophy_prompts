I spent my working life as a field biologist studying corvid cognition, and now that I'm retired I've been reading my way through early modern philosophy for fun -- currently stuck on Descartes and animal minds. Everyone flattens him down to "animals are just clockwork," which bugs me a bit, because its actually a fairly careful two-part argument (the language test, plus reason as a "universal instrument" where organs need a particular setup for each task), and from what I can tell it was aimed squarely at people like Montaigne, who in the Apology for Raymond Sebond had already piled up specific cases -- Chrysippus's dog working out a disjunctive syllogism at a crossroads, a fox gauging the thickness of river ice by ear -- to argue the human/animal line is one of degree, not of kind.

Could you write me a dialogue between the two of them actually hashing this out? I know they never met -- Montaigne died four years before Descartes was even born, though Descartes had certainly read the Essais -- so just treat it like one of those old imagined "dialogues of the dead." I want Montaigne pressing his real examples rather than just "animals are clever," and Descartes giving his actual reasons rather than looping back to "they're automata," including being honest about the murkier question of whether his view really commits him to animals feeling nothing at all. Neither one should win just because the other runs out of things to say -- I want real back and forth, each landing points the other actually has to deal with.
