I've been working through the literature on "hard choices" for a seminar paper, and I'd like to see the central technical dispute worked out in dialogue rather than in its more popularized form. Ruth Chang argues that some pairs of alternatives are related by "parity": genuinely comparable, yet neither better, worse, nor precisely equal to one another. She motivates this fourth relation with a small-improvement argument. Take a hard choice that resists ranking -- say, between a secure, well-paid position at a large firm and a precarious but personally meaningful career as a composer -- and suppose the firm sweetens its offer slightly, with a modest raise that wouldn't obviously settle the matter on its own. If the original two options had merely been vaguely or imprecisely equal, the thought goes, a sweetener like this should eventually be enough to tip a merely rough tie into a determinate advantage for the job, much as nudging one of two roughly-equal-looking piles of sand should eventually make one determinately bigger. But intuitively, the sweetened offer still seems merely on a par with the composer's life rather than better than it, and this holds up across a whole range of possible sweeteners, not just one borderline case. Chang takes this to show that parity cannot be reduced to vague or imprecise equality and must be treated as its own primitive relation. A rival, more deflationary tradition -- associated especially with John Broome, though defended by others too -- holds that comparability failures of this kind are simply a species of ordinary vagueness, and that the small-improvement argument doesn't force anything beyond that, typically by pointing to features like higher-order vagueness, or to the fact that we should never expect to locate a precise threshold at which "roughly equal" gives way to "determinately better."

Write a dialogue between two philosophers -- one who defends parity as a genuine, irreducible fourth relation, and one who thinks the vagueness account handles hard choices without positing anything new -- working through this case, or a comparably concrete substitute of your choosing. Neither should simply restate their opening position: the vagueness theorist needs an actual answer to the small-improvement argument, not just an assertion that vagueness can cover it, and the parity theorist needs to say something real in response to the charge that parity is doing no more explanatory work than a label for our uncertainty. Let the dialogue end wherever the exchange actually leaves it, rather than forcing a tidy resolution.
