A friend and I spent most of last night arguing about whether the Condorcet Jury Theorem gives us any real reason to trust majority verdicts — juries, hiring committees, online polls — over the judgment of a single expert. My friend's position was that once each voter is even slightly more likely than not to be right, the majority becomes almost certain to be correct as the group grows, so we should generally defer to it. I argued that this leans on idealizations that real committees rarely satisfy — independent voters, competence reliably above one-half, sincere rather than strategic voting — and that the guarantee is far more fragile than the slogan suggests: push competence just under one-half, for instance, and the theorem's conclusion flips, so that the larger the group, the more likely the majority is to be wrong. We never quite settled the matter.

Could you write a short dialogue between two social epistemologists serving on a hiring committee that has just reached a split decision by majority vote — one mounting the strongest case for treating the vote as epistemically privileged on Condorcet grounds, the other pressing on exactly which assumptions are doing the work and what happens once they fail? I would like both characters to be equally sharp, genuinely responding to each other's points rather than talking past one another.
