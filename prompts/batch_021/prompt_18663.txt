A friend of mine is extremely confident in her ability to size up new acquaintances within minutes of meeting them, and she has recently started acting on that confidence in a fairly consequential way, using her snap judgments to decide which people to bring on as partners in a small business she is starting. When I asked how she knows her instincts can be trusted, she explained that she has been informally tracking this for years: the people she pegged early on as dependable have, in the great majority of cases, gone on to prove dependable, while the ones she distrusted have gone on to reveal themselves as flaky or dishonest, and this track record is what convinces her that her snap judgments are sound. The complication is that when I pressed her on how she decided, after the fact, whether someone had "proven" dependable or not, it turned out that this determination was almost always just her own later impression of the person, rather than anything checked against an independent record, such as a background check, a mutual acquaintance's account, or some hard outcome that did not simply come down to her own read of the situation.

Something about this reasoning nags at me, even though each step she describes sounds sensible taken on its own, and I have had trouble pinning down exactly what, if anything, is wrong with the overall argument. I am not looking for generic advice about learning to trust one's gut; I would like to know whether there is a genuine flaw in the structure of how she is arguing for her own reliability here, and if so, what that flaw is.
