Since a bout of long-COVID left me with persistent parosmia, roses now smell like burning plastic and coffee like sewage, and I can no longer trust my own nose to report what anything actually smells like. As a professional perfume critic, this has turned an old puzzle in aesthetics, sometimes discussed under the heading of aesthetic testimony, into a genuine occupational hazard. The so-called acquaintance principle holds that no one can be justified in judging a scent, or any artwork, as beautiful, crude, or subtle unless they have experienced it firsthand; yet my own firsthand experience is now the least trustworthy evidence available to me, and I increasingly rely on colleagues' descriptions to write reviews I still have to sign my name to. I would like a short story that takes this dilemma seriously: a perfumer, critic, or apprentice "nose" whose own senses have become unreliable, forced to decide whether a public aesthetic verdict should rest on a distorted firsthand impression or on a trusted colleague's secondhand account. Please resist any tidy ending in which the character's sense of smell is simply restored. I am more interested in a story willing to sit with the possibility that direct acquaintance is not quite the reliable gold standard for aesthetic knowledge we ordinarily assume it to be.
