I'm about a third of the way into a novel that follows two conscripted soldiers on opposite sides of a border war I made up on purpose, so people argue about the characters and not about which real country was in the right (working title is bad, I'll fix it once I like the ending). I mentioned the premise at dinner last week and accidentally set off a whole argument between my friend Marcus, who did two tours overseas and now teaches high school history, and my housemate Noor, who's finishing a philosophy PhD on the ethics of war. It went on long enough that my food got cold, and I wrote down as much as I could remember after because it was too good to lose, but I honestly cant tell who came out ahead.

(1) Marcus opened by saying a soldier on the invading side who follows the rules of engagement and doesn't target civilians isn't a murderer — he's doing the same job under fire as the guy defending his own country, and the people who sent him are the ones with something to answer for, not him. (2) Noor said that can't be right: if the war itself is unjust, the invading soldier is aiming lethal force at people who have every right to be there and every right to shoot back, so he isn't actually acting in self-defense, he's the threat — and following a rulebook about how you kill someone doesn't make the killing permitted, any more than a burglar who's scrupulously polite about it stops being a burglar. (3) Marcus came back with: you're assuming a soldier can know, in the moment, which side is unjust, when historians spend decades arguing about exactly that — you can't hold someone to a standard he had no realistic way of meeting in the field — and if invading soldiers have no permission to fight at all, they've got nothing left to lose by breaking the rules altogether, so treating both sides as equally bound is what actually keeps wars from turning into massacres. (4) Noor answered that not being able to know you're on the unjust side might excuse you personally, but that's a question about blame, which is separate from whether the killing was permitted in the first place — and the incentives point is really an argument about what the law of war should punish to stop things spiraling, not about the deeper morality of who's allowed to kill whom, so you can consistently want both sides prosecuted under the same rules while denying they had an equal moral right to fight to begin with. (5) Marcus's last word was that a morality soldiers supposedly can't apply until historians settle the question decades later isn't much of a guide to anyone actually holding a rifle, so maybe that's the wrong kind of morality to be reaching for here.

Neither of them budged and by then i was mostly just refilling glasses.
