A couple weeks back one of these detoured into something called the "no-trade theorem," which I gather traces back to a Milgrom and Stokey paper from the early 80s and leans on an older result of Aumann's about why two honest Bayesians who share a common prior supposedly can't knowingly agree to disagree. I tried reading the original paper and the notation lost me almost immediately, which is a little embarrassing given that I spent my whole career staring at error bars for a living.

What I can't shake is the intuition, or rather my failure to get one. As best I can tell, the claim is that if you start from some efficient allocation and two rational people share a common prior, then the mere fact that it becomes common knowledge each one wants to trade with the other -- on the basis of some private information -- should already tell each of them enough to update away any expected gain, so any edge from trading purely on private information should evaporate before a single share changes hands. If thats right it seems totally wild to me, since actual markets move enormous volume every day on exactly this kind of asymmetric information. So either real traders fail to be rational/common-knowledge-y in whatever precise sense the theorem needs, or I've mangled the claim somewhere. Could you walk me through the gears of why the result is supposed to hold in the first place -- the "aha," not the measure theory -- and then get into which particular assumption is doing the work when people wave this away as a reason real markets shouldn't be as chatty as they obviously are? I'd also be curious what you think it's supposed to tell us, epistemically, about how much a market price can be trusted as a stand-in for the sum of everything all the traders secretly know, versus just being Tuesday's opinion with extra steps, before I loose the thread entirely and just defer to whatever Wikipedia says.
