I am working on a piece that traces classic just-war reasoning into the era of automated combat, and I keep returning to G. E. M. Anscombe's 1956 pamphlet "Mr Truman's Degree." There she opposed Oxford's decision to award Harry Truman an honorary degree, arguing that the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki amounted to murder — the intentional killing of innocents that no amount of consequentialist bookkeeping about lives saved could excuse. The argument rested on the doctrine of double effect and on the theory of intentional action she set out in her 1957 book Intention, especially her insistence that the real object of an act cannot be relabelled away by appeal to some further good end it serves. Anscombe died in 2001, well before lethal autonomous weapons systems — which identify and engage targets without any human choosing the specific individual to be killed — became a live policy question. Working strictly from her own concepts rather than generic just-war talking points, how do you think she would have judged such systems, and would she even grant that the machine, as opposed to whoever designed or deployed it, can be said to "intend" anything at all?
