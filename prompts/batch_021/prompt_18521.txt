I'm drafting a historical novel whose protagonist adheres, without exception, to Augustine's position that no lie is ever permissible—not to protect the innocent, not even in jest. To render her reasoning convincingly rather than as a caricature, I'd like help untangling a specific wrinkle in his account. Across *De Mendacio* and *Contra Mendacium*, Augustine defines a lie, in effect, as an assertion of what one believes false, made so that another will believe it; on this basis he still condemns even a lie told purely as a joke, yet he seems to treat openly fictional discourse—fables, stage plays—as exempt from the charge of lying altogether, since neither author nor audience is asserting that the events depicted really happened. My own novel, however, uses a "found-manuscript" conceit and a documentary style meant to make readers forget, for long stretches, that they are reading an invention rather than a record. Could you explain how Augustine's exemption for fiction is meant to work, and then help me reason through whether it can really extend to fiction as immersive and fact-mimicking as mine, or whether, by his own lights, that kind of writing collapses back into a lie?
