I run a small driving school and have spent almost twenty years teaching people to drive, so when my city put out a call for a community seat on its newly formed Autonomous Vehicle Safety Advisory Board, a few of my longtime students talked me into applying, and rather to my own surprise I was appointed. I have no formal background in philosophy, law, or game theory — most of what I know outside of cars comes from a couple of years spent reading AI safety forums in the evenings, out of plain curiosity about whether these systems can be trusted with high-stakes decisions. That is where I first came across an idea I cannot stop thinking about now that a real version of it has landed on the board's agenda: the "commitment race" problem.

Here is the situation. One of the two robotaxi operators in our pilot program, a company called Solace Mobility, has asked the board to approve a software update that makes its vehicles "assertive" at intersections and merges that don't have stop signs or lights to settle who goes first. Rather than defaulting to caution, as the fleet does now, an approaching car would commit itself to proceeding through the junction and advertise that commitment a couple of seconds ahead of time — over vehicle-to-vehicle radio to other connected cars, and with a distinct exterior light pattern for human drivers and cyclists. Solace's own data shows this cuts rider trip times noticeably, and the company argues it is actually safer overall, since the fleet's current caution invites human drivers to cut in front of these cars in ways that produce hard braking and near misses. Two engineers on the board raised the objection that worries me most. The whole safety case for the update depends on the commitment being credible and irreversible, which is exactly what would leave no room for negotiation if a second such vehicle — a future Solace car with a software fault, or a rival's car running a similar feature — reached the junction at the same moment, since neither could back down without the feature failing at its one job. A broadcast, machine-verified commitment, they added, removes the hesitation and body-language bluffing that quietly keeps ordinary encounters between human drivers from becoming collisions nearly as often as the underlying logic would predict. Our board's traffic economist raised a further, uncomfortable point: even if the feature works exactly as advertised, the other operator in our pilot will have a strong incentive to request the same capability within months rather than be the company whose cars always yield — at which point neither fleet could unilaterally return to caution even if both operators privately preferred it. The board is now split down the middle between treating this as a genuine safety improvement over a status quo in which our cars are routinely taken advantage of, and treating it as manufacturing, feature by feature, exactly the standoff that ordinary right-of-way law exists to prevent. I have been asked to circulate a short written recommendation before next Tuesday's meeting.

Could you draft that memo for me? I would like it to state plainly whether the board should approve the update, reject it, or approve it with specific conditions, with a timeframe attached, and to walk through the reasoning in terms the rest of the board — none of whom has heard the phrase "commitment race" — could actually follow and act on. Please take Solace's efficiency and safety argument seriously rather than waving it away, since simply banning assertiveness does not obviously make anyone safer while human drivers keep exploiting cautious cars; address whether approving the feature for one operator effectively forces the board's hand with the other; and say something concrete about whether any design requirement or regulatory condition could capture the benefit Solace is describing without recreating the standoff risk the engineers flagged.
