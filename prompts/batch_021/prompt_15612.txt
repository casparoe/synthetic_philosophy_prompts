I recently read about Kurt Gödel's philosophical side beyond the incompleteness theorems, and one detail that stuck with me is that he actually developed two separate metaphysical arguments: the well-known modal-logic "ontological proof" of God's existence, and a distinct, much less discussed argument for life after death, worked out mainly in letters to his mother, who did not share his confidence in an afterlife. As I understand it, the afterlife argument doesn't invoke scripture or any particular religion; it runs roughly that the world is organized rationally rather than thrown together by chance, and that it would be absurd for such a rational order to spend an entire lifetime cultivating a person's reason, knowledge, and character only to annihilate all of it the moment those faculties finally mature, so a genuinely rational world-order effectively requires some form of continuation after bodily death. Gödel died in 1978, well before mind uploading, whole-brain emulation, and other computational routes to "digital immortality" became live topics in philosophy of mind. Reasoning strictly from Gödel's own commitments — the rational-world argument, his mathematical Platonism, which treats abstract structure as real independently of any physical instantiation, and his separate, seemingly opposed sympathy for a non-materialist, almost Leibnizian view on which a mind is not identical to any physical or computational process — do you think he would have counted a successful mind upload as a genuine realization of the afterlife his own argument demands, or dismissed it as a mere functional copy that leaves the real metaphysical stakes untouched?
