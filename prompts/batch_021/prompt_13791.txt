I sat on a graduate fellowship committee this past spring, and an exchange that took place there has stayed with me longer than I expected. We had narrowed the field to two finalists who were, on paper, equally strong. One had grown up with considerable advantages — stable, well-educated parents, excellent schools, no financial worries — and had simply done what was expected of him at each stage. The other had grown up in real hardship and had, by every account, forced her way to the same level of achievement through sheer tenacity and self-discipline. Most of my colleagues wanted to give the second candidate the edge, on the grounds that her accomplishment was more genuinely hers, earned through grit rather than simply handed to her by circumstance.

One committee member dissented, and, as the resident philosopher, I was expected to have something useful to say. His objection was this: the very tenacity we wanted to reward is itself just another feature she happened to end up with — a product of temperament and upbringing no less than the first candidate's comfortable home life was a product of his. Push the story back far enough, he said, and neither candidate did anything to become the kind of person capable of that achievement, so rewarding grit over privilege just amounts to rewarding one piece of good luck over another while dressing it up as desert. A few of us objected that choosing, moment to moment, to exercise one's self-discipline is surely different from simply inheriting a comfortable environment. But he kept pushing the same question back a level further, asking where our capacity to make those choices well had itself come from, until none of us had a clean answer left, and I was reduced to mumbling something about practical necessity rather than principle. It occurred to me afterward that this is close to a puzzle I sometimes pose to my own ethics students in the abstract, though I had never seen it argued out with such real stakes attached, and I was considerably less prepared for it than I would like to have been.

I would value your take on it, partly for its own sake and partly so that I acquit myself better next time it comes up. Is there a principled way of maintaining that effort and self-discipline are more genuinely earned, and hence more praiseworthy, than a comfortable upbringing? Or does my colleague's line of questioning generalize into something that ought to unsettle our ordinary practices of praising and rewarding people for anything at all, not merely in cases like this one?
