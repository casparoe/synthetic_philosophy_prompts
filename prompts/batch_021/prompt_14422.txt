One thing that has stuck with me from the old Overcoming Bias and LessWrong threads on disagreement is the dichotomy built into Aumann's agreement theorem: two Bayesians who share a common prior cannot knowingly sustain a disagreement once their posteriors are common knowledge, so any persistent, honest disagreement has to trace back to either asymmetric private information or a violation of the common prior assumption. Robin Hanson leaned on this to argue that most real-world disagreement is evidence of hidden irrationality or self-deception rather than a legitimate difference in starting points.

What I've never seen is a genuinely convincing example of the second horn, since a difference in priors that traces to upbringing or life history can usually just be redescribed as a difference in private information instead. Construct a concrete case involving two agents -- both honest, both fully aware of each other's reasoning and evidence -- whose persistent disagreement about some claim is best explained by an irreducible difference in priors, not by any information asymmetry between them. Briefly explain why your example resists being redescribed as an information difference, and what this suggests about whether rationality alone requires a common prior.
