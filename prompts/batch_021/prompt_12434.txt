I'm putting together a session for my graduate seminar on philosophy of mind and machine learning, and I want a dialogue that pushes students past "stochastic parrot" sloganeering and into the actual methodological weeds of mechanistic interpretability. The occasion is a specific kind of finding that keeps coming up in that literature. Researchers using causal-tracing methods — systematically corrupting and then selectively restoring internal activations to see which components are causally responsible for a model completing a piece of factual recall, e.g. something like "The Eiffel Tower is located in ___" — have argued that particular mid-layer components function as something like key-value stores for individual facts, which is naturally read as evidence that the network really represents the fact at a locatable site. But subsequent work that instead tries to edit that same fact by directly modifying weights has found that the layers where editing is most effective often do not line up with the layers causal tracing flags as causally responsible. Localizing a fact for purposes of explanation and localizing it for purposes of intervention seem to come apart. This sits against a backdrop in which individual neurons are already known to be polysemantic, with concepts distributed across many units in superposition, so that even the choice of basis in which to look for "the" representation — raw neurons versus some learned dictionary of sparse features — is itself up for grabs.

I'd like you to write a dialogue between two equally sharp philosophers of cognitive science reacting to this case. One should defend continued realism about representation in neural networks: the mismatch just shows that our current localization tools are crude, not that there is no underlying representational fact, and something like Dennett's notion of a "real pattern" — real because it lets us compress and predict the system's behavior better than any rival description — can be invoked to say what the representation would be even if no single method pins it down yet. The other should argue that the mismatch is symptomatic of something deeper: that "the fact is represented here" is at best true relative to a choice of intervention, basis, and explanatory purpose, with no observer-independent fact of the matter underneath all the equally legitimate descriptions. Have them engage directly with the causal-tracing/editing mismatch rather than retreating to slogans — each should be made to spell out what evidence would even count in their favor, and to say exactly where, for them, the inference from "our methods disagree" to "there is or isn't a real representational fact" goes through or breaks down. Neither side should be handed an easy, final win.
