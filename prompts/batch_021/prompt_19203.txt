I spent thirty-some years doing solid-state physics before I retired, and I've been filling the time since by finally reading all the philosophy of science I never got round to. Last month I fell into a structural realism rabbit hole, starting with that line of Poincare's from Science and Hypothesis about how physics never really gets at the things themselves, only the relations between them, and that those relations are exactly what survive when one theory gets swapped out for the next.

From there I read Worrall's old paper reviving the idea with the Fresnel-to-Maxwell story about light, and then Ladyman and French pushing it into something weirder still, "ontic" structural realism, where there arent even any objects sitting behind the relations anymore, just structure, full stop. What's been nagging at me is Newman's old objection to Russell, that "structure" all by itself is nearly empty, since you can slap some structure or other onto pretty much any big enough pile of things, so saying you only know the structure barely tells you anything at all.

Here's what I keep chewing on though: Poincare died in 1912, before quantum mechanics and before general relativity even existed yet, so way before anything came along to really twist the knife on what an "object" is supposed to be (identical particles with no fact about which one is which, spacetime points that seem to have no identity apart from the metric relations they sit in). If he'd somehow lived to see all that, do you think he'd have taken the full plunge into ontic structuralism, objects be damned, or would that older, more Kantian-ish instinct of his about there being some unknowable "something" behind the relations have made him dig his heels in and stay on the epistemic side, Newman's objection or no? I'd like an actual argued answer, tied to what Poincare really thought convention and geometry were doing in physics, not just a "oh he'd have found it fascinating" cop-out.
