I'm about two-thirds through a novel set in Linz in the mid-1610s, and my protagonist is a fictionalized Kepler — I'm using the real biography as scaffolding and inventing the scenes on top of it. There's a chapter I keep rewriting where a skeptical physician corners him at a dinner and asks how a man who spent the afternoon proving the planets move in ellipses can spend his mornings casting horoscopes for coin, and I want Kepler's answer to be something like his actual, weirder position instead of the two boring defaults everyone reaches for ("secret rationalist just cashing checks" or "true believer in the zodiac"). My understanding, cobbled together from a biography, some translated excerpts, and the Stanford Encyclopedia entry (my Latin is nonexistant so I'm entirely at the mercy of other people's paraphrasing), is that he actually drew a real line: the twelve houses and zodiac signs he thought were arbitrary bookkeeping with no physical basis, worth mocking right alongside his critics, but the aspects — the actual angles planets make to each other — he thought were real, because they correspond to the same harmonic ratios he was obsessed with everywhere else in his work, and could act on a soul roughly the way one plucked string sets a nearby one humming.

Here's the paragraph I've drafted for him to actually read aloud at that dinner (he's more or less restating his own real Tertius Interveniens in spirit here, though I'm not reproducing the real text, just gesturing at the argument): "You take me for a defender of almanac-sellers and their beast-shapes nailed up on the sky, and I am not that man. The twelve houses are chalk marks on an empty wall. But when two wandering stars stand at true angle to one another, sextile, trine, or square, there is a sounding between them and the soul of the Earth not unlike the sympathy by which one untouched lute answers another played across the room — that is geometry doing what geometry always does, not superstition. My opponent, in purging the one, purges the other, and leaves the sky mute to spite fools who couldn't hear it rightly anyway." My writing group liked the lute line best in the whole chapter, which worries me a little, since none of them can tell me if its actually earned.

So, comment as harshly as you want: is that two-tier move (junk zodiac vs. real harmonic aspects) a fair compression of his actual position in things like Tertius Interveniens and the astrology book of Harmonices Mundi, or am I flattening something more genuinely ambivalent than that? Also — I've got a line rattling around in my head that I'm pretty sure is his, about astrology being astronomy's foolish daughter who keeps her wise mother fed anyway; is that roughly right, or am I merging two anecdotes that were never actually connected, and is it even making the same point as the lute-and-aspects stuff or a totally different kind of argument? And if there's a stranger, more specific real wrinkle from his actual astrology writing I should be stealing instead of my invented lute metaphor, I'd much rather have that than something that merely sounds plausible.
