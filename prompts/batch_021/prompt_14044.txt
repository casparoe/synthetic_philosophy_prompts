"Adams' Thesis says our confidence in 'if A, then B' should track the conditional probability P(B|A) rather than the probability of some proposition -- this is often written as the Equation, P(A>B) = P(B|A). Stalnaker's possible-worlds semantics (the conditional is true at w iff B holds at the closest A-world to w) was attractive partly because, given some constraints tying the selection function to a rational agent's credences, it seemed to deliver the Equation as a theorem -- so really the two theses are just the same claim dressed up in different vocabulary.

Lewis's triviality argument shows this can't be sustained in general. Take a partition of logical space into A&B, A&¬B, and ¬A, each with positive probability. If you conditionalize on A&B, B becomes certain given A, so by the Equation your new credence in 'A>B' has to jump to 1; conditionalizing on A&¬B instead forces it down to 0. Plugging P(A>B|A&B)=1 and P(A>B|A&¬B)=0 into the law of total probability, and setting the result equal to P(B|A) (as the Equation demands of the original, unconditional credence function), forces P(A>B|¬A) to equal P(B|A) too -- regardless of what you actually think about B given ¬A. Run the same trick on finer partitions and you eventually pin P(B|A) to the unconditional P(B) for pretty much any A and B, i.e. it forces every proposition to be probabilistically independent of every other one, which is clearly unacceptable for any realistic credence function.

Since you can't keep both truth-conditional semantics for '>' and the Equation as a fully general law, Lewis concluded that indicative conditionals must not express propositions at all, and that talk of their 'probability' is really just conditional probability in disguise -- a view worked out in more detail later on by Edgington's suppositional theory."
