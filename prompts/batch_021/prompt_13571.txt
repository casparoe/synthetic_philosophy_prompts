I'm reporting a long-form piece on the science and philosophy of self-control, and the hardest knot I've run into surfaced while reading Donald Davidson's classic paper on akrasia back-to-back with George Ainslie's Breakdown of Will for background. Davidson frames weakness of will as a genuine puzzle: how can an agent, at one and the same time, judge — all things considered — that one course of action is better, and yet intentionally do something else, without severing the ordinary link between judgment and intentional action? Ainslie's hyperbolic-discounting research redescribes that very same "acting against my better judgment" behavior in quite different terms, as a preference reversal produced by a discount curve on which a smaller-sooner reward temporarily overtakes a larger-later one as it draws near, so that there need be no single, self-undermining judgment at all — only a succession of locally coherent preferences, held by different temporal selves, that happen to conflict.

For the piece, I'd like to stage this disagreement rather than merely describe it, as though it were a transcript from a panel I moderated. Could you write a dialogue between a philosopher defending something close to the Davidsonian picture of akrasia and an economist defending the hyperbolic-discounting picture, in which each tries to persuade the other that their own framework is the one that actually captures what's philosophically at stake in weakness of will? I'd like the economist to press the philosopher on why we should posit a single "better judgment" being violated at all, rather than just distinct, temporally located preferences that differ from one another; and I'd like the philosopher to push back on whether the economist's model can accommodate the distinctively normative, present-tense character of akrasia — the sense of doing something one currently, and not merely previously, believes one shouldn't be doing. Please have both interlocutors engage seriously with each other's strongest objections rather than talking past one another, and don't feel obliged to manufacture a tidy resolution if the disagreement turns out to be a genuinely hard one.
