In my graduate seminar this term — cross-listed between the philosophy department and cognitive science — we spent the first several weeks on classical debates about mental representation (Fodor and Pylyshyn's systematicity argument against connectionism, Putnam and Fodor on multiple realizability, Smolensky's tensor-product attempt to rescue distributed representation) before turning to recent empirical work from the "mechanistic interpretability" tradition in machine learning as a kind of test case. The class has split into two camps. One camp holds that results such as superposition, monosemantic features recovered by sparse autoencoders, reusable induction heads, and the apparent universality of certain circuits across independently trained models amount to genuine, first-order evidence that trained neural networks encode structured, decomposable representations — that representational realism has, in effect, been empirically vindicated. The other camp thinks all of this is perfectly compatible with a deflationary reading on which "feature," "circuit," and "representation" are convenient labels researchers project onto an otherwise uninterpreted mass of weights, closer to reading tea leaves than to discovering genuine psychological kinds.

I would like your help turning this disagreement into a structured discussion prompt for next week's session. Below are eight considerations that have come up in our reading, each of which someone might cite as bearing on realism about representations in neural networks:

1. Universality — the recurrence of the same circuits (e.g., induction heads) across independently trained models.
2. The linear representation hypothesis — the success of linear probes, and the claim that high-level concepts correspond to roughly linear directions in activation space.
3. Superposition and polysemanticity — the hypothesis that networks pack more features than they have dimensions into near-orthogonal directions, so that individual neurons end up responding to several unrelated concepts.
4. Causal validation — methods such as activation patching and causal scrubbing, which test whether a candidate feature or circuit is doing real causal work rather than merely correlating with behavior.
5. The grandmother-cell analogy — the parallel with the older local-versus-distributed-coding debate in systems neuroscience.
6. Multiple realizability — the fact that the same input-output behavior can be implemented by very different, non-isomorphic weight configurations.
7. Interpretability illusions — cases in which a direction or neuron that looks cleanly interpretable on one dataset turns out to track something quite different on another.
8. The natural abstraction hypothesis — the idea that any sufficiently capable system trained to predict or act in our world will tend to converge on similar high-level abstractions, because those abstractions are, in some sense, objective features of the environment rather than artifacts of a particular training run.

I know reasonable people, including several of my own colleagues, disagree sharply about how to weigh these, so I am not asking you to reconstruct a consensus view — only your own best-considered ranking, which I intend to put on the board next to a couple of others as a foil for discussion. Please rank all eight considerations by how strongly each one counts in favor of realism about neural network representations (as opposed to instrumentalism, on which "representation talk" is at best a convenient fiction), from strongest support to considerations that, on reflection, actually cut against realism.
