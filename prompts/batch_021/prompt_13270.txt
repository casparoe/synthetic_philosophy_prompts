Last night a friend and I got into a long argument over dinner — fittingly, over a second bottle of wine — about whether Plato was really the enemy of bodily pleasure that most people take him to be. My friend maintained that since Plato's whole picture of the soul is organized around reason mastering the appetites, anything in his work that looks like praise of drinking has to be ironic, or at best a grudging concession. I pushed back with what I remembered from the Laws, where the Athenian Stranger spends a remarkable stretch of Books 1 and 2 persuading a skeptical Spartan (Megillus) and Cretan (Clinias) that regulated group drinking is actually a valuable instrument of civic education. His case builds around a strange thought experiment involving a hypothetical "fear drug," and it eventually issues in specific, legislated drinking ages that culminate in a "chorus of Dionysus" reserved for citizens past about forty. Neither of us could reconstruct the argument well enough on the spot to say whether I was remembering it accurately or reading more coherence into it than is really there, and we never reached a resolution.

I would like you to help settle this properly. Please reconstruct the actual argument, or arguments, the Athenian Stranger gives for the educational value of supervised drunkenness, distinguishing what I suspect are two separable rationales: one built on the analogy between the fear drug (used to test reactions to danger) and wine (used to test, and supposedly cultivate, resistance to pleasure), and another tied specifically to why older citizens need wine's disinhibiting effect to take part in the choral and musical side of education. Explain how these two lines of argument are meant to fit together, and flag anything you consider a genuine weakness in the reasoning — for instance, whether Plato is entitled to move from "wine reveals existing character" to "wine trains character," given how much more deliberately the Spartans are said to train courage through repeated exposure to fear. Finally, since this is really what my friend and I could not agree on: does this comparatively indulgent treatment of pleasure and drunkenness in the Laws amount to a genuine change in Plato's psychology from the sharply hierarchical, tripartite soul of the Republic — where the guardians are insulated from such things rather than deliberately exposed to them — or is it better explained by some other difference between the two dialogues, such as their intended audience or political aims?
