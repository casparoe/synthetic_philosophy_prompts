I spent most of my working life as an evolutionary biologist studying the cichlid species flock of Lake Victoria, and since retiring I have been filling a long-standing gap in my education by reading philosophy of biology, especially the literature on the species problem (Mayr, Kitcher, Ghiselin and Hull, Ereshefsky, de Queiroz). I have drafted the following notes toward a short essay connecting my own fieldwork to that debate, and I would welcome your comments before I take it any further.

Notes toward the essay:

The Lake Victoria cichlid flock is famous for having arisen at almost embarrassing speed: several hundred endemic species, most of that diversity built up within a few tens of thousands of years of the lake's late-Pleistocene desiccation. A great deal of it is maintained by nothing more than female mate choice tracking male breeding colour. Pairs that any field guide would call textbook "good species" — fully sympatric, never seen to hybridize in clear water — will readily produce fertile hybrids in aquaria, and do so in the wild too, wherever agricultural runoff and algal growth have made the water turbid enough that females can no longer tell males of different species apart. In several of the sites I knew well, that is more or less what happened over a couple of decades: distinctions that took tens of thousands of years to build eroded within a human lifetime, and did not obviously re-establish themselves even where the water later cleared, though I will grant the evidence on that last point is thinner than I would like.

I used to file this away as a fact about cichlids. Reading Mayr, I now see it as a fairly direct problem for treating reproductive isolation as the criterion of species status, since here isolation is graded, environment-dependent and reversible rather than a switch that is simply on or off. My inclination is to side with de Queiroz: that "species" just names separately evolving lineages, and that Mayr's criterion, along with the phylogenetic and ecological ones, are best read as different, fallible operational tests for that one underlying process rather than rival definitions of the word — my cichlids being a case where the tests disagree because the process has not gone very far. But I cannot quite square this with Kitcher's pluralism, which as I understand it wants the different classifications to be equally real and simultaneously correct, not competing proxies for a single target. Nor am I sure either view sits comfortably with Hull and Ghiselin's idea of species as individuals, since I do not see how one historical individual can have two correct but non-coincident boundaries.

I would welcome your comments, especially on three things: whether I have represented Mayr, Kitcher, Hull/Ghiselin and de Queiroz accurately; whether the tension I describe in that last sentence is a genuine problem for holding their views together, or one I have manufactured through inexpert reading; and whether this cichlid angle on the species problem is actually distinctive, or a point already well worn in that literature that I am simply late to.
