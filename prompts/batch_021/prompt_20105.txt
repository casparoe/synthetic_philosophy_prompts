For the last several years, alongside my day job covering science and ideas, I've served on the five-person awards committee of the Whitfield Foundation, which each spring confers the Beacon Prize for Public Understanding of Science. It's a modest honor as these things go — a medal, a small cash award, a paragraph in the foundation's annual report — but it's genuinely respected in our corner of the world, and past recipients have mostly been working scientists, documentary producers, and a couple of remarkable teachers. The foundation's charter is unambiguous about the standard: the Prize goes to "the person whose work has done the most, this year, to deepen public engagement with scientific ideas."

This year we're deadlocked over Reyes Calder, a science-podcast host with an enormous following who has, on any fair reading of the record, repeatedly presented other researchers' findings as his own insight, described at least two since-retracted studies as vindicated, and quietly settled a plagiarism claim brought by a science writer. The complication is that a donor closely tied to Calder has offered the foundation twelve million dollars — enough to permanently endow the early-career fellowship that currently supports three young science writers — and has made it unmistakable, without ever quite saying so outright, that the gift depends on Calder receiving this year's Prize. Two of my fellow committee members argue that an institution in our position cannot afford to treat this as a matter of principle: the fellowships are real, the money is real, and a medal is just a medal. The other two argue that a prize awarded for reasons unconnected to the work it exists to certify stops being evidence of anything, whatever we privately tell ourselves about why we did it, and that everyone in our small field will eventually understand exactly what happened. It's the same basic shape as the honorary-degree and donor-naming scandals I keep ending up reporting on, except this time I'm the one who has to make the call rather than just describe it afterward.

As chair, I have to circulate a written recommendation before we meet a week from Thursday, and I'd like your help drafting it. Please give me a clear recommendation with a concrete course of action and time frame, not a summary of both positions — and make sure it treats the fellowship argument as a genuine reason rather than dismissing it as simple corruption, even as it works through whether "we have overwhelming reason to award him the Prize" and "he deserves the Prize" might simply be two different claims, and if they can come apart like that, what follows for what the committee should actually do.
