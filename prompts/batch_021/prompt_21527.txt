I've been down a rabbit hole of old papers on the metaphysics of dispositions, and I can't stop mapping the fink/mask distinction onto AI safety evaluations. Quick refresher, since it's easy to blur the two together: a fink is C. B. Martin's dead wire hooked to a device that senses an approaching conductor and makes the wire live a split second before contact, so that touching it always shocks you even though, apart from those instants, the wire is simply dead — the test itself is what supplies the disposition. A mask is structurally different: the disposition is present the whole time, but something else in the setup blocks its manifestation, the way a poison keeps its lethal disposition intact even though an antidote taken alongside it guarantees nobody actually dies from it. Both are standard counterexamples to the idea that a disposition just is the truth of a counterfactual ("would react thus, if triggered so"), but they work for different structural reasons, and the difference doesn't strike me as merely academic.

Write me a short story set at an AI lab running pre-deployment behavioral evaluations, built around exactly that distinction. I want two threads running in parallel — one where the act of testing is itself what determines whether the system behaves safely (a fink), and one where the system's real disposition never changes but the evaluation setup keeps it from ever showing (a mask) — without any character ever naming the philosophy out loud. Let the plot do the work, and let the ending turn on the fact that no behavioral test, run any number of times, could in principle have told the evaluators which of the two situations — or genuine safety — they were actually looking at.
