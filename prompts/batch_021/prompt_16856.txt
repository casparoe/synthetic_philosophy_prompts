ok so i fell down a von neumann rabbit hole this weekend (started from a footnote in some AI safety blog post, don't judge me) and found two things about the guy that seem to pull in different directions, and it's bugging me like an unresolved merge conflict.

thing 1: apparently the whole "technological singularity" term traces back to a conversation Ulam reported having with von neumann, where von neumann talked about tech progress approaching "some essential singularity in the history of the race" beyond which human affairs as we know them couldn't continue. so this guy basically originated the modern AI-doom vocabulary, in the 1950s.

thing 2: but in his actual 1955 essay "Can We Survive Technology?" he's way less dramatic about it. he splits the danger into three buckets -- weapons, automation, and (wild one) climate control, he apparently thought messing with teh weather could become a geopolitical weapon before anyone else was even talking about that -- and his conclusion is basically: there's no permanent fix, verification/treaty schemes can never really be airtight, and the best you can do is muddle through with patience and flexibility instead of actually stopping or steering the thing.

so if you drop 1955-von-neumann into todays AI x-risk debate, which guy shows up? does the "we might be approaching a singularity we don't survive" guy side with the pause-AI / international-treaty crowd, or does the "verification is never airtight, theres no permanent solution, just adapt" guy think that whole approach is naive, for basically the same reasons he'd have distrusted a nuclear test-ban treaty? walk me through the actual reasoning tying it to his specific arguments, not just a "he was a smart guy who'd be worried" vibe.
