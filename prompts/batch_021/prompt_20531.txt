I'm preparing supplementary material for a graduate seminar on moral responsibility, and this week's sessions are built around P. F. Strawson's "Freedom and Resentment" together with the responses it has provoked over the past six decades. In discussion, my students have a habit of flattening the whole debate into a single slogan — "resentment and gratitude are just natural facts about us, so the metaphysics of determinism is beside the point" — and I would like a dialogue I can hand out that makes that slogan much harder to rest on, by showing two people who genuinely know the terrain disagreeing with each other in detail.

Could you write a dialogue, somewhere in the range of 700 to 1000 words, between two philosophers who both take Strawson extremely seriously but end up on opposite sides of him. The first should be a committed Strawsonian naturalist, who holds that Strawson's own apparatus — the distinction between participant and objective attitudes, together with the ordinary categories of excuse and exemption that modulate our reactive attitudes — is basically enough to dissolve the traditional worry about determinism and responsibility, so that any further "normative question" about whether people really deserve resentment or gratitude in some desert-laden, metaphysical sense is a confusion rather than a live problem. The second should be a contemporary skeptic about basic-desert responsibility, in the spirit of a thinker like Derk Pereboom or Bruce Waller, who is willing to grant Strawson nearly all of his descriptive psychology but denies that description settles anything normatively — and who presses this by way of the kind of case Gary Watson raises in connection with the murderer Robert Harris: when we come to understand, in horrifying causal detail, how someone was made into the person who did monstrous things, that is exactly the sort of consideration Strawson's own framework treats as pulling us toward the objective attitude, and yet our actual practice runs the other way, wanting to hold the worst offenders more answerable, not less. Doesn't that tension suggest that Strawson's machinery cannot, by itself, tell us which attitude is the fitting one?

Please make the two figures evenly matched and have them genuinely engage each other's strongest moves rather than talk past one another: the skeptic shouldn't simply assert that determinism rules out responsibility by fiat, and the naturalist shouldn't be allowed to win just by restating the naturalistic point more forcefully. I would rather you not manufacture a tidy resolution at the end — it's fine, and probably more honest, if the exchange closes with the disagreement still standing.
