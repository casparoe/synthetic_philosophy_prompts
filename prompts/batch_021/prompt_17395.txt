I work on smart-contract design at a small decentralized-finance protocol, and a disagreement in a recent design review has left me wanting a much firmer grip on an idea I had previously only half-understood. We were debating whether two on-chain agents belonging to rival protocols — say, market-making contracts that could either race one another for the same arbitrage opportunity, burning gas and value in the process, or quietly split the proceeds — could be written so that the cooperative outcome becomes rational without an escrow, a trusted intermediary, or repeated interaction to build a reputation, simply because each contract's code is public and can be inspected by the other before it commits to anything. A colleague with an AI-safety background said this is essentially Tennenholtz's original "program equilibrium" proposal, and that the obvious naive implementation — cooperate only if the counterparty's code is byte-for-byte identical to your own — is notoriously brittle, but that a more sophisticated construction exists, built around something called "FairBot" and an appeal to Löb's theorem, which lets two programs guarantee mutual cooperation in a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma without either one ever fully simulating the other.

That last step is where my understanding breaks down. Naively, deciding whether to cooperate by asking "will the other program cooperate with me?" seems like it should force each agent into simulating the other simulating it, and so on without end; yet apparently substituting formal provability for simulation closes this loop in a finite, well-founded way, with Löb's theorem doing the crucial work. I don't have a background in modal or provability logic beyond a rough sense of what Gödel's incompleteness theorems say, so I would appreciate an explanation pitched at roughly that level. Could you walk me through, as intuitively as possible, how the Löbian argument actually blocks the regress rather than merely relocating it, why the trick depends specifically on reasoning about provability rather than about truth or direct simulation, and whether the resulting cooperation guarantee reflects a genuine discovery about self-referential trust or is, in some sense, already smuggled in by the assumptions about what the two programs can prove about one another?
