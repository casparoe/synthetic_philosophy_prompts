Here's the student's answer, copied exactly:

Davidson's radical interpretation is his answer to the question of how one person could ever figure out what another person's words and sentences mean if they're starting totally from scratch, with no dictionary or bilingual speaker around to help. He imagines an interpreter who has access only to a speaker's utterances plus the observable circumstances they're made in, and has to build, from that alone, both a theory of what the speaker believes and a theory of what their sentences mean. The two have to be solved together, because you can't hold belief fixed and read off meaning, or vice versa - if someone assents to a sentence, that could be because they believe what the sentence (correctly interpreted) says, or because they mean something nonstandard by the words. This is basically the same puzzle as Quine's gavagai case, where a field linguist watches a native speaker point at a rabbit and say "gavagai," and can't tell if the word means "rabbit," "undetached rabbit part," or "rabbit-stage" since all three fit the evidence equally - gavagai is really just Davidson's radical interpretation problem shrunk down to a single word instead of a whole language.

To get the whole project off the ground, Davidson brings in the Principle of Charity: the interpreter has to assume the speaker is, on the whole, right about the world, and interpret their sentences so as to maximize the truth of what they come out saying. Without some assumption like that we'd have no way to break into the belief/meaning circle, since literally any utterance could be paired with literally any belief. Charity is also supposed to do anti-skeptical work: since we can only interpret someone by attributing mostly true beliefs to them, it has to be the case that most of anyone's beliefs (including our own) are true, which rules out the possibility that we're radically wrong about the external world the way a Cartesian skeptic worries about.

Where this differs from Quine is that Quine thought translation was indeterminate - there could be two rival translation manuals for a language that disagreed with each other on individual sentences, with no fact of the matter about which one was correct. Davidson avoids this by using Tarski's theory of truth to specify meaning through a T-sentence for every sentence in the speaker's language, which gives radical interpretation a rigorous, determinate structure and closes off the indeterminacy Quine was worried about. So Quine's project basically ends in skepticism about meaning, while Davidson's charity principle plus the truth-theoretic machinery secures a single correct interpretation of a speaker.
