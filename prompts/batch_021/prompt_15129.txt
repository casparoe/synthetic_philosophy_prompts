I'm a third-year philosophy student taking a seminar on formal epistemology this term, and we recently spent two weeks on the lottery paradox and the preface paradox: Kyburg's puzzle about being individually justified in believing, of each ticket in a large fair lottery, that it will lose, even while knowing for certain that some ticket will win, and Makinson's puzzle about an author who is individually justified in believing every sentence of her book, while also being justified, given the ordinary fallibility of any long piece of writing, in believing that the book contains an error somewhere. This year I'm also one of the student members on my university's Honor Council, and a case coming up at our next meeting struck me as a live version of both puzzles at once, so I'd like your help with the memo I've been asked to draft beforehand.

Here is the case. In a 1,100-student introductory statistics course, the final exam was administered remotely with the order of answer choices randomized per student, and the university's statistical consulting office was asked to check for copying by comparing wrong-answer patterns pairwise across the whole class (matching right answers isn't informative, since everyone converges on those independently). Comparing every pair of students yields roughly 600,000 comparisons; the office only flags a pair once the odds against an innocent coincidence exceed 200,000 to 1, and its cover memo notes that even if nobody in the class had copied at all, we should still expect about three pairs to clear that threshold by chance alone. This term the algorithm flagged eleven pairs. The memo states plainly that each of the eleven, taken on its own, clears the Council's "clear and convincing evidence" standard, and just as plainly that the office thinks it likely that a couple of the eleven are innocent coincidences — while insisting it has no way of telling which ones.

The Council is now split down the middle, and not along the usual lines. Half the members want to open cases against all eleven pairs, on the grounds that we don't get to set aside evidence that individually clears our standard just because we can guess that some fraction of the whole set is probably innocent. The other half thinks that opening all eleven, in the full knowledge that doing so will quite likely get at least one innocent student expelled under our single-sanction policy (any finding of responsibility means expulsion, with no lesser penalty available), itself violates the fairness that standard exists to protect, and that we should hold off on all eleven until we have some corroborating evidence that isn't just more output from the same algorithm. Could you draft the short memo I'll circulate before the meeting? It should come down on one concrete policy — both for these eleven pairs and for statistically-flagged cases going forward — with a timeframe for acting on it, and it should take the "each pair individually clears the bar" side seriously rather than waving it off. Separately from the memo, and purely for my own benefit, I'd be curious which of the two paradoxes from class you think this case tracks more closely, if either, and whether that difference should actually change what the Council does.
