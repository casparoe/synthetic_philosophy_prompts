So my lab just had one of those journal-club-nights-that-turns-into-an-argument over the replication crisis, and it's been stuck in my head since. A few of the senior grad students in my social psych program are pushing hard for all of us to ditch t-tests and ANOVAs and move to Bayesian analyses (Bayes factors, JASP, the whole deal), and the argument that basically won the room was this: since Bayesian updating follows the likelihood principle, your posterior (and the resulting Bayes factor) doesnt care why you stopped collecting data or what your original sampling plan was — unlike a p-value, which gets inflated the moment you peek at the data partway through and stop as soon as it looks significant. Optional stopping is supposed to be one of the classic "researcher degrees of freedom" that fed into the replication crisis in the first place, so the reasoning goes: switch to Bayes and that whole problem just evaporates.

That felt almost too good to be true to me, but nobody in the room could really say what was wrong with it, and honestly neither could I. So I wanted to sanity check this with you: is it actually right? Could our lab, in good conscience, check our Bayes factor every few days as data trickles in and just stop whenever we cross something like BF > 3 (or BF < 1/3 for the null), without worrying that we're basically p-hacking with extra steps? And zooming out a bit, is "the replication crisis was fundamentally a frequentist statistics problem" a fair way to put things, or are we giving Bayesian methods credit they havent fully earned yet? Would love your take before I become the person who tells the whole lab we've basically solved this.
