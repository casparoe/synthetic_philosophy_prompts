I'm writing a historical novel centered on Avicenna, and one chapter needs him to confront a case that puts real pressure on his own account of self-awareness. His flying man argument holds that a person created whole and suspended in mid-air, deprived of every sensation of his own body, would still affirm his own existence without a moment's hesitation — proof, he thought, that self-awareness is a permanent feature of the soul, quite independent of the body. For the scene, I want to bring him a patient with what we would now call Cotard's delusion: fully conscious, coherent, and otherwise lucid, yet utterly convinced that he is dead and does not exist. Reasoning strictly in Avicenna's own terms, how might he try to account for such a man? Does the case genuinely embarrass the certainty he claims for self-awareness, or does his own psychology already contain the resources to explain it away — and if so, at what cost to the original argument?
