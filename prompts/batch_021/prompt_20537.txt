A friend and I spent much of a long dinner arguing about whether Zhuangzi and Epicurus are really making the same move against the fear of death in different vocabularies, or whether they are doing two fundamentally different kinds of philosophical work that merely happen to land on a similar attitude. We had both just reread a cluster of passages from the Zhuangzi: the scene in the "Zhile" (至樂) chapter where Huizi finds him drumming on a basin and singing after his wife's death, and Zhuangzi defends himself by describing life and death as successive phases of one formless, continuous process, no more to be mourned than the turning of the seasons; the dream, later in the same chapter, in which a skull he had been questioning on the road describes the freedoms of the dead — no ruler above, no subjects below, none of the four seasons' toil — and refuses his offer to have it restored to a body and a family; and his own deathbed instructions in the "Lieyukou" chapter, where he refuses an elaborate funeral on the grounds that heaven and earth will serve as his coffin and the sun and moon as his grave-goods, since being eaten by crows above ground is no different from being eaten by worms below it. My friend's view is that all of this is just Epicurus's "death is nothing to us" wearing different clothes: both are trying to talk you out of fearing death by changing how you conceive of the self that death would supposedly harm. I think that elides a real difference in strategy: Epicurus builds an explicit argument out of a specific view of sensation and harm, the kind of argument that Thomas Nagel's deprivation account of the harm of death can engage on its own terms, whereas Zhuangzi never quite argues for a thesis about what death is — he seems more interested in dissolving the perspective from which life looks preferable to death than in proving that preference mistaken.

Neither of us budged, so I would like your help settling it in writing. Please compare Zhuangzi's treatment of death with Epicurus's, working from the specific material above rather than the one-line slogans each is usually reduced to. I am especially interested in whether Zhuangzi's passages amount to anything that functions as an argument against fearing death, as opposed to a rhetorical or perspectival exercise that is not playing by the same rules as Epicurus's; whether a Nagel-style deprivation objection has any real purchase against Zhuangzi, given that his metaphysics of continuous transformation is not obviously committed to the kind of annihilation Epicurus assumes; and, once that is sorted out, which view comes closer to actually accomplishing what it sets out to do. Please defend an actual verdict rather than just laying out both sides evenhandedly.
