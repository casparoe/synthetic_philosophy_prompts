I am writing a novel centered on a decades-long, prickly friendship between two philosophers — both committed expressivists about morality — who have never managed to agree on how to answer the Frege–Geach problem. I want the argument I give them at a conference-hotel bar to rest on a genuine technical disagreement rather than a plausible-sounding fudge of my own invention, so I'd like to understand the terrain properly before I draft the scene. Please compare Simon Blackburn's quasi-realist strategy for handling embedded moral sentences with Allan Gibbard's norm-expressivist strategy, including his later appeal to "hyperplans" to fix normative content precisely. Concretely: what does each theory say is happening when someone accepts a sentence like "if lying is wrong, then getting your little brother to lie is wrong" without thereby asserting that lying is wrong? And in your own assessment, does either account give a more satisfying explanation of why the corresponding modus ponens is valid, or do the two of them ultimately founder on the same rock, just dressed differently?
