During my working life as a behavioral ecologist, I spent a good many field seasons studying contest behavior in animals: the sort of prolonged, costly standoffs between rivals—over a territory, a mate, a carcass—that evolutionary game theorists, following John Maynard Smith, call the "war of attrition." In that model, neither contestant escalates to outright combat; the resource eventually goes to whichever individual is willing to persist, and to absorb the mounting cost of persisting, longer than the other. A further, somewhat counterintuitive result is that no fixed persistence time is stable against invasion, so the evolutionarily stable strategy is necessarily a randomized one. Since retiring, I have been reading much more widely, including in game theory and philosophy, and I recently came across Martin Shubik's "dollar auction" in a discussion of escalation of commitment: a dollar bill is auctioned off as usual, except that the second-highest bidder, as well as the winner, must pay their final bid while receiving nothing for it, which is supposed to explain why bidders sometimes end up paying many times the value of the prize rather than cut their losses.

I raised this at a small philosophy reading group I joined last year, half-expecting people to agree that the dollar auction is really just a version of the animal contests I used to study, and was surprised at how contested the point turned out to be. I would like a more careful comparison than we managed among ourselves. Could you set out the formal structure and the equilibrium concept of each model, and then assess whether the dollar auction is best understood as a special, perhaps pathological, case of a war-of-attrition-type dynamic, or whether the resemblance between the two is largely superficial once the details are made precise? I would also value your judgment on which of the two models does more genuine explanatory work when applied to real cases of runaway human escalation—protracted litigation, price wars, arms races, and the like—and on why one might be preferable to the other for that purpose.
