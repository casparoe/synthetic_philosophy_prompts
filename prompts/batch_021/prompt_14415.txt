I'm finishing a column on the philosophy hiding inside your weather app, and I'd value a critical, philosophically literate read before I file it. The draft — working title "The Trouble With Tomorrow's Rain," a little under 500 words — is reproduced in full below.

Every evening, tens of millions of people glance at a number — "70% chance of rain tomorrow" — without asking what it actually claims. Philosophers of probability have spent the better part of a century arguing about exactly that question, and forecasting turns out to be an unusually clean test case for their disagreement.

Start with frequentism, which identifies probability with a long-run relative frequency across repetitions of some setup. Applied to tomorrow, this runs into trouble immediately, since tomorrow happens exactly once. The standard fix is to locate tomorrow within a reference class of "relevantly similar" days — same season, similar pressure pattern — but as Hans Reichenbach recognized, there is no principled way to choose that class. Narrow it enough and you are left with a single case; widen it and you dilute away whatever makes tomorrow distinctive.

Subjectivists have a tidier answer: probability is simply the forecaster's degree of belief, fixed by which bets she would accept. That handles single cases without strain, but at a cost — it makes "70%" a fact about a meteorologist's head rather than the sky, which sits oddly with the public's sense that the number is being read off the atmosphere rather than off someone's opinion. It sits even more oddly alongside the fact that several national weather services officially define the figure as the product of two separate judgments — confidence that rain will fall somewhere in the area, multiplied by the fraction of the area expected to get it — which looks less like a personal credence than a considered actuarial estimate.

Propensity theory, which Karl Popper developed to rescue single-case probabilities in quantum mechanics, looks like the natural rescue: an objective, physical tendency of tomorrow's atmosphere, right now, to produce rain. But propensities were built for genuinely indeterministic processes. Tomorrow's weather is chaotic, not indeterministic; its unpredictability is epistemic, arising from our ignorance of initial conditions, not from any missing fact about what will happen. Calling that a "propensity" may just relabel our ignorance as physics.

Modern forecasting adds a final wrinkle. Agencies increasingly generate these numbers by running dozens of simulations from slightly perturbed starting conditions and reporting the fraction that produce rain at a given point. That is frequentist in form, but the "trials" are hypothetical model runs rather than repetitions of the actual world, and their spread reflects our uncertainty about initial conditions rather than any objective chance setup in the atmosphere itself. This isn't obviously frequentism, subjectivism, or propensity as classically defined — it may be something the standard three-way taxonomy has no box for.

My hunch is that no existing interpretation of probability fully entitles forecasters to the number they give us.

Please be direct if you think the central argument doesn't hold up.
