I'm a software engineer at a consumer-lending fintech, and for reasons that still aren't entirely clear to me, I've spent the last two quarters as the "engineering representative" on our Model Risk Committee. We meet next Thursday to decide whether to approve our new underwriting model for full production rollout, and the discussion has stalled on something that stopped being a technical or legal question a while ago, even though it started as both.

Some background: our current model (the "champion") is a fairly transparent scorecard built on standard variables like debt-to-income and delinquency history, and everyone on the committee can tell you roughly why it declines who it declines. The new model (the "challenger") is a much more complex ensemble that has been running in shadow mode for eighteen months and has beaten the champion on every outcome metric we track, most importantly a meaningfully lower default rate among the loans it would have approved. The catch is that nobody can tell you why it makes any individual decision: our data science team ran three standard explanation techniques on the same batch of declined applications and got three different, sometimes contradictory, rankings of "the reasons," none of which they're willing to vouch for as the actual causal story. We've already worked around the narrow legal problem this creates -- compliance is comfortable generating a defensible adverse-action notice for each applicant regardless -- so this isn't really a "can we do it legally" question anymore. Our head of data science argues that once a model has cleared eighteen months of rigorous, out-of-sample validation, demanding a "story" on top of that is asking for something we've already gotten, in the only form that should matter: a track record of being right. Our Chief Risk Officer disagrees, and not on compliance grounds; she keeps saying, in slightly different words each time, that a model which works without anyone understanding why is a shakier thing to build a lending business on than one that works and is understood, even though she admits she can't name a concrete future scenario where the difference would actually show up. Our written model validation policy, which borrows its structure from the standard federal guidance on this stuff, requires us to separately assess a model's "conceptual soundness" and its "outcomes performance," but nobody on the committee can say why conceptual soundness should count for anything once outcomes performance is this strong -- which is exactly the fight we keep having, meeting after meeting.

Could you draft the memo I'll circulate to the committee before Thursday? I need an actual recommendation -- approve the challenger for full rollout, hold it back, or approve it subject to specific conditions -- along with reasoning solid enough that either the head of data science or the CRO could see exactly where they'd need to push back if they still disagreed. In particular, if you end up siding with the CRO, don't just assert that "understanding matters" as though that settles things; take the head of data science's position seriously first, since "a validated model is a validated model" is a genuinely strong argument on its own terms, not just impatience, and I want to actually understand why a track record like this one wouldn't already count as knowing the model works.
