For a side project I've been slowly writing a small rigid-body physics engine, mostly as an excuse to finally understand quaternions properly, and that sent me down a Wikipedia rabbit hole a few weeks ago that ended, somewhat unexpectedly, at the Scholium in Newton's Principia. I already half-knew the rotating-bucket story, but the argument that actually stuck with me was the lesser-known cousin a few paragraphs later: two globes, joined by a cord, alone in an otherwise completely empty universe, revolving around their common center of gravity. Newton's claim is that you could still measure genuine tension in that cord, and so detect that the pair is truly rotating, even though there is nothing else in existence for them to be rotating relative to -- which he takes as proof that motion, and space itself, must be absolute rather than a mere relation between bodies.

What I hadn't appreciated is how much mileage people have gotten out of that exact scenario since. Leibniz's and later Mach's rejoinder is that "rotating" can only mean rotating relative to something -- for Mach, relative to the rest of the matter in the universe, the "fixed stars" in the old phrasing -- so two genuinely solo globes, with nothing else in existence to rotate relative to, shouldn't feel any tension at all; if we just assume they still would, we've quietly smuggled absolute space back in through the side door. I gather this never got cleanly resolved and just migrated into general relativity, where it gets messier rather than easier: frame-dragging looks like a point for Mach, since nearby rotating masses really do drag local inertial frames around with them, while Gödel's rotating-universe solution and Earman and Norton's "hole argument" from the 1980s both get cited on the other side, as reasons to think spacetime has enough independent structure of its own that facts about rotation, and even about the identity of specific points of space, don't fully reduce to relations among bodies. I have no formal philosophy training -- everything above is reconstructed from Wikipedia and a couple of Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy pages, so I may well have some terminology slightly off -- but the underlying question feels sturdy no matter how it's dressed up: can a lone system, with nothing else in existence, have genuine facts about its own motion?

I'd like you to write me a short story, somewhere in the 1,200-1,800 word range, that makes this specific tension felt rather than explained. You don't need to use the two globes literally, and you don't need hard SF with characters reciting field equations -- I'd rather you invent whatever concrete situation lets a character, or a very small cast, actually confront the question of whether motion, or rotation specifically, can be a fact about a system with nothing external for it to be moving relative to. The one thing I'd ask you to avoid is the framing where a professor, an AI, or a conveniently well-read side character stops the plot to explain the concept out loud; the physics should be load-bearing for what the characters do and decide, not a lecture delivered as dialogue. And please don't let the ending settle the debate in either direction -- an honestly unresolved ending would actually be the accurate one, since that's the real state of the argument.
