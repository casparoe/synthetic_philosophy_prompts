My friend Jonah and I both belong to a small discussion group that meets once a month to work through papers on rational choice and formal epistemology. Last week's session concerned money-pump arguments and what, if anything, they show about the demands of rationality over time, and afterward Jonah and I stayed on for another two hours arguing about it without reaching anything close to agreement. What has stuck with me since, though, is not our own argument but one that happened during the session itself, between two other members, Elena and Marcus, which the group never resolved and which I would like an outside verdict on.

(1) Elena opened with the standard cycle: suppose someone ranks option A above B, B above C, and yet, comparing them directly, ranks C above A — not merely a hypothetical curiosity, she added, since psychologists have actually produced choices like this in the laboratory when options differ along two dimensions and get compared pairwise on a rough "good enough" basis. Because such a person can be walked through a sequence of trades, paying a small fee to swap C for B, a little more to swap B for A, and a little more again to swap A for C, and end up back with C but strictly poorer, with no natural stopping point, Elena argued that the cyclic ranking itself must be irrational, which is why transitivity is treated as a basic requirement of rationality rather than a convenient modeling assumption. (2) Marcus objected that this only works on someone taking the trades one at a time, without looking ahead: anyone who noticed the pattern in advance would simply decline the first trade, keep C, and pay nothing, so the story indicts short-sighted choosing, not cyclic preference as such, since a sufficiently far-sighted cyclic chooser is never actually pumped. (3) Elena replied that declining the first trade is not the free escape it looks like: at the moment of declining, this person prefers B to C by hypothesis, and is nonetheless turning down B, plus a little money, in favor of C; foresight, she said, does not make the cycle harmless, it just lets you choose which bad outcome you get — a guaranteed loss of money, or a guaranteed choice against your own ranking. (4) Marcus answered that choosing "against your ranking" in that sense is no cost at all if it is the output of a considered plan: we do not say someone betrays their preferences by turning down dessert because they had already decided, for good reason, to keep to a diet, and the in-the-moment craving does not automatically outrank the policy; the same, he said, should go for someone who settles on a policy for handling the three items in advance and sticks to it. (5) Elena's comeback, where matters stood when the session ended, was that the policy is doing more work than Marcus was crediting it with: for it to avoid the pump in a principled rather than an accidental way, it has to rank the available trade-sequences consistently, which simply re-imports something transitivity-shaped at the level of plans, even while the person's pairwise, gut-level comparisons among the three items remain cyclic — so, Elena maintained, Marcus had not shown that transitivity was dispensable, only that it can be relocated from items to plans, which if anything concedes her original point.

Neither Jonah nor I could agree on how to score this exchange even after going over it ourselves, so I would like your independent judgment.
