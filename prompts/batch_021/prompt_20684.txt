Here's the question as I gave it: "IIT claims to solve the 'boundary problem' -- why it's you, and not some sub-part of your brain, or some larger system that includes your brain, that is the subject of experience -- via the exclusion postulate. (a) Explain how the exclusion postulate is supposed to do this work, and how it relates to maximal Φ. (b) IIT is usually presented as a genuine rival to functionalism rather than just a variant of it -- explain why, using the classic brain-simulation case. (c) Give one serious objection to IIT from the recent literature, and say how a defender of the theory might respond to it."

And here's the student's answer, copied more or less verbatim: "IIT's five postulates -- intrinsic existence, composition, information, integration, and exclusion -- are supposed to be self-evident truths about what any experience is like, and from these Tononi derives axioms that any physical system has to satisfy if some part of it is going to be the substrate of consciousness. The exclusion postulate is what does the boundary-problem work: your brain, your left hemisphere, and your brain-plus-desk are all overlapping candidate systems, and exclusion says only one of these wins -- whichever one maximizes Φ, which for a normal human just is the whole brain, and that's why it's you, and not your hemisphere or a single neuron, that has the experience. On functionalism: IIT is not a functionalist theory because what matters is the actual causal structure realized by the physical system, not just the input-output mapping, so a computer simulating a brain step by step wouldn't be conscious even though it produces the same outputs, since a serial simulation has a totally different causal architecture, and much lower Φ, than the brain it's simulating. As for objections, Aaronson's unfolding argument shows that IIT is unfalsifiable, because for any conscious system you can build a 'twin' that behaves identically but was engineered to have low Φ, so no possible behavioral test could ever tell IIT and a functionalist rival apart -- Tononi's best reply is basically to bite the bullet and say we should trust the theory's mathematics over our intuitions about which systems 'deserve' to be conscious."
