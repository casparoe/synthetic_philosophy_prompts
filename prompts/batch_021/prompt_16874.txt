Okay so I fell down this whole rabbit hole because a philosophy youtuber I watch did a video on "Kripkenstein" (apparently thats the nickname people use for Saul Kripke's reading of Wittgenstein on rule-following) and he used this example where you define a weird function "quus": x quus y = x + y as long as x and y are both less than 57, but equals 5 otherwise. The point being, if I've only ever added numbers under 57 before, how do I actually know that when I compute 68 + 57 I mean "plus" and should get 125, instead of secretly having meant "quus" the whole time, in which case the answer should be 5? Nothing about my past behavior seems to rule that out. Kinda broke my brain not gonna lie.

So I went and found the actual Wittgenstein passage this is supposedly based on, in an old paperback copy of Philosophical Investigations my school library has (its §201, though my copy might be an older translation so the wording could be slightly different from other editions). It goes:

"This was our paradox: no course of action could be determined by a rule, because every course of action can be made out to accord with the rule. The answer was: if everything can be made out to accord with the rule, then it can also be made out to conflict with it. And so there would be neither accord nor conflict here."

and then a bit later, after going on about interpretations for a paragraph, he says the fix is that "there is a way of grasping a rule which is not an interpretation, but which is exhibited in what we call 'obeying the rule' and 'going against it' in actual cases."

I've read this like five times now and i'm stuck on two things. First, how does "everything accords with the rule" flip into "everything ALSO conflicts with it" and then that's somehow supposed to equal "neither," instead of just being a flat contradiction? Second, what does "grasping a rule that isn't an interpretation" even mean — isnt interpreting a symbol just what understanding it IS? It kind of feels like Wittgenstein is just restating the problem in fancier words instead of solving it. My philosophy teacher said this passage is basically "the exact same paradox" as the quus thing, but I can't actually see the gears connecting the two. Can someone walk me through what's going on in the quote and how it lines up with the quus example?
