I'm partway through a novel built around two invented former doctoral students of A. J. Ayer, and I've hit a chapter I'd like to think through before I draft it myself. The real-life hook is Ayer's 1988 cardiac arrest at University College Hospital — he is supposed to have choked on a piece of smoked salmon and gone without a heartbeat for several minutes — after which he wrote up the strange experience he'd had on the other side, in a piece he called "What I Saw When I Was Dead": a red light, a sense that space itself had come apart, and that something depended on him putting it right. He remarked afterward, in a line that's been quoted ever since, that the whole business had "slightly weakened" his conviction that his real, fast-approaching death would be the end of him, while insisting he hadn't become a believer in anything and that the likelier explanation was a brain running short of oxygen.

My chapter is set at the small gathering after his actual death the following year, where his two former students — now academics in their own right, and characters who've been quietly at odds for the whole book — end up cornering each other over the drinks table about what, if anything, that episode does to his own signature argument: that talk of a person surviving their bodily death isn't false so much as strictly meaningless, since it fails his verification principle and no one can specify what would even count as verifying it. Ruth has stayed loyal to that argument without qualification: a wobble in someone's confidence isn't evidence of anything, their teacher never actually underwent the kind of death his argument is about, and "surviving bodily death" is exactly as unverifiable in principle now as it was before, whatever his brain got up to while briefly starved of oxygen. Owen has drifted, partly because of what happened to their teacher, toward something like John Hick's old eschatological-verification move: that a claim about surviving death can be perfectly meaningful right now because it is verifiable in principle later, by the very person whose survival is in question — even though, if the claim is false, there will never be anyone able to establish that.

I'd like you to write their exchange in full, as something I could plausibly lift straight into the manuscript, each turn marked by name. Give me real back-and-forth — each pressing the other on what they actually just said, rather than a pair of rehearsed set-pieces — running to a dozen or so exchanges, and let it end in a genuine impasse, with each of them having landed at least one point the other has to work to answer, rather than either one folding.
