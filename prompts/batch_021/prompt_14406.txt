Okay this is gonna sound pretty nerdy but here goes. A while back I went down a youtube rabbit hole about Kurt Godel (the incompleteness theorem guy) and found out he also wrote out this whole formal "proof" that God exists using modal logic, one that apparently didn't really surface publicly until after he died. I got kind of into it and mentioned it to my friend Marcus, who then got way more into it than me, and at his barbecue last weekend he brought it up again in front of our friend Elena (who does something with data for work, no formal philosophy training, but she's the closest thing our group has to a "logic person"). What followed was a solid 25 minutes of them going back and forth while the rest of us just refilled our drinks. I'm recounting this from memory so I might be garbling the exact wording, but I'm confident I've got the substance of each move right.

(1) Marcus opened with: this isn't the cheap "existence is a perfection so a perfect being must exist" trick, Godel built the whole thing from precise axioms about what counts as a "positive" property and ran it through actual modal logic (he name-dropped "S5," which he clearly learned that same week), so once you grant the starting premises the god-exists conclusion is forced on you with no hand-waving, and that alone deserves more respect than people give it. (2) Elena said being rigorous doesn't save you if it turns out your axioms prove way more than you bargained for -- she'd read that those exact same axioms also generate something called "modal collapse," basically that literally everything true becomes necessarily true, no such thing as it could've gone another way, which is nuts -- not just that God exists necessarily but that her ordering a second beer was somehow locked into the fabric of logic since the dawn of time. (3) Marcus's comeback was that "nuts" isn't the same as "false" -- there are definately people out there who are totally fine biting that bullet if God's nature really does necessitate everything, so Elena would need an actual argument against that view rather than just going "ew, no." (4) Elena said that's a bait and switch: the whole selling point of the proof was that you didn't need to already accept some hardcore deterministic theology to find the premises believable, and if the only way to keep it alive is to also buy one of the most extreme, unpopular metaphysical pictures around, that's not a defense, its the bill arriving late -- like getting "1=2" out of an algebra proof and deciding numbers must just work differently now instead of hunting for your mistake. (5) Marcus pushed back that she still can't say which axiom is actually false, and that even the philosophers who reject the proof don't agree on that among themselves -- some blame the axiom that says positive properties stay positive under logical entailment, others blame calling "necessary existence" a positive property to begin with -- and if the critics can't agree where the bug is, maybe that's a sign there isn't an obvious one. (6) Elena's closing line was that you don't need to find the exact broken premise before you're allowed to bail on the whole set -- if some premises together spit out something absurd, that alone tells you at least one of them is false, that's just what a reductio is (her word, not mine, I had to look it up after), and it's on Marcus to show the premises are independently trustworthy, not on her to win a game of whack-a-mole first.

Nobody at the barbecue could settle who came out ahead and its honestly been rattling around in my head since.
