So last night my friend Theo and I got into a two-hour argument over Parfit's Hitchhiker -- the case where you're dying of thirst in the desert and a driver who's basically a perfect judge of whether you're lying says he'll only take you into town if you promise to pay him once you get there. Theo's position was that once you're actually standing in town, refusing to pay is just correct, full stop, that's what rational means, and anyone who pays is a sucker who happened to get lucky. I kept saying that if a theory of rationality tells you to die of thirst rather than hand over some cash, the theory's what's broken, not the person who'd pay. We went around and around on whether "it's rational to be the kind of agent who'd pay" secretly collapses into "it's rational to actually pay once the moment comes," and neither of us landed a knockout blow (also mildly funny, given Parfit spent his whole career on whether you're "the same person" over time, that his own thought experiment turns on exactly that, right at the fork in the road).

Weirdly good timing: I co-run a small philosophy podcast, and next month's guest is a decision theorist coming on specifically to defend functional decision theory and argue, hard, that paying really is the rational move once you're in town, not just that it was rational to have committed to paying beforehand. Can you write me eight to ten interview questions that skip past re-explaining the setup (she doesn't need to walk us through the lie detector again) and instead dig into where this actually gets hard? I want at least one question that forces her to keep "rational disposition" and "rational act" separate instead of quietly sliding between them, one asking how her answer relates to older stuff like Gauthier's constrained maximization or McClennen's resolute choice, one that runs a "why ain'cha rich"-style challenge on her and asks whether it doesn't boomerang back onto FDT in some other case the same way it's meant to sink causal decision theory here, and one on what happens to the whole argument once the driver's only a pretty good lie detector instead of a flawless one. For each, throw in a quick follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely give, in case she tries to dodge back into just restating the view.
