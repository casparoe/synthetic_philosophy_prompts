I spent thirty-some years doing observational cosmology, so general covariance was always just a bookkeeping device to me: make sure your equations don't secretly depend on which coordinates you happened to chalk onto the blackboard. Since retiring I've been sitting in on a philosophy of science reading group at the university near me, and a couple weeks ago we did Earman and Norton's hole argument, which apparently a lot of philosophers treat as a real threat to the idea that spacetime points are "substances" with an identity of their own, independent of whatever metric or matter fields happen to sit on them. Everyone else in the group treated it as a genuinely open problem; I came away thinking it was basically a non-issue, so I typed up my reasoning below to see if I'm missing something. My grandson, who's mid-PhD in high energy theory, just said "yeah, sounds about right" when I ran it by him over the phone, which was not the rigorous takedown I was hoping for.

Here's the reasoning, roughly: you've got a solution (M, g) to the field equations, and a diffeomorphism h that's the identity everywhere outside some hole region H but does something non-trivial to points inside it. Drag the metric along with h to get a new field h*g. (M, g) and (M, h*g) solve the same equations and agree on everything outside H, but if the points of M have an identity that doesn't depend on the fields sitting on them, the two represent different assignments of metrical properties to those same points inside H, with nothing in the dynamics or the boundary data to pick between them -- hence indeterminism, baked right into any generally covariant theory over a substantival manifold, unless substantivalism goes. My reaction is that this is just gauge freedom, full stop: exactly like shifting the electromagnetic potential by a gradient, g and h*g are two labels for one physical state of affairs, theres no extra fact left hanging, and I don't see why you need something dressed up as "sophisticated substantivalism" to say so.

What I want from you is a genuinely critical read, not a pat on the back: is my gauge-freedom move actually doing the same work philosophers mean by "Leibniz equivalence," or am I smuggling in something that just begs the question against the substantivalist? And is there any real difference between what I'm doing and Einstein's own point-coincidence way out of his 1913 version of this puzzle, or have I basically rediscovered that and missed whatever makes the Earman-Norton version a distinct, harder problem? Tell me plainly where the physicist's instinct breaks down, if it does.
