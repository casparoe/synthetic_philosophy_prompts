I teach a seminar cross-listed between our philosophy department and the law school on perception, bias, and criminal responsibility, and this week's session on the "cognitive penetrability" debate in philosophy of mind turned into a genuinely difficult discussion that I would like help thinking through before we reconvene. The technical question I put to my students was whether states like fear, stereotypes, or expectation can directly alter the phenomenal content of perceptual experience itself, as Fodor's modularity thesis and Pylyshyn's talk of the "cognitive impenetrability" of early vision would deny (favorite evidence for this camp being the persistence of visual illusions, such as the Müller-Lyer, even once you know perfectly well that the lines are equal), or whether, as Susanna Siegel and others have argued using cases like hillsides that look steeper when you are tired, or a face that suddenly looks hostile once you suspect its owner of betraying you, cognition can genuinely penetrate downstream into what we see and not merely into how we judge or react to what we see.

This stopped being a merely academic dispute for us once we turned to the "shooter bias" literature, where experimental subjects, including trained police officers, are measurably faster and more error-prone at classifying an ambiguous handheld object as a weapon, and faster to decide to "shoot," when it is held by a Black target rather than a White one. Legal doctrine on police use of force, built around asking whether an officer's belief that they faced a lethal threat was "objectively reasonable," tends to treat the moment of perceiving a weapon as a kind of black box that precedes whatever bias might enter later, through judgment, memory, or decision-making. Several students wanted to know whether that legal framing is quietly resting on a philosophically contestable assumption, namely that the perceptual moment itself is bias-proof, and whether the outcome of the cognitive penetrability debate should actually change how such standards are written or applied, rather than being an interesting complication that legal doctrine can safely set aside.

Could you describe what you take to be the most important implications of the cognitive penetration debate, on either side of it, for how we ought to construct and apply legal and moral standards for claims like "I perceived a weapon" or "I perceived a threat" in self-defense and use-of-force cases? I am especially interested in whether the two competing positions in the philosophy of perception actually pull apart in their practical verdicts here, and in whether there is a version of this argument that should worry someone who currently finds the "black box" legal framing basically sensible.
