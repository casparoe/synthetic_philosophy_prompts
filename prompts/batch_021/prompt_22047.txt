I'm several drafts into a generation-ship novel that hinges on a piece of anthropic reasoning. My characters recover indirect evidence suggesting that technological civilizations tend to be short-lived, and one of my leads, a cosmologist, spends a key chapter arguing her more anxious crewmates out of treating this discovery as confirmation of the Doomsday Argument. She does it by invoking the self-indication assumption: once you weight competing hypotheses about the total number of observers by how many observers each one predicts, the pessimistic update that Carter and Leslie's classic reasoning derives from your own birth rank is exactly cancelled out, so anyone still finding "we're probably near the end" compelling after that is simply mishandling the probabilities.

That is more or less how I came to understand the debate myself, after reading around it and hashing it out with a friend who did a philosophy of science MA. Since the self-indication assumption seemed like the more even-handed way of factoring your own existence in as evidence, and since it happens to dissolve the Doomsday worry as a byproduct, I had assumed working philosophers mostly treat this as a settled, tidy resolution, with only fiddly implementation details left over rather than anything that would hand a rival character real ammunition.

Before I write the scene where a skeptical crewmate pushes back, I would like to know whether my cosmologist's framing is actually defensible, or whether adopting the self-indication assumption carries a cost significant enough that specialists in anthropic reasoning still don't treat the Doomsday Argument as closed just because it's on the table. If there is such a cost, I would like to understand it precisely enough to give her opponent a rebuttal that a philosophically literate reader wouldn't wave away as a strawman.
