I'm a software engineer with no real background in philosophy, but a few friends and I run an informal monthly dinner where we take turns picking a book or documentary on twentieth-century history, and this month's pick was about the decision to drop the atomic bombs on Hiroshima and Nagasaki. Someone mentioned, almost as an aside, that when Oxford awarded Harry Truman an honorary degree in 1956, the philosopher G.E.M. Anscombe, who was teaching there at the time, published a pamphlet opposing it on the grounds that ordering the bombings made him a murderer. That one fact set off a long argument between two people at the table, Marcus and Leah, and I haven't been able to stop turning it over since.

The exchange went roughly like this:

1. Marcus opened by saying the "murderer" charge was unfair: a full invasion of the Japanese home islands was projected to kill far more people, Japanese and American alike, than the bombs did, so given the actual choice on the table, dropping them was the right call.
2. Leah said that isn't what makes something murder on Anscombe's account. Her objection wasn't that too many people died -- it was that the bombs weren't aimed at a military target with civilians incidentally caught in the blast; killing the civilian population was the mechanism that was supposed to force Japan's surrender. On her view, intentionally killing the innocent as a means to an end is murder no matter how the resulting arithmetic looks, and pointing to a worse hypothetical doesn't change that.
3. Marcus asked whether that reasoning doesn't take down half the Allied war effort with it: the firebombing raids on Tokyo, Dresden, and Hamburg were also aimed at civilian populations and morale. If that counts as murder too, the argument stops being a specific claim about Truman and turns into a claim about how nearly everyone fought that war -- which struck him as a sign that something had gone wrong with the reasoning, not a discovery.
4. Leah didn't back down: she said that yes, the firebombing campaigns were murder by the same standard, and that an indictment being broad doesn't make it mistaken. Widely shared guilt is still guilt.
5. Marcus's next move was against the intend/foresee distinction itself: actual target-planning documents talked about industrial and military significance, not about killing civilians, so how could anyone be confident the deaths were intended rather than merely foreseen, rather than Leah simply reading a bad intention backward out of a bad outcome?
6. Leah's answer was that intention shows up in the design of an act, not in what planners wrote down: choosing city centers over the more distant factories, giving no warning that would have let residents evacuate, and relying on the psychological shock of a devastated population as the actual mechanism of surrender, rather than as an unfortunate side effect of one, only make sense if killing the population, and not merely damaging capacity, was the plan.
7. Marcus's last move was that even granting all of this, calling the result "murder" flattens a situation that may have had no clean option into a slogan. Leah replied that this was exactly Anscombe's point in objecting to the degree: she didn't need a clean alternative to have existed in order to deny that the act was permissible, and refusing to use the word "murder" just because the situation was desperate was, in her view, precisely the move Oxford was making by honoring him anyway.

Neither of them budged, and I don't know this material well enough to referee it myself. Could you go through the exchange move by move and say, for each numbered point, whether it holds up, overreaches, or gets something backwards, rather than just declaring an overall winner? I'd also like to know whether Leah's version of Anscombe's argument is actually faithful to it, and whether either of them is missing a stronger form of their own case.
