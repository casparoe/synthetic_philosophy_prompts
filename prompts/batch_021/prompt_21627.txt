Here is the question, worth 20 points:

"In his 1953 experiments, Maurice Allais presented subjects with pairs of choices between lotteries that most people evaluate in a way inconsistent with expected utility theory. (a) [8 pts] State the independence axiom (sometimes called the substitution axiom) of von Neumann-Morgenstern expected utility theory precisely, and, using the 'common consequence' version of Allais's choices (the $1 million / $5 million example), show exactly which pair of preferences violates it and why. (b) [12 pts] Some theorists initially treated this pattern as an error to be corrected once explained to subjects; Allais himself treated it as revealing something normatively defensible about how people weight certainty. Characterize ONE alternative decision theory developed in response to this pattern, and explain specifically how it accommodates Allais's choices by weakening independence, rather than some other axiom of expected utility theory."

And here is the student's answer:

"(a) The independence axiom states that for any lotteries L1, L2, L3 and any probability p, L1 is preferred to L2 if and only if the mixture pL1 + (1-p)L3 is preferred to pL2 + (1-p)L3: preferences between two options shouldn't be changed by mixing in some third, common possibility. This is essentially the same requirement as Savage's sure-thing principle, since both say that a component shared by two options shouldn't affect the ranking between them.

In the common consequence version, subjects choose between A ($1 million for certain) and B (10% chance of $5 million, 89% chance of $1 million, 1% chance of $0), and separately between C (11% chance of $1 million, 89% chance of $0) and D (10% chance of $5 million, 90% chance of $0). Most people pick A over B, and D over C. But A and B both contain a 0.89 chance of $1 million; strip that common piece out and each reduces, on the remaining 0.11, to a choice between $1 million for certain and a 10/11-vs-1/11 gamble between $5 million and $0. C and D are exactly that same reduced choice, just with the common 0.89 piece replaced by $0 instead of $1 million. Independence says that swapping the common piece shouldn't flip which side of the reduced choice you prefer, so choosing A over B while choosing D over C violates it directly.

(b) Kahneman and Tversky's rank-dependent utility theory responds to exactly this pattern. Rather than weighting each outcome by its own probability, it weights outcomes by a nonlinear transformation of their rank in the cumulative probability distribution, which lets the theory overweight the jump from 99% to 100% (moving to certainty) relative to an equivalent jump elsewhere on the probability scale, while still respecting first-order stochastic dominance and preserving transitivity and completeness. Because the fix operates on how probabilities become decision weights rather than on the ordering axioms themselves, it's standardly described as weakening independence specifically, which is what part (b) is asking for."
