Question. Consider an agent who may wager any fraction f ∈ [0,1] of their current bankroll on repeated, independent flips of a coin that comes up heads with known probability p = 0.6 and pays even money (a winning bet of size x returns 2x; a losing bet returns 0).
(a) [8 pts] Derive the Kelly fraction f* that maximizes the expected exponential growth rate of wealth per round, and compute its value for p = 0.6. State the general formula for a bet at odds of b-to-1.
(b) [9 pts] Contrast the Kelly strategy with the strategy that maximizes the bettor's expected wealth after N rounds. Identify that latter strategy, and explain, invoking the distinction between ensemble averages and time averages, why it leads almost surely to ruin despite having the highest expectation of any strategy.
(c) [8 pts] Ole Peters and others have argued that considerations like those in part (b) show that expected utility theory — including the traditional idea that a "rational" agent facing multiplicative gambles must simply happen to have a logarithmic utility function for wealth — is not the right framework for justifying Kelly-optimal behaviour. Explain the alternative, ergodicity-based justification for the Kelly criterion, and say whether you find it more compelling than the expected-utility justification.

Student's answer.
(a) The single-round growth rate is g(f) = p ln(1+f) + (1-p) ln(1-f). Setting g'(f) = p/(1+f) - (1-p)/(1-f) = 0 and solving gives f* = p - (1-p) = 2p - 1. For p = 0.6, f* = 0.2: the bettor should stake 20% of the bankroll each round. More generally, for a bet paying b-to-1, the Kelly fraction is f* = p - (1-p)/b = (bp - q)/b, where q = 1 - p.

(b) If instead the bettor wants to maximize E[W_N], note that E[W_1] = W_0[p(1+f) + (1-p)(1-f)] = W_0[1 + f(2p-1)]. Since 2p - 1 = 0.2 > 0, this is strictly increasing in f, so the expected-wealth-maximizing strategy is f = 1: stake the entire bankroll every round. Under f = 1, however, a single tail wipes the bettor out completely, and since the probability of never once flipping tails falls to zero as N grows (it equals 0.6^N), the bettor is ruined with probability tending to 1, even though E[W_N] = (1.2)^N W_0 grows without bound. The two criteria disagree because this is a non-ergodic process: the ensemble average E[W_N] is dominated by a vanishingly unlikely all-heads sequence in which wealth is astronomical, while the time average — what happens along essentially every individual path, including whichever one the bettor actually lives through — converges to ruin. This distinction between ensemble and time averages is exactly the one that Kelly drew on in his original 1956 paper when he showed that the growth-optimal strategy almost surely comes to dominate any other fixed-fraction strategy in the long run.

(c) Peters's "ergodicity economics" holds that the puzzle in (b) arises from asking the wrong question. Because wealth evolves multiplicatively, the raw wealth process is non-ergodic, but the logarithm of wealth evolves additively and is therefore ergodic, so for log-wealth — unlike wealth itself — the time average and the ensemble average coincide, and expectations of log-wealth are a trustworthy guide to what happens to an actual individual over time. On this view, log(W) is not merely a convenient utility function reflecting one bettor's taste for risk; it is the objectively correct utility function for anyone facing a genuinely multiplicative, repeated gamble, fixed by the dynamics of the process rather than by preference. I find this fully persuasive: it shows that expected utility theory was right all along in insisting on a concave utility function, and simply supplies the missing argument for why log utility specifically, rather than some other concave function, is the one objectively mandated choice.
