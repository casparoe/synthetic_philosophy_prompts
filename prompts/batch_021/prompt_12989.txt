I'm a senior engineer and one of a dozen active maintainers on a mid-sized open-source data-processing library that has, somewhat to our surprise, ended up embedded in production systems at companies far larger than the volunteer group that maintains it. Because a couple of those companies now treat the project as critical infrastructure, we decided last year to move away from an informal, whoever-shows-up-on-Slack style of decision-making and set up a proper nonprofit foundation, complete with a written charter, a steering committee, and real elections. I ended up on the working group drafting the bylaws mainly because I was the only maintainer willing to read the draft bylaws line by line, and we've since hit a snag on what looked like a minor procedural question but has become the most contentious issue we face: who actually gets to vote when the charter itself is put up for ratification.

Three groups each have a reasonable claim, and every rule we've proposed ends up treating one of them as illegitimate. About forty people have merged at least one substantial commit in the last five years — call them the sweat-equity contributors. Six corporate sponsors currently supply roughly 80% of the foundation's budget and would like voting weight proportional to that funding. And according to our download telemetry, something like fifteen thousand companies run the library in production without ever having filed a patch or so much as an issue — they contribute neither code nor money, but a bad governance decision could break their systems overnight, which arguably gives them the largest stake of anyone. Whichever group gets to vote on the founding charter will, in effect, decide the charter's contents, which means the working group writing the eligibility rules is quietly pre-deciding the outcome before any actual vote takes place, and I can't find a neutral vantage point from which to settle that fairly. One of our advisory board members, who studied political theory before moving into venture capital, waved the whole thing off with "oh, that's just the boundary problem — nobody's ever really solved it," which I found reassuring and completely unhelpful in equal measure.

The working group meets in three weeks to finalize the bylaws before they go to a project-wide vote, and I've been asked to write the memo the group will actually deliberate from. Could you draft it for me? I need a concrete recommendation for who should count as an eligible voter on the charter-ratification vote specifically, as distinct from whatever rule we eventually adopt for steady-state elections, along with the reasoning behind it and a genuinely fair statement of the strongest case for whichever group the recommendation leaves out — both my co-maintainers and the sponsors' counsel will notice if that part reads thin.
