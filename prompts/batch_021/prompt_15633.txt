I'm putting together this week's session for my grad seminar on blame and hypocrisy, and there's a move that basically every paper on the topic makes but that I keep fumbling when I try to explain it live in front of students. Ever since Cohen's old piece about who has standing to condemn terrorists, the standard line in this literature (Wallace's equal standing paper, then a bunch of newer stuff from Fritz and Miller, and Todd's attempt at a unified account) is that hypocritical blame is defective NOT because the content of the blame is false -- the hypocrites target can really have done something wrong -- but because the hypocrite lacks STANDING to level the charge. That's fine as a slogan, but one of my students pushed back hard on this last week and i honestly didn't have a great answer: every time I try to unpack what "lacking standing" actually amounts to, I just end up re-describing the phenomenon ("the hypocrite's a fraud," "do as I say not as I do") rather than naming the mechanism, and I worry that without more detail the whole apparatus just collapses back into a bog standard tu quoque -- i.e., why isn't "you're one to talk" simply an ad hominem that's irrelevant to whether the target actually did wrong?

So could you walk me through, in a fairly granular way, what these standing-based accounts think is actually going wrong in the act of hypocritical blame -- what is the hypocrite doing, over and above just being inconsistent, that's supposed to disqualify them from the moral "conversation"? I'm especially curious what's doing the real philosophical work in something like Wallace's picture (the equal standing / implicit exemption-claiming move) versus a more fairness-flavored story like Fritz and Miller's, and whether either one actually escapes the tu quoque worry or just relocates it one level up.

Side question if you've got room for it: this standing worry seems to attach almost entirely to blame and barely ever comes up for hypocritical praise (someone gushing over an act they'd never bother doing themselves). That asymmetry has always bugged me and none of the readings I assign really explain why it's there -- is there a principled story for why standing issues are so blame-specific, or is that just an accident of what people happen to have gotten interested in?
