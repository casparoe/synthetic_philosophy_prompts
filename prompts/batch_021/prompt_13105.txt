I've somehow ended up on my university's Faculty Senate grievance committee this year, and we've been handed a case I can't stop turning into a Pascal problem in my head (I teach the Pensées every other spring, so apologies in advance for the professional deformation). Here's the setup: the History department ran a full search and its hiring committee voted 6-1 for their top candidate, an intellectual historian. The dean overruled them and directed the department to make the offer to the runner-up instead, on the grounds that the runner-up can also cover sections of the college's new quantitative-reasoning gen-ed requirement -- and per the faculty handbook, final hiring authority does sit with the dean's office, full stop, no rule was broken. The department is furious anyway, not because anyone disputes the dean's authority to do this, but because they think the outcome is just wrong: that she has no real basis for overriding the near-unanimous judgment of the people actually qualified to assess these candidates as historians. Our committee has to write an opinion on whether to formally register an objection with the provost's office. Half of us want to say "she followed procedure, that's the end of it," the other half wants to say "procedure isn't justice, and we should say so" -- except our opinion is purely advisory, it can compel nothing, which is exactly the bind Pascal describes: justice without force is impotent, force without justice is tyrannical, and our committee is sitting right in the middle of that, possibly about to issue some toothless justice, possibly about to help legitimate a tyranny, and I honestly can't tell which.

Could you draft the actual memo I'd bring to committee (we meet again next Tuesday, so I need something circulatable before then)? I want a real recommendation on the table -- uphold the dean's decision, object, or object with specific conditions attached -- and I want the reasoning to acutally draw on Pascal's own thinking about justice and force (and, if you think it's relevant, which I suspect it is, his bit about the different "orders" of greatness), rather than a generic "power corrupts" gesture at him. And don't let the dean's side off easy just because I've clearly editorialized above -- if there's a genuinely serious Pascalian case for deferring to her, I want it represented on it's own terms, not as a strawman we knock down on the way to the conclusion I obviously want.
