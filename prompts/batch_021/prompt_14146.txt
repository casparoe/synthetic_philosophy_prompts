I'm prepping a lecture on the classical argument from illusion, and something about the standard disjunctivist response still nags at me. As I understand it, disjunctivists block the argument by rejecting the Common Kind Assumption: the idea that a veridical perception and a subjectively indistinguishable illusion -- the stick that looks bent in water, say -- must be experiences of the same fundamental kind. But surely rejecting that assumption commits them to denying the very thing the argument starts from, namely that the illusory case can feel exactly like the veridical one from the inside. I don't see how a disjunctivist gets to keep the indistinguishability as a fact about the two experiences while denying that it reflects any shared underlying nature. Is there a move here I'm missing, or is this simply a cost disjunctivists have to accept?
