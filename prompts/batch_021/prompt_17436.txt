So I don't have any background in philosophy, I just went down a YouTube rabbit hole a few weeks back on A.J. Ayer, the logical positivist guy. From what I understand his whole thing was that a sentence only really means something if you could in principle check it against experience (or its true/false just by the meaning of the words, like math), and everything else — god, the soul, whether "something" survives death — isn't even false, its just noise, it doesn't actually say anything. But then the wild part is that near the end of his life he choked on some food at a dinner in his hospital room and his heart stopped for a few minutes, and afterward he wrote about seeing a red light and some kind of "ministers" running the universe who'd messed up space and time, and he admitted it rattled him a little, though he later insisted he still didn't believe in an afterlife, just that he was slightly less sure of himself than before.

That got me thinking about how he'd react to stuff that obviously didn't exist yet in his lifetime, like cryonics companies that freeze your body hoping to revive you later, the tech-bro dream of "uploading" your mind into a computer, or these new apps that turn a dead person's old texts and voicemails into a chatbot you can keep talking to. Using his own verification principle, would Ayer say those modern ideas are at least meaningful (maybe false, but checkable in principle, so not nonsense the way "the soul survives death" is nonsense on his view), or would he find some way to lump the tech versions in with traditional afterlife talk as equally empty? And does his own post near-death wobble tell us anything about whether he'd cave a bit if a cryonically revived patient, or an "uploaded" mind, ever actually showed up one day talking and acting convincingly like the original person? I want an actual worked-through answer here, not just a one liner like "he'd probably say it's still meaningless" — that feels like it dodges the interesting part.
