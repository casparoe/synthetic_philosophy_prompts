In Word and Object, Quine uses the scenario of radical translation, and in particular the "gavagai" example, to argue for the indeterminacy of translation. (a) Explain what the gavagai example is meant to show, and why Quine takes the resulting indeterminacy to persist even for a linguist with complete knowledge of every native speaker's dispositions to assent to or dissent from sentences under all possible stimulations. (b) Quine maintains that the indeterminacy of translation is a distinct thesis from the underdetermination of physical theory by observation, even though both appeal to evidence failing to settle between rival theories. Explain the distinction he draws between the two. (c) Briefly relate Quine's thesis to at least one other position in the interpretation or translation literature of your choosing.

Here is the student's answer, reproduced exactly as submitted:

Radical translation is Quine's thought experiment in which a field linguist must translate a language with no known connection to any language already familiar to the linguist, relying solely on correlating native utterances with the observable circumstances in which they occur. The gavagai case illustrates the resulting indeterminacy: whenever a rabbit hops into view, the native utters "Gavagai," which the linguist is initially tempted to render as "Rabbit." But the very same pattern of assent and dissent to the presence or absence of a rabbit is equally compatible with translating "Gavagai" as "undetached rabbit part," "rabbit stage," or "rabbithood," since none of these alternative readings would ever diverge from "rabbit" in what counts as evidence for or against the sentence on a given occasion. Since all the linguist has, or could ever have, to go on is exactly this pattern of stimulus and response — what Quine calls the stimulus meaning of the sentence — no amount of additional behavioral observation, however complete, could break the tie. This is why the indeterminacy is principled rather than practical: it is not that the linguist lacks enough data, but that the rival hypotheses are, by construction, empirically identical in everything bearing on assent and dissent.

Quine's holism already commits him to the underdetermination of physical theory by observation: for any body of evidence, more than one overall theory of the world will fit it equally well. Indeterminacy of translation looks like this same thesis showing up in a new setting. A translation manual just is a kind of theory — a theory of what the native speaker means — and rival manuals are rival theories that both accommodate every disposition to assent and dissent, which are the linguist's observations. So Quine's famous new example does not add a new kind of skeptical result to his philosophy; it shows that meaning, like everything else answerable to a holistic web of evidence, is never pinned down uniquely by the observations that bear on it.

Donald Davidson's radical interpretation takes up Quine's scenario under a different name, replacing translation manuals with Tarski-style theories of truth for the speaker's language. Because the interpreter is in the same position as Quine's linguist — fitting a theory to a pattern of held-true sentences — Davidson accepts that more than one such theory can be equally adequate to the evidence, and he draws Quine's conclusion that there is, in the end, no fact of the matter about which theory is correct. What Davidson adds is mainly methodological: the principle of charity, which constrains how an interpreter should go about constructing such a theory in the first place, rather than any disagreement with Quine about what the indeterminacy ultimately shows.
