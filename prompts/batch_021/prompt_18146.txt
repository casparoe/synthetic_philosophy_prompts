So this is a nice problem to have, but I'm stuck. My old undergrad -- a tiny liberal arts college, maybe 400 kids, the kind of place where the entire incoming class still reads Whitehead's Aims of Education during orientation week -- asked me to give this year's commencement address, and I said yes before I'd thought it through. The complication is that I've spent the last eight months buried in that same book for research, because my novel-in-progress is about an invented 1920s "experimental school" whose founders took Whitehead's ideas way more literally than he probably intended, and it slowly falls apart. I mentioned this to my writing group at dinner last week and one of them pointed out, not all that helpfully, that commencement speeches are themselves a textbook case of what Whitehead called an inert idea -- "follow your passion," "go change the world," phrases everybody receives and nobody actually does anything with.

That's the actual bind. This graduating class did a whole capstone seminar on The Aims of Education, so a good chunk of them will clock it immediately if I stand up there and do the thing I just described. But the rest of the students, plus every parent, grandparent and little brother in the folding chairs, have never heard of Whitehead and are just there to feel something about their kid graduating. I don't know how to solve both problems in the same eight minutes, which is where you come in.

Could you write me the actual address -- spoken out loud, so plain sentences over long ones, nothing that reads like an essay? I want Whitehead doing real work in it, not just getting namechecked: the romance-precision-generalization rhythm he thinks all real learning moves through, his insistence that freedom and discipline have to alternate rather than discipline simply coming first, and if it fits naturally, his line about there not being seperate "subjects" at all, just life, looked at from different angles. And it still has to land as a genuinely moving commencement speech for somebody's grandma who could not care less about 1920s philosophy of education. No inert ideas allowed, if you can manage it.
