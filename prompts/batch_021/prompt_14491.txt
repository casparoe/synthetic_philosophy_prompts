Hobbes argues in Leviathan that covenants of mutual trust made in the state of nature cannot bind: absent some power to enforce them, each party always has sufficient reason to defect once defection becomes advantageous, so that, as he puts it, "covenants, without the sword, are but words, and of no strength to secure a man at all." Only a sovereign capable of punishing breach can make cooperation rational for creatures like us.

Contemporary game theory has explored a strikingly different escape from this predicament: program equilibrium (introduced by Moshe Tennenholtz in 2004), in which players do not choose actions directly but submit algorithms, fully visible to one another, that decide how to act on the basis of the opponent's actual code. Under this sort of radical mutual transparency, strategies exist — exploiting techniques from provability logic, sometimes called "Löbian cooperation" — that provably sustain mutual cooperation in a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma with no external enforcer whatsoever, simply because each side can verify in advance that the other will cooperate only with those who would themselves cooperate with it.

Would Hobbes regard program equilibrium as a genuine way of securing covenants without a sword, one that forces a real revision of his argument for sovereignty, or would he maintain that the underlying problem merely resurfaces in a new guise — for instance, in whatever must guarantee that the exhibited "program" is not itself a deceptive front? Answer on Hobbes's own terms, drawing on his specific views of human nature, reason, and trust, and commit to a definite verdict rather than simply cataloguing considerations on each side.
