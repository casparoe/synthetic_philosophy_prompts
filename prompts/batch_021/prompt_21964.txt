I'm a novelist, and through a slightly ridiculous chain of events I've agreed to give the opening remarks -- five minutes, tops -- at a small two-day workshop a physicist friend is running at Cambridge next month, half cosmologists and half philosophers, loosely themed around whether anything in modern cosmology should actually make us feel small. She asked me because my last book had a grieving astronomer in it and apparently I "get" the cosmic stuff, which is generous of her. I want to build the whole thing around Frank Ramsey, the Cambridge mathematician-philosopher who died absurdly young -- twenty-six, in 1930, though dont hold me to the exact date -- because I remember reading that he flatly refused to be humbled by the size of the universe: something about drawing the world in perspective rather than to scale, the stars being no bigger than threepenny bits, and him not being impressed by size because stars can't think or love. Its a great line and I'd love to open with it, but I only know the mood of the argument, not the argument itself, and I'm nervous about standing up in front of actual working cosmologists and either sounding like I'm telling them their own sense of scale is naive, or landing on some soft "we're all just stardust, aren't we lucky" line that any first-year in the room would see through in about four seconds.

So here's what I'd like: draft me those opening remarks. They need to actually reconstruct why Ramsey thought significance doesn't track size -- the real move, not just the vibe of it -- solidly enough that the philosophers in the room recognize an actual position and not a quote off a poster. But it can't read as a novelist, or a philosopher, lecturing physicists about physics, since half that room has spent their careers getting the scale of the universe exactly right and that isn't the part I want to relitigate. And structurally it has to work as an opening and not a closing: it should leave everyone with a real question to spend two days on, rather than a tidy bow that makes the rest of the workshop feel beside the point.
