So in my econ elective (its basically AP Micro but the teacher loves throwing in extra game theory units) we spent like two weeks on Thomas Schelling and Cold War strategy — I think the reading was an excerpt from The Strategy of Conflict, but don't quote me on that — and I got kind of obsessed with this idea that you can gain power by taking away your OWN options. Like burning your boats so retreat isn't possible, or the whole doomsday-machine logic from Dr. Strangelove, where the most credible threat is supposedly one where no human can even step in to stop it once its triggered. My teacher kept saying credibility is basically the whole game in bargaining, more than being strong or smart.

Then this weekend my friend sent me down a rabbit hole of AI safety blog posts and I ran into this thing people call the "commitment races" problem. The basic worry is that once AI agents get good enough to negotiate on behalf of people, companies, or governments, everyone has an incentive to make their AI lock itself into an irreversible commitment as early and as hard as possible, before the other side does, because whoever commits first and most credibly is supposed to win the bargaining game. Except committing early also means committing before anyone (human or AI) has fully thought it through, so you could end up with a race where agents lock in genuinely disastrous threats or promises just because speed mattered more than wisdom. The part that actually confuses me is whether a commitment coming from a program even counts as more "credible" than one from a person, or if that breaks Schelling's whole logic instead of just scaling it up.

I looked it up and apparently Schelling died in late 2016, right around when this stuff was starting to become a real conversation in AI safety circles, so here's what I want to know: what do you think he would've actually said about commitment races if he'd lived to see the debate? Would he treat it as basically his own 1960s theory finally playing out at full speed (a "told you commitment was everything" moment), or is there something about AI agents specifically that breaks or goes beyond what his framework can handle? Please use actual specific ideas from his work to make the case instead of just a generic "game theory says X" answer.
