So a while back my uncle ended up in the ICU after a bad accident and the doctors were talking about "brain death" and whether they'd need to run more tests, and it kinda sent me down a rabbit hole reading about how we actually decide someone is dead. I always just figured "brain dead" and old fashioned "heart stopped" dead were basically two different ways of measuring the same underlying thing, like two thermometers giving the same reading. But then I found this neurologist, Shewmon I think his name was, who documented a bunch of cases where people declared brain dead and kept on machines still did all this integrated body stuff for months or even years afterward — healing wounds, fighting off infections, growing — and theres even cases of pregnant women who were declared brain dead being kept on support long enough to deliver a baby. That made me wonder if "the brain died" is really the same claim as "the organism as a whole died."

Then on the other side I got curious about organ donation after circulatory death, and apparently doctors only wait a few minutes (I've seen numbers anywhere from like 2 to 5 depending on where you look) after the heart stops before calling it and taking the organs, even though everyone seems to admit that in those first few minutes the heart could sometimes still be restarted with CPR or a shock. So "irreversible" doesn't actually mean "could never come back," it apparently means more like "we've decided not to try." That seems like a pretty different concept of dead than what regular people mean when they say the word.

Can you compare these two ways of declaring death for me, the whole brain standard and the circulatory standard, and help me figure out whether theyre really just two roads to the same single destination (like "irreversible loss of the organism functioning as a whole," which is the official line I keep seeing) or whether doctors and lawmakers are quietly using two different concepts of death under one legal label because it's convenient, especially for stuff like transplants, to be able to call someone dead sooner. Is there a version of this where both standards genuinely point at the same underlying thing, or is it basically what one bioethicist I read called a "legal fiction" everyone's agreed to go along with? I want you to actually walk through the philosophical reasoning behind both criteria rather than just explaining the medical procedures, and tell me which side you find more convincing and why.
