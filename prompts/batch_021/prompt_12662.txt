I'm a retired geophysicist who's gotten completely hooked on philosophy since I retired, and my book club just worked through a biography of Kepler, which sent me down a rabbit hole afterward. I knew the broad strokes -- elliptical orbits, the laws of planetary motion, all that -- but I had no idea he spent years defending a scaled-down version of astrology against a physician named Feselius who wanted to throw the whole enterprise out. Apparently Kepler cast himself as the "third man in the middle" between Feselius (total rejection) and another astrologer, Röslin, whose predictions Kepler thought were way overblown, and he wrote a whole pamphlet comparing most of traditional astrology to a dung heap that, if you're patient, occasionally has a pearl buried in it. What really got me, as someone who spent a career actually measuring the planet, is that Kepler seems to have thought the Earth itself has a kind of "soul" that reponds to the angles between planets -- not the zodiac signs or houses, which he apparently considered arbitrary human bookkeeping, but the actual geometric aspects, because those correspond to musically consonant ratios and can supposedly resonate with something in a soul the way a chord does.

Anyway, I'd love to see this actually fought out rather than read another paragraph summarizing it. Could you write me a dialogue between Kepler and Feselius, imagined as a real face-to-face argument rather than the pamphlets-years-apart thing that actually happened? I want Feselius to be genuinely sharp, not just there to be corrected -- really pressing Kepler on the mechanism question, on why "the angle matters but the sign doesn't" is any less arbitrary than what he's rejecting outright, and on the fact that Kepler's own predictions didn't always hold up. And I want Kepler to actually defend his specific reasons for keeping some of this instead of retreating into vague mysticism -- the harmonic/geometric resonance idea, and whether that's really so different from the occult influences he otherwise claims to despise. Basically two people who've read each other's work closely, responding to the actual substance of what the other just said rather talking past one another -- I don't want either one to "win" just because the dialogue conveniently runs out of steam for one side.
