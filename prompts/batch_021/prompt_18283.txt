Three weeks ago I started a research rotation on an interpretability team, and the most contentious meeting of my short time there turned out to be about metaphysics rather than code. We had just trained a new batch of sparse autoencoders to decompose a model's residual stream into "features," and the write-up described the resulting features and the circuits connecting them the way a field guide describes newly catalogued species: as if we had discovered fixed, mind-independent structures that the network itself uses to compute. One of the senior researchers pushed back hard, pointing out that a different dictionary size or sparsity penalty yields a different, equally sparse and equally "interpretable" set of features, that features routinely split or merge depending on such choices, and that nothing forces the network's actual computation to respect whatever human-legible carving we happen to impose afterward. The disagreement escalated into a real argument about whether interpretability is more like biology, discovering real, causally load-bearing kinds, or more like drawing constellations onto stars that don't care how we group them, and it ended unresolved because we had to get back to our sprint.

I would like to see that argument played out properly, with each side given its best shot rather than the truncated version we managed the first time around. Could you write a dialogue, picking up where we left off at the whiteboard, between two interpretability researchers: one a realist who holds that features and circuits are discovered, mind-independent components of a network's algorithm, and the other a skeptic who thinks all this talk of "representations" is at best a convenient, non-unique redescription with no deeper fact of the matter, closer to instrumentalism about scientific theories than to the discovery of real kinds in biology. I want them to engage with each other's strongest moves rather than talk past one another: the realist should have to answer the non-uniqueness and superposition worries head-on instead of just asserting that features are real, and the skeptic should have to reckon with cases like linear probes recovering a board state from inside a model trained only to predict the next move, which feels like more than an artifact of whatever basis the analyst happened to choose.
