I spent thirty-five years as an atmospheric chemist and have only taken up philosophy seriously since retiring, mainly through a reading group that meets at the local university library. A disagreement flared up at our last meeting that I haven't been able to let go of. It started from a news story about a prominent climate scientist who was accused of hypocrisy for flying to conferences around the world while telling the rest of us to cut our emissions, and it split the group cleanly in two. Half thought the hypocrisy charge was simply beside the point: if his warnings about emissions are true, they remain true regardless of his own flying habits, so bringing up his hypocrisy is just a "you too" move that settles nothing. The other half thought this was too quick, insisting that there is something real called standing to blame that a hypocrite forfeits, so that even a wholly accurate accusation can be one its target is entitled to simply wave off -- not because it's false, but because of who is making it.

I'd like you to write a dialogue between two philosophers, equally sharp and equally well prepared, who take these two sides -- picture them staying behind after our session to argue it out properly over coffee. Please have them get precise about what losing standing could actually amount to: does hypocrisy silence a piece of blame outright, so its target owes it no answer at all; does it merely discredit or weaken it, the way a biased witness's testimony is weakened without being worthless; or is it really only a separate charge against the blamer's character (rudeness, inconsistency) that leaves the original accusation completely untouched? I'd also like them to test their positions against a couple of harder cases rather than just trading intuitions in the abstract: first, whether someone who has stopped doing the very thing they now condemn -- a reformed tax cheat berating tax cheats, say -- stands differently from someone still doing it; and second, a puzzle I recall from an essay by G. A. Cohen on who has standing to condemn terrorism (assuming I'm remembering our reading correctly), about whether a bystander who is "pure" only because they were never in a position to commit the wrong in question thereby has any better standing than the hypocrite, or whether that sort of untested purity is its own kind of disqualification. Above all, I want a real exchange rather than two monologues: each side should be made to concede a point, narrow a claim, or otherwise visibly adjust their position at least once in direct response to something the other actually says.
