As someone who has spent years reading LessWrong and the broader rationalist blogosphere, I've absorbed the view that conditionalization on evidence is close to the whole story of rational belief revision. Digging into the academic philosophy-of-science literature complicated that picture: the problem of old evidence, pressed by Clark Glymour against orthodox Bayesian confirmation theory, points out that a fact E already known with probability 1 cannot, by conditionalization, move the probability of any hypothesis H — yet General Relativity's explanation of the already-known anomalous precession of Mercury's perihelion was treated by physicists at the time as genuinely important confirmation, not a non-event.

I'd like you to compare two responses to this puzzle. The first, most associated with Daniel Garber, dissolves the tension by dropping the assumption that agents are logically omniscient, so that realizing H entails E counts as new evidence in its own right even when E itself was already certain. The second, John Earman's more skeptical verdict in *Bayes or Bust?*, holds that this move buys its solution only by trading one mystery for another, since it leaves unexplained how a non-omniscient agent should assign credences to entailment claims in the first place — and so does not really vindicate Bayesianism as an account of why the Mercury case counted as confirmation. Which response do you find more convincing, and is there any way to accommodate old evidence within Bayesian confirmation theory without simply relocating the difficulty? Justify your answer.
