I'm a philosophy PhD student with a bioethics focus, serving as the graduate fellow on my teaching hospital's clinical ethics committee. Our patient has complete locked-in syndrome and communicates only through a brain-computer interface that classifies her EEG activity into one of two output categories, which the team labeled "yes" and "no" based on her answers to a few questions with known answers, since there is no independent way to tell which category means what. Over several sessions she has since given inconsistent answers about withdrawing dialysis and ventilation, including one exchange where, asked twice whether she understands that withdrawal would end her life, she answered "yes" and then "no." Hospital policy requires "a reliable means of ascertaining the patient's wishes" before honoring such a request but doesn't say what reliability means for a system like this. The committee is split between those who want to treat the inconsistent and "no" answers as noise and act on her more frequent "withdraw" responses, which match what her family has always understood her values to be, and those who argue that once we explain away whichever answers surprise us, we have no independent check left on what the machine is telling us. Please draft the short opinion I'll circulate before Friday's meeting: it should recommend a concrete course of action with a timeframe, and it should take seriously the possibility that "discounting for unreliability" here is just a respectable-sounding way of overriding what she is actually, if inconveniently, telling us.
