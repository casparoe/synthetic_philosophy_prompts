I've been reporting on misinformation and belief for a piece I'm working on, so I've had "when is it actually reasonable to suspect people are hiding something" rattling around in my head for weeks — which is probably why an argument at my friend Dan's birthday dinner has stuck with me way more then it should have.

Dan's cousin thinks his university deliberately understaffs the campus shuttle on Fridays to push students toward a rideshare app the school has a secret revenue-sharing deal with. Dan was defending the theory at the table and his housemate Meg tore into him. (1) She said it's a textbook bad conspiracy theory because it's built so nothing can count against it: if a records request came back empty the cousin would just say the deal is verbal, and if an audit came back clean he'd say the school is just careful, so the belief is irrational by design, not because of anything specific to the evidence. (2) Dan said unfalsifiability can't be the whole test, because plenty of real conspiracies — corporate price-fixing, intelligence agency programs — looked exactly this "unfalsifiable" for years, with clean records and flat denials, right up until a leak or a lawsuit blew it open; you can't use "it looks unfalsifiable today" to dismiss a claim whose entire premise is that the truth hasn't surfaced yet. (3) Meg agreed real conspiracies can look like that for a while, but said there's a difference between a claim that's simply unconfirmed and one where every possible result of looking — a record, no record, a clean audit — gets read as more proof, and that the cousin has explicitly done the second thing by saying in advance that a clean audit would just show they're good at hiding it. (4) Dan conceded that specific move was bad, but said Meg was now just attacking his cousin's worst sentence instead of the actual claim, and that the real question is just how common this kind of vendor kickback is at universities generally, which is a boring empirical question about procurement and not a referendum on anyone's psychology. Meg's comeback was that this concession basically proves her point: if that's the real question, the cousin should be citing procurement patterns and vendor contracts instead of treating denials and clean audits as confirmation, and the fact that he's doing the latter is exactly the problem.

Nobody at the table agreed on a winner and I still can't decide. Could you go through the exchange move by move, 1 through 4, and say who's got the better of it at each step rather than just picking an overall winner? I'm especially curious whether Meg's unfalsifiability point in (1) and (3) is doing the work she thinks it is, and whether Dan's move to "it's just a procurement question" in (4) is really a clean escape from it — and if you think they're both missing some further distinction, I'd like to hear that too.
