In epistemology, Jonathan Vogel's bootstrapping problem — illustrated by his gas-gauge case, in which an agent seems able to vindicate an instrument's reliability just by repeatedly trusting the instrument's own readings, with no independent check — is often discussed alongside Stewart Cohen's more general problem of easy knowledge. Please compare the two: is bootstrapping simply one instance of the broader phenomenon Cohen has in mind, or does it involve a distinctive, and perhaps especially acute, kind of epistemic illegitimacy? Support your answer either by describing a genuine case of easy knowledge with no bootstrapping structure, or by arguing that every case of easy knowledge must, on reflection, turn out to be a case of bootstrapping.
