Over the past year I've been slowly returning to religious observance after a long secular stretch (something like a ba'al teshuva, if that term is familiar to you), and I now learn weekly with a chavruta at my synagogue. This has mostly been rewarding, but it has produced at least one real tension: I became a committed vegetarian on animal-welfare grounds well before this return to observance, and a few weeks ago my chavruta and I worked through the opening chapters of Sefer Vayikra (Leviticus), which consist almost entirely of exacting procedural detail about animal sacrifice. When I admitted to my study partner that I found this material more alienating than illuminating, he mentioned that this was not a new problem at all: Maimonides and Nahmanides had a serious disagreement about why the Torah commands sacrifice in the first place, one that goes well beyond "because God said so."

I've since read a little about this and think I understand the rough shape of it, though I suspect I'm missing a lot of nuance. In the Guide for the Perplexed (III:32), Maimonides seems to argue that the sacrificial cult was essentially a concession to the idolatrous norms of the ancient Near East — a kind of pedagogical "weaning" that he compares to the Torah's own explanation for why, immediately after the Exodus, the Israelites were led by the long route through the desert rather than the short route through Philistine territory, since a people not yet ready for abrupt change should not be shocked into it — rather than something God valued in itself. Nahmanides, commenting on Leviticus 1:9, rejects this quite forcefully and defends a very different account, on which the specific details of the rite (the substitution of the animal's blood and body for what the sinner deserves) are intrinsically meaningful, and he points, among other things, to the sacrifices of figures like Abel and Noah, offered long before any idolatrous culture existed for Israelites to be weaned off of. (Please correct me if I've gotten any of this wrong.)

What I would like from you is a genuine philosophical comparison of these two positions rather than a summary of each in isolation. Please reconstruct the strongest version of each side's argument, identify the single piece of textual or historical evidence each leans on most heavily, and say explicitly what broader view of the commandments (mitzvot) — roughly, instrumental/pedagogical versus intrinsically meaningful — each position commits its holder to. Finally, tell me which account you find more defensible as an explanation of animal sacrifice in particular, and note the strongest objection the other side would raise against the view you prefer.
