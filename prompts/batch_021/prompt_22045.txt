I'm a professor of chemistry and, for the first time this year, chair of my department's five-member Tenure and Promotion Committee. Our bylaws require the committee to find both that a candidate's research record meets department standards and that their teaching record meets department standards before recommending tenure; service wasn't at issue in the case we just reviewed, since all five of us agreed it was more than adequate. Each member submitted a confidential ballot recording a separate judgment on the research record, a separate judgment on the teaching record, and an overall recommendation.

When I tallied the ballots, three of the five members judged the research record sufficient and four of the five judged the teaching record sufficient — clear majorities on both individual questions. Yet on the overall-recommendation line, only two of five voted to grant tenure, while three voted to deny it. I went back and checked each ballot against its own reasoning, and no one was being inconsistent: every member's overall vote follows correctly from that same member's own two judgments, once you apply the bylaws' requirement that both be satisfied. It's simply that combining the majority verdicts criterion-by-criterion produces one answer, while tallying everyone's bottom-line vote directly produces the opposite one, and our bylaws never specify which of these two procedures is supposed to control. One of my colleagues thinks this resembles some voting paradox she ran into in a class years ago, but she can't recall its name — and even knowing the name wouldn't tell us what we should actually do about this candidate.

Our report is due in the Dean's office in twelve days, and since our votes have always been close to unanimous in past years, this exact situation has never come up before, so there's no departmental precedent to fall back on. Could you draft the memo I'll circulate to the other four committee members before we meet to finalize our report? I'd like it to recommend, with reasons, how we should resolve this particular candidate's case, and to propose a standing rule we could add to the bylaws so we're not improvising from scratch the next time our criterion-by-criterion judgments and our direct votes come apart this way. Please be candid about what's lost by whichever approach you recommend — I doubt there's a resolution here that doesn't have a genuine cost, and I'd rather weigh that now than be blindsided by it later.
