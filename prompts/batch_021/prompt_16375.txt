One of the liveliest disputes in contemporary work on moral worth concerns what more, beyond performing the right action, is required for an agent to deserve moral credit for it. On the Kantian view, an action has moral worth only if the agent performs it from the motive of duty; motives such as sympathy or personal affection confer no moral worth on an action, however benign they are and however reliably they lead the agent to do what duty requires. On a rival "de re" view, which Nomy Arpaly develops through her discussion of Twain's Huckleberry Finn, moral worth requires only that the agent be responsive to whatever considerations actually make the action right; the agent need not conceive of the action as morally required, and may even explicitly, if mistakenly, judge it to be wrong, as Huck does when he helps Jim escape slavery in defiance of his own professed conscience. Construct an original scenario — distinct from Huckleberry Finn, Kant's dutiful shopkeeper, or other standard textbook cases — in which these two accounts deliver opposite verdicts on whether the agent's action has moral worth. Spell out both verdicts, then say which strikes you as more defensible and why.
