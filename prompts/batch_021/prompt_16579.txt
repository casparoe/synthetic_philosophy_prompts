So I grew up in a pretty hardcore Reformed church, and a few years back I went through this phase of watching Calvinism vs Arminianism debates on youtube way too late at night, which is how I first ran into this 1500s Jesuit guy named Luis de Molina and his idea of "middle knowledge." As I understand it, the whole point is that God doesn't just know everything that WILL happen or everything that COULD happen, he also knows this weird third category in between: exactly what every possible person would freely choose in every possible situation they could ever be put in. So God gets to pick which circumstances to actually create, already knowing in advance what everyone will freely do in them, and that's supposedly how you get real libertarian free will (you genuinely could have chosen different) AND complete divine providence over every last detail, without God ever having to force anyone's hand.

Fast forward to more recently and I fell down a totally unrelated philosophy youtube hole about free will and moral responsibility, and ran into Harry Frankfurt's old thought experiment (turns out it's the same guy who wrote that "On Bullshit" essay) about a man with a chip in his brain that would force him to steal money from the register at work if he ever started to hesitate, except the chip never actually has to switch on because the guy goes through with it on his own anyway. The point being he still seems fully morally responsible even though, because of the chip, he literally could not have done otherwise. And it hit me that this seems like a real problem for Molina, because his whole reason for inventing this crazy complicated "scientia media" apparatus is to protect the idea that you have to be ABLE to do otherwise for a choice to be truly free and something you're responsible for. If Frankfurt's right that responsibility doesn't actually require being able to do otherwise, why would God even need middle knowledge in the first place? Couldnt he just be a straight up determinist about grace and everything else, and people would still be genuinely, truly responsible for what they do?

Obviously Molina never heard of Frankfurt, the guy died something like 350 years before that paper existed, but based on everything else he was committed to in the Concordia, what do you honestly think he'd say if someone put this to him? Does Molinism have a real way out of this, or does it expose something the whole system was quietly leaning on that doesn't actually hold up?
