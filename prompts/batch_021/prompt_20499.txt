I'm writing a longer piece on why some people's core beliefs seem totally immune to fact-checking — the "post-truth" stuff, conspiracy communities, that whole territory — and an epistemologist I interviewed for background kept throwing around the phrase "hinge propositions," tracing it back to Wittgenstein's On Certainty. So I actually dug up the book (hadn't touched it since one undergrad seminar a decade ago) and found what I think is the actual passage where he introduces the idea, in the Anscombe/Paul translation, remarks 341 through 343. Apparently this is all part of his very slow-motion argument with G.E. Moore's "here is a hand" business, which rings a distant bell from school, but I'm mostly just stuck on the passage itself, which I've now read about ten times and im still tripping over.

Here's the bit: "That is to say, the questions that we raise and our doubts depend on the fact that some propositions are exempt from doubt, are as it were like hinges on which those turn. That is to say, it belongs to the logic of our scientific investigations that certain things are in deed not doubted. But it isn't that the situation is like this: We just can't investigate everything, and for that reason we are forced to rest content with assumption. If I want the door to turn, the hinges must stay put." (Side question, probably dumb: is "in deed" split into two words there on purpose, or is that a typo that just survives in every printing? Feels almost too on-theme for a book obsessed with action vs. belief to be an accident.)

What I can't pin down is what kind of claim a "hinge" is even supposed to be. Is Wittgenstein just saying these are propositions we happen to be maximally sure of, the way an axiom is more secure than a theorem — or is the point weirder, that words like "know," "doubt," and "true" don't even properly apply to hinges, so they're not really beliefs at all, just the stuff believing gets done against? And in that last line, is "must" doing logical work, or grammatical work, or something else entirely — because those seem like very different claims to me. Basically I'd love a real walkthrough of what's happening in this passage before I put any of it in front of readers, plus an honest gut check on whether the "immune to facts" angle I want to use it for is a fair extension of what Wittgenstein's actually saying, or a distortion of it.
