My grandmother, Marguerite, died nine days ago at eighty-six, and I have been asked — as the family's resident novelist, apparently a transferable skill — to write and deliver her eulogy this coming Saturday. The room will be a genuinely mixed one: her three surviving sisters, most of her old bridge club, a dozen grandchildren, and my brother, who is two years from finishing a philosophy doctorate and will be listening for something none of the others will even notice if I get it wrong.

For the last decade of her life Marguerite had Alzheimer's, and she managed it, long after she had stopped reliably recognizing most of us, through an elaborate system of index cards that had begun decades earlier as the ordinary habit of a woman who liked to keep track of things, and hardened by necessity into something closer to a second memory: who everyone was, what she had already told you twice, which pills came before lunch. My brother has taken to calling her, only half in jest, "the most literal Otto anyone in this family will ever meet." He means Otto the Alzheimer's patient from an old thought experiment by Andy Clark and David Chalmers, who consulted a notebook the way the rest of us consult memory, and whom Clark and Chalmers argued had, in a perfectly literal sense, part of his mind sitting on the page rather than behind his eyes — not a prosthetic standing outside his cognition but a working piece of it, precisely because he checked it constantly, trusted what it told him without a second thought, and had put every word there himself, over years, by his own earlier hand. My brother wants the eulogy to lean on this, gently: to say that Marguerite was not fading behind her cards during those ten years but was, in some real sense, distributed across them — as fully present as anyone whose memory happens to live entirely on the inside.

I want to get this right, and I can see two ways to fail. The philosophical way is to let the idea run away with the eulogy: half the room has never heard of the extended mind thesis and did not come to a funeral for an argument, however elegant, about the metaphysics of memory — they want to feel something true about where their sister or grandmother actually was, not follow a proof that she was there. The other is a constraint from Marguerite herself, who, in one of her last unambiguously lucid weeks, made my mother promise that nobody would stand up at this service and describe her as having "gone behind the cards" once she came to depend on them, since she considered that both maudlin and, by her own lights, false. What I'd like, then, is a eulogy of about five minutes that takes the philosophy seriously enough to let it do real work — shaping what I am and am not entitled to claim about the last decade of her life — without turning into a seminar, and without helping itself to a certainty about the metaphysics of mind that even my brother admits the theory was never quite built to supply.
