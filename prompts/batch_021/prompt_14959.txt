I'm the patient representative on my hospital's genomics ethics advisory committee -- they always want atleast one non-doctor in the room so it's not just clinicians talking to clinicians. We do whole-genome sequencing for a bunch of reasons, diagnostic and research both, and every so often it turns up what the geneticists call a "secondary finding" that has nothing to do with why the test was ordered in the first place. Nobody argues about the findings you can actually act on, like a BRCA mutation. The fight that's eaten our last three meetings is about the other bucket: serious, well-established findings for conditions with no treatment or prevention at all, like a variant that hugely raises your lifetime odds of something like Huntington's or early-onset Alzheimer's. Right now patients tick a box up front opting out of ever hearing about that bucket, and roughly a third of them do.

Two of our physicians want to kill the opt-out entirely, and one of them keeps citing something he calls "Good's theorem" from decision theory. I looked it up on my own since none of this is my background -- as far as I can tell it's an old result showing that for a rational Bayesian decision-maker, cost-free information can never leave you worse off in expectation, since you can always just act like you never saw it. His argument is basically that withholding accurate information from a competent adult can't make them better off, full stop, so the opt-out is just paternalism wearing an "autonomy" costume. Our nurses and the hospital chaplain are pushing back with "right not to know" arguments -- dread, insurance and family fallout, and the fact that there's no decision left to make once nothing can be treated or prevented. I've been reading around this on my own time and I have to admit the "information can't hurt a rational person" argument sounds pretty airtight to me, but something about applying it here feels off and I can't pin down what.

Could you write the short decision memo I'd actually bring to our next meeting? I need it to land on one concrete recommendation -- keep the opt-out, drop it, or change what it covers -- with a rough timeline for rolling whatever we pick into next year's consent forms. And it has to really engage with the Good's-theorem argument rather than just wave it off, because the doctor pushing it is sharp and will notice immediately if the memo dodges instead of showing exactly where, if anywhere, the argument stops applying to a case like this.
