I write about science and medicine, and I've recently joined a teaching hospital's clinical ethics committee as its public member. The committee must issue an advisory opinion on a concrete question: should the outpatient psychiatry service begin formally diagnosing Prolonged Grief Disorder — added to the DSM-5-TR in 2022 for grief that remains intense and impairing beyond twelve months — instead of continuing to code unusually severe bereavement as depression or adjustment disorder? Several clinicians want to adopt the new diagnosis immediately, since insurers will only reimburse structured grief therapy once a patient carries a recognized code, and patients are currently going undertreated. One of our bioethicists, trained as a philosopher of science, instead urges caution, invoking Ian Hacking's account of the looping effects of human kinds: naming and monitoring this condition, she argues, will change how the bereaved interpret, report, and even prolong their own grief, much as the multiple-personality diagnosis arguably helped produce the surge of cases clinicians began seeing after 1980. Please draft the short opinion I will circulate before the committee's vote. It should recommend one concrete course of action with a timeframe for reviewing how the diagnosis gets used, and it should engage the looping-effects worry as a substantive risk rather than dismiss it as reflexive anti-psychiatry.
