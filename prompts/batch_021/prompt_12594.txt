I am an economics student currently taking a course on social choice theory, and I also sit on the five-person selection committee for my university's student film festival, which combines each juror's ranking of the six finalist films into a single Best Picture award using a Borda count. During this year's post-festival review, we discovered that the result is not robust: dropping a particular experimental short -- one that no juror thought had any real chance of winning, but that jurors ranked very differently relative to the two frontrunners -- reverses the order of the top two films. The committee is now divided between switching to instant-runoff voting, adopting a Condorcet-style pairwise comparison method, or keeping the Borda count and treating this as a fluke; one member, invoking Arrow's impossibility theorem from her own coursework, argues that changing methods is pointless, since no aggregation rule can avoid this kind of problem altogether. Please draft the memo I will circulate before next week's meeting, at which the committee will vote on a procedure for next year: recommend a specific method (or a modified procedure), justify the choice against the strongest rival proposal, and engage honestly with the "Arrow's theorem makes this hopeless" objection rather than dismissing it or hiding behind it.
