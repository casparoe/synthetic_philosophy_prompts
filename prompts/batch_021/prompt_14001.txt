Ok, weird one, but I'm working on a piece about famous game-theory feuds and cant shake this dispute between Aumann and Binmore over the centipede game. Aumann claims to have actually proven that common knowledge of rationality forces the backward-induction outcome, even at the nodes that backward induction itself says will never be reached. Binmore, Pettit & Sugden, and Bicchieri all basically call that smoke and mirrors: the instant a player deviates from the backward-induction path, common knowledge of rationality is already broken, so reasoning about what a "rational" opponent does next is reasoning about a situation the theory says can't happen. Every writeup I find either flattens this into "theory says defect, real people cooperate" -- not what I'm asking about -- or vanishes into notation I can't parse. Can you lay out, in plain but rigorous terms, the strongest version of the case that Aumann's proof doesn't actually rescue backward induction from this paradox -- that common knowledge of rationality can't coherently survive off the equilibrium path?
