I have been reading through the classic dispositions literature -- Martin's finkish live wire, Johnston's masks, and Lewis's patch requiring the causal basis to persist through the stimulus -- and recently discovered that Kadri Vihvelin, and in a related vein Michael Fara, redeploy this same apparatus in the free will debate, treating the ability to do otherwise as itself a disposition. The resulting view holds that Frankfurt's counterfactual intervener doesn't remove this ability but merely finks or masks it, so Frankfurt-style cases never really refute the Principle of Alternate Possibilities -- a compatibilist "dissolve-the-puzzle" move dressed in far more technical machinery than the versions usually aired in rationalist-adjacent writing on free will. Randolph Clarke and other critics, who have dubbed this the "New Dispositionalism," call it overreach. Could you lay out the most important implications of the finks-and-masks debate for this dispute -- particularly whether the fixes that rescue ordinary dispositions from finks and masks really transfer to something as fine-grained as a choice, whether Frankfurt's intervener is better modeled as a fink or a mask (and whether that even matters here), and what it would mean for compatibilism if the transfer fails?
