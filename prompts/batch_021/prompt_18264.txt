I'm working through John Dewey's *The Public and Its Problems* right now for a reading group on pragmatism and democratic theory (we picked it over just doing the greatest-hits chapters of *Democracy and Education*, since apparently this is the book where he actually tries to work out a political philosophy rather than just an educational one). The basic setup in chapter 1 makes sense to me -- a "public" comes into being whenever the indirect consequences of people's conjoint actions get serious enough that they need looking after by officials, and that's basically what a state is for him. But right near the end of the chapter he writes this bit that's tripping me up:

"Nevertheless, our conception gives a criterion for determining how good a particular state is: namely, the degree of organization of the public which is attained, and the degree in which its officers are so constituted as to perform their function of caring for public interests. But there is no a priori rule which can be laid down and by which when it is followed a good state will be brought into existence. In no two ages or places is there the same public. Conditions make the consequences of associated action and the knowledge of them different. In addition the means by which a public can determine the government to serve its interests vary. Only formally can we say what the best state would be. In concrete fact, in actual and concrete organization and structure, there is no form of state which can be said to be the best: not at least till history is ended, and one can survey all its varied forms. The formation of states must be an experimental process. The trial process may go on with diverse degrees of blindness and accident, and at the cost of unregulated procedures of cut and try, of fumbling and groping, without insight into what men are after or clear knowledge of a good state even when it is achieved. Or it may proceed more intelligently, because guided by knowledge of the conditions which must be fulfilled. But it is still experimental. And since conditions of action and of inquiry and knowledge are always changing, the experiment must always be retried; the State must always be rediscovered."

One guy in my group read this as Dewey basically admitting his whole theory is empty -- if there's no fact of the matter about which state is best, even "formally," then what is this "criterion" he claims to actually have a criterion for? Sounds like a pretty useless criterion to me too, if it can't rank anything against anything else. But that reading feels too quick, and I dont think Dewey is trying to be a relativist here. Could you help me work out what's actually going on in this paragraph? Specifically I'm curious what work "criterion" is doing that's supposed to be different from an "a priori rule," and what he means by saying the state must be "rediscovered" over and over again -- is that just a fancy way of saying institutions need periodic reform, or is it a stronger claim, tied to his general theory of inquiry, that political knowledge can never really accumulate into anything settled? Basically I want to know if this passage actually holds together as part of his experimentalist picture of inquiry (where knowledge is always provisional and gets tested against consequences) or whether my groupmate has a point and it's just a dodge.
