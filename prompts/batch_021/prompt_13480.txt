I've spent a long time reading rationalist-adjacent blogs and forums -- Overcoming Bias, then LessWrong, then the various Substacks that grew out of that scene -- and one strand I keep coming back to is the game-theoretic literature on commitment: Schelling's old observation that a bargainer can gain by publicly and irrevocably narrowing his own options (burning the bridge, throwing the steering wheel out the window in a game of chicken), and the considerably darker gloss a LessWrong post put on it years later under the heading "the commitment races problem" -- the worry that once agents understand that moving first and committing hardest wins, they are pushed toward committing earlier and more rigidly than their actual state of knowledge or reflection warrants, and that a world full of such agents can back itself into mutually terrible outcomes that no individual agent chose or would endorse on reflection.

This has stopped being armchair interest for me, because I now work in policy at a frontier AI lab and sit on what we internally call the Commitments Review Committee -- the body that has to sign off before the company makes any public, binding pledge about how it will train or deploy future models. Comms leadership and a couple of senior researchers are pushing hard for us to announce a "conditional matching" pledge: if a named competitor crosses a specified capability or compute threshold without adopting comparable safety practices, we commit now, publicly and irrevocably, to doing the same -- the idea being that a credible promise to match removes any advantage from moving first, and so should deter the rival from crossing the threshold at all. Two other members, who read the same corner of the internet I do, are refusing to sign off, on the grounds that this is close to a textbook commitment race: we would be locking in a hair-trigger, symmetric-retaliation policy on the basis of unavoidably incomplete information about a rival's real intentions and capabilities, with no real mechanism for verifying that a "crossing" has actually happened rather than being rumored or staged, and no graceful way to back out if it later looks, with more reflection, like a mistake. The committee is scheduled to vote in three weeks.

Could you draft the memo I'll circulate before that vote? I need an actual recommendation -- approve the pledge, reject it, or approve a modified version -- argued in a way the pro-commitment faction could engage with rather than dismiss, since their deterrence rationale is genuinely the strongest thing the proposal has going for it and I don't want the memo to strawman it. If you land on a modified or conditional recommendation, please be concrete about what would need to change -- verification, scope, sunset or review provisions, whatever you think actually matters here -- and about the timeline on which the committee should revisit the question.
