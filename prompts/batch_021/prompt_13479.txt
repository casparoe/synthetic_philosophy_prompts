This semester, in a graduate seminar on the metaphysics and ethics of death, we spent a session on the criteria used to pronounce death for purposes of organ procurement. As background, we discussed the Uniform Determination of Death Act's two-pronged definition -- irreversible cessation of circulatory and respiratory function, or irreversible cessation of all functions of the entire brain, including the brain stem -- and how "donation after circulatory death" (DCD) protocols apply the first prong: clinicians withdraw life support, wait for the heart to stop, observe a short interval (in some protocols just a couple of minutes) to rule out auto-resuscitation, and only then declare the patient dead and proceed to retrieve organs.

One of my sharper students raised an objection that the rest of the class found immediately persuasive, and that I was not able to answer cleanly on the spot. Her point was this: since the very heart that has just been declared to have irreversibly stopped is, minutes later, transplanted into a recipient and beats again, its cessation in the donor plainly was not irreversible; DCD protocols must therefore either be equivocating on "irreversible," or simply declaring people dead who do not yet meet the statutory definition -- which would make the whole practice a violation of the dead donor rule dressed up in respectable-sounding legal language. I would like to put the same challenge to you: does this argument succeed, and if not, exactly where does it go wrong? I am especially interested in whether there is a principled way of understanding "irreversible" in this context that does not simply collapse into question-begging stipulation.
