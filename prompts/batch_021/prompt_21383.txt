My grandmother, a developmental geneticist, spent her career arguing against what she called 'code talk': describing DNA as a blueprint or message that specifies an organism, rather than a molecule that is chemically read by cellular machinery whose behavior depends on countless other factors that the metaphor erases. She used to joke that if anyone said she'd 'live on in the code of her grandchildren,' she would come back just to argue about it. She died last month. Her department is holding a memorial symposium, and after two of her former doctoral students give technical retrospectives on her work, I -- her grandchild, still in high school, whom she spent years walking through this very argument -- have been asked to close with a few personal remarks, in front of a room split between family who never quite followed it and colleagues who will notice immediately if I slip into the very language she spent her career resisting. Please write those closing remarks, about five minutes long: they need to leave the family with something true and comforting about what does continue in us, without reaching for the metaphors she rejected, and without turning into a philosophy lecture.
