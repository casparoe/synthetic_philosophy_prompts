After thirty-five years as a theoretical physicist working mostly in cosmology, I have spent my retirement reading through the philosophy of religion, and the fine-tuning argument is the one place where I keep suspecting that philosophers have missed something a physicist would catch immediately. It seems to me that Brandon Carter's weak anthropic principle already disposes of the puzzle: any observer capable of measuring the constants and asking why they permit life must, as a matter of necessity, already find themselves in a universe where the constants do permit life, so the fine-tuning we observe is simply a feature of who is around to notice it, not evidence crying out for a designer or a multiverse. Yet writers on natural theology, from Swinburne to Robin Collins, continue to treat fine-tuning as a live explanatory problem decades after Carter's principle became available. Could you help me see what, if anything, the anthropic response actually leaves unexplained, or tell me honestly whether this is a settled matter that the philosophical literature has simply been slow to absorb?
