Last month a friend and I spent the entire drive home from a performance of Don Giovanni arguing about its ending, and neither of us budged. She holds, with Kierkegaard in Either/Or, that Don Giovanni exists as pure musical sensuousness, and that the final sextet -- in which the survivors regroup to moralize that "such is the end of the evildoer" -- is a leftover opera-buffa convention that domesticates something the music has already shown to be far darker; the conductors who cut it, from the Romantic era through Mahler, were simply being honest about the piece. I argued, more along the lines Bernard Williams takes in his essay on the opera, that Don Giovanni is a dramma giocoso rather than a straight tragedy, and that the return to ensemble after the damnation is part of the work's irony rather than a loss of nerve. The question has since become practical: I sit on my city opera company's artistic advisory committee, and the director engaged for our season-opening production has requested exactly this cut, on the grounds that it will land harder with a modern audience. Our charter instructs the committee to weigh "fidelity to the composer's and librettist's dramatic intentions" against "the director's legitimate interpretive latitude" without saying which governs when the two conflict, and the committee itself is evenly split. Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate before Tuesday's production meeting, where the director needs a final answer so that blocking and orchestral parts can be locked in. It should recommend one concrete course of action -- keep the finale, cut it, or some third option -- and it should take the strongest version of the losing argument seriously rather than dismissing it.
