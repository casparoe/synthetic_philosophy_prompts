I spent close to 40 years studying beak-size variation in Darwin's finches, so adaptation was basically my day job before I retired. Lately I've been reading philosophy of science for fun, and Gould and Lewontin's old spandrels paper keeps needling me. Here's my hangup: even granting they're totally right that lots of traits are just byproducts of development or history rather then products of selection, that doesn't seem to touch the actual methodology field biologists use -- you propose an adaptive hypothesis, test it against data, and drop it if it fails. That's falsifiable science no matter how things turn out. So it feels like they're knocking down a much dumber version of adaptationism than what working biologists actually practiced. Can you argue the strongest version of that case: that the spandrels critique, whatever its other merits, fails to lay a glove on adaptationist methodology as opposed to some cruder 'everything is optimal' doctrine? Please actually engage with what's in the paper itself, not the pop-science version everyone repeats.
