Kind of a weird ask for you but I sit on a little committee that hands out a yearly grant to help a science writer turn a book proposal into an actual book — small potatoes, nowhere near a real fellowship, but the money's real and so is the fight we're having this year. One of our two finalists wants to write a popular book about "downward causation" in biology — ant colonies, the immune system, slime molds solving mazes, that kind of thing — and she leans hard on real physics cred (she name-drops Anderson's "more is different" essay a lot) to argue these higher-level systems have genuine causal power over their own parts, power that no amount of physics or neuroscience could, even in principle, explain away. Two of the other judges love it and think it'll be a Gödel-Escher-Bach for the complexity-science crowd. The fourth member of our committee, who has an actual philosophy of science background from before she became a writer, thinks the manuscript is quietly smuggling in a much stronger and shakier metaphysical claim than the physics she's citing actually supports, and that the proposal doesn't even seem aware its doing this.

I have to send the committee's decision memo out by Monday — fund as is, pass, or fund with specific conditions attached — and I don't want to just split the difference so everyone feels heard. Could you draft that memo for me? I want it to actually take a side and lay out reasoning the writer herself could read and respond to, not just vibes, and I want it to be honest about the strongest thing that could be said for whichever option we don't go with. Bonus points if you get into whether the move from "genuinely new laws and patterns show up at higher levels of complexity" to "those levels have causal powers science can never in principle capture" is legitimate, or just a sleight of hand dressed up in physics.
