I sit on my hospital's surgical quality and peer-review committee, which operates under a standing policy: any surgeon whose raw 30-day mortality rate exceeds 1.5 times the hospital-wide average over a rolling two-year window automatically triggers a formal competency review. Dr. Alvarez, a trauma surgeon, has just crossed that line, with an overall rate of 4.2% against a hospital average of 2.6%. Her defense is that once her cases are split by acuity she beats the hospital average in both groups — 0.8% versus 1.1% for low-acuity cases and 9.5% versus 11.3% for high-acuity cases — because she absorbs a disproportionate share of the hospital's most severe transfer and trauma cases. Several committee members are unconvinced: they argue the bright-line policy exists precisely so surgeons cannot argue their way out of bad aggregate numbers case by case, and that carving out an exception here would quietly gut the policy for everyone else. Draft the committee's decision memo for next week's meeting: recommend what should happen to Dr. Alvarez's review and to the underlying policy, with reasons the skeptical members could actually engage with, not just a restatement of her numbers.
