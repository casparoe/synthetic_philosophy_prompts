I recently began a short-term research contract with a small organization that studies cooperation and conflict failure modes among advanced AI systems, and one of my first assignments has been to prepare an internal briefing on "surrogate goals" as a proposed defense against acausal threats. I understand the background well enough: under decision theories that let an agent's choices be correlated with a threatener's predictions even in the absence of any causal channel between the two parties (the same logic underlying Roko's basilisk and similar scenarios), an agent can be extorted through a threat against something it values, and the literature associated with groups like the Center on Long-Term Risk proposes that an agent inoculate itself by publicly committing, in advance, to treat some cheap, invented "surrogate" value exactly as it would treat a real one, so that a rational threatener is drawn to extort the decoy rather than the genuine target.

What I have not managed to resolve to my own satisfaction, and what a couple of colleagues and I have gone back and forth on over lunch without landing anywhere stable, is what looks like a fatal circularity in the proposal. For the mechanism to work, the commitment to defend the surrogate has to be credible enough that an accurate predictor of the agent cannot tell it apart from a genuine commitment -- otherwise the threatener simply sees through the ruse and goes straight for the real target. But if the commitment is that convincing, the agent seems to have manufactured, by the very act of making it, a second thing it will really pay to protect, which should be just as exploitable as the first, or exploitable in addition to it. If, instead, the commitment is understood by the agent itself as somehow "not really real," it is hard to see why an ideal predictor would be taken in by it at all. I would like an intuitive, gut-level account of how, or whether, surrogate goals actually thread this needle: what specific asymmetry in the underlying threat game is supposed to make diverting a threat onto a surrogate goal a genuine improvement rather than a wash, and, if you think the standard proposal does not fully succeed, where exactly the argument breaks down.
