I spent forty years as a biologist, a good part of it wrestling causal claims out of observational data in which confounding variables lurked everywhere; now retired, I have taken up reading decision theory for pleasure, and I keep returning to Newcomb's problem alongside its lesser-known cousin, the Smoking Lesion problem. The pairing feels close to home, since the Smoking Lesion case is essentially a formalized version of Ronald Fisher's old, and now discredited, suggestion that the smoking–lung cancer correlation might reflect a shared genetic predisposition rather than any causal effect of the cigarette itself. As I understand matters, whichever decision theory recommends one-boxing in Newcomb's problem seems thereby committed to recommending that the agent in the Smoking Lesion case abstain from smoking purely to avoid unwelcome news about her genes, which strikes most people as poor advice, while whichever theory happily permits her to smoke seems thereby committed to two-boxing. I would like a careful comparison of the two problems on precisely this point: can any single decision theory deliver what we would intuitively regard as the correct verdict in both cases, or does every remedy on offer, including Eells's "tickle defense", merely relocate the tension rather than dissolve it? Please offer your own reasoned judgment as to whether the two problems are, at bottom, pulling decision theory in incompatible directions, and, if so, which pull you think we ought to resist.
