I am a backend engineer with no formal background in philosophy or economics, but a disagreement between two colleagues that played out at a recent post-retrospective gathering has stuck with me, and I would like a neutral outside reading of it, evaluated move by move rather than as a single overall verdict. Some context: over roughly three weeks last quarter, four of our seven engineers migrated their services off RabbitMQ and onto Kafka, one after another, without anyone running a proper comparative benchmark against our actual production workload; each successive switcher seemed to be influenced mainly by how many teammates had already made the move. Two colleagues, Marta and Devon, ended up arguing over drinks about whether that pattern of adoption counted as rational, and I wrote down the exchange afterward as faithfully as I could, because I genuinely could not tell who had the better of it.

(1) Marta opened by calling it plain herd behavior: nobody individually evaluated Kafka against RabbitMQ for our workload, people just copied whoever had switched already, and dressing that up as "engineering judgment" was generous. (2) Devon pushed back: properly benchmarking a broker under our production load would cost each engineer a couple of weeks, so given that cost, it was actually rational for each successive person to treat "three teammates already switched" as stronger evidence than their own single weak signal -- a good blog post here, a decent experience at a previous job there. He called this an "informational cascade" and said it was a well-studied rational pattern, not a bias. (3) Marta replied that if the switch really reflected pooled evidence, it shouldn't have unraveled in a single afternoon when our infra lead mentioned, almost as an aside, that Kafka had burned his last company on message-ordering guarantees under load; one offhand comment outweighing the supposed judgment of four engineers sounded to her less like rational updating and more like proof that the original "consensus" had never been real evidence in the first place. (4) Devon answered that the reversal was exactly what the theory predicts, not a problem for it: once people start copying instead of contributing new independent signals, the appearance of a four-person consensus is really backed by only the first switcher's original information, so a single credible new fact can rationally outweigh that thin foundation -- meaning everyone could have been rational both in switching and in reversing. (5) Marta pointed out that, on Devon's own account, the second switcher had privately admitted he moved partly because he didn't want to be the last person maintaining "legacy" queue code if the migration later became mandatory -- which is reputation management, not belief-updating, and she said it was misleading to describe the whole episode as one rational informational process when at least one link in the chain was doing something else entirely. (6) Devon conceded the career-concern motive was probably real for that one engineer, but argued it doesn't rescue Marta's original claim that the episode was simply irrational: protecting one's standing by not being the visible outlier is also individually rational, just for a different reason than updating on evidence, and either way, individually sensible behavior by each person produced a team-level outcome that had little to do with which broker actually suited their workload -- which he thought was the more interesting point than assigning blame.

Could you go through this move by move rather than declaring an overall winner? I'm especially unsure about three things: whether Devon is using "informational cascade" correctly in (2) and (4); whether Marta's point in (3) actually damages the rationality claim, or, as Devon says in (4), confirms exactly what the theory predicts; and whether the reputational motive raised in (5) shows that "rational herding" and "conforming for career reasons" are being run together in a way that should change how we score (6).
