I'm several drafts into a novel whose protagonist is a game theorist who left academia to work as a kidnap-and-ransom negotiator, and in the interest of getting her professional instincts right, I've been auditing a graduate game theory seminar at a university near me (the professor has kindly let a novelist sit in the back and take notes). During last week's session, two students, Marisol and Theo, got into a real dispute over a homework problem on iterated elimination of dominated strategies, and it ran on long enough, and grew sharp enough, that I copied most of it into my notebook, half-suspecting it might end up, suitably disguised, in the novel itself. The setup: Player 1 can choose Top, Middle, or Bottom; Player 2 can choose Left or Right; and only Player 1's payoffs matter for what follows, since the disagreement stayed entirely on Player 1's side of the game. Top pays 3 if Left and 0 if Right; Middle pays 0 if Left and 3 if Right; Bottom pays a flat 1 regardless of what Player 2 does.

(1) Marisol said Bottom survives the first round, since neither Top nor Middle beats it in both columns -- Top loses to Bottom when Player 2 plays Right, Middle loses to it when Player 2 plays Left, so nothing dominates it yet.
(2) Theo said that's only true if you restrict yourself to pure strategies: a fifty-fifty mix of Top and Middle earns 1.5 whichever column Player 2 picks, which beats Bottom's guaranteed 1 either way, so Bottom is dominated too, just not by anything on the original list.
(3) Marisol objected that this felt like a sleight of hand: Top alone doesn't beat Bottom, Middle alone doesn't beat Bottom, and reaching for some blend of the two that nobody actually committed to playing seemed like solving a different problem than the one on the handout.
(4) Theo answered that whether the mixture is ever "actually played" is beside the point: once it's available, a rational Player 1 would never end up at Bottom, since something guarantees strictly more no matter what Player 2 does, and that, he said, is the whole content of the word "dominated."
(5) Marisol, willing to grant the arithmetic but not yet the procedure, asked whether this made the elimination process order-dependent in a worrying way: if knocking out Bottom on these grounds changes what Player 2's own strategies have to beat, could eliminating rows and columns in a different sequence land them on a different surviving set entirely?
(6) Theo said no: it's a basic feature of iterated elimination of dominated strategies that the order never changes the outcome, mixed strategies included, so there was nothing there to worry about.

They then turned to me to settle it, which put me in the strange position of arbitrating a technical argument despite the fact that my own expertise runs to dialogue and plot, not payoff matrices. Could you go through the six points in sequence and say which side has the better of each one specifically, rather than just calling an overall winner? I'd also be curious whether there's a genuine subtlety in the theory that both of them glide past without quite noticing.
