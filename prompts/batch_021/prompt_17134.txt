I spent thirty-five years as a field biologist studying animal communication before retiring, and for the last two years I have finally been reading the philosophy of science and philosophy of biology I never had time for during my career. Last month, at dinner with two old colleagues from my postdoc days, a remark about a mutual acquaintance who had made a great show of donating his annual bonus to charity the week before his firm's partnership vote set off a real argument about the foundations of costly-signaling theory — one that went well beyond the usual dinner-party version of the idea, and that I haven't been able to settle in my own mind since.

Marcus and Renata spent their careers on, respectively, pursuit-deterrent signaling in gazelles and status signaling among cooperatively breeding birds. The argument ran roughly as follows (tidied up a little, but not, I think, in substance):

(1) Marcus opened with the classical Zahavian claim: when signaler and receiver have conflicting interests, an honest signal of quality must actually cost the signaler something substantial — close to what a low-quality individual could not afford. That, he said, is why stotting in front of a cheetah works: remove the cost and every gazelle would do it, and the display would carry no information at all.

(2) Renata answered that this isn't quite what the equilibrium models require: the formal condition is that the marginal cost of increasing the signal falls as quality rises, which says nothing about how much a genuinely high-quality signaler must pay at equilibrium — that could be modest. What has to be expensive is cheating, not honesty.

(3) Marcus replied that "expensive to cheat" doesn't describe a different mechanism, only the same one under another name: somewhere in the system, on the path actually taken or off it, low quality has to be costly to fake, and that just is a handicap.

(4) Renata said that once purely counterfactual, off-path costs count, the claim that honest signals require cost stops ruling anything out — it's satisfied automatically by any stable separating equilibrium, wasteful or not, animal or human. A firm handshake or a sincere promise can be perfectly honest without being costly, provided insincerity would backfire somehow; on that reading, the wastefulness of the peacock's tail or the danger of stotting in full view of a predator is no longer something the theory predicts, only something it happens to tolerate.

(5) Marcus answered that the general game-theoretic condition is indeed that permissive, but Zahavi's substantive claim concerned which mechanism actually satisfies it in the systems he cared about: where interests genuinely conflict and there is no reputation, punishment, or contract available to enforce honesty independently, on-path cost is the only lever there is, so it isn't optional there the way it can be for a species, like ours, with other enforcement mechanisms to fall back on.

Neither would concede the other's central point, and I didn't feel qualified to settle it myself. Could you work through the exchange move by move?
