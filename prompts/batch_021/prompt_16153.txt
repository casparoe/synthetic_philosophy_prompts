Over the past few months, a methodological dispute I had always regarded as somewhat abstract has become uncomfortably concrete for me. I am finishing a PhD in social psychology, and a paper drawn from my dissertation was recently returned for revisions largely because of how the data were collected: following a practice that was standard in my lab, I checked the results every couple of weeks and stopped recruiting participants once the key comparison crossed p < .05. The reviewer, an unapologetic frequentist, argued that this kind of optional stopping invalidates the reported p-value, since it inflates the effective long-run false-positive rate regardless of what I "intended" when the study began. My co-advisor, who has been converting the lab to Bayesian methods, countered that the objection dissolves once one adopts a likelihood-based view: the likelihood function for the data does not change depending on why data collection stopped, so on her account the stopping rule simply should not matter to what conclusions the data support. I have since spent several evenings reading about what I now know is called the Likelihood Principle, including some of the classic Bayesian arguments from the 1960s for why the reasons an experimenter stopped collecting data ought to be irrelevant to how the data are interpreted, but I still cannot work out for myself who has the better of the argument, and my revisions are stalled while I try to decide how worried to be.

Since I cannot get my reviewer and my co-advisor into the same room, I would like you to stage the argument they would have had. Please write a substantive dialogue between a committed frequentist statistician and a committed Bayesian statistician who end up debating this question at a conference bar after a session on questionable research practices and the replication crisis. Have them work through the optional-stopping case specifically: the frequentist should press the claim that ignoring the stopping rule means ignoring real, checkable facts about the error rate that a given stopping procedure produces under repeated use, while the Bayesian should defend the Likelihood Principle and press the frequentist to say what ought to happen when the "intention" behind stopping was never sharply defined in the first place (for instance, a researcher who simply stops "whenever it feels like enough data"). I would like both figures to be equally sharp and to respond to each other's strongest points rather than caricaturing the other side, and I would like the dialogue to make some real dialectical progress rather than ending in a flat agreement to disagree.
