This all kicked off on the drive back from our philosophy of law seminar, which in hindsight is a pretty on-the-nose place to have it. We'd just read Nagel's essay called (confusingly) "Moral Luck," plus the Williams essay of the same title that it's replying to, and my friend Theo latched onto the reckless-driver case -- two people equally careless behind the wheel, and the only difference is that a kid runs into the road in front of one of them and not the other -- to argue that if you actually take the logic seriously, blaming the one who hits the kid more than the other is basically indefensible. His conclusion was you've got two options: go full Williams and admit morality was never the clean, luck-free system Kant wanted it to be, or go full "control principle" and only ever judge the recklessness itself, never what happens after. He also kept bringing up Williams' idea of agent-regret, and the Gauguin bit about not being able to know if leaving your family for painting was justified until you find out whether you're actually any good, to say that even our gut emotional reactions are soaked in outcomes we never chose, so pretending morality can be purified of luck is a fantasy nobody actually lives by.

I pushed back that theres a version of the control principle that survives this: maybe the driver who hits the kid did do something worse -- an actual death happened because of him, thats just true -- without him being a worse *person*, or more blameworthy in the deep sense, than the other driver. That way you keep "only what's yours to control counts against you as an agent" while still explaining why we react so differently to the two cases, without the difference being irrational. Theo said that's just smuggling the control principle back in through a side door, that in real life we blame the unlucky one more as a person and not just for a worse "act," and that any theory saying we shouldn't is refusing to look honestly at how blame actually works. Forty minutes of this, parked outside his apartment, and neither of us budged an inch.

I want to see this fought out properly by two people who actually know the material cold, not two people just repeating themselves louder like we did. Could you write a dialogue between a philosopher defending resultant moral luck specifically (as opposed to circumstantial or constitutive luck) as real and genuinely damaging to the idea that only the will can be judged, and one trying to rescue something like the control principle -- through my act/agent move if you think it actually works, or a stronger version of your own if you don't, as long as neither side is a strawman. Stage it wherever makes sense to you, I don't care about the trappings. What I want is them actually responding to each other's specific moves rather than re-asserting their opening position louder, and if the honest upshot is a genuine standoff rather than a clean win for either side, don't force a tidy resolution just to wrap it up nicely.
