I spent most of my career as an observational cosmologist, doing large-scale structure surveys, and it's only in retirement that I've let myself get seriously interested in philosophy rather than just borrowing its vocabulary when convenient. Lately I've been working through Bostrom's writing on observation selection effects, and I keep getting stuck on the fork between the self-sampling assumption (SSA), which has you reason as if you were a random draw from the observers who actually exist within some reference class, and the self-indication assumption (SIA), which has you reason as if you were a random draw from all possible observers — so that a theory predicting far more observers gets an automatic boost in credibility just from the fact that you exist at all. This isn't idle: the same move keeps surfacing whenever cosmologists gesture at eternal inflation and the sheer number of observers it predicts as if that number itself were evidence for the theory.

I'd like you to write me a dialogue between two old colleagues of mine, Renata and Yusuf, who run into each other in the corridor after a colloquium in which the speaker made exactly that move — citing the vast observer count under eternal inflation as a point in the theory's favor. Renata has become a committed SIA partisan and opens by defending that inference; Yusuf, who has stayed loyal to SSA, should immediately press her with the presumptuous-philosopher worry: that her reasoning seems to let you settle open empirical questions about the size of the universe from an armchair, no new data required, which is more or less what just happened at the colloquium. I want Renata to actually answer that charge rather than wave it away. Then I'd like the pressure reversed: Renata should press Yusuf on the reference-class problem that has never been fully resolved for SSA, and remind him that SSA is precisely what generates the Doomsday argument's pessimistic update on how much longer humanity has left — then ask him whether he is willing to bite that bullet, or whether SIA's apparent power to cancel the Doomsday argument's force altogether shows that SSA is simply the wrong horse to back. If it fits naturally, I'd also like one of them to say a word about why they aren't tempted to sidestep the whole dispute via something like Radford Neal's proposal for full non-indexical conditioning, rather than choosing between SSA and SIA at all. Please have them genuinely engage with each other's strongest points rather than talk past one another — neither one is a pushover — and you needn't steer the conversation to a tidy resolution, since so far as I'm aware, none exists yet in the literature.
