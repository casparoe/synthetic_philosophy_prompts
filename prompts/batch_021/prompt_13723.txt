I am deep into revisions on a historical novel set in twelfth-century Fustat, and one of my viewpoint characters -- a young physician drawn into Maimonides' circle -- becomes quietly fixated on a passage in the Guide for the Perplexed where his teacher explains animal sacrifice as a kind of scaffolding: a concession to a people who had never known any other form of worship, meant to be taken down again once prayer alone could stand without it. I want the arguments running through this character's head to be real arguments rather than a novelist's paraphrase of them, so, for a small history-of-ideas podcast I keep on the side, I have arranged to interview a contemporary rabbi and Maimonides scholar who has built much of her career defending this "weaning" theory of the sacrifices against its critics, Nahmanides chief among them.

I do not want the episode to simply let her restate the theory for listeners meeting it for the first time; I want the questions to go where the real disagreement lives. Could you draft eight or nine interview questions to that end? At least one should press on Nahmanides' objection that sacrifice shows up long before there was any idolatrous habit for it to be weaning people away from -- Abel's offering is accepted with favor, and Noah's is explicitly called a pleasing odor to God, in both cases well before any pagan sacrificial culture existed at all. Another should press on the tension between treating sacrifice as a temporary concession and Maimonides' own ruling, in the Mishneh Torah, that the messianic king will rebuild the Temple and restore full sacrificial worship, rather than the practice finally giving way to prayer as the theory's own logic seems to predict. And at least one should ask whether the weaning analogy is doing any real explanatory work, or whether it is simply a story fitted after the fact to whatever practice the Israelites happened to have inherited. For each of these, please add a short follow-up question that anticipates the answer she is most likely to reach for, so the conversation doesn't simply stop at the first parry.
