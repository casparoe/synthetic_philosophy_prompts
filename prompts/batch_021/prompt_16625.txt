As a philosophy PhD student writing a dissertation chapter on Gödel's unpublished philosophical remarks, I've run into a tension between two of his settled private convictions that I haven't seen addressed anywhere in what I've read. In letters to his mother, Gödel defended personal immortality on broadly rationalist grounds: because the world is fundamentally rational and nothing in it happens in vain, it would be absurd for a mind to spend a lifetime developing toward maturity only to be permanently annihilated just as it starts to bear fruit, so some form of afterlife must exist to complete what earthly life leaves unfinished. Around the same period, though, he was defending -- in the 1951 Gibbs lecture and in his remarks on the mind-machine question more generally -- a strong anti-mechanism about the mind: he took it to be more likely than not that the human mind's grasp of mathematical truth infinitely outstrips what any Turing machine could achieve, precisely because that grasp depends on a quasi-perceptual mathematical intuition of an independent Platonic realm rather than on the execution of a fixed, finite set of mechanical rules.

Given both commitments, what do you think Gödel would have made of present-day proposals for "digital immortality" via whole-brain emulation, where a computational simulation of someone's brain is claimed to continue that person's existence? Would he have welcomed emulation as a plausible technological route to the continuation his rationalist argument already committed him to, or would his anti-mechanism imply that any such upload is at best a functional impersonator lacking whatever it was that made the original mind what it was? And if the latter, would that consideration push him toward favoring something like cryonics -- which preserves the original physical substrate rather than replacing it with a computational model -- as more consonant with his overall metaphysics? Please work through the tension itself rather than just declaring a winner.
