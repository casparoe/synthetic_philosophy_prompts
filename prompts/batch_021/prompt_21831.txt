In my school's philosophy club, we just finished a unit on the Epicurean argument that death is not bad for the person who dies, working mainly from Epicurus's Letter to Menoeceus and the relevant sections of Lucretius's On the Nature of Things. I found both strands of the argument surprisingly hard to dismiss: Epicurus's claim that "death is nothing to us" because there is no subject left to be harmed once we no longer exist, and Lucretius's related but distinct move of arguing that the eternity of non-existence after we die is symmetrical to the eternity of non-existence before we were born, so that if the latter doesn't trouble us, the former shouldn't either.

What I can't stop thinking about is how Epicurus himself, reasoning strictly from his own premises, would judge two technologies that obviously did not exist in ancient Greece but that people now discuss quite seriously as ways of getting around death: cryonic preservation of the body in the hope of future revival, and "mind uploading," where a sufficiently detailed scan of someone's brain is used to run them as software after their biological body has died. On the one hand, his whole therapeutic project is aimed at dissolving the fear of death and securing ataraxia, or freedom from mental disturbance, which sounds like it should make him unsympathetic to elaborate, expensive efforts to cheat death. On the other hand, Epicurus is also a strict materialist who holds that the soul just is a particular arrangement of very fine atoms that scatters at death, so it isn't obvious he even has the resources to say that a revived cryonics patient, or an uploaded computational copy, would be the same person as the one who died, rather than a new person who merely resembles them, a conclusion that would actually reinforce his original argument rather than give him any reason to bless either technology.

Could you work through how Epicurus's two arguments, the "no subject of harm" point and the birth/death symmetry point, treated as distinct rather than lumped together, bear on these two cases separately, taking his atomism seriously as a real constraint on what he could consistently say about personal identity rather than just assuming continuity across the gap? I'd also appreciate it if you flagged any places where you think his actual texts leave the answer genuinely underdetermined, rather than smoothing over the hard parts to give a tidier verdict than the sources support.
