A friend and I got into it this weekend over I.J. Good's old theorem on the value of information - the one saying that a Bayesian expected-utility maximizer should always be willing to look at free evidence before acting, since the expected payoff from conditioning your choice on the evidence can never be lower than just acting on your prior (the intuitive reason being that whatever posterior you end up with, it has to average back out to your prior over all the ways things could go, so there's no way to expect the evidence to systematically bite you - its basically a convexity/Jensen's-inequality argument wearing a decision-theory costume). My friend kept sliding from that precise result into a much bigger slogan, something like "a rational agent should never want to stay ignorant of anything, and no rational society should ever suppress or avoid information, since information literally can't hurt you." I pushed back with some half-remembered stuff about info economists showing that revealing information can straight up destroy insurance markets by wrecking the risk-pooling that made the trade worthwhile in the first place, plus the whole strand of game theory on the strategic value of credibly not knowing things (bargaining, commitment devices, that sort of thing) - but I couldn't turn that into an actual rebuttal, just a pile of examples that felt relevant.

So here's what I want from you: argue, as rigorously as you can, against generalizing from Good's theorem to that bigger "information can never hurt a rational agent or a rational society" slogan. Don't give me the cheap answer about information not really being free in real life - assume it's free, that's not the interesting objection. I want you to go premise by premise through Good's actual setup and say exactly which one my examples (or better examples of your own, if you've got them) are quietly dropping once you leave the single-decision-maker, fixed-problem, non-strategic case the theorem was actually proved for.
