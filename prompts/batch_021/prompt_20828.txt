okay so for my intro to philosophy elective we had to actually read the whole second essay of Nietzsche's Genealogy of Morals (not just the famous "blond beast" bits everyone always quotes), and i got kind of stuck near the beginning, in the part where he's talking about how humans turned into animals capable of making promises. here's the actual passage, typing it out from the reading packet our teacher gave us (not sure whose translation it is):

"To breed an animal with the right to make promises—is not this precisely the paradoxical task that nature has set itself in the case of man? is it not the real problem regarding man? ... That task of breeding an animal with the right to make promises evidently embraces and presupposes as a preparatory task that one first makes men to a certain degree necessary, uniform, like among like, regular, and consequently calculable. ... If we place ourselves at the end of this tremendous process, where the tree at last brings forth fruit, where society and its morality of custom finally bring to light the end to which they were only the means: then we find as the ripest fruit on its tree the sovereign individual, that resembles only himself, that has broken loose from the morality of custom, the autonomous supra-ethical individual (because 'autonomous' and 'ethical' are mutually exclusive terms), in short, the man who has his own independent, protracted will and the right to make promises—and in him a proud consciousness, quivering in every muscle, of what has at length been achieved and become flesh in him, a consciousness of his own power and freedom, a sensation of mankind come to completion... this sovereign man calls it his conscience."

what's throwing me off is that this "sovereign individual" guy sounds like Nietzsche's ideal person — autonomous, self controlled, reliable, keeps his promises, basically the opposite of "the herd." but isnt that the total opposite of what the rest of the book is supposedly arguing? i thought the whole point of the second essay was that guilt and "bad conscience" are like a sickness that happens when humans get civilized and have to turn their cruelty inward instead of outward, and that this is supposed to be a bad/tragic thing he wants us to see through, not something to admire. so why does he suddenly sound so approving of a guy whose whole deal is self control and being able to make promises, which seems like exactly the same internalized discipline he's supposedly diagnosing as a problem?? my friend told me she read somewhere that some Nietzsche scholars actually think this whole passage is kind of a trap or a set-up and isn't meant sincerely, but i dont really see how you'd know that just from reading it. can someone help me actually break down what's going on here — like whats the strongest case that he DOES mean it sincerely as his ideal, whats the strongest case that its more ironic/critical than it looks, and which reading actually fits better with the rest of the essay's argument about guilt and bad conscience?
