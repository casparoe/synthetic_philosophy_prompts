I'm a philosophy PhD student currently drafting a dissertation chapter on the epistemic regress problem, and I keep getting stuck on the same move in Peter Klein's defense of infinitism. Klein's setup rests on two principles he calls the Principle of Avoiding Arbitrariness and the Principle of Avoiding Circularity: roughly, that a chain of reasons cannot terminate in a belief for which no further reason is available without being arbitrary, and that no belief can serve as a reason for itself, even several steps removed, without being circular. He argues that these two principles jointly rule out foundationalism and coherentism, leaving an infinite, non-repeating series of reasons as the only structure that can satisfy both. The obvious objection is that finite creatures like us cannot possibly possess infinitely many reasons, and Klein's answer is to distinguish propositional justification -- there being a reason available for a belief, whether or not anyone has ever entertained it -- from doxastic justification -- someone's actually having thought a reason through -- and to insist that a belief only needs the former, so the regress never has to be consciously completed by anyone.

What I can't settle for myself is whether that distinction is doing real philosophical work or is just redescribing the problem in modal clothing. If "availability" doesn't require that a reason has ever been entertained, occurred to anyone, or entered into any actual inference, then it starts to look less like a fact about reasons a person possesses and more like a bare disposition attributed to her from the outside -- and if that's all it takes, I don't see why a foundationalist couldn't say exactly the same thing about her basic beliefs ("a further reason is available, it's just never needed"), or a coherentist about the beliefs in her loop. Could you reconstruct Klein's argument for why only the infinite series can satisfy both principles, and then assess, as charitably to Klein as the position allows, whether the propositional/doxastic distinction genuinely blocks this parity charge against foundationalism and coherentism, or whether it just relocates the original arbitrariness-and-circularity worry onto whatever it is that is supposed to make a reason "available" in the first place?
