I never studied philosophy formally. For thirty-two years I was the administrative officer handling zoning permits for a small New England town, and for most of that time I also served as recording secretary for the historic district commission, since a town our size couldn't afford to split the job. Something from that work has stayed with me, and after a year or so of reading about paraconsistent logic and Graham Priest's dialetheism on my own, I've written up the notes below. I'd like your honest comments on whether the central claim actually holds up — I'd rather be told precisely where it fails than be reassured in general terms.

Here are the notes.

For most of those thirty-two years, our zoning bylaw capped residential fence height at four feet without a variance, no exceptions written in. At the same time, the historic district commission's design guidelines, adopted separately with equal legal standing and never reconciled with the zoning bylaw because reconciling them would have reopened a fight nobody wanted, required "contributing" properties on one particular block to maintain street-facing fencing consistent with the surviving nineteenth-century fabric, which for those lots meant fences well over five feet. Both provisions were, and as far as I know still are, simultaneously the law of the town. Over the years the two boards ruled often enough in favor of their own provision that you can point to actual, official, on-the-record decisions establishing both "this fence is permitted" and "this fence is not permitted" for materially identical properties. Nobody thought the town was talking nonsense. Nobody thought either rule was meaningless or secretly not a rule. They were simply, deliberately, indefinitely inconsistent.

When I started reading about dialetheism, the example that keeps coming up is always the liar sentence — "this sentence is false" — together with the reassurance that a contradiction doesn't have to let you prove absolutely anything, provided your logic refuses to validate that step, which is what I understand paraconsistent logic to be for. What strikes me is that our fence rules look like a cleaner example of a true contradiction than the liar sentence does, not a messier one. Almost nobody doubts that the liar sentence is grammatical, but plenty of serious people suspect it doesn't manage to say anything at all, which is exactly why there's a whole industry of hierarchy theories and "revenge" versions trying to explain it away rather than accept it at face value. Our fence rule has no such escape hatch. It unambiguously says something, both halves of the contradiction have been separately and officially adjudicated as true of the same kind of property, and there is no clever reformulation that dissolves it — only a repeal, which nobody has bothered to pass in decades. If dialetheism needs a paradigm case where we can be confident we have a genuine, meaningful contradiction rather than a trick of self-reference, I don't see why the fence rule isn't the better candidate.

What I'd like from you:

1. Have I actually kept paraconsistent logic (refusing to let a contradiction prove anything you like) and dialetheism (the claim that some contradiction is actually true) properly separate here, or have I quietly slid from the first into the second somewhere in that argument?
2. Is the fence case really the same kind of phenomenon as the liar sentence, or only superficially similar? Is there a principled reason a logician would treat an inconsistency between two humanly repealable rules as fundamentally different from a contradiction that seems to be forced on us by the very rules of truth and self-reference?
3. Is there an obvious objection to my central claim that I'm too close to the example to see?
4. Is this a point that's already been made, and made better, by someone in the literature I haven't come across?

Please don't soften any of this on my account.
