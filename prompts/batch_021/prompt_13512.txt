"Explain the disagreement between methodological individualism and methodological holism in the social sciences, using Max Weber's concept of social action and Émile Durkheim's concept of social facts as contrasting illustrations. Then use 'Coleman's boat' (the macro-micro-macro diagram we discussed in class) to explain how a methodological individualist can still make sense of a macro-level cause, such as the one Weber describes in his Protestant Ethic thesis. Finally, state whether methodological individualism commits its defenders to denying that collective entities such as 'the state' or 'a social class' are real."

Here is my partner's answer:

"Weber and Durkheim represent the two classic poles of this debate. For Weber, the basic unit of explanation is social action: an action is 'social' when the actor attaches a subjective meaning to it that takes account of, and is oriented toward, the behavior of others. Because meaning only exists for individuals, any institution — a bureaucracy, a market, a church — ultimately has to be explained through the beliefs and actions of the people who make it up. This bottom-up requirement is methodological individualism. Durkheim takes the opposite view: social facts, ways of acting, thinking, and feeling that are external to any one individual and exert a coercive power over them, are a distinct, sui generis reality that can only be explained by other social facts, not by individual psychology. This is methodological holism.

Weber's own boat model shows how an individualist can still talk about macro-level causes: Protestant theology (a macro fact) doesn't act directly on capitalism (another macro fact); instead, it works by first changing individual believers' motivations and habits, which then aggregate back up into a new macro-level economic system. That is the whole point of drawing the diagram as a loop down and back up rather than a straight arrow across the top.

Because explanation always has to bottom out this way, methodological individualism really does commit Weber to denying that entities like 'the state' or 'a social class' are real in their own right — they are convenient names for very large, coordinated patterns of individual belief and action, not separately existing things in the way Durkheim's social facts are supposed to be."
