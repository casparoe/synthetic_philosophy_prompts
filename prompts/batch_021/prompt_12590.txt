I'm a philosophy PhD student working in formal epistemology, currently auditing a graduate game theory seminar that spent a week on the epistemic foundations of backward induction. The seminar's official summary was that common knowledge of rationality forces the backward-induction outcome in any generic finite game of perfect information (Aumann's 1995 result), but the assigned replies by Stalnaker and Binmore convinced me the real disagreement is subtler than that summary lets on, and I'd like to see it played out properly rather than just take the seminar's word for it.

Write a dialogue, set against the centipede game, between two epistemic game theorists who take opposite sides of this dispute. One defends Aumann's conclusion: that common knowledge of rationality, correctly formalized, really does pin down backward induction, even at nodes the theory says will never be reached. The other, arguing in the spirit of Stalnaker and Binmore, holds that this is an artifact of an illegitimate counterfactual -- that a player's rationality "off the path" cannot coherently be common knowledge, since reaching a node that was supposed to have zero probability is itself evidence that the common-knowledge structure has already broken down there, so nothing about subsequent play can be validly inferred from it (which is why persistent cooperation early in the game need not be a failure of rationality).

Push them to fight over the actual crux rather than a cartoon of it: the counterfactual buried inside "substantive rationality" -- roughly, that at every node, if that node were reached, the player would go on to play rationally. Have each press the other on how that counterfactual should be evaluated -- holding the epistemic model fixed, versus revising beliefs about the past once an "impossible" node is reached. Both are experts of equal caliber, and neither should get away with simply restating their conclusion: each has to answer the specific objection the other just raised.
