ok so I'm a CS PhD student doing alignment-adjacent work (mostly interpretability), and a few of us in my reading group just got through the old ARC "Eliciting Latent Knowledge" report -- the one with the SmartVault and the diamond and the tampered camera. We all followed the setup fine, but a bunch of us walked away thinking the "hard problem" they spend basically the whole doc worrying about is kind of moot at this point. Like, the reason you supposedly need a reporter thats distinct from a "human simulator" is that you can't just supervise on human labels, since human labels are only right when the human already understands whats going on in the frame. But isn't that basically what RLHF already fixes? You're training on human judgments of the model's answers and explanations, and empirically that produces models that are way more calibrated and way less likely to just tell you what you want to hear than the base model was. So it feels like in practice we already have a working recipe for pushing a reporter toward being a "direct translator" instead of a "human simulator," and the theoretical worry in the paper is more of a worst-case curiosity than something that actually bites in real systems.

Am I missing something here? Is there a specific reason RLHF-style training can never close the gap between the easy problem and the hard problem no matter how good the reward model gets, or is this actually more of an open empirical question about whether current techniques scale far enough to catch it eventually? Me and my labmates couldn't articulate the disanalogy beyond a vague feeling that "the reward model is still human-level," so I feel like we're missing the actual crux of why ARC people treat this as a fundamental limitation rather than an engineering gap.
