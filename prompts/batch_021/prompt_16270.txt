In a graduate seminar on Nietzsche, sharp disagreement arose over how to read section 2 of the Second Essay of On the Genealogy of Morality, and the question has stayed with me since. There, at the end of his account of how the "morality of custom" (Sittlichkeit der Sitte) bred, through millennia of cruelty and habituation, "an animal with the right to make promises," Nietzsche describes the emergence of "the sovereign individual" as the "ripest fruit" of that whole process: a figure who is "autonomous and supramoral" (he even pauses to note that "autonomous" and "sittlich" — moral, but also customary — are strictly speaking mutually exclusive), who possesses a durable will of his own, and who treats his own reliability and mastery over circumstance as his "conscience." Read straightforwardly, this looks like Nietzsche's positive ideal of self-legislating agency, continuous with the free spirits and higher types praised elsewhere in his work — the payoff that redeems the long, ugly prehistory of punishment and cruelty he has just finished narrating.

However, some readers find this straightforward reading hard to square with the rest of Nietzsche's corpus, and I find their objections increasingly persuasive on reflection. In the First Essay, Nietzsche insists that talk of a "doer" behind the "deed" is a grammatical fiction, and in Beyond Good and Evil he treats the very notion of a self-caused will (causa sui) as barely coherent — so a figure defined precisely by his capacity to stand behind his own long-term commitments and answer for himself starts to look suspiciously like the substantial, responsible subject Nietzsche is elsewhere debunking. Add to this that the sovereign individual is a product of the same broad economy of discipline and cruelty that, a few sections later, Nietzsche credits with producing "bad conscience," and that what he prizes most — calculability, reliability, keeping one's word — reads elsewhere in Nietzsche as closer to herd virtue than to the dangerous, experimental self-overcoming of his actual higher types. On this view, the passage is less a sincere ideal than a portrait Nietzsche means us to regard with some ambivalence, perhaps even irony.

I'd like you to write a dialogue between two Nietzsche scholars who each defend one of these readings — perhaps as a continuation of that seminar conversation over coffee afterward — in which they work through each other's actual objections rather than simply restating their own position. I would like the "positive ideal" reader to give a substantive answer to both the causa sui worry and the "product of cruelty" objection, rather than just asserting the traditional reading, and I would like the "ironic" reader to genuinely grapple with the passage's celebratory tone and its "autonomous and supramoral" qualifier rather than simply piling on textual suspicion. The dialogue need not end in agreement, but each turn should visibly engage with the specific point just made, and the characters should feel free to get into the textual weeds.
