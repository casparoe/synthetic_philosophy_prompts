I cover the history and philosophy of science for a general-interest magazine, and I've just joined the advisory committee for a science museum's new permanent gallery on the Copernican revolution. The two curators developing the Galileo section are at an impasse over how to frame it. One wants the familiar morality tale: an authoritarian Church suppressing an established scientific truth. The other objects that this flattens the 1615 dispute. Galileo's own Letter to the Grand Duchess Christina mounted a serious scriptural argument, drawing on Augustine, for why heliocentrism and scripture could not really conflict. Bellarmine's reply to Foscarini, meanwhile, was not simple dogmatism but an explicit offer to revisit scriptural interpretation given conclusive proof -- proof Galileo did not yet have, since his own best evidence, the tidal argument, was wrong, and stellar parallax wouldn't be measured until the nineteenth century. The director wants a one-page decision memo from the committee by Friday, recommending how the panel should frame this dispute, including a short sample of the actual text, before it goes to the exhibit designers. Write that memo: come down on one framing, but do it by engaging honestly with what's right in the losing curator's objection, rather than retreating to "there are two sides."
