I'm a backend engineer, and a few of us on my team recently started an informal lunchtime reading group on decision theory, partly motivated by a side project: an internal tool meant to help prioritize engineering bets under uncertainty (e.g., choosing between a safe, incremental win and a riskier project with a higher expected payoff). This week we worked through the Allais paradox, and before I bake any conclusions into the tool, I want to make sure I've actually understood why it's supposed to matter.

The setup, if I have it right, is something like this: people are offered a choice between (A) $1 million for certain and (B) a 10% chance of $5 million, an 89% chance of $1 million, and a 1% chance of nothing, and most people pick A. Then the same people are offered a choice between (C) an 11% chance of $1 million and otherwise nothing, versus (D) a 10% chance of $5 million and otherwise nothing, and most people pick D instead. This combination is apparently treated as a landmark counterexample to expected utility theory, but as far as I can tell it's really just describing ordinary risk aversion: people love the certainty in A, and once certainty is off the table in the second choice anyway, they might as well go for the bigger prize in D. So my plan for the tool is to fold this in by replacing raw expected value with a concave, risk-averse utility function over dollar amounts (something like a CRRA curve fit to the team's revealed preferences), while still always picking whichever option maximizes expected utility under that curve. Does that fully capture what the Allais paradox is pointing at, or is there some deeper problem with expected utility theory here that no amount of concavity in the utility function can patch over?
