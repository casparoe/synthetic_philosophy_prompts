I'm a backend engineer with no formal training in philosophy, so please forgive any butchered terminology below. A few months ago, my co-founder and I spent an entire day arguing about whether to rewrite our matching engine in Rust. We'd read the same benchmarks, sat through the same design review, and neither of us thought the other was stupid or arguing in bad faith — we simply never converged, even after going line by line through every argument on the whiteboard. That non-convergence bothered me more than the technical question itself did, and it eventually sent me down a rabbit hole into Aumann's agreement theorem and, more specifically, into the assumption doing most of the load-bearing work underneath it: the idea that two rational people share a "common prior" and differ only in the information each has since received. I wrote up a few hundred words trying to pin down what I actually think that assumption amounts to, and I'd like a sanity check before I take it any further.

Here's roughly where I've landed. Aumann's result is usually summarized as "rational people can't agree to disagree," but that's a slightly misleading way to put it: what the theorem actually shows is that two Bayesians with the same prior can't have common knowledge of differing posteriors on some event. Once you notice how much of the result's force depends on "same prior," it stops feeling like a discovery about rationality and starts feeling closer to a definition — of course two people with identical starting beliefs and identical subsequent information end up agreeing, because agreement was built into the setup. Bayesian rationality, as far as I can tell, constrains how you update a prior, not where the prior comes from, so the actually interesting question — whether it's irrational for two equally rational people to start out with different priors — is exactly what the theorem stays silent on, even though it gets invoked (including, once, by me, mid-argument, which is embarrassing in retrospect) as though it settles something about real disagreements. The most serious attempt I've found to rescue the common prior assumption from being pure modeling convenience is Robin Hanson's argument that you should treat facts about your own prior's origin the same way you'd treat any other piece of evidence, so an unexplained difference between your prior and the prior of someone in an otherwise symmetric position is itself suspicious and demands an explanation — an "origin dispute" — rather than a shrug. I can't tell whether that move actually derives something like a common prior from more basic rationality principles, or whether it just reintroduces the common prior as a default and dresses it up as an evidential requirement.

I'd find it most useful to get comments on three things in particular: whether I've represented Aumann's theorem and the common prior assumption accurately or flattened something important in the process; whether the "this is closer to a definition than a discovery" framing is defensible, or whether there's a standard response to it that I'm clumsily reinventing without realizing it's already been settled; and whether my closing read of Hanson's argument is fair, or whether I've just restated the assumption in fancier language and mistaken that for a justification. General reactions to whether any of this is interesting enough to be worth developing into an actual post are welcome too — I'd rather hear "this equivocates between two different notions of prior" now than after I've published it.
