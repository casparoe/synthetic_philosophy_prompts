Hume's essay 'Of Money' does this weird two-step: he insists the actual amount of money a country has doesnt matter for its real wealth (prices just scale up or down to match whatever's in circulation), but then argues a country should still want its money supply constantly increasing, because in the 'interval' before prices catch up, all that fresh cash stimulates merchants and industry. Then in 'Of the Balance of Trade' he lays out the price-specie-flow story, where any attempt to hoard more than your fair share of the world's specie is automatically self-correcting through trade. Doesn't that mechanism kind of undercut the whole reason for wanting an ever-increasing money supply in the first place? Write an essay on whether Hume's monetary economics actually hangs together once you separate the short-run and long-run claims, or whether he's just smuggling in a mercantilist assumption that his own trade theory should have killed off. Weigh the main considerations but actually commit to a verdict instead of landing on 'it's complicated.'
