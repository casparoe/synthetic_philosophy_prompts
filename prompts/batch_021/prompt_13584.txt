I'm drafting the climactic chapter of a novel whose protagonist, a mathematical logician, spends the book trying to convince a skeptical former colleague that a machine could never truly think the way she does. The proof she gives him, which I've pieced together from a few popular accounts of Gödel's incompleteness theorem, runs roughly as follows: for any consistent formal system rich enough to encode arithmetic, Gödel's theorem produces a sentence that is true but which the system itself can never prove; a human mathematician, unlike the system, can stand outside it and simply see that the sentence is true; so no formal system, and therefore no computer, since a computer is just such a system, can ever be the whole story of what a human mind does. From what I've read, most logicians now regard this argument (usually credited to J. R. Lucas, and later given a more elaborate physical grounding by Roger Penrose, who invoked some new, non-computable physics in the brain to explain how the feat is even possible) as basically decisive, even though that verdict hasn't yet filtered into the popular debate about AI. My plan had been to have the colleague raise a string of objections that get progressively weaker and more flustered, so that the protagonist's proof stands unrefuted by the end of the chapter.

Before I commit to writing it that way, I mentioned the plan to a friend who teaches formal logic, and she said the "proof" was shakier than I'd assumed, though she couldn't quite reconstruct the objection for me on the spot. So I'd like your help getting this right: is the argument, as I've summarized it, actually regarded as sound, and if not, exactly where does it break down? I'd rather you point me to something more specific than "philosophers disagree about this" -- I want the colleague's best line of attack to be sharp enough that an actual logician reading the finished book wouldn't wince at it, even if I ultimately decide to have the protagonist parry it rather than be defeated by it outright.
