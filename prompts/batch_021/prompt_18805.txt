So my nan died back in March, and near the end the whole family agreed to tell her the scan results were fine when they weren't, because she was terrified and it felt like the kind thing to do. I've been chewing on that ever since, and it just so happens I'm taking this adult-ed course on early Christian ethics where we spent last week on Augustine's De Mendacio and Contra Mendacium. Turns out the guy definately thought literally every lie is a sin, no exceptions, even one told to save someone's life -- and he's got this whole ranked list of like eight-ish "degrees" of lying, running from lies about church doctrine (worst) down to something like lying to keep someone's body safe from harm (least bad of the bunch, but still not allowed).

Here's what's bugging me: Augustine keeps insisting the wrongness of a lie has nothing to do with outcomes, that it's all about the mismatch between what's in your heart and what comes out of your mouth, completely apart from who gets helped or hurt. So how does he end up with a severity ranking at all, if not by quietly smuggling in exactly the harm/benefit calculus he says is irrelevant? Make the strongest case you can that Augustine's own gradation of lies undercuts his absolutism -- that the ranked list is basically evidence he doesn't fully believe lying is wrong "as such." Please actually engage with how the taxonomy is supposed to work alongside the intrinsic-wrongness claim, not just the one-line "Augustine thought lying is always wrong" version.
