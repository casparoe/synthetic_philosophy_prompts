Over the past few weeks I have been recovering from an emergency appendectomy, and somewhere between the tedium of bed rest and a lingering indignation at having lost an organ, I ended up reading everything I could find about what the appendix is actually for. The picture turned out to be far less settled than I expected: the traditional view that it is a functionless leftover from a larger ancestral cecum has been challenged by immunologists who argue that it may serve as a refuge where beneficial gut bacteria are preserved and later used to reseed the intestine after illness, while other biologists respond that even if the appendix does this now, that fact alone does not tell us whether harboring bacteria is a genuine function of the appendix or merely something it happens to be useful for. My sister, who is midway through a philosophy MA, told me this maps onto a real fault line in the philosophy of biology, between "etiological" theories of function (associated with Wright, Millikan, and Neander) and "causal-role," or "dispositional," theories (associated with Cummins) -- and that the textbook illustration of the split is a heart that both pumps blood and makes an audible thumping sound.

I would like your help working through this properly rather than just taking her summary on faith. First, explain what actually distinguishes the etiological account of function from the causal-role account: what problem each was meant to solve, and why the heart example is supposed to bring out the disagreement between them. Then apply both accounts to the appendix case, plus at least one further case of your choosing (a vestigial structure, an exaptation such as feathers that originated for thermoregulation, or a "spandrel" in Gould and Lewontin's sense would all work), showing concretely where the two theories converge and where they would hand down different verdicts. I am especially interested in how each theory handles malfunction -- a trait failing to do what it is "supposed to" do -- since my sister suggested this is much easier to accommodate on one view than the other, and I would like to understand precisely why. Finally, tell me which of the two accounts, if either, you find more defensible on the whole, or whether you think the dispute is ultimately more terminological than substantive.
