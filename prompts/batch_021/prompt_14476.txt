A friend and I spent an entire dinner arguing about Keynes's 1930 essay "Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren," and neither of us budged. We agree that his forecast of a roughly fifteen-hour working week by now was badly wrong, but we disagree about why. My friend blames exactly the mechanism Keynes himself identified: "relative" needs, the kind satisfied only by having more than one's neighbor, simply expanded fast enough to absorb whatever slack was left by the satisfaction of absolute needs. I think the more interesting failure is psychological. Keynes explicitly worried that ordinary people, "trained too long to strive and not to enjoy," would struggle to fill their new leisure meaningfully. What he didn't anticipate was that societies would solve this problem not by learning to enjoy leisure but by outsourcing it to algorithmically engineered, semi-compulsive entertainment that demands no cultivation at all. Whose reading do you find more persuasive, and what do you think Keynes himself would make of smartphones and infinite-scroll feeds as the answer modern societies actually found to the "problem" of leisure he predicted?
