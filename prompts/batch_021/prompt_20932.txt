Below is the question, worth 20 points, followed by the student's answer in full.

Question 3. The table below gives pass/fail inspection results for two annealing processes, run separately on samples drawn from two feedstocks of differing purity.

High-grade feedstock: Process A, 18/20 pass (90%); Process B, 153/180 pass (85%)
Low-grade feedstock: Process A, 54/180 pass (30%); Process B, 4/20 pass (20%)
Combined: Process A, 72/200 pass (36%); Process B, 157/200 pass (78.5%)

(a) [5 points] Explain why Process A has the higher pass rate within each feedstock grade, while Process B has the higher pass rate overall.
(b) [7 points] State, in general terms, what this phenomenon — Simpson's paradox — shows about the relationship between statistical association and causal inference.
(c) [8 points] One common statement of the "moral" of Simpson's paradox is: "When the aggregated and disaggregated figures disagree, the disaggregated ones are the ones to trust." Is this an adequate general statement of the lesson? Defend your answer, with reference to the different causal roles a third variable can play in relation to a treatment and an outcome.

Student's answer:

Simpson's paradox — sometimes also called the ecological fallacy — occurs when an association between two variables reverses depending on whether the data are pooled or split into subgroups. In this case, Process A has the higher pass rate than Process B within both the high-grade feedstock (90% vs 85%) and the low-grade feedstock (30% vs 20%), yet across the combined sample Process B has the better overall record (78.5% vs 36%). The reversal happens because feedstock grade is unevenly distributed across the two processes: Process A was mostly run on the low-grade material, which has a low pass rate no matter which process is used, while Process B was mostly run on the high-grade material, which has a high pass rate no matter which process is used. Once the combined totals are weighted by these very different base rates, Process B's overall record looks better even though it is the worse process within every feedstock grade that actually exists.

The general lesson is that a raw statistical association — here, "samples run through Process B pass more often than samples run through Process A" — is not by itself evidence for a causal claim about which process is more effective. Feedstock grade is a common cause of both which process a batch was assigned to and whether it passed inspection, so the marginal, combined comparison is confounded, and the numbers cannot be read causally until that confound is accounted for. This is why some philosophers of statistics describe the Simpson reversal as a veridical rather than a falsidical paradox: both the aggregated and the disaggregated figures are correct as descriptions of the sample; the puzzle is about which of them answers the causal question we actually care about, namely which process to run on the next batch.

Given that, I think the standard slogan — trust the disaggregated numbers over the pooled ones — is basically right, and it is really just an informal statement of Pearl's back-door criterion: once you condition on the variable that opens the spurious path between process and outcome, the confound is blocked, and the stratified association becomes the one to read off causally. Feedstock grade is exactly this kind of variable here, so stratifying by it is the right call in this example. I would generalize this into a rule: whenever a pooled comparison and a stratified comparison disagree, the right response is to stratify by whichever third variable is generating the split, since that is what the back-door criterion tells us to do in general. So the slogan, although a little loosely worded, gets the substance of the lesson right.
