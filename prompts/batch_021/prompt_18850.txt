In the causation reading group I help organize as part of my philosophy PhD, two of the other members got into a disagreement last week that we never resolved. The case that started it: a hospital patient on a cardiac monitor died overnight after the monitor's alarm — muted earlier in the shift because of repeated false positives — went off with nobody hearing it.

(1) Theo said the on-duty nurse's failure to check on the patient caused his death: had she looked in when the alarm sounded, he would have lived, and that counterfactual dependence is all causation ever amounts to, whether the cause in question is an action or an omission.

(2) Mara said this proves too much. The same counterfactual holds of the decision to staff the ward thinly that night, of a colleague on break who could have covered the monitor, even of a distant engineer who could have built an alarm nobody could mute. If bare counterfactual dependence were enough, all of these would count as causes too, which is absurd — so something more must be doing the work when we single out the nurse.

(3) Theo granted the point and proposed a fix: only the omissions of people under some specific duty to act count as causes. The nurse was assigned to that patient for that shift; the engineer wasn't.

(4) Mara replied that this saves the verdict about the nurse only by smuggling a normative notion, duty, into what was supposed to be a purely metaphysical analysis. On this view, whether the nurse's inaction caused the death turns on hospital scheduling policy and convention about whose job it was — which looks less like a theory of causation than a theory of blame wearing its clothes.

(5) Theo answered that causal claims are always filtered through some background of normal conditions anyway — nobody calls the ambient oxygen a cause of a house fire, even though the fire counterfactually depends on it too — so leaning on norms to pick out a cause is business as usual, not a special problem for omissions.

(6) Mara said that analogy elides a difference. Picking the noteworthy cause among several things that are, on anyone's view, already causes (the spark, the wiring, the oxygen) is one job; using a norm to decide whether something is a cause at all, rather than a complete non-participant, is another — and it's the second job the duty restriction is actually doing.

Neither of them budged, and I'd like an outside read. Could you go through the exchange move by move?
