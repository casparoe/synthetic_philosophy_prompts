I spent thirty-five years as a population geneticist, and since retiring I have been working through the philosophy-of-biology literature on the units-of-selection debate while also running a small podcast on foundational questions in evolutionary theory. Next month's guest is a philosopher who defends genic selectionism: the view that change in gene frequency is the only genuine causal process underlying evolution by natural selection, and that any formalism that appears to locate selection at the level of the organism, the colony, or the group is, as she likes to put it, 'a change of coordinates, not a change of ontology.' Please draft seven or eight interview questions that press on this deflection rather than let her simply restate it. I want at least one question on each of three things -- the fact that the Price equation admits multiple equivalent partitions of total selection across levels; Sober's distinction between selection of a trait and selection for it, together with the screening-off argument built on that distinction; and cases of genuine group-level reproduction, what Okasha calls MLS2, such as eusocial colonies that bud off daughter colonies, where the vehicle of selection looks irreducibly supra-organismal -- and for each, a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give. Close with one further question asking what finding, empirical or conceptual, would ever count for her as evidence that some level above the gene has autonomous causal standing.
