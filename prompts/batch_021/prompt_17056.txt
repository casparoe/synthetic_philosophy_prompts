A friend and I spent an entire dinner arguing about Robert Aumann's 1995 result that common knowledge of rationality forces the backward-induction outcome in games of perfect information, such as the centipede game, and neither of us gave ground. My friend defended Aumann's own line: rationality, on his construal, is a property of a player's entire strategy (a complete contingent plan covering every node, reached or not), so common knowledge of rationality is simply a fact about a state of the world that happens to pin down the backward-induction path; there is no need to ask what a player should infer upon reaching a node that occurs only if someone deviated, because the framework never requires anyone to reason "live" from inside such a node. I pressed the objection associated with critics such as Reny and Binmore: the argument for backward induction is only doing real work if it tells players what to do at nodes that, on the story so far, should never be reached by rational players, and it is far from obvious that common knowledge of rationality can coherently be supposed to survive at those nodes, since reaching one is at least weak evidence that somebody upstream was not rational after all. We went in circles for an hour and gave up.

Write an essay that settles the dispute. Explain precisely what would have to hold for common knowledge of rationality to entail backward induction, and why critics doubt that this can be coherently maintained off the equilibrium path. Then argue for a definite verdict: does common knowledge of rationality really force backward induction in perfect-information games like the centipede game, or does the standard argument for backward induction covertly rely on an epistemically unstable supposition? A qualified verdict is fine (for instance, one that hinges on a specific theory of counterfactuals or belief revision, provided you name and defend it), but your final word should not simply be that reasonable people disagree.
