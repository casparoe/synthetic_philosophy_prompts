I'm working on a feature about longtermism and effective altruism, and the "repugnant conclusion" keeps coming up as this thing everyone traces back to Parfit in the 80s. But while I was digging around for the piece I found out Sidgwick was basically already doing population ethics over a hundred years earlier, and honestly it kind of blew my mind that nobody in the EA debates I've been reading seems to dwell on the original passage much. This is from Book IV, Chapter 1, section 2 of the 7th edtion of The Methods of Ethics (1907), right where he's defining what Utilitarianism even means:

"Assuming, then, that the average happiness of human beings is a positive quantity, it seems clear that, supposing the average happiness enjoyed remains undiminished, Utilitarianism directs us to make the number enjoying it as great as possible. But if we foresee as possible that an increase in numbers will be accompanied by a decrease in average happiness or vice versa, a point arises which has not only never been formally noticed, but which seems to have been substantially overlooked by many Utilitarians. For if we take Utilitarianism to prescribe, as the ultimate end of action, happiness on the whole, and not any individual's happiness, unless considered as an element of the whole, it would follow that, if the additional population enjoy on the whole positive happiness, we ought to weigh the amount of happiness gained by the extra number against the amount lost by the remainder. So that, strictly conceived, the point up to which, on Utilitarian principles, population ought to be encouraged to increase, is not that at which average happiness is the greatest possible, as appears to be often assumed by political economists of the school of Malthus -- but that at which the product formed by multiplying the number of persons living into the amount of average happiness reaches its maximum. It may be well here to make a remark which has a wide application in Utilitarian discussion. The conclusion just given wears a certain air of absurdity to the view of Common Sense; because its show of exactness is grotesquely incongruous with our consciousness of the inevitable inexactness of all such calculations in actual practice."

What I can't quite untangle is the actual logic, as opposed to just the headline conclusion everyone quotes ("maximize the product, not the average"). When he says we should "weigh the amount of happiness gained by the extra number against the amount lost by the remainder," what's the precise move being made there, and is that move already the full total view as population ethicists define it today, or is there some extra assumption sneaking in? And separately -- when he says the conclusion "wears a certain air of absurdity," is that basically Sidgwick flinching at an early version of what we'd now call the repugnant conclusion (huge numbers of barely-happy people beating out a smaller, very happy population), or is he actually complaining about something else entirely, like the pretense of mathematical precision in ethical calculation? I've seen this passage cited a bunch as "Sidgwick basically invented the total view," but nobody spells out whether he had any real sense of how weird its implications would get, and I don't want to botch that distinction in the article.
