I spent close to thirty-five years as a field ecologist, much of it wrestling with the practical problem of delineating the ranges of elk and mule deer populations that migrated every year across the borders of several national parks, national forests, and private ranches. Since retiring, I have joined a small philosophy discussion group that meets monthly at our local library, and this term we have been working through some readings on democratic theory. One issue we kept returning to, which our facilitator called "the boundary problem" — the question of who is entitled to count as part of "the people" whose consent is supposed to legitimate a democratic decision — struck me as oddly familiar, almost like a formalized version of arguments ecologists have been having for decades about where one population or ecosystem ends and another begins.

I drafted the following short argument for the group and would value a more philosophically literate reader's reaction before I present it. As I understand the problem, it is fundamentally a regress: any procedure for deciding who belongs to the demos would itself need to be authorized by some already-constituted demos, so the membership question can never be settled democratically from the inside (this is close to the point Frederick Whelan made back in the early 1980s, and it is captured, a little glibly, in the observation that you cannot hold a vote on who gets to vote, since you would already need to know who counts as a voter in order to hold it). The two standard remedies I have since read about — enfranchising everyone whose interests would be affected by a decision, or everyone who would be subject to its coercive force, a view associated with Arash Abizadeh's work on border control — seemed to me strikingly parallel to the two ways ecologists try to bound a population non-arbitrarily: by tracing the reach of some causal process, such as seed dispersal, nutrient flux, or a predator's home range, or by tracing the extent of some controlling structure, such as a watershed divide or a management jurisdiction. And just as Robert Goodin's well-known expansive reading of the "affected interests" principle threatens to dissolve almost every local boundary into a single global constituency once sufficiently indirect and merely probable effects are counted, ecologists who take causal-flux boundaries seriously enough eventually run into the fact that everything is connected to everything else, so that any stopping point looks like an analyst's convenience rather than a fact about nature. My tentative conclusion, offered with real diffidence given how new I am to this literature, was that this parallel reflects a structural feature rather than a defect: ecology mostly gave up looking for the one true boundary of an ecosystem and settled instead for the idea, sometimes discussed under the heading of hierarchy theory, that the right boundary is relative to whatever process is under study, so that a line drawn for tracking carbon is simply the wrong line for tracking wolves. Perhaps political theorists should likewise abandon the search for one true demos and accept that legitimate constituencies are plural and issue-relative rather than singular and fixed.

I would be grateful for your candid reaction to three things in particular. First, have I represented the boundary problem and the affected-interests/subjected-to-coercion debate accurately, or am I flattening distinctions that political theorists consider important? Second, does the ecological analogy actually illuminate anything, or does it trade on a category mistake, given that nobody's standing to be coerced or outvoted is at stake in how an ecologist draws a watershed line, whereas a great deal hangs on how a demos is bounded? Third, is my "issue-relative demoi" conclusion already a familiar, and perhaps already refuted, position in this literature? My own suspicion is that it merely relocates the regress to a prior question of who decides which issue or scale is relevant, rather than resolving it, but I would like to know whether I am on the right track or missing something more fundamental. Please also flag anything else in the argument that strikes you as confused or overstated — I would rather be corrected now than embarrass myself in front of the group next month.
