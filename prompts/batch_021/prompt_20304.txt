I'm taking a seminar this term on decision theory, cross-listed between the philosophy and economics departments, and last week we spent an entire session on Piccione and Rubinstein's absent-minded driver problem. The setup, as our professor presented it, involves a driver on a highway who needs to exit at the second of two successive intersections in order to get home; the intersections are visually identical, so once the driver has passed the first one, there is no way to tell whether the next intersection reached is actually the first or the second. Exiting at the first intersection is worth 0, exiting at the second (which gets the driver home) is worth 4, and driving past both without exiting at all is worth 1. Because the driver cannot tell the two decision points apart, the same probability of continuing has to be used at both of them, and our professor emphasized the feature that makes this example famous: working out how likely it is that "this" intersection is the second one rather than the first seemingly requires reasoning from the very continuation probability the driver is simultaneously trying to decide on -- a circularity between belief and strategy with no real counterpart in ordinary decision problems with perfect recall. Optimizing the whole plan in advance yields a continuation probability of 2/3, but she mentioned that Piccione and Rubinstein used exactly this wrinkle to argue that what is optimal to plan in advance and what seems locally rational to do once you are actually sitting at an intersection can come apart -- a claim contested enough to draw a direct published response from Aumann, Hart, and Perry, and one that keeps coming up alongside the Sleeping Beauty problem in discussions of self-locating belief.

What I can't stop thinking about is how closely this maps onto a couple of my own bad habits. Late at night, deciding whether to hit snooze one more time, or whether to watch just one more episode before finally going to sleep, has roughly this structure: each instance of the choice feels subjectively identical to the last, I have no reliable memory of how many times I have already made this exact call that night, and there is a clear gap between the rule I would have committed to earlier in the evening ("one snooze, no more") and whatever feels locally reasonable once I am actually groggy and facing the decision fresh again. I know the payoffs in my case are not literally 0, 4, and 1, but the underlying structure -- indistinguishable repeated decision points, plus a live question about how to reason about which one you are probably at -- seems genuinely analogous. Could you lay out the most important implications of the absent-minded driver problem for how I should think about these repeated, self-indistinguishable decisions of mine? I am especially interested in whether the moral is that in-the-moment reasoning is simply untrustworthy in cases like this, so that external commitment devices have to do all the real work, or whether there is a way of correctly reasoning about my own self-location in the sequence of decisions that would let in-the-moment judgment recover the better rule on its own.
