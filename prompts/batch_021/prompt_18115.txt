THE QUESTION WE WERE GIVEN:
"In Naming and Necessity, Kripke argues that 'Water is H2O' is a truth that is both necessary and a posteriori. In roughly 150–250 words, explain (a) what it means for 'water' and 'H2O' to be rigid designators, (b) why the identity can only be known a posteriori, (c) why it is nevertheless necessarily true, and (d) how this challenges the traditional, Kant-style assumption that necessity and apriority go hand in hand."

DEBORAH'S ANSWER:
"A rigid designator is a term that refers to the same thing in every possible world where that thing exists, rather than to whatever happens to satisfy some description there. 'Water' and 'H2O' are both rigid in this sense: 'water' was introduced by pointing at samples of the stuff in our lakes and rivers, but once the reference was fixed that way, the word sticks to that very substance in every world, even worlds where it isn't clear, or isn't drinkable, or doesn't fill any lakes at all. This is why the identity is a posteriori: nothing about the words themselves tells us the chemical composition of the stuff they refer to, so it took real chemistry to discover that the substance picked out by 'water' is the same substance picked out by 'H2O.' Kripke's Twin Earth thought experiment makes the same point from the other direction: a liquid on some other planet that looked and behaved exactly like water but turned out to be XYZ rather than H2O still wouldn't be water, since it isn't the same underlying stuff. But given that water is in fact identical to H2O, that identity holds in every possible world, since both terms designate the same thing everywhere — there's no world where water fails to be H2O. That's what makes the statement necessary even though experiments were needed to discover it — and because necessity is a much stronger status than mere well-confirmed science, it also means the claim is fully certain in a way ordinary empirical laws never are: no future finding could overturn it, the way a future finding could in principle overturn a law of physics."
