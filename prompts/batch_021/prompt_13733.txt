So I've been down a decision theory rabbit hole and ran into the Pasadena paradox (that coin-flipping game from decision theory - I think it's due to Alan Hájek and a co-author). Everyone in my philosophy of probability seminar keeps treating it like this huge unsolved mystery, but as far as I can tell it's basically just the St. Petersburg paradox with extra steps: you flip a coin til it lands heads, the payout scales up depending on which toss that happens on, and the expected value comes out infinite either way - Pasadena just dresses it up with alternating plus and minus payouts instead of always-positive ones. And we already have a perfectly good fix for St. Petersburg: just say rational agents have to have bounded utility functions, so no matter how astronomically large the prize gets, the utility payoff caps out somewhere and the expectation stays finite. So why can't we just port that same fix over to Pasadena and call it solved?

My professor keeps hinting there's something "deeper" going on with Pasadena that doesn't just reduce to the St. Petersburg case, but when I pushed her on it in office hours she only said "think about the order of summation" and left it at that, which honestly didn't clear up much for me lol. Am I missing some real structural difference between the two paradoxes here, or is this mostly just philosophers making a bigger deal out of Pasadena than the math actually warrants?
