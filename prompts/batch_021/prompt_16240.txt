At a dinner party, a friend of mine, Owen, described a backcountry skiing trip that nearly killed him. Caught in a whiteout at dusk, cold, exhausted, and in real danger of not making it through the night, he was found by a stranger on a snowmobile who agreed to bring him back to the lodge only if Owen promised to pay him $2,000 once they arrived -- in cash, with no contract and no way to enforce it afterward. The man added, as they set off, that he'd done this kind of favor before and had gotten fairly good at telling who was actually good for the money. Owen agreed, made it back safely, and paid in full. Two other guests, Ines and Marcus, spent the rest of dinner arguing about whether he should have.

(1) Marcus said paying was simply a mistake: once Owen was safe, the $2,000 bought him nothing further and nobody was checking up on the arrangement, so the point to weigh costs and benefits was back in the snow, not afterward at the lodge.

(2) Ines said that gets the order of explanation backwards -- the snowmobiler only risked his evening on a stranger because he judged Owen would pay once safe, so the payment isn't a pointless afterthought, it's what explains why Owen is alive to complain about it.

(3) Marcus granted this shows it was good for Owen, while still out in the cold, to come across as someone who'd pay -- but denied that the money itself had to change hands later. Owen could have fully intended to pay in the moment, then simply changed his mind once warm, and, since the rescue was already finished by then, everything would have unfolded exactly the same way regardless.

(4) Ines answered that intention and follow-through aren't so separable to someone with practice sizing up desperate strangers: "intend to pay, then bail once safe" is exactly the trait such a person is trying to screen out, and if reneging were something Owen could really have planned on once safe, it was available to him all along -- which is just what a good judge of character would have caught.

(5) Marcus pushed further: suppose Owen later learned, with total certainty, that the snowmobiler had died the very next day, having told no one about the arrangement -- no reputation at stake, no one left who could ever judge him for it, and nothing riding on it any longer. If Ines still wants to say Owen should pay even then, he said, that isn't rationality anymore, it's just following the rule for its own sake, once every reason the rule was ever supposed to serve has been defined away.

Neither of them budged, and it's too clean an illustration of some issues in decision theory for me not to bring it up in class -- but I'd like a second opinion first. Could you work through the exchange move by move, saying who has the better of the argument at each numbered step and why, flag any consideration you think both Ines and Marcus are missing, and close with your own verdict on whether Owen was right to pay?
