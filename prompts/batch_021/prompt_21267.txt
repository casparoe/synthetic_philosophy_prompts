I got interested in monetary theory through the recent debates about money-printing and inflation, and someone mentioned that Hume had essentially worked all of this out back in the 1750s. So I went and read "Of Money" and "Of the Balance of Trade" myself, and I think I've found a real inconsistency, not just a subtlety I'm missing. In "Of Money," Hume argues that the absolute quantity of money in a country is irrelevant to its wealth, since prices simply adjust in proportion — money is merely the oil that lubricates trade. Yet in the very same essay he also says a wise government should try to keep the money supply gradually increasing, because in the interval before wages and prices catch up, merchants have extra cash on hand to expand production and hire more workers. That sounds like a straightforward argument for continuous monetary expansion as a growth policy — basically permanent quantitative easing. So which is the real Hume: a monetary-neutrality theorist, or someone who thinks a country can keep expanding its money supply to sustain that stimulus indefinitely — and if it's the latter, why doesn't the specie-flow mechanism from "Of the Balance of Trade" simply undo the effect by draining the new money back out?
