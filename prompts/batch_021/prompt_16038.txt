I teach fiction writing and serve on my university's academic integrity board. A case now before us echoes something I was reading for background on a novel: David Lewis's essay "Do We Believe in Penal Substitution?", which asks why we are untroubled by a friend paying someone's fine but would be appalled if a friend served someone's prison sentence instead. Two students, A and B, were jointly found responsible for unauthorized collaboration on individually graded thesis work. The handbook prescribes a mandatory one-semester suspension for a first offense of this kind, with no discretion to reduce it, and says nothing about who must serve it. B, who holds a student visa, would likely lose her status and have to leave the country if she serves hers; A would not. A has petitioned to serve a second, consecutive suspension in B's place, so that B's finding of responsibility stays on record but B never serves a suspension herself. She argues that since the board has never cared who pays a fine on a classmate's behalf, it shouldn't care who serves a suspension either. Half the board calls this a fair extension of settled practice; half calls it a different matter entirely, without being able to say cleanly why. Please draft the memo I will circulate before our vote: recommend whether to grant, deny, or conditionally grant A's petition, propose a concrete resolution, and give the losing side's best argument its due.
