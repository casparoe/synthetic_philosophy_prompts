A friend of mine, Priya, and I got into a proper arguement last night — started over dinner, ended around 1am with neither of us backing down — about the classic money-pump case against intransitive preferences. You know the one: you like a banana better than an apple, the apple better than an orange, but (here's the cyclic kicker) the orange better than the banana. A bookie who knows this can always find some tiny fee that gets you to trade whatever fruit you're currently holding for the one you like slightly more, and just walks you around the loop forever — paying every lap, ending up with the exact same fruit and less money each time round. Priya's line was that this settles things completely: since the cycle guarantees you can be pumped dry, transitivity isn't a modelling convenience economists impose for tractability, it's a flat-out requirement of rationality, on the same footing as not believing outright contradictions, full stop, regardless of anything else about how the agent in question actually goes about choosing across time. I caved and agreed with her by the end mostly because I was too tired to keep going, but it's been bugging me since, so I figured I'd get a second opinion.

Here's my actual question: if the bare money-pump case already proves that cyclic preferences guarantee you'll get exploited, full stop, then what is all the "dynamic choice"/diachronic rationality literature actually doing — the stuff on naive versus sophisticated versus resolute choosers, people like McClennen arguing an agent can rationally commit to a plan and stick with it even with a cyclic preference underneath, and never get pumped at all? Is that whole line of work basically just a footnote on how to be a slightly-less-naive money pump, or does it actually undercut the "full stop" in Priya's claim — i.e. does whether you actually get exploited depend on more than just whether your preferences happen to cycle? I want to know who's actually right here, whether there's a settled answer on this in the decision theory literature, and if there's a name for the distinction I'm gesturing at, because right now I genuinely can't tell if I caved too early.
