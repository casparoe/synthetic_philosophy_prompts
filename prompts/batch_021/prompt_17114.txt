I work on interpretability at a small AI lab, and over the past few weeks we've trained linear probes on our language model's internal activations that track the truth of a factual claim better than the model's own stated answer does: on a non-trivial share of prompts, the model asserts P in its output while the probe flags P as false with high confidence. This has become lab-meeting shorthand for "the model secretly believes not-P even though it says P," and that phrasing is starting to creep into how we describe the work externally, which makes me want to get the underlying philosophy right. On my reading of Dennett, the intentional stance entitles us to attribute beliefs to a system just insofar as doing so buys genuine predictive traction on its behavior under an assumption of rationality, and the account is deliberately neutral on whatever internal machinery realizes that pattern — that's precisely the work done by treating beliefs as "real patterns" rather than as facts about discrete internal representations. Please argue that our probing result, however striking, is actually irrelevant to whether belief-attribution is licensed here: finding such a direction cannot by itself do any of the work our lab-meeting talk assumes it does, except at the cost of quietly trading Dennett's view for the more full-blooded representational realism about belief that it was meant to avoid.
