I spent the better part of four decades doing lattice gauge theory -- mostly lattice QCD, if I'm honest -- which is the business of putting continuum fields onto a discretized spacetime so that a computer can actually grind through the path integral. Since retiring, I've drifted, somewhat to my own surprise, into a philosophy reading group, and you can probably guess why Bostrom's simulation argument caught my attention: the idea that our universe might itself be a computation running on someone else's discretized lattice feels uncomfortably close to what I did for a living, minus the "someone else." I even recall a speculative paper from some years back -- Beane and collaborators, if memory serves -- that searched for literal lattice artifacts in the arrival directions of the highest-energy cosmic rays, treating that as a possible observational test of the idea. Our reading group runs a small podcast, and next month we're interviewing a philosopher who has built her career defending and elaborating Bostrom's argument -- not the one-paragraph popular version, but the full trilemma together with its anthropic scaffolding. I've been asked to conduct the interview. In her published replies to critics, she has a favored way of handling objections about the reference-class problem: she says the indeterminacy there is simply "the price of admission for any anthropic argument, not a defect peculiar to mine," a move that folds her difficulty into a much larger debate and makes it feel ungenerous to keep pressing her on it specifically.

Could you draft eight to ten interview questions that do not just let her restate the trilemma, and that make that particular deflection progressively harder to lean on? Please include at least one question that forces her to say whether the argument's force actually depends on choosing the self-sampling assumption over the self-indication assumption, instead of resting on anthropic reasoning as an undifferentiated monolith; at least one that presses on whether defending the simulation as something that only renders what is currently being observed or measured -- in order to economize on computation -- thereby buys unfalsifiability rather than mere efficiency, and whether she would welcome or resist an empirical test like the cosmic-ray proposal I mentioned; and at least one on whether the entire argument quietly presupposes a computationalist view of mind that she has never really had to defend against, say, Searle-style biological naturalism. For each of these three, add a short follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give, and close the sequence with a single question asking her to state, concretely, what observation or argument would actually lower her own credence in the simulation hypothesis, rather than being folded back in as further support for it.
