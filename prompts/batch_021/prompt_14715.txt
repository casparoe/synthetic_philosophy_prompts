And here is the student's answer, reproduced in full: "Rowe's argument turns on a single, vivid case rather than the abstract claim that evil as such is incompatible with God's existence. He asks us to imagine a fawn trapped by a forest fire started by a lightning strike; it is badly burned and lies in agony for several days before dying, and Rowe's point is that no good we can identify is served by its suffering, and none seems necessary to secure any good an omnipotent being couldn't have brought about more directly. From this he infers, inductively rather than deductively, that it is unlikely that an omniscient, omnipotent, and wholly good being exists. This is a considerably weaker and more defensible claim than Mackie's, since Mackie needed evil and theism to be strictly logically inconsistent, whereas Rowe only needs the existence of apparently gratuitous evil to count as strong evidence against theism. Skeptical theists accept Rowe's premise that we can't see what good the fawn's suffering serves, but deny that this licenses his conclusion. The strategy is to argue that God, if He exists, would have reasons and a vantage point so far beyond ours that we should expect not to be able to identify them, and this is exactly what is going on with the fawn: its suffering serves the purpose of testing and deepening human faith in a way that is simply invisible to us from the inside. Because Plantinga's free will defense already showed, via the idea of transworld depravity, that it is possible God could not have actualized a world containing the relevant free creatures and no sin, the same strategy generalizes naturally to Rowe's case: once we grant that some suffering is the logically necessary cost of a greater good tied to creaturely freedom, the fawn's suffering is no longer clear evidence against God's existence, since it may likewise be the by-product of a world God had to permit in order to secure that greater good. Hick's soul-making theodicy makes a similar move but grounds it in Irenaeus rather than Augustine, treating the world as what Hick, borrowing a phrase from Keats, calls a 'vale of soul-making,' in which hardship is instrumentally necessary for moral growth, rather than in the more formal, modal machinery on which Plantinga relies."
