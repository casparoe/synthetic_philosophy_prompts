I've spent the past couple of months reporting a piece on proposals for a more steeply graduated income tax, and one exchange from my interviews has stuck with me ever since. When I asked a welfare economist why it's fair to take proportionally more from high earners to fund benefits for the poor, she told me the intuitive justification — that a given sum of money simply "means less" to a rich person than to a poor one — rests on a comparison that can never really be verified, since there is no way to get inside two people's heads and confirm whose gain or loss is actually bigger. A colleague of hers brushed this off as an academic scruple that nobody drafting actual policy loses sleep over.

I hadn't thought much more about this until a nearly identical disagreement broke out in my own family. My grandmother left my brother and me a joint inheritance that comes to about $90,000 after taxes. I have a stable, well-paying job; he has been out of work for over a year and is behind on rent. He argues the money shouldn't be split evenly, since the same dollars would obviously relieve far more hardship for him than they would for me, so a 50/50 split would actually be less fair than an uneven one. Part of me finds this persuasive, but another part suspects it quietly assumes something that may not be so easy to establish: that we can meaningfully weigh how much good the money would do in his life against how much it would do in mine. I'd be curious whether you think his reasoning actually holds up, and, if not, whether there is some more principled way for two people in our position to arrive at a split we could both genuinely regard as fair, rather than just defaulting to a 50/50 as though that were obviously the neutral choice.
