Every discussion of algorithmic fairness eventually circles back to the ProPublica/Northpointe dispute over the COMPAS recidivism tool: ProPublica showed that Black defendants who did not reoffend were flagged 'high risk' at a much higher rate than white defendants who did not reoffend, while Northpointe countered that the tool was equally well-calibrated for both groups. I've read enough to know this isn't a case of one side doing sloppy statistics -- there's a genuine impossibility result here, I believe due to Kleinberg, Mullainathan, and Raghavan, with a related derivation by Chouldechova, showing that calibration within groups and equalized error rates across groups can't both hold when base rates differ, except in the degenerate case of a perfect predictor. I can follow the algebra, but what I actually want is the gears-level intuition: something tied to Bayes' rule and differing base rates that makes the trade-off feel inevitable before you've done any calculation, rather than a fact you just accept from staring at a derivation. Could you also walk through the standard proposed fix -- using a different risk threshold for each group -- and say plainly which fairness property that move actually restores and which one it necessarily sacrifices, rather than just asserting that it 'doesn't really solve the problem'?
