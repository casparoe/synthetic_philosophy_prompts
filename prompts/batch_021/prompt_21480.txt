I've spent years reading rationalist blogs and forums, and Newcomb's Problem -- together with the whole one-box/two-box literature it spawned -- has always been a particular favorite of mine. I now TA a graduate seminar on decision theory, and I'm grading take-home answers to the following question:

"Newcomb's Problem is standardly glossed as a conflict between the dominance principle and the principle of maximizing expected utility. The 'smoking lesion' case has exactly the same formal skeleton -- an act correlated with, but not a cause of, a good or bad outcome -- yet intuition treats the two oppositely: one-boxing looks rational, abstaining from smoking looks irrational. Explain why the two cases seem to pull apart despite sharing this structure, and then assess whether the 'tickle defense' available to the evidential decision theorist restores this asymmetry, or only does so at a cost the evidential decision theorist should not want to pay."

Here is one student's answer:

"Newcomb's Problem forces a choice between two principles that usually agree. The dominance principle says that if one option is at least as good as another in every possible state of the world, you should take it; since taking both boxes leaves you $1,000 richer whatever the predictor put in the opaque box, dominance recommends two-boxing. The principle of maximizing expected utility instead has you condition on how likely each state is given your act, and since one-boxers are almost always the ones who end up rich, it recommends one-boxing.

The smoking lesion case has the same skeleton: smoking doesn't cause the disease, it is merely evidence of a lesion that causes both the urge to smoke and the disease. A decision theory that tells you to avoid smoking purely because smoking is bad news, when it has no causal downside, is getting something wrong. Evidential decision theory blocks this via the tickle defense: a fully self-aware agent already feels the urge -- the 'tickle' -- before choosing, so once that felt desire is factored into the conditional probabilities, the further choice of whether to smoke carries no additional evidence about the lesion, and abstaining stops looking obligatory. This is essentially the same device David Lewis introduced when he had agents condition their expected-utility calculations on dependency hypotheses rather than on raw outcomes, which is why evidential decision theory does not have to give up one-boxing in Newcomb's Problem in order to get the lesion case right: once everything the agent already knows about herself prior to acting is factored in, both cases come out exactly as intuition says they should, at no remaining cost to the theory."

Please grade this out of 20 points. Rather than a general impression, give me point-by-point comments keyed to specific claims in the answer, flagging anything that is an outright error rather than a defensible simplification. I'd find it useful to have this as JSON, with a numeric score and short justification for (i) the dominance/expected-utility setup, (ii) the diagnosis of why Newcomb's Problem and the lesion case pull apart, and (iii) the treatment of the tickle defense, plus an overall total and letter grade.
