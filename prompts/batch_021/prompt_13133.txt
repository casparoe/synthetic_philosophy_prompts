I've been a lurker on LessWrong and its offshoots for well over a decade, and one thread that keeps resurfacing there is Graham Priest's work on paraconsistent logic and dialetheism — the thesis that some contradictions are simply true. A recent round of that discussion pointed me to the paper Priest co-wrote with Jay Garfield, "Nagarjuna and the Limits of Thought," which argues that Nagarjuna's Mulamadhyamakakarika is best read as a deliberately paraconsistent text. That sent me to the primary source for the first time, in translation, and I've gotten stuck on something I can't work out on my own.

The trouble is reconciling two uses of what's called the catuskoti, or tetralemma, within the same work. Chapter 1, verse 1, on the examination of conditions, opens by negating all four of the available options for how anything arises: not from itself, not from another, not from both, and not from no cause at all — nothing, anywhere, is ever produced in any of these four ways. That "rule out all four corners" move recurs throughout the text, applied in turn to motion, to the self, to the Tathagata, and is standardly glossed as showing that no coherent account of the phenomenon survives conceptual analysis, which is exactly the point: emptiness. But chapter 18, verse 8, on the examination of self, appears to run the same four-part schema in reverse, affirming all four corners at once as a positive teaching — that everything is real, that everything is unreal, that everything is both real and unreal, and that everything is neither real nor unreal — and calls this the Buddha's own teaching. Taken at face value, that flatly contradicts the very options that were supposedly exhaustive and mutually exclusive a few chapters earlier. It doesn't help that the very next verse, 18.9, immediately pivots to a single, perfectly consistent description of ultimate reality as peaceful and free of conceptual proliferation, which makes me suspect 18.8 might be doing something more like describing graded, audience-relative teaching than asserting a claim about how things actually are.

What I'd like help with is this: is there a principled way to read verses like 1.1 and 18.8 as two structurally different deployments of "the tetralemma" — via the two-truths distinction, say, or the idea of upaya, skillful means calibrated to a listener's capacity — that dissolves the apparent contradiction without following Garfield and Priest all the way into treating Nagarjuna as a dialetheist for whom the negation and the affirmation are both simply, literally true? Or is that kind of deflationary move just a way of declining to take the text seriously, closer to Richard Robinson's older complaint that Nagarjuna's logic trades on an equivocation between senses of negation and doesn't actually hang together? I'd rather get a sense of the real shape of that disagreement than a summary of "Madhyamaka charts a middle way between existence and non-existence," which is an answer I already know how to give myself.
