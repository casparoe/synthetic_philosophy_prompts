I'm a PhD student in political philosophy and our distributive justice reading group (we've been working through the Sen/Dworkin "equality of what" debate) spent a whole session on a case nobody could resolve, so I honestly want an outside read on it.

The case: one of them brought up a friend, Beth, who's had chronic fatigue syndrome for years. She gets the exact same stipend and resources as everyone in her cohort, needs no special equipment or accommodations, but can do way less with her time -- no extra RA-ships, can't travel to conferences like her peers do. She insists she's genuinely fine with it: she scaled her ambitions back years ago and doesnt feel deprived.

Two people in the group, Theo and Nadia, went at it over what this case actually shows.

(1) Theo: on Dworkin's equality of resources there's nothing here to fix, since she holds the exact same resource bundle as everyone else -- and welfarism agrees, since she's not unhappy either. No plausible view says she's worse off.

(2) Nadia: that's exactly the problem. She has less real freedom to do things she and her peers have reason to value, and that capability deficit is there whether or not it ever shows up in her resources or her self-reports. That's the gap Sen built the capability approach to catch.

(3) Theo: Dworkin already has a device for exactly this, the hypothetical insurance market -- a prudent person behind a veil of ignorance about their future health would insure against something like chronic illness, and Beth's compensation gets fixed by that hypothetical payout, not by her happiness. Resourcism isn't blind to her at all.

(4) Nadia: that just pushes the problem back a step. You can't price that insurance without already knowing what the condition costs someone in terms of functioning -- a capability fact wearing a market costume. Plus the whole device needs made-up assumptions about risk-aversion and how complete the hypothetical market even is.

(5) Theo: fine, but drop a single metric like resources for a plurality of functionings and how do you even compare across people, or sum up one person's own gains and losses, without smuggling a resource-like common measure back in anyway?

We ran out of time right there. Could you go through (1)-(5) and say who's got the better of it at each step -- not just who wins overall -- and flag what you think both of them are missing?
