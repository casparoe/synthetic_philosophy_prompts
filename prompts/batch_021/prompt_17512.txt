A friend and I spent most of last night's reading-group afterparty arguing about vagueness, and neither of us walked away convinced. It started when she casually described the food truck outside as "basically a diner," which set off a half-serious sorites argument about how many booths and how much menu a place needs before it counts as one — and from there we ended up rehashing the whole epistemicism-versus-supervaluationism debate our seminar covered a few weeks ago. She's firmly in Williamson's camp: for her, there really is a precise, fact-of-the-matter cutoff for predicates like "tall," "heap," or apparently "diner" — some exact point where true flips to false — we simply can't know where it is, since knowing it would require a kind of precision our use of language never gives us (something like a margin-for-error constraint on any knowledge of the boundary). I kept defending the supervaluationist line instead: there's no such hidden fact at all; "diner" comes out true on some acceptable ways of making the term precise and false on others, so a borderline case is neither true nor false — it's "supertrue" only when every admissible precisification agrees — and the impression of a sharp fact out there is just what happens when you force bivalence onto a case of unresolved semantic indecision.

Where we really got stuck was on higher-order vagueness: the border between "clearly a diner" and "borderline diner" is itself blurry, and so on up through "definitely borderline," "borderline borderline," and so forth. She argued this is actually worse for my view than for hers, since epistemicism can just re-run the same ignorance story at every level, while supervaluationism needs some account of vagueness in the very notion of an "admissible precisification," or in the "definitely" operator used to state the theory — and that this either collapses into something epistemicist-like or triggers a regress that never bottoms out. Neither of us could reconstruct Fine's or Williamson's actual arguments precisely enough on the spot to know whether that objection really lands.

Could you go through, in some depth, how epistemicism and supervaluationism each try to handle higher-order vagueness, and assess whether my friend's objection is actually a good one? And more broadly, I'd like your own verdict: all things considered, which view pays the steeper theoretical price — epistemicism's commitment to unknowable sharp boundaries, or supervaluationism's revisions to classical semantics, including its handling (or mishandling) of higher-order vagueness — and which do you think is the more defensible general theory of vagueness? Please justify the verdict rather than just restating the standard back-and-forth.
