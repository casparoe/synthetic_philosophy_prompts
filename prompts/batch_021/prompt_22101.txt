I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums for close to fifteen years now, long enough that a good part of my friend group reflexively reaches for phrases like "common knowledge" and "Schelling point" without remembering, or much caring, where either one actually comes from. The first of those we can blame substantially on one book: David Lewis's Convention, which gave the notion of common knowledge its first rigorous philosophical treatment, formalizing an idea Schelling had used informally a decade earlier. One of the people most responsible for that habit sticking around is Priya, who is marrying Daniel in three weeks and has asked me to give one of the toasts. Priya wrote a dissertation chapter on Lewis's account of convention, and has spent a decade insisting, half as a running joke and half in earnest, that our friend group's own habits — who hosts, how we split the bill, which jokes are allowed about which people — are conventions in Lewis's exact technical sense, not just customs we happen to share.

I'd like you to write that toast. Priya and Daniel come from families with quite different wedding traditions — hers Gujarati Hindu, his lapsed Catholic — and rather than defaulting to either, the weekend is visibly stitching the two together: two ceremonies, some blended elements, a couple of things invented from scratch because neither family's precedent gave an obvious answer. I want the speech to actually use Lewis's apparatus on this, not borrow "coordination" as a loose metaphor: the common knowledge condition, the idea of a coordination equilibrium selected from among others that could have arisen instead (his criterion for calling a regularity arbitrary rather than necessary), and the role salience and precedent play in breaking a symmetry when neither family's default has priority. I'd also like it to confront, rather than quietly step around, an objection Priya has fielded at parties more than once — that calling something a "convention" makes it sound arbitrary and interchangeable, as though it wouldn't have mattered much who ended up at the altar — and to use the theory's own resources, not sentiment poured on top of it, to explain why that inference fails. A line or two on how later writers such as Skyrms tried to explain how conventions get established and hold themselves in place without anyone first reasoning the whole thing out together would be welcome if it fits naturally.

The room will have perhaps a dozen people who would notice a misstated condition and seventy or eighty who have never heard of David Lewis and have no need to. Please keep the speech to about four minutes read aloud, have it find at least one real laugh — I'd suggest somewhere in cataloguing exactly which parts of the wedding weekend are arbitrary Lewisian conventions and which, on the theory's own terms, are not — and have it end in an actual toast. It should read as a tribute to Priya and Daniel specifically, not a seminar that happens to have their names attached.
