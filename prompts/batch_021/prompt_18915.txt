And here, verbatim, is the student's answer:

(a) Stalnaker's Thesis says that the probability of the indicative conditional "if A, then B" -- understood as the probability that the corresponding proposition A>B is true -- equals the conditional probability P(B|A), for any A with positive probability: P(A>B) = P(B|A). Adams' Thesis is standardly written the same way, except that Adams speaks of the "assertability" or "acceptability" of "if A, then B" rather than the probability of a proposition: As(A>B) = P(B|A). Since the two equations have the same right-hand side and differ only in what we call the left-hand side, the two theses really come down to the same underlying claim -- that our confidence in a conditional, however we choose to label it, should track our conditional credence in the consequent given the antecedent. Adams' formulation is best seen as a way of stating Stalnaker's insight without first having to sign on to a possible-worlds semantics on which "if... then" generates a new proposition A>B.

(b) Suppose, for contradiction, that some non-trivial probability function P satisfies Stalnaker's Thesis for every A, B in its domain with P(A) > 0, and that this has to hold not just of P itself but of any probability function reachable from P by conditioning on further information -- otherwise the Thesis would be a strange kind of accident, true of our current credences but not of the credences we'd have on learning something new. Pick A and B so that A&B, A&¬B, and ¬A all have positive probability under P. Now consider the two functions we get by conditioning P on B and on ¬B. Conditional on B, the probability of B given A is trivially 1, so applying the Thesis to this new function gives P(A>B | B) = 1. Conditional on ¬B, the probability of B given A is trivially 0, so the Thesis gives P(A>B | ¬B) = 0. But by the law of total probability, P(A>B) = P(B)·P(A>B|B) + P(¬B)·P(A>B|¬B) = P(B)·1 + P(¬B)·0 = P(B). Combining this with Stalnaker's Thesis applied to the original P, P(B|A) = P(A>B) = P(B): A and B turn out to be probabilistically independent. Since A and B were constrained only by the mild non-triviality condition above, this has to hold of any such pair, which no informative, real-world credence function can satisfy.

(c) A probability function is trivial, in the sense the proof needs, exactly when there is no pair of propositions A, B such that A&B, A&¬B, and ¬A are all possible -- that is, when the space of live possibilities it distinguishes has at most two cells. That's an extreme restriction: essentially no credence function anyone would use for practical or scientific reasoning satisfies it, since we constantly entertain more than two mutually exclusive possibilities at once. So the result shows that Stalnaker's Thesis, read as a claim about the probabilities of genuine propositions, cannot hold for any remotely realistic believer -- which is bad news for the view that indicative conditionals are ordinary propositions that can be negated, conjoined, and embedded inside further conditionals just like any other sentence, since it's exactly that view which made sense of asking after "the probability that A>B is true" in the first place.

(d) One influential response is to deny that indicative conditionals express propositions at all -- the "No Truth Value" view associated with the probabilistic tradition in the theory of conditionals. If "if A, then B" has no truth conditions, there is no such thing as "the probability that A>B is true" to begin with, so the quantity Stalnaker's Thesis needs was never well defined, and the triviality proof never gets started; all that survives is Adams' Thesis about assertability, which the proof doesn't touch. This is, in fact, essentially the moral Lewis draws from his own theorem: rather than let "if" introduce a further proposition into the field over which P is defined, we should treat conditional sentences as devices for conditional assertion, with acceptability fixed directly by conditional credence. The remaining difficulty is compositionality: if conditionals lack truth values, it's hard to say what a conditional is doing when it's negated or embedded as the antecedent of a further conditional, since there's no truth value for the outer connective to operate on. Even so, since this concession -- that "if" doesn't build ordinary, embeddable propositions -- is already the conclusion the triviality argument itself extracts from Stalnaker's Thesis, it isn't really a further cost specific to the No-Truth-Value theorist; that theorist is just the one who states the consequence outright instead of resisting it.
