I'm a final-year philosophy major taking a small, advanced seminar on decision theory this semester. We've worked our way from expected utility theory through Newcomb's problem and the split between causal and evidential decision theory, and most recently to the more exotic "logical" or updateless approaches that try to justify one-boxing on principled grounds. Right before reading week, our professor closed class with an informal aside on "acausal trade" — the idea, more at home in AI-safety and rationalist-adjacent writing than in our assigned readings, that two agents who can never causally interact (civilizations in regions of space causally disconnected by cosmic expansion, say, or agents in different branches of a quantum multiverse) might still coordinate their behavior for mutual benefit, purely because each can predict that the other reasons similarly. This set off an argument between two people in my cohort, Callum and Freya, that carried on well after class ended. I mostly listened, so I'm reporting it rather than taking a side.

(1) Callum opened by saying "acausal trade" is a misleading label for something that isn't trade at all: since neither party can causally affect the other, neither one's action is ever actually contingent on what the other does — each is just independently computing "what would a rational agent in my position do," and if their reasoning is similar enough, they land on correlated answers. That, he said, is superrationality, not trade. (2) Freya replied that ordinary trade doesn't require real-time causal contingency either — when you ship something to an anonymous buyer overseas, your action and theirs are connected only by mutual prediction and contract, not a live feedback loop — and that what actually makes something trade is a kind of counterfactual or "subjunctive" dependence: if one party would defect, the other, being similar enough, would also defect, and both can know this in advance. (3) Callum answered that this notion of dependence smuggles in a specific, contested decision theory — something like functional or updateless decision theory — since a causal decision theorist would insist that once the other agent's choice is fixed, whether temporally earlier or just causally walled off, nothing you do now actually influences it, so the "dependence" is mere uncertainty about an independent fact, not real leverage; calling this trade already assumes one side of a live dispute in decision theory. (4) Freya countered that the identical objection applies to Newcomb's problem itself, where most people feel real pull toward one-boxing despite the causal isolation of the predictor's decision, and that acausal trade is just Newcomb's problem generalized to two independently existing parties instead of one agent and a predictor — so if one-boxing can ever be rational, we're most of the way to granting that the "trade" can be real too. (5) Callum pushed back that Newcomb's problem stipulates a fixed, extremely high predictor accuracy, whereas real acausal trading partners come with no such guarantee — just a hope of relevantly similar reasoning that, by hypothesis, can never be checked, since the parties can never communicate — which is an enormous amount of unearned mutual confidence to assume. (6) Freya's last word was that this is a difference of degree rather than kind: ordinary trade under incomplete information, like selling something "as is" to a total stranger, also rests on an unverified model of the other side, and all that acausal trade really needs is a high enough correlation and high enough stakes that the expected gains from cooperating exceed the expected losses from misjudging the other party — exactly the cost-benefit logic of any trade made under uncertainty.

Neither of them budged, and my own grip on the decision-theory background is still shaky enough that I couldn't confidently referee it myself.
