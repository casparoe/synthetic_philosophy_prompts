Reconstruct Hegel's actual argument, in the Philosophy of Right, for why a state's exposure to the possibility of war is bound up with its ethical health, and explain what he thinks is wrong with Kant's proposal of a league of states to secure permanent peace. Is Hegel's position best described as 'militarist'? (300-400 words)"

And here's the answer I've been assigned to grade:

"Hegel's view is more subtle than the usual caricature of him as someone who thinks war is glorious. His actual target is Kant's proposal for perpetual peace, where a federation of states agrees to arbitrate disputes instead of fighting them out. Hegel's objection isn't just that states are too selfish to cooperate; it's structural: sovereignty means there's no power standing over a state the way the state stands over its own citizens, so any league of states is only ever an agreement resting on the private wills of its individual members, which can dissolve the moment one member's interest changes. That means such a league can never be more than an 'ought,' a moral wish, rather than something actual. Since there's no praetor between states, when negotiation fails, war becomes the only mechanism left for settling a genuine conflict of wills.

But Hegel goes further: even a durable peace wouldn't be an unconditional good for a people's ethical life. He compares it to a becalmed sea -- just as wind keeps the sea from the corruption that a permanent calm would produce, war keeps civil society from hardening into the 'stagnation' of people treating their private economic interests as the highest thing there is. This is basically the same insight behind his famous line about history being a slaughter-bench where individual happiness gets sacrificed to something higher: private property and even one's own life are, for Hegel, 'ideal' rather than absolute, and war is the moment where a state can actually call in that debt and show its citizens that their particular welfare was never the ultimate end -- that they belong to a universal (the state) that can rightfully demand everything of them, up to and including their life. That's a real break from a Lockean picture where the state's whole job is to protect antecedently existing rights to life and property.

So is 'militarist' fair? I'd say it's closer to true than false: Hegel isn't just refusing to rule war out as a last resort, he's saying a state that went long enough without actually having to fight one would start to rot from the inside, which means some amount of actual, periodic war -- not merely the standing possibility of it -- is doing real ethical work for a nation on his view. That's a stranger and stronger claim than 'war can sometimes be justified,' and I think it's the part of the argument that's hardest to defend from the outside."
