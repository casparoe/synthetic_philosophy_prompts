I have been reading Tolstoy's late treatise "What Is Art?" for a seminar on the philosophy of art, and I would like help thinking through how his position holds up against a closely related theory. Tolstoy defines art as the activity by which a person who has experienced a feeling consciously transmits it to others, who are then "infected" by that same feeling, and he specifies three conditions that govern the strength of this infection: the individuality of the feeling conveyed, the clarity with which it is conveyed, and the sincerity of the artist in having genuinely felt it. On this basis he reaches some famously severe verdicts, counting Wagner's operas and much of Shakespeare among "counterfeit" art produced for a narrow class rather than for humanity at large, and even disowning much of his own earlier fiction, including novels like War and Peace and Anna Karenina, as failing to meet the standard he now demanded.

I would like you to compare this infection theory with R. G. Collingwood's expression theory of art in "The Principles of Art." Collingwood distinguishes genuine "art proper" from two forms of pseudo-art: "magic," in which emotion is aroused and discharged for a practical, communal purpose (ceremonial, patriotic, or religious works meant to reinforce shared feeling and action), and "amusement," in which emotion is aroused and discharged purely for the private enjoyment of experiencing it. For Collingwood, genuine expression is fundamentally a matter of the artist coming to understand and articulate a feeling that was initially unclear even to themselves, rather than a matter of successfully transmitting an already-formed feeling to an audience.

With that contrast in view, I would like you to work through two cases and say what each theory implies about them, before adjudicating which theory gives the more defensible answer overall. First, consider a poem that sincerely and lucidly articulates a highly individual feeling but leaves every reader besides its author unmoved. Second, consider a rousing patriotic anthem that successfully unites a mass audience in an intense, shared emotion. Do Tolstoy's criterion of infection and Collingwood's distinction between "art proper" and "magic" converge on the same verdicts about these two cases, or do they pull apart? And, on balance, whose framework better explains why Tolstoy himself was willing to expel so much canonical "great art," including his own novels, from the category of art properly so called? Please justify your answer by reference to the specific mechanisms each theory proposes, rather than simply to the general family resemblance between "expression" theories of art.
