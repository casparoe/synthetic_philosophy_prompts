"Frederick Douglass began his public career as a close ally of William Lloyd Garrison, and for roughly his first decade as an abolitionist he held to the Garrisonian position on the Constitution: that it was fundamentally a pro-slavery compact. Garrisonians pointed to clauses like the three-fifths clause, which boosted Southern congressional representation by counting each enslaved person as three-fifths of a person for purposes of apportionment, and the fugitive slave clause, which obligated free states to return escaped slaves to bondage. Garrison summed up the whole document with the phrase 'a covenant with death, an agreement with hell,' and for years Douglass repeated this judgment nearly word for word, along with the broader Garrisonian strategy of moral suasion and disunion ('no union with slaveholders') rather than voting or holding office under such a government.

Around 1851, Douglass broke publicly and painfully with Garrison on this very question. The immediate catalyst was Garrison's own close reading of the text: Garrison had pointed out that the words 'slave' and 'slavery' never once appear in the Constitution, and he pressed this observation on Douglass as proof that the document could not fairly be read as sanctioning an institution it refused even to name. Douglass found the argument more convincing than his old position, and from 1851 on he insisted that the Constitution, read on its own terms rather than through the gloss slaveholders and their judges had put on it, gave no sanction to slavery at all and left Congress no constitutional power to create or protect the institution anywhere.

This reinterpretation had a real practical payoff: no longer viewing the Constitution as hopelessly pro-slavery, Douglass concluded that voting and party politics were compatible with abolitionism after all, and he drifted toward the Liberty Party and then the Republicans. Garrison came to much the same conclusion about the text around the same time, so by the later 1850s the two men's remaining disagreement was really more a matter of temperament and tactics than of constitutional interpretation as such. Douglass gave his fullest public defense of the new reading in an 1860 Glasgow speech, 'The Constitution of the United States: Is It Pro-Slavery or Anti-Slavery?,' insisting that whatever private bargains individual framers had struck, the ratified text itself could not honestly be squared with slavery."
