I turn forty next month, and for the past year the small reading group I belong to — four or five of us, meeting on Thursday evenings, working steadily through the dialogues — has been making its way through Plato's Laws, which none of us had read before. I came to it expecting more of the wary, appetite-suspicious Plato of the Republic, and was genuinely struck by Books One and Two, where the Athenian Stranger devotes a surprising amount of argument to defending the Athenian custom of the wine party against the disapproval of his Spartan and Cretan companions, on the grounds that a properly supervised drinking party is actually a way of testing and training self-control rather than simply abandoning it. What struck me most, given the timing, is the claim that men who have reached roughly my own age should no longer be restrained the way the young are, but positively encouraged, at the common feasts, to drink to real intoxication — because wine, on this account, is the god's specific remedy for the stiffness and gloom that come with age, loosening a man's character the way fire softens iron, and giving him the boldness to sing and dance in the choruses he would otherwise be too self-conscious to join sober.

I am hosting the party myself and would like to open the evening with a short toast, no more than three or four minutes spoken aloud, built around this passage. Roughly a third of the guests are from the reading group and will recognize it at once — one of them has already summarized the whole argument, not entirely unfairly, as "Plato thinks forty-year-olds should get wasted," and I would like the toast to be visibly truer to the text than that without turning into a seminar. The rest of the room has never opened the Laws and needs the point to land as something warm and a little funny about aging and loosening up, not as an assignment. It would also be faithful to the text, and I think it would make a good line, to mention that Plato insists no drinking party should go forward without one sober person left in charge to keep it from sliding into chaos — and that I have already appointed my sister to that role for the evening. Could you write the toast for me: affectionate rather than lecturing, and careful not to flatten the philosophy into a punchline even while using it to make people laugh?
