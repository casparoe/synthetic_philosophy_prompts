So in my formal epistemology seminar we just spent two lectures on van Fraassen's Reflection Principle -- the claim that if I'm certain I'll have credence q in some proposition tomorrow, rationality requires me to already have credence q in it today, on pain of being Dutch-booked across time. My professor sold it with the usual "defer to your more informed future self" story, but then in discussion section a classmate brought up a case that's been bugging me since: say you know for a fact that your opinion on something is about to shift, not because you'll learn anything new, but because of a totally non-evidential process, like your about to go through a week of basic training, or a religious retreat that's specifically designed to wear you down, or you're just going to get blackout drunk at a party tonight. You can predict with total confidence what you'll believe after, so naive Reflection seems to say you should just adopt that opinion right now, which feels obviously wrong. One of the TAs said van Fraassen actually worried about something like this himself, bringing up Ulysses and the sirens, where Ulysses has to bind himself to the mast precisely because he doesn't want to defer to the guy he's about to become.

Could you walk me through the most important implications of the Reflection Principle for cases like this? I'm mainly trying to figure out whether there's a principled way to block the drunk/boot-camp counterexamples without just gutting the whole motivation for the principle in the first place, and whether any of this actually has teeth for real-life decisions -- like, does it tell us anything about why "Ulysses contracts" (e.g. psychiatric advance directives where you bind your future self against what that future self will actually want) can be rational, even though on the face of it they look like flatly refusing to defer to your own future opinion?
