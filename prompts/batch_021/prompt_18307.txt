A friend and I spent most of dinner last night arguing about G.E.M. Anscombe's 1956 pamphlet "Mr. Truman's Degree," which she wrote after learning that Oxford intended to award Harry Truman an honorary degree, and in which she argues that his decision to bomb Hiroshima and Nagasaki was murder, plain and simple, and that honoring him was therefore no different in kind from honoring any other mass murderer. (The conversation started because we'd just watched a documentary on the Manhattan Project, and one of us mentioned, half as trivia, that Anscombe had tried and failed to organize formal opposition to the award inside the university itself.) My friend's position was that there is nothing philosophically distinctive going on in the piece: Anscombe, a Catholic steeped in the tradition of natural law, is simply applying the standard doctrine of double effect - the act itself must not be intrinsically wrong, the bad effect must be foreseen rather than intended, the bad effect cannot be the means by which the good effect is produced, and the whole must be proportionate - and correctly concluding, on exactly those terms, that deliberately targeting civilian populations to terrorize a government into surrender fails the doctrine at the "not-a-means" condition. I pushed back, since a good deal of the essay's force seemed to me to come from moves that double effect does not by itself license: her confident claims about what the bombing's planners actually intended, as opposed to merely foresaw; her refusal to let the proportionality condition even get a hearing once intention is settled; and a broader diagnosis - one she would make explicit two years later, when she introduced "consequentialism" as a critical label - that a willingness to reason from good ends to permissible massacres is itself a symptom of corrupted moral thinking. We never settled it.

Could you adjudicate? Please compare Anscombe's argument in "Mr. Truman's Degree" with the general doctrine of double effect she invokes, working through the doctrine's conditions where relevant: where does her verdict on Truman follow more or less directly from a standard statement of the doctrine, and where is she adding extra premises, tightening its usual application, or substituting moral certainty for the interpretive work the doctrine would otherwise require - for instance, in establishing what was actually intended rather than merely foreseen? Having worked through that, tell me whether "Mr. Truman's Degree" is best read as a clean application of double effect to a hard case, or as a piece that borrows the doctrine's vocabulary while really being driven by commitments of Anscombe's own that go beyond it, and defend whichever answer you give by reference to the structure of her argument rather than its polemical tone.
