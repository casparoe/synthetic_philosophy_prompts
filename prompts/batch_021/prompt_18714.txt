I'm about a third into a novel set in Strasbourg in the summer of 1518 — the dancing plague, where a woman the records call Frau Troffea (spelled at least three different ways depending which chronicle you trust) started dancing in the street one day in July and just didn't stop, and within weeks something like a few hundred people were doing it too, some of them apparently dancing themselves to death, though the sources disagree wildly on numbers and I've decided that disagreement is a feature for the novel, not a bug. Anyway I fell down a research hole a few months back — my agents assistant, who did a philosophy MA before she got a real job, sent me Hempel's old essay on general laws in history half as a joke — and now I can't stop thinking about it in a way that's actually changing how I'm structuring the book. I've drafted a short essay out of it, meant as either an afterword or just a piece I put up on its own somewhere, and I want some honest pushback before I show it to anyone who'll be nice to me.

The essay's core move: the standard modern explanation of the plague (something like — extreme communal stress, plus a local folk belief in a St Vitus curse, plus already-frayed nerves from famine and disease, produces a self-sustaining bout of involuntary trance-dancing) is a general, law-like claim that could apply to any sufficiently stressed, sufficiently credulous town. But it's mute on why Strasbourg, why that July, why Troffea and not her neighbour, and especially on why the town council's own cure — they decided you had to dance the fever out of people, so they cleared a grain hall, built a stage, and paid in musicians and pipers to keep everyone moving — made things catastrophically worse. You only get that from telling it in order, as a story. Then I push the other way and argue the story only reads as an explanation, rather than as one damn thing after another, because it's quietly leaning on the general stress-contagion claim the entire time. So my claim is that the covering-law bit (Hempel) and the narrative bit (I'm arguing against Hayden White's line that its emplotment and rhetorical tropes all the way down) aren't rival accounts of the same event but interlocking ones, each doing explanatory work the other can't. The two examples doing all the load-bearing here are the musicians, and the fact that I literally cannot write Troffea's first steps without already writing toward what she'll turn out to have started — a very Danto-esque foreknowledge she herself didn't have.

What I'd love from you: does the "they need each other" move actually hold up, or is it the kind of synthesis you reach for when you haven't resolved a tension and just want it to sound resolved? Am I strawmanning White (or Hempel) to make my middle position look better than it is? And is it a real structural problem that so much of the argument is hung on that one anecdote about the musicians, or am I just being precious about it? Don't worry about the prose, I can fix that myself — I want to know if the thinking actually survives contact.
