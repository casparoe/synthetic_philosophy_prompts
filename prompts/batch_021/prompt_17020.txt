My father spent thirty-six years as an intensive-care physician. A good part of that work -- more than anyone at his memorial will want said out loud -- was deciding to stop a treatment and let a patient die, rather than ever doing anything to end a life himself. He never allowed the word "killing" anywhere near what he did on those days, and it wasn't delicacy. As a resident he had worked out a position he held for the rest of his career: that switching off a machine and administering something lethal are not the same kind of act, because in the first case the disease is still doing the causal work and he is merely stepping out of its way. What has stayed with me for twenty years, though, is that he also kept an old photocopied article in his desk that argued the opposite. It described some philosopher's thought experiment about two men, each standing to inherit money if his young cousin died: one sneaks in and drowns the boy himself; the other finds the boy already slipping underwater unassisted and simply lets it happen, standing ready to push him back under if he has to, though he never has to. The point of the story is that we judge the two men identically, which is supposed to show that doing and merely allowing don't carry the moral difference my father staked his whole career on. He told me he never found a real answer to it, and not long before he died he said, quite deliberately, that he didn't want anyone at his service letting him off the hook by saying "the disease killed them, not him" -- because he had spent forty years not entirely believing that himself.

I am the one giving the eulogy, this coming Saturday, in a room that will be roughly half his former colleagues from the unit -- who will know exactly which argument I am circling if I go anywhere near this -- and half relatives and old friends who have never heard the phrase "acts and omissions" in their lives. Could you write it for me? I would like something in the eight-to-nine-minute range that takes seriously the fact that he spent his working life doing something he was never fully certain he had the right to do, and that finds the comfort in that honesty rather than in a tidy resolution he himself never reached -- without turning into a philosophy seminar for the family, or condescending to the colleagues who already know there isn't an easy answer.
