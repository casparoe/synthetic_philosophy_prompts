I'm reporting a feature on whether classical epistemological traditions have anything rigorous to say about trusting chatbot output, and I want to get past the usual "ancient wisdom meets AI" framing. In Nyāya epistemology, verbal testimony (śabda) counts as a genuine pramāṇa only when it comes from an āpta — a speaker with first-hand knowledge of the matter and no intention to mislead — and later Naiyāyikas hold that a hearer must also grasp the speaker's tātparya, or intended sense, before any sentence yields valid cognition at all. Udayana is the natural figure to put this question to, since his Nyāyakusumāñjali argues against the Mīmāṃsā claim that the Veda is an authorless (apauruṣeya) text precisely on the grounds that meaningful sentences require an author. Using his own criteria rather than imported modern philosophy of language, how would Udayana rule on whether a large language model's output could ever count as śabda pramāṇa? Would the absence of a unified knowing, intending subject behind any given sentence place LLM testimony outside the pramāṇa altogether, as a new and more troubling version of the "authorless speech" problem he thought he had already closed off, or could he consistently locate the required author elsewhere, in the system's designers or its training corpus?
