Ok this is a bit of a random one but I've been chewing on it for weeks and want a sanity check from someone who isn't my editor. I cover tech and media stuff for a living, and a philosopher I interviewed for a totally unrelated story mentioned, kind of in passing, that Kierkegaard basically called the internet in the 1840s. Normally I'd just nod and move on but this time I actually went and read the relevant bits of "Two Ages" (the part everyone quotes gets republished on its own sometimes as "The Present Age") plus the whole saga with the Corsair, instead of pretending I already knew it. Now I've got Opinions and a draft of a newsletter item I'm not sure is any good. Here's the draft, would love your take before I send it out into the world:

Every "Kierkegaard predicted social media" take reaches for the same move: the anonymous, cruel, speed-of-light "public" he describes in the 1840s press sounds a lot like a pile-on today, and Kierkegaard himself got mocked for months by a gossip paper called the Corsair (they spent weeks on his trousers and the way he walked) after he picked a fight with one of its writers in print. Prophet of cancel culture, case closed. I think that's basically right but for the wrong reason, and it undersells how bad his actual diagnosis was. Kierkegaard's complaint in "Two Ages" isn't really that the public is mean, its that the public is a "leveling" force: it doesn't just go after individuals, it dissolves the possibility of being one at all. His answer, all over the journals, is that a person has to be able to step outside the crowd into silence and become what he calls "the single individual," answerable to themselves and God rather then the crowd. That's the bit the "Twitter is just the new Corsair" columns miss, I think: our situation is arguably worse than getting mocked in a gossip rag, because there's no outside left to step into anymore. Deleting your accounts is itself a public gesture now, and most people's sense of who they are is partly built out of anticipating an audience that's always watching. If leveling works, on Kierkegaard's own account, by closing off the space where the single individual could stand, then the real prediction isn't "there will be trolls," it's "eventually there will be nowhere left to hide from being seen" -- which is a much bleaker line and feels like the world we're actually in now. (Also, it's a little rich that the great critic of being turned into a public spectacle basically started the fight himself by outing one of the Corsair's writers in print. I can't decide if that undercuts the whole argument or is exactly the kind of thing that would prove it.)

So a few things I want pushback on specifically: is my read of what "leveling" and "the public" actually mean for Kierkegaard fair, or am I flattening a more careful argument to make my point land? Does the "there's no outside left" extension actually follow from his own framework, or is that me smuggling in a very 2024-brained idea and dressing it up in his vocabulary because it sounds cooler? And that last parenthetical about him basically starting his own scandal -- does that cut against my reading, or does it support it? Go at it however you naturally would, I just don't want to file something a Kierkegaard scholar would wince at.
