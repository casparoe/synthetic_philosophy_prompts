I am taking a seminar on philosophy of language this term, and we have just moved from Kripke's Naming and Necessity into the two-dimensionalist apparatus that Chalmers and Jackson developed as a refinement of Kripke's picture. After last week's session on natural kind terms, two of my classmates, Elena and Raj, ended up in a genuinely heated argument in the hallway outside the seminar room, and I have not been able to settle it in my own head since, even after rereading the relevant sections of Chalmers on two-dimensional semantics.

The trigger was our professor's standard example: 'water is H2O' is necessary a posteriori. (1) Elena argued that the two-dimensional framework actually shows Kripke overstated his case, because the sentence's primary intension is not necessary: there is a coherent scenario, considered as actual, in which the clear liquid filling our oceans and lakes is XYZ rather than H2O, so relative to that intension 'water is H2O' comes out false, and there is therefore a perfectly legitimate sense in which the statement is contingent. (2) Raj replied that this is not a correction of Kripke but a restatement of his view in different vocabulary: Kripke never denied that 'water' could, epistemically, have turned out to name something other than H2O — that concession is exactly why he calls the necessity a posteriori rather than a priori in the first place. (3) Elena responded that it is more than relabeling, because Kripke's own way of explaining away the appearance of contingency is to say that we are really just misdescribing a qualitatively similar world containing a different substance, never that 'water' itself might pick out something else; the two-dimensionalist move of letting the primary intension of 'water' genuinely vary across scenarios is a substantive addition to Kripke, not a translation of him, and once that addition is made explicit, the unqualified claim that identity statements between rigid designators are 'simply necessary' turns out to be true only once you have silently restricted attention to the secondary intension. (4) Raj answered that this proves too much: a primary intension is a function from scenarios to extensions, not a proposition, so it is not even the kind of thing that can be 'necessary' or 'contingent' in any metaphysically loaded sense; at most one can say the sentence is not a priori, which nobody was disputing, and that is perfectly compatible with its being metaphysically necessary. (5) Elena closed by pointing out that Chalmers cannot really intend the deflationary reading, since he explicitly treats primary possibility as doing real modal work elsewhere — most importantly in the zombie argument, where the conceivability of a physical duplicate of a person who lacks consciousness is a claim about primary possibility that is supposed to license a conclusion about what is genuinely, metaphysically possible; if Raj's reading of primary intensions were correct, it would undercut the very apparatus Chalmers needs against physicalism, not just this example.

Neither of them gave any ground, and having sat with it for a few days, I no longer think either side is simply confused. Could you go through the exchange move by move, numbered as above, and say who has the better of it at each step, rather than just declaring an overall winner? I would also value your sense of what, if anything, each of them is missing — my hunch is that part of the disagreement is really about what the two-dimensional framework is supposed to be doing in the first place (a piece of purely technical, deflationary semantics, or a tool with independent modal and metaphysical import), but I cannot tell whether that is a genuine diagnosis or just another way of dodging the object-level question.
