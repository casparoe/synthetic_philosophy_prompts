During my career as an observational cosmologist, I grew accustomed to a very concrete kind of limit on what can be found out about the world: because the expansion of the universe is accelerating, there are events happening right now whose light will never reach Earth, no matter how long we wait. My colleagues and I always spoke of this cosmic event horizon as marking facts that are not merely unknown to us but genuinely unknowable to us, as a matter of physical law rather than of technological shortfall. Since retiring I have joined a small philosophy reading circle that meets at the university library — an odd late-life hobby, but one I have come to enjoy — and our most recent session was devoted to Fitch's paradox of knowability. As I followed the argument, it starts from the modest premise that not every truth is in fact known by anyone, and derives from this, by a short chain of reasoning about what knowledge itself requires, the striking general conclusion that at least one truth must be strictly unknowable, by any mind whatsoever.

This struck me as an elegant confirmation, arrived at from a completely different direction, of something I already believed on physical grounds: that reality is stocked with facts permanently sealed off from every possible observer. I have since been describing Fitch's result to my reading group as a kind of logical counterpart to the cosmological horizon I spent my career studying, generalizing that old observational limit into a necessary truth that would hold quite apart from any facts about dark energy, the speed of light, or the geometry of spacetime. Before I repeat this comparison at our next meeting, I would be glad if you could check whether I have understood the relationship correctly, and specifically whether the unknowable truth that Fitch's argument delivers is really the same kind of thing as a fact lying beyond a cosmic horizon, or whether I am quietly running together two quite different senses of "cannot be known."
