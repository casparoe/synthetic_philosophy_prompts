I'm a software engineer, not a philosopher, but I recently fell down a rabbit hole reading about the epistemic status of the Church-Turing thesis, and I came across a striking argument of Saul Kripke's: that the thesis is not the unprovable, quasi-empirical article of faith it is usually taken to be, but a special corollary of Gödel's completeness theorem. The idea, as I understand it, is this. Turing's own analysis treats a computation as a deterministic process moving through discrete configurations according to a fixed, finite instruction table. Kripke's move is to say that any such process can be axiomatized as a first-order theory in which each configuration is a logical consequence of the instructions plus the previous one -- an assumption he calls, if I am remembering the label correctly, "Hilbert's thesis." Given Hilbert's thesis, completeness guarantees that this logical consequence is also a finite syntactic derivation, and since first-order proofs can be mechanically enumerated and checked, the whole process turns out to be Turing-computable, with no further, independent leap needed to connect "effectively calculable" to "Turing machine." Please work with the argument as I have reconstructed it here even if I have mangled some detail of Kripke's actual presentation.

The obvious objection is that this is a shell game: Hilbert's thesis is just doing, under a new name, exactly the job the Church-Turing thesis used to do, so nothing has really been proven. Please argue against that objection. Make the strongest case you can that Hilbert's thesis is a genuinely more secure and less committal assumption than the Church-Turing thesis itself -- one that, unlike the Church-Turing thesis, is not hostage to being overturned by some future exotic model of computation -- so that Kripke's derivation counts as a real, if partial, epistemic achievement rather than a relabeling of the mystery.
