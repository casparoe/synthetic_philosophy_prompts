I've been chewing on Kavka's toxin puzzle again ever since it got brought up in a rationalist forum thread about updateless decision theory and Parfit's hitchhiker, and I think I might have actually found a clean way to dissolve it, but I want a sanity check before I start telling people. Quick recap in case its not fresh in your memory: an eccentric billionaire tells you that if, by midnight tonight, you intend to drink a nasty-but-harmless toxin tomorrow afternoon (makes you sick for a day, no lasting damage), he'll wire a million dollars into your account by morning, no strings attached. He doesn't care whether you actually drink the stuff later, only whether you intend to at midnight. And obviously, once tomorrow afternoon actually rolls around, you'd have zero reason to go through with it, since the money's already been paid (or not) based purely on the intention.

Here's my "solution": since nobody, not even a weird billionaire, can literally peer into your skull and verify what you "really" intend deep down, surely the rational move is to just sincerely tell yourself at 11:58pm "yes, I intend to drink it," collect the money the next morning, and then quietly skip the toxin at noon since there's no upside left to drinking it. People write whole papers treating this as some deep puzzle about the metaphysics of intention and instrumental rationality, but if the entire thing hinges on some inspector accurately reading your inner mental state, isn't that just smuggling in a Newcomb-style perfect predictor through the back door and then acting shocked when intentions "can't" be formed? Either the billionaire can verify intentions reliably (fine, sure, weird sci-fi premise, but then its a totally different kind of puzzle) or he can't (in which case just lie to yourself and cash the check) — it feels like there's no version where the puzzle is actually hard.

I ran this by a friend from my old philosophy of action reading group over drinks last week and she just gave me a look and said "that's not really what's going on" without elaborating further, which was unsatisfying. So: am I actually dissolving something real here, or is there a specific reason philosophers don't just wave the puzzle away as easily as I'm doing?
