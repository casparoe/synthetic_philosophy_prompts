I'm a PhD student in philosophy working on a dissertation chapter about David Lewis's Humean Supervenience and, in particular, its treatment of objective chance. The part of the literature I keep circling back to is the "undermining" problem that Lewis raises in "Humean Supervenience Debugged": because chance at a time t, on a Humean best-system analysis, is fixed by the pattern of the entire history of the world rather than just the segment of history up to t, there will generally be possible continuations of history after t that, were they to occur, would make the best system come out differently — so the very chance function in force at t assigns some nonzero chance to a continuation whose occurrence would show that this was never really the true chance function at all. Lewis called this the "big bad bug," since it threatens to make the Principal Principle either incoherent or true only relative to a gerrymandered class of "admissible" evidence. His proposed fix, the New Principle, conditions credence on a proposition about which complete theory of chance obtains at a world, rather than on a bare chance proposition, and treats historical facts (including facts about the future) as admissible in a way the original formulation could not.

My advisor thinks the New Principle fully rehabilitates the Principal Principle for Humeans and considers the matter closed; I've never quite believed this, but I haven't managed to turn the unease into an actual argument, and I'd like your help doing that. Specifically, please construct the strongest case you can that the New Principle, whatever its formal merits, fails to preserve what made the original Principal Principle philosophically important in the first place — namely, an explanation of why we are rationally required to defer to chance — rather than merely showing that some conditional or other can be satisfied by Humean chances. In building the case, please take an explicit stand on at least three sub-issues: whether a chance-theory-relative conditional can do the same normative work as a simple chance proposition; whether treating full historical propositions as admissible is independently well-motivated or just reverse-engineered to dodge undermining; and whether non-Humean, primitivist accounts of chance genuinely avoid an analogous problem or merely relocate it. I'm not looking for a survey of the back-and-forth in the literature so much as your own sharpest, most carefully argued case for the skeptical conclusion, which I intend to stress-test as part of the chapter.
