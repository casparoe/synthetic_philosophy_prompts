I am several drafts deep into a novel whose central figure, Professor Miriam Calloway, has spent her career as one of the more exacting interpreters of Rawls's writing on religion. Her specialty is not the theory of justice in its popular form but the later, more technical apparatus of public reason developed in Political Liberalism and sharpened in "The Idea of Public Reason Revisited" — the claim that on questions of constitutional essentials and matters of basic justice, citizens owe one another reasons that do not depend on any comprehensive doctrine, religious or otherwise, even as comprehensive doctrines remain entirely free to flourish in what Rawls called the background culture of civil society. Miriam's father, an Episcopal priest who led the same small parish for nearly forty years — the tradition Rawls himself was raised in, for what it's worth — never forgave her for what he considered a polite, tenured way of telling believers to be quiet, and the two of them spent decades arguing about whether her "neutrality" was really neutral at all. He has just died, and, because there is no one else, Miriam has been asked to deliver his eulogy from his own pulpit.

The chapter turns on who is sitting in the pews. Most of the mourners are his parishioners, who knew Miriam only as the pastor's difficult daughter and want, understandably, to hear that he is with God. A smaller cluster are her own colleagues and former students, several of whom have built careers glossing her account of the difference between the background culture and the public political forum, and who will be listening for whether she actually believes what she has always argued in print: that the distinction was never a way of gagging believers, only a way of marking off where the strict duties of public reason do and don't reach — and that a funeral, of all things, was never inside their scope to begin with. Miriam has spent thirty years certain of that argument on paper and considerably less certain of it standing at a coffin.

I'd like you to write the eulogy itself, not a description of it — something that would run to roughly five or six minutes read aloud. She will not borrow any language about heaven or reunion that she does not believe, and she refuses to let her father's funeral turn into a seminar; within those limits, I want the speech to actually resolve, on the page, how much of a believer's voice her own philosophy leaves her free to use here, rather than gesturing vaguely at the question. If you think it earns its place, I wouldn't mind a quiet echo of Rawls's own trajectory — the senior thesis on sin and faith, written by a considerably more devout young man than the one the war eventually left behind — but only if it serves Miriam's voice rather than announcing its own cleverness. I've written and discarded three versions of this scene already, each either too reverent or too pleased with itself, so I would rather see what someone else does with the same material before attempting a fourth.
