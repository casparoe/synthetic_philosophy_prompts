A friend and I recently had a long, unresolved argument about a single passage in Nietzsche's On the Genealogy of Morality. Early in the second essay (section 2), right after describing the millennia-long process by which prehistoric humanity was disciplined into a promise-keeping, calculable animal, Nietzsche introduces the sovereign individual: autonomous, self-legislating, proud of his power to bind himself and stand as his own guarantor, a master of his own free will. My friend takes this figure to be Nietzsche's positive ideal, the ripest fruit of everything worthwhile in that long process of discipline. I suspect that reading cannot survive contact with the rest of the essay, since the sovereign individual's pride in his own responsibility and conscience sounds remarkably like the internalized cruelty and self-surveillance that the later sections diagnose as bad conscience, which would make the passage at least partly ironic. Neither of us could settle the question that night. Write an essay that weighs both readings against Nietzsche's broader treatment of guilt, responsibility, and free will elsewhere in the Genealogy and in his other work, and that defends a definite verdict rather than leaving the matter open.
