For the past six years I've run a small monthly workshop for novelists in my city — eight or nine of us, depending on the season, all working on long-form fiction and reading each other's chapters as we go. Two months ago, one member, whom I'll call R., brought in a new chapter built around a disgraced surgeon and the estranged daughter who nurses him back to health while slowly realizing he let her mother die on his operating table — a plot that was, in its overall shape and in several individual scenes, almost identical to a novel another member had workshopped with us two years earlier, before she left to do an MFA elsewhere. I said so in front of everyone, more bluntly than I'd planned, and told R. that whatever he called it to himself, this wasn't something one working writer does to another.

A few days later, Priya, a friend from the group who'd been present for both sessions, took me aside and reminded me of something I'd rather forgotten: three years ago, the climactic courtroom scene in my own novel had grown, in an early draft, directly out of a scene we'd workshopped from a member who has since stopped writing entirely. I never copied his dialogue, and I reshaped the scene enough that I don't think anyone else in the room ever noticed, but the bones of it were his, and I quietly rewrote my version before publication without telling him, or anyone, what I'd taken from it. Priya didn't dispute that R.'s chapter was worse — his was far closer, and left undisguised, with no sign he'd even tried to change it — but she still thought that, given my own history, I was hardly the right person to be lecturing R. about it, and that I should either have said nothing or said it very differently.

I keep circling the same question without quite landing anywhere. Does what I did three years ago actually bear on whether I had any business calling R. out now, or is that a separate matter entirely from whether his chapter is a problem in the first place? And does it matter that what I did looks like a smaller version of the same kind of thing, quietly corrected rather than ever owned up to, whereas R.'s was left exactly as he wrote it, for everyone to see? I'd like to work through this with some real rigor rather than just going with whatever mix of guilt and self-righteousness I happen to feel on a given afternoon, not least because I suspect some version of this exact dilemma is going to end up in the novel I'm currently drafting, and I'd like to understand it properly before I put it on the page.
