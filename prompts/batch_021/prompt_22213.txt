And here's the student's answer pretty much as submitted (I cleaned up a couple of spelling slips but left the substance, including all the reasoning, untouched):

"(a) The simple CA says x is disposed to M when C just if the conditional 'x would M if C' is true. Martin's electro-fink is supposed to show this isn't good enough, because you can hook up a device -- a 'fink' -- that watches for the stimulus and switches the disposition on or off right around the time the stimulus happens. In the classic version, a wire that is actually dead gets connected to a fink that senses a conductor approaching and instantly makes the wire live, so that 'the wire would conduct if touched' comes out true, even though, sitting there before anyone touches it, the wire has no disposition to conduct -- it's dead. So CA says the wire is disposed to conduct exactly when intuitively it isn't: the conditional is true but the categorical basis for conducting (being charged) isn't there until the instant of the stimulus. A fink in general is anything that changes whether the disposition's basis is present, and it does this in a way that's keyed to the stimulus itself, so the timing of the change is what defeats the simple conditional analysis.

(b) Lewis's fix builds the causal basis into the analysis itself: x is disposed to M when C iff there's some intrinsic property B of x such that, if x were to undergo C and were to retain B for a little while afterward, C and B together would be an x-complete cause of M. This blocks the electro-fink because the counterfactual we're now evaluating explicitly holds fixed that x keeps whatever basis it has for a bit after the stimulus, and a fink works precisely by making x lose (or gain) the basis right at the moment of the stimulus. Once retaining the basis is written into the antecedent, the fink's mechanism has nothing to bite on in the scenario we're evaluating, so the conditional gets the right value and the reformed analysis correctly calls the dead wire undisposed and the live one disposed.

(c) Masks are a different kind of case, first introduced (as an extension of Lewis's own discussion of finks) using examples like a poison that's administered together with an antidote. Here's a different one: imagine a smoke detector that's disposed to sound an alarm when smoke reaches its sensor. Suppose someone has stuck a little foam earplug over the tiny speaker grille on the unit -- the sensor and circuit still trip exactly as normal when smoke arrives, but the sound that comes out is too muffled to register as an alarm. The detector is still disposed to alarm when there's smoke -- nothing about its wiring or sensitivity has changed -- the disposition is just masked because the earplug gets in the way after the circuit has already done its job. That makes it a mask and not a fink: unlike the electro-fink, nothing about the detector's own basis for the disposition is touched at any point; an external factor just interferes with the output. And Lewis's reformed analysis handles this the same way it handled the wire case: since the detector keeps its basis B (its intact sensor and circuitry) throughout, and B together with the stimulus is what's doing the causing, the reformed analysis still correctly predicts that the detector counts as disposed to alarm, even though, because of the earplug, no one would actually hear an alarm."
