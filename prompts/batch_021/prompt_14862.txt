Between Pearl's *Book of Why* making yet another round through the rationalist blogosphere and the recurring LessWrong disputes over whether the "logical counterfactuals" invoked by functional and updateless decision theory are even coherent, I've been trying to pin down something more basic: what actually distinguishes David Lewis's counterfactual theory of causation from James Woodward's interventionist theory, given that both cash out causal claims in broadly counterfactual terms.

Write a dialogue between a committed Lewisian and a committed Woodwardian arguing over the standard late-preemption case of Suzy and Billy, who both throw rocks at a bottle: Suzy's rock gets there first and shatters it, and Billy's would have shattered it a moment later had Suzy's missed. Have the two of them first hash out whether their frameworks actually disagree about what caused the shattering, and then push into the deeper dispute — the charge that Woodward's account is covertly circular, since "intervention" is itself defined in causal terms, versus Lewis's insistence, rooted in his commitment to Humean supervenience, on a fully reductive analysis that never presupposes the very notion it's meant to explain. Give each side its strongest moves, including whatever the best interventionist reply to the circularity charge actually is, and don't let either of them win by default.
