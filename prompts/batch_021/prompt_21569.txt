And here's the student's answer:

"The discursive dilemma arises when a group needs to reach a decision on some conclusion that is logically determined by more basic premises, and the group decides by voting on each proposition separately rather than treating the issue as a single up-or-down vote. The standard illustration, due to List and Pettit, is a three-member court deciding a breach of contract case. There are two premises: whether a valid contract existed (P) and whether the defendant breached it (Q), and the legal doctrine says the defendant is liable (R) just in case P and Q both hold. Suppose Judge 1 accepts P and Q, Judge 2 accepts P but rejects Q, and Judge 3 rejects P but accepts Q. A majority accepts P (Judges 1 and 2), a majority accepts Q (Judges 1 and 3), so applying the legal rule to the majority position on each premise yields R. But a majority actually rejects R directly, since only Judge 1 individually believes the defendant is liable. So whether the court finds the defendant liable depends entirely on whether it votes premise by premise or votes on the verdict itself, even though every judge's own beliefs are perfectly consistent.

List and Pettit's theorem shows that no aggregation rule can simultaneously satisfy universal domain (the rule has to handle any possible combination of individual judgments), anonymity (no individual gets special weight), and independence (the group's judgment on a given proposition is fixed only by what individuals think of that same proposition), while still guaranteeing a complete and consistent collective judgment set. Any rule satisfying the first three conditions either collapses into a dictatorship or produces inconsistent verdicts, which is the formal content of the impossibility result.

Moving to a premise-based procedure would resolve this. On a premise-based procedure, the court doesn't bother aggregating judgments about the individual premises at all - each judge simply casts a vote on the bottom-line question of liability, and the court's verdict is whatever the majority says about R, full stop. Because there is only a single proposition being voted on, there's no way for the outcome to be inconsistent with anything else the court has said, so the whole problem simply can't arise. This is generally treated as fully resolving the tension between individual and group rationality that the dilemma brings out, and it's the main reason the premise-based procedure is treated in the literature as the standard response to the discursive dilemma."
