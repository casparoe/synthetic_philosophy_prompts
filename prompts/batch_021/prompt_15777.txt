I'm a retired chemist (spent about 35 years running a physical-organic lab before I fully hung up my lab coat), and these days I fill my mornings with philosophy instead of grant paperwork - much less stressful, even if my brain creaks a bit more doing it than it used to. My discussion group has been chewing on Jaegwon Kim's causal exclusion argument for a few weeks now, and I think I've worked out a clean way around it, but when I ran it by the group nobody was fully convinced either way, so I'm curious what you make of it.

Here's how I've got the problem in my head: it only bites because non-reductive physicalists want two things at once - mental properties that are genuinely distinct from physical properties, and mental properties that still do real causal work - while also granting that every physical event already has a fully sufficient physical cause (the closure principle) and denying that we've got systematic overdetermination running through all of nature. Put those together and the mental property looks like it's just along for the ride, excluded by its own physical realizer. So my "solution" was: why not just go back to good old interactionist dualism? If the mind is a genuinely seperate substance rather than some distinct-but-dependent property of the brain, it isn't competing for the exact same causal job as a physical cause - it's got it's own causal lane, so to speak. It seems to me Kim's puzzle is really a problem you only get into by trying to keep mental distinctness on the cheap, inside physicalism, and that a card-carrying dualist just walks around the whole exclusion worry by not accepting physicalism's terms in the first place.

Is that basically sound, or is there a reason dualism doesn't actually get you off the hook here either? My gut says I'm missing something, since none of the philosophy-of-mind people I've stumbled across seem to treat "just be a dualist" as the easy out, but I can't quite put my finger on where the mismatch in my reasoning is.
