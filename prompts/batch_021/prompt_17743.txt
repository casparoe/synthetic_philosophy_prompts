I spent almost forty years as a field ecologist before I retired a couple of years back, and I've been using some of the extra time to finally read the philosophy of science I always ment to get to instead of skimming for the one paragraph I needed to justify a methods section. Lately that's meant working through Popper, since he gets namedropped constantly in anything about "the scientific method" and I wanted to see it from the source instead of the textbook version.

In his intellectual autobiography, "Unended Quest," there's a passage in the section where he discusses natural selection that genuinely threw me, given that I spent my whole working life assuming evolutionary biology is about as solid a science as they come. He writes, "I have come to the conclusion that Darwinism is not a testable scientific theory, but a metaphysical research programme." He's not trashing the theory -- he goes on to say it has huge explanatory power -- but the worry seems to be that it can't be falsified in his own technical sense, because more or less any trait we find can be explained afterwards as an adaptation, without the theory ever risking a specific prediction beforehand. It sounds uncomfortably close to the old "whatever survives, survives" complaint about "survival of the fittest."

I mentioned this to an old colleague and she said, almost as an aside, that this exact line is a favorite of creationists trying to argue evolution isn't real science -- which hadn't even occured to me before that. I've also seen it claimed, though I've never tracked down the original myself, that Popper more or less recanted this later on. So here's what I'm hoping you can help me sort out: what exactly is the argument for calling natural selection "metaphysical" rather than scientific, by Popper's own falsifiability standard -- and is it actually a good argument, or does it quietly conflate "hard to test with our current methods" with "untestable in principle"? And if he really did recant afterward, does that mean the original worry was just a mistake on his part, or was there something to it that a serious philosophy of biology still has to reckon with, recantation or not?
