I'm a junior at my high school, and a few of us who are into philosophy run an informal lunch club that meets on Fridays; each week someone picks a short reading and we spend the period arguing about it. Two weeks ago the reading was on the epistemology of disagreement, and it laid out the split between conciliationist views, especially the "equal weight view," on which finding out that an epistemic peer disagrees with you should move your credence significantly toward theirs, and steadfast views, on which it can be rational to hold your ground even once you know someone equally qualified disagrees. Our faculty advisor, who teaches AP Statistics and likes to follow serious readings with a live demonstration, had us answer ten obscure trivia questions on our own, attach a confidence level to each answer, and then compare in pairs once everyone had handed in their phones for the meeting, which is a standing club rule that for once actually mattered. Two of my friends, Ilana and Marcus, had matched each other answer for answer and confidence level for confidence level all the way down the list, then landed on opposite, equally confident answers to the last question, how many countries share a land border with Germany, with no way for either of them to check. What followed was less a discussion of the reading than a live test of it, and I haven't been able to decide who came out ahead.

(1) Ilana went first, arguing that since their track records and confidence had matched all night, they were about as close to textbook epistemic peers as two people get, so on the equal weight view, learning that Marcus was exactly as sure of "nine" as she was of "eight" should knock her own confidence down toward the middle, and his down by the same amount. (2) Marcus objected that this can't be a general policy, because if they mechanically split the difference every time they were equally confident and disagreed, they would end the night at fifty-fifty on all ten questions, erasing whatever real difference exists between them on any given question; averaging away a disagreement, he said, discards information instead of using it. (3) Ilana clarified that the view only calls for splitting the difference when neither party has independent reason to think they're the more reliable one on that particular question, and said their identical scoring all night was exactly that kind of independent reason, since it showed the two of them really were symmetric here rather than her just assuming it. (4) Marcus replied that "identical scoring all night" is itself only an impression the two of them formed by watching and half-trusting each other's answers, so it can't be used as neutral proof of symmetry without begging the question; he then tried a different tack, pointing out that he could, right there, name all nine countries around Germany's border from memory, and argued that having actually walked through and checked his own reasoning gave him first-personal evidence Ilana simply didn't have, which entitled him to stay closer to his own answer. (5) Ilana answered that this move is available to literally anyone in any disagreement, including people who are confidently wrong, since rehearsing your reasoning and having it "check out" feels identical whether or not you made an unnoticed mistake somewhere in it; if that kind of feeling were allowed to break ties, she said, no disagreement, however symmetric, could ever actually move a person, which seems like too high a price. (6) Finally, Marcus pointed out that plenty of professional epistemologists reject the equal weight view Ilana had been defending all along, and asked whether, by her own logic, that expert disagreement should now lower her confidence in conciliationism itself. Ilana didn't have a clean answer on the spot, and neither did the rest of us listening in.

Could you go through moves (1) through (5) and say who has the better of each specific point, rather than just handing the whole argument to one of them? I'd also like your own view on the challenge Marcus raises in (6): does the equal weight view have a principled way of surviving being applied to itself, or does taking conciliationism seriously actually commit Ilana to being less of a conciliationist about it than she'd like? Please finish with a short paragraph on anything you think both of them are missing that a more careful version of either position would need to address.
