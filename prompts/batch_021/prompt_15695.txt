I have been asked to give the toast at the wedding reception of two former doctoral students of mine, who first started arguing with each other, as far as I can tell, in a graduate seminar I taught on moral worth, and by all accounts have not really stopped since. She defended a broadly Kantian line in her dissertation: an act has moral worth only if performed from duty, so that Kant's honest shopkeeper, who never overcharges his customers but does so only because honesty is good for business, deserves no moral credit for his fair dealing, however fair it is. He has spent the years since arguing the opposite, drawing on Arpaly's reading of Huckleberry Finn's decision to help Jim escape and on Markovits's "coincident reasons" account of acting for the right reasons, on which what matters is being responsive to the reasons that actually make an act right, not conformity to an explicit maxim of duty. Roughly a third of the guests are philosophers who examined or sparred with them both over the years and will hear every echo of that old argument in whatever I say; the rest are relatives for whom "moral worth" has never been anything but an ordinary phrase, and must not be made to feel otherwise. I cannot get away with the toastmaster's stock line that the two of them are marrying each other "for all the right reasons" — between them, that phrase is live ammunition. Please write the toast: about five minutes, built around their disagreement and the pun already sitting inside it, fair to both positions, and landing, for every guest in the room, as a genuine toast rather than a referee's decision.
