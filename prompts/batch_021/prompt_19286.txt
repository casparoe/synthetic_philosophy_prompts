I'm a novelist, and I've been asked to give the toast at the wedding of my oldest friend, Nora, a philosopher who's marrying Sam next spring. She wrote her dissertation on the "Qiwulun" chapter of the Zhuangzi -- the one that ends with the butterfly dream -- and about a third of the room will be her old advisor and cohort, who know the passage well enough to wince at the version everyone already half-remembers: a man dreams he's a butterfly, wakes up, and can't be sure which one is dreaming which, so maybe nothing is real -- Zhuangzi doing a Chinese Descartes. The rest of the guests have never read a word of it and just want something funny and true. I'd like the toast built instead around how the passage actually ends: the insistence that the man and the butterfly are still, necessarily, two different things, and that what's just happened is best described as a transformation rather than a puzzle about which one is real. I want to use that, honestly, to say something about two people staying entirely themselves while becoming, unmistakably, something else together -- and I'd rather not stand up at someone's wedding and imply that none of it is real, which is both bad philosophy and bad luck. Write the toast: about three minutes read aloud, warm rather than professorial, with one line in it that only the philosophers in the room will fully catch.
