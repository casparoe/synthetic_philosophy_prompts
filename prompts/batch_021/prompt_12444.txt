Over the past few months, a small dharma study group I joined has been working slowly through Jay Garfield's translation of Nagarjuna's Mulamadhyamakakarika, and it has been more unsettling than I expected. I originally trained as a mathematician and have taught introductory logic at a community college for years, so I had always treated the law of non-contradiction and the law of excluded middle as essentially non-negotiable. Encountering the verses where Nagarjuna runs some question through all four horns of the catuskoti — affirming a claim, denying it, both affirming and denying it, and neither affirming nor denying it — and appears, in different chapters, to reject all four positions at once or to endorse all four at once, sent me down a rabbit hole of secondary literature. That is how I ended up reading the argument, associated above all with Graham Priest and Jay Garfield, that Nagarjuna is best read as a dialetheist: someone committed to literal true contradictions at the limits of thought, rather than someone using the tetralemma as a merely rhetorical or therapeutic device that dissolves once conventional and ultimate truth are properly distinguished.

I co-host a small podcast on comparative philosophy, and, as it happens, our next guest is a scholar of Madhyamaka thought who defends exactly this Priest-Garfield-style dialetheist reading of the tetralemma.

Could you draft eight to ten interview questions that go beyond inviting her to simply restate the dialetheist position? I want at least one question that presses on how she keeps the acceptance of "true" contradictions principled rather than ad hoc, given that paraconsistent logics avoid explosion only by restricting, in some motivated way, which contradictions are admissible; at least one question that forces a direct comparison with the rival, logically conservative reading on which the appearance of contradiction dissolves once the rejection of all four horns is read as a non-implicative (prasajya) negation that commits her to no positive claim, so that no genuine contradiction ever arises; and at least one question about whether Nagarjuna's further claim that emptiness is itself empty counts, on her reading, as one more true contradiction or as something that escapes the tetralemma altogether, since either answer seems to invite a regress. For each of these three, please add a follow-up question keyed to the response she is most likely to give, and close the sequence with a question asking what specific textual or philosophical consideration would actually lead her to abandon the dialetheist reading, since positions of this kind are, in my experience, very easy to render unfalsifiable in conversation.
