I ended up on my state's law revision commission almost by accident. A few years back I wrote a feature on "zombie laws" still sitting in the criminal code — the sodomy statute nobody enforces anymore, an old fornication ban, that sort of thing — which was a fun detour from my usual science-and-ideas beat, and apparently someone on the commission read it and decided I'd be a decent fit for the "public member" seat their enabling statute requires (one non-lawyer, presumably so the lawyers don't just talk to each other). It's been a real education since. We're most of the way through a decade-cleanup pass on the old morals chapter of the code, and almost everything is dying without a fight — nobody's defending the cohabitation or fornication bans anymore. But we're stuck on the bigamy statute, and because I'm apparently "good with words" I got voluntold to draft the recommendation memo for next month's public session.

Our charter says we're supposed to recommend repealing any statute that no longer serves "a legitimate public purpose apart from enforcing private morality" — a phrase nobody thought hard about until now, because usually it's obvious which laws are just dead weight. Here's the fact pattern that's actually splitting the comission, stipulated to strip out the messy stuff: three adults, all single on paper (divorced or never married to each other), who've lived as a family for over a decade and consider themselves married even though only one marriage license exists between any two of them — no fraud on taxes or benefits, no minors involved, everyone got into it as a consenting adult. One camp wants to just carve cases like this out of the statute: there's no identifiable victim, so what's the legitimate public purpose? A second camp, led by a retired judge who keeps invoking the old Hart-Devlin fight from her law school days, says that's the wrong question entirely — the criminal law is entitled to protect monogamous marriage as a shared institution and treat its erosion as a harm to the community's moral fabric, whether or not any particular defendant hurts anybody in particular. A third camp isn't fully sold on either argument, but worries that carving out "clean" cases like this makes it practically harder to prosecute the genuinely coercive polygamous compounds the statute was originally aimed at, and that a visible repeal sends a signal regardless of which philosophy wins the room.

Could you draft the actual memo — repeal, retain, or something narrower — with a recommendation Im prepared to stand behind out loud in front of the judge? I don't want three paragraphs of harm-principle-101 that quietly assumes the moral-fabric argument isn't real; I want it treated seriously enough that she couldn't wave it off as a strawman, even if you end up recommending against her. And it'd help if you were honest about whether the third camp's worry is really an argument about this statute, or an argument about an entirely different one.
