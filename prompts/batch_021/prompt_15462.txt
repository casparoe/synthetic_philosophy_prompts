I'm taking a seminar this semester on the ethics of blame and accountability, and although most of what we've covered so far concerns what makes someone a fitting target of blame in the first place, something that happened in my project group last week doesn't fit neatly into any of that and has been bothering me since.

Here's the situation. One member of my four-person team, Dan, has turned in his portion of our work late on three separate occasions this semester, and each time the rest of us quietly picked up the slack rather than make an issue of it so close to finals. Last week I was late for the first time all semester, and Dan sent a fairly pointed message to our group chat about how unreliability "drags the whole team down" and how everyone needs to respect shared deadlines. When I brought this up with him afterward, privately, he said it was beside the point: I really had missed the deadline, it really had held things up, and raising his past behavior was just a way of avoiding responsibility for mine.

When I mentioned this to a couple of classmates, they said Dan was being a hypocrite and left it at that, but that label doesn't really settle anything for me. Nothing about Dan's own lateness makes it false that I was late this time, or that it caused a problem, so in one sense his message seems perfectly accurate. But it still seems to matter, somehow, that it was Dan rather than one of my other teammates who sent it, and I can't work out whether that reaction is just defensiveness on my part. Is there a genuine distinction between whether a piece of blame is correct and whether a given person is the right one to deliver it, and if so, what would Dan need to do — acknowledge his own lateness, apologize for it, actually change his behavior — before he would be entitled to say this kind of thing to me again?
