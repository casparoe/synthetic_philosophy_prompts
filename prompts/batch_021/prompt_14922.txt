A friend and I spent a long evening arguing about a passage in Plato's Laws that, in hindsight, neither of us handled with much rigor. In Book 1, the Athenian Stranger defends supervised drinking parties on the grounds that wine, by loosening a person's self-command, works as a diagnostic test of character, much as controlled exposure to fear is used elsewhere to test and train courage; in Book 2 this is extended into the claim that older citizens especially benefit from wine, since it undoes the stiffness of age and makes them willing to sing, dance, and take part in the choral education that keeps the city's values alive. My friend thought this was a genuine and defensible extension of Plato's psychology of the soul; I argued that it sits badly with his other commitments about habituation and the rule of reason over appetite, and that the diagnostic rationale and the pedagogical rationale for licensed drunkenness are really two different arguments he runs together without ever noticing the tension between them. Neither of us settled the matter. Write a dialogue between two Plato scholars, one defending the coherence of his position and the other pressing the objections above, in which each responds to the other's strongest point rather than simply restating their own -- make sure the defender directly answers the habituation worry, and the critic directly answers the fear/courage analogy, before the exchange closes.
