So a while back I wrote a little naive-bayes-ish classifier at work just for fun, and that sent me down a rationalist-blog rabbit hole (LessWrong, Astral Codex Ten, that whole scene) reading about Bayes' rule and how you're supposed to update on evidence. Ordinary conditionalization I feel like I've got down fine: you learn E, you become certain of it, P(E) goes to 1, and everything else just gets renormalized conditional on E. Simple enough.

Then I ran into Richard Jeffrey's version of this, "Jeffrey conditionalization" (sometimes called "probability kinematics"), which is for cases where the evidence itself isn't certain -- the classic example is something like looking at a piece of cloth by candlelight and becoming more confident its green rather than blue, without ever being fully sure either way because the lighting's bad. The rule -- new P(A) is a weighted average of the old P(A|green), P(A|blue), etc over whatever partition of hypotheses you're using, weighted by your new confidence in each -- makes sense to me mechanically. What actually broke my brain was mentioning this to a friend doing a philosophy PhD, and she casually dropped that Jeffrey updates don't commute in general, meaning if you get two separate uncertain pieces of evidence, the order you process them in can literally change where your probabilities end up. Apparently that's not a problem for regular certain-evidence conditionalization, where you'd land in the same place either way regardless of order.

That really doesn't sit right with me intuitively -- I'd have assumed the same info should land you in the same place no matter what order it shows up in. Could you walk me through, in a fairly hand-wavy/intuitive way, why this order-dependence actually creeps in for Jeffrey conditionalization but not for the normal kind? I don't need the fully general theorem, just enough of a small concrete example that I actually get why it's true, instead of just believing it because a philosophy PhD told me to.
