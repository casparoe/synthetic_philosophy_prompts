This term I'm taking a seminar cross-listed between philosophy and economics called Epistemic Foundations of Game Theory, and a few weeks ago our professor had the discussion sections actually play a short centipede game in pairs for extra-credit points, partly to make the backward-induction proof from lecture feel less abstract. The rules were standard: the pot starts small and doubles every round that the player whose turn it is chooses to pass; whoever instead chooses to take pockets the larger share of the pot as it stands that round and ends the game on the spot, leaving the other player the smaller share. Two people in my section, Ines and Marco, were paired together, and what actually happened in their round — Ines passed on round one, Marco passed on round two, Ines passed again on round three, and Marco finally took on round four, leaving both of them with noticeably more than either would have gotten from an immediate round-one split — turned into an argument at the debrief that the class never really resolved.

Marco opened by saying Ines's very first move had simply been a mistake: our professor had just finished proving that if it is common knowledge that both players are rational — each knows the other is rational, knows that the other knows it, and so on without end — backward induction shows the only outcome consistent with that is taking immediately on round one, and Ines had passed anyway. Ines answered that the proof's conclusion only kicks in if its premise actually holds, and nothing about two students randomly paired for extra credit guaranteed that airtight, infinitely-iterated condition; she had no special reason to think Marco met it, and given even a modest chance that he might be the type to pass rather than take on round two, passing herself was the payoff-maximizing move — an application of rationality under realistic uncertainty, not a lapse from it. Marco pushed back that this move, generalized, would let anyone escape the theorem's force in every real instance of the game, since nobody ever has ironclad proof of common knowledge of rationality about an actual opponent; if "I wasn't sure you met the assumption" always excuses passing, the result the class had spent a week proving would never bind on anyone, which struck him as too convenient to be the right way to use it. Ines's closing reply was that the theorem cuts both ways: it also entails that two players with common knowledge of each other's rationality would never actually be sitting at round three or four to begin with, so the mere fact that their game reached round four is already proof, on the theorem's own terms, that the assumption failed somewhere between them — and once that is true, insisting that she was still bound to have taken on round one isn't applying the theorem to their situation, it is insisting on a conclusion whose premise their situation had already falsified. Neither of them budged, and our professor moved on to the next topic before anyone could settle it.

I'd like an outside adjudication, since I keep going back and forth on this myself. Could you work through the exchange move by move — Marco's opening charge, Ines's defense, Marco's rebuttal, and Ines's closing reply — and say, for each, who has the better of it, or whether they are both onto something real? I'm especially interested in whether either side is quietly asking the theorem to do more work than it actually can: Marco in treating "take on round one" as an unconditional obligation regardless of what you believe about your opponent, Ines in treating the mere fact that they reached round four as a free pass for her opening move. Please also say whatever else you think both of them are missing about what common knowledge of rationality does and doesn't deliver in a game like this one, and close with a short overall verdict on whether Ines's decision to pass on round one was, all things considered, rationally defensible.
