I spent thirty-five years running collider experiments before I retired, and for the past year or so I have been using the extra time to finally read the philosophy that physicists like to gesture at without ever quite explaining — mostly the literature on anthropic reasoning, since I got tired of hearing "the anthropic principle" invoked at conferences as though it settled arguments about the cosmological constant. That reading led me, via Bostrom, into the dispute between the Self-Sampling Assumption and the Self-Indication Assumption, and I confess that the "presumptuous philosopher" thought experiment has been keeping me up at night in a way I did not expect a probability puzzle to.

Here is the version of it that has been bothering me. Suppose two rival cosmological models are equally consistent with every observation collected to date. Model A holds that space is finite and that the universe will recollapse in a few hundred billion years, implying that only some fixed, moderate number of observers, N, will ever have existed. Model B holds that space is infinite, or that inflation never really stops, implying that the eventual number of observers is many orders of magnitude larger than N. A consortium has spent a decade building a satellite whose measurement of spatial curvature will finally settle which model is correct. A week before launch, a philosopher walks into mission control and announces that the launch is pointless: by the Self-Indication Assumption, the mere fact of one's own existence as an observer is evidence favoring whichever theory predicts more observers, and since Model B predicts vastly more, its probability is already, before a single photon has been collected, arbitrarily close to one. I cannot locate the flaw in the argument, yet the thought that reasoning about how many minds like mine exist could make an actual measurement redundant feels backward to someone who spent a career trusting data over armchair argument — a former colleague tells me this is a cousin of the "measure problem" in eternal inflation, which I gather is a related but distinct headache. What keeps me from simply rejecting the Self-Indication Assumption is that its rival, the Self-Sampling Assumption, comes with its own well-known embarrassments: the Doomsday Argument, the reference-class problem, and the odd "Adam and Eve" and "UN++" cases used to stress-test it. Trading one horn for the other does not obviously help.

I would like you to write a dialogue between the presumptuous philosopher and the mission's lead physicist, staged in mission control in the minutes before launch, in which each tries to talk the other out of their position. Both should be the strongest possible versions of their views rather than caricatures, and each should have to answer the other's actual objections rather than simply restate their own: the philosopher should be pressed on the reference-class problem as it arises for the Self-Indication Assumption too, on the worry that infinite universes and Boltzmann brains make the relevant sums diverge or misbehave, and on why mere headcount should count as evidence at all; the physicist should be pressed on what alternative principle, if not something in the spirit of the Self-Indication Assumption, licenses ordinary inferences from the bare fact of one's own existence, and should have to face the Doomsday Argument and the reference-class troubles that come with retreating to the Self-Sampling Assumption instead. I would rather see exactly where the disagreement actually bottoms out than watch either side concede too easily.
