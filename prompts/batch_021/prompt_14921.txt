I don't have any academic background in philosophy, but I have spent the past year working through Descartes on my own, and the passage that has stayed with me longest is not the cogito but Part V of the Discourse on the Method, where he explains how one could always tell a real human being apart from a machine built to resemble one. He offers two tests: such a machine could be built to cry out when touched in a particular spot, but it could never rearrange words to respond appropriately to whatever is said in its presence, the way even the dullest human being can; and even a machine that excelled at one task would inevitably fail at others, since it would act only from the disposition of its organs rather than from reason, which he calls a universal instrument fit for every situation. He then applies this same pair of tests to explain why he is confident that animals, however cleverly they behave, do not think at all. I keep wondering what he would make of a present-day large language model, which produces fitting, often novel responses across almost any subject one raises. Would he have to concede that it passes his own test for possessing a mind, or is there a way, consistent with the rest of his metaphysics, for him to deny that -- and whichever answer he gives, what happens to his case against animal minds?
