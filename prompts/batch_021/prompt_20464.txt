Two weeks ago I closed out a substantial position on an election-forecasting market by betting directly against another trader whose long-run calibration is, by any measure available to me, at least as good as my own. Before taking opposite sides, we spent an evening walking each other through our full reasoning — the same polling averages, the same turnout models, the same historical base rates — and each of us adjusted slightly, then traded anyway. A friend in economics has since introduced me to Aumann's agreement theorem and the no-trade results built on it, which imply that two Bayesians sharing a common prior cannot rationally keep betting against each other once their posteriors are common knowledge between them. Our exchange came uncomfortably close to satisfying that description, and yet we disagreed just the same. Write an essay that uses an episode like this to adjudicate a real dispute about what the theorem actually shows: when reflective, informed people trade against one another after openly comparing reasoning, is the better diagnosis that their priors were never truly common, that "common knowledge" of each other's posteriors was never really achieved, or that one of us was simply being irrational — and given how demanding those assumptions are, does the theorem tell us anything substantive about disagreement outside idealized models at all? Weigh these possibilities and commit to a specific verdict, even a conditional one (e.g., "X unless Y"), rather than resting on "it depends."
