I spent my working life as a plant geneticist, breeding cereal cultivars for yield, and it is only in retirement that I have had the leisure to read philosophy properly. One of the pleasures of this second career has been recognizing old acquaintances in new clothes. Goodhart's Law is a case in point: every breeder learns early that this season's highest-yielding line is partly a matter of luck, and that its offspring will regress toward the population mean rather than repeat the extreme performance — the same phenomenon Francis Galton described in his study of human height, which is why statisticians call it "regression" at all. When I later came across David Manheim and Scott Garrabrant's taxonomy of Goodhart effects, I found that same mechanism formalized under the label "regressional Goodhart": selecting on a proxy that is correlated with, but not identical to, some deeper goal will always select partly for noise, so the top performers by the proxy disappoint on the goal — with no scheming agent required anywhere in the story. Their taxonomy also names an "adversarial" variant, in which the damage is done not by statistics but by agents who notice that the proxy has become a target and adapt their behavior to exploit the gap between it and the goal.

I would like to see these two mechanisms argued out directly. Please write a dialogue between two consultants called in to explain why a university's new metric for research quality collapsed shortly after being tied to funding decisions (you may invent the specifics of the metric and of its collapse). One consultant should maintain that the failure is at bottom regressional — a statistical artifact that would have appeared even had nobody changed their behavior in response to the metric — and should press for remedies such as shrinkage estimators or multiple independent measures. The other should maintain that the failure is at bottom adversarial, and should press instead for redesigning the underlying incentives. Have the two respond to each other's actual arguments rather than simply restating their own positions, and let the dialogue close with them clarifying, as best they can, whether their disagreement is empirical (about what actually happened at this university), conceptual (about whether "Goodhart's Law" should cover both mechanisms under one name), or some mixture of the two.
