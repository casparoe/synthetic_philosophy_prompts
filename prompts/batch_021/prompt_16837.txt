I spent almost forty years as a field ecologist, and a fair share of that career went into turning survey data into figures a non-specialist could take in at a glance: population trends, range contractions, and the like. Since retiring I have been working through the history of the Vienna Circle, and I was delighted to find that one of its founders, Otto Neurath, spent decades on essentially the same problem. His Vienna Method of Pictorial Statistics, later developed into Isotype together with Marie Neurath and the graphic artist Gerd Arntz, followed strict design rules — most famously, that a larger quantity should be shown by repeating an icon of fixed size rather than by enlarging a single icon, so that the eye is not misled by area. Neurath plainly saw more in this than a design trick: in his more programmatic essays he writes as though a sufficiently disciplined picture language might go some way toward the Circle's larger ambition of a language for unified science that would not trip over the ambiguities of words.

That suggestion is what I would like your help thinking through, because it sits oddly next to something I had just finished reading when I came across it. Wittgenstein's Tractatus, a text the Circle famously worked through line by line, builds an entire theory of meaning on the claim that any meaningful proposition is itself a picture (Bild) of a possible fact, in virtue of sharing its logical form with that fact. The two uses of "picture" seem to me to pull in different directions: Neurath's pictograms, at least in the examples I have seen, have no clean way of depicting negation, generality, or a conditional, whereas Wittgenstein's picturing proposition is meant to cover every meaningful sentence, including ones that do exactly that. So I am left wondering whether these are two projects related in some philosophically serious way, or whether the shared vocabulary is closer to a coincidence between two people who happened to move in the same circle. I would like you to compare the two notions of "picture" with some care, stating as precisely as you can what each requires of something for it to count as a picture in the relevant sense, and then tell me, with reasons, in which respects Neurath's picture language deserves to be seen as a special case or application of something like Wittgenstein's picture theory, and in which respects the two are doing different enough jobs that treating them as instances of a single idea would be a mistake.
