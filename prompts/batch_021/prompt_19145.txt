I retired three years ago after a career as a research chemist, and in the time since I have finally been able to read moral and political philosophy properly rather than in the odd stolen evening. Partly on the strength of that reading, and partly, I suspect, because the appointing committee wanted someone from outside the legal and military professions, I was recently made a civilian member of our region's Conscientious Objection Review Board, which hears applications from service members seeking discharge or reassignment on grounds of conscience. A case coming before us next month has occupied me rather more than I expected, and I would like help thinking it through before I draft the short opinion I am expected to circulate to my four fellow members ahead of the vote.

The applicant is a logistics sergeant with an unblemished record across two previous deployments, and he is careful to say that he is not a pacifist: he would serve without hesitation if the country itself were invaded, and he is not alleging that he has personally been ordered to do anything unlawful. His objection is to the character of the specific campaign he is now assigned to, which he has come to believe, on the strength of casualty figures and rules-of-engagement he has seen in the course of his duties, amounts to a punitive campaign against a civilian population rather than anything resembling self-defense. Our board's founding regulation grants objector status only to those "conscientiously opposed to participation in war in any form," and is silent on someone who accepts the legitimacy of war-fighting in general but has concluded, on the facts available to him, that this particular war fails the test. Three of my fellow members want to deny the application: they argue that a sergeant is simply not well placed to adjudicate the justice of the war he is sent to fight, that such judgments belong to elected officials rather than to soldiers, and that granting an exception here would collapse a distinction the whole apparatus of military law depends on—between how a soldier fights and what he is fighting for—and would commit the board to relitigating the merits of every deployment that comes before it. The fourth thinks it arbitrary, and possibly self-undermining, to deny a claim solely because it is selective rather than total, since that seems to concede that the sergeant could be right about this particular war while still compelling him to fight in it.

I keep wanting to titrate this down to a clean endpoint, the way I would have in the lab, but it refuses to settle however carefully I add the titrant. Could you draft the opinion I am meant to circulate? I would like it to recommend one specific disposition of the case—grant, deny, or some intermediate outcome such as reassignment to a noncombat role—with reasoning my colleagues could actually engage with, including a fair account of why the view that ordinary soldiers should not be in the business of judging the wars they are sent to fight has the force it does, even if you conclude it should not decide this particular case.
