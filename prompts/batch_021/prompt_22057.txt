I'm a backend engineer at a mid-sized tech company, and we recently found out that the company has started offering an unusual long-term benefit: enrollment in an experimental "continuity program" run by a partner biotech firm. The pitch is that if an enrollee dies unexpectedly, or simply reaches the point where anti-aging treatments stop working, the firm performs an extremely high-resolution scan of their brain -- a process that, given current sensor technology, destroys the tissue as it reads it, somewhat like how you cannot read the most degraded old magnetic tape without damaging it further in the process -- and uses that scan to configure a synthetic body that wakes up with all of the person's memories and mannerisms intact, picking their life back up where it left off. A colleague of mine on the infrastructure team, who has already signed up, describes it as "basically a live migration of a VM onto new hardware": the workload keeps running continuously as far as any outside observer can tell, so nothing of substance has been lost just because the original hardware is gone.

I am seriously considering enrolling myself, but I keep getting stuck on a detail buried in a footnote of the informational materials: in a handful of early cases, the scan malfunctioned partway through, so the original body survived the procedure, albeit with enough incidental damage that doctors did not expect it to last more than a few more days, while the synthetic version, built from that same incomplete scan, had already been activated elsewhere and was going about an entirely normal life. Apparently the company's standard guidance to the dying original patients in these cases is that they should take real comfort in knowing they will "carry on" through the synthetic version, and, according to the materials, patient reactions to this news have been decidedly mixed. That detail bothers me far more than the ordinary case does, even though my colleague insists it is a distinction without a difference: just an unusually well-documented instance of the exact same transition that every other enrollee undergoes instantly and unconsciously.

I do not have any background in philosophy, so I have no idea whether there is already a rigorous way of thinking about this, but I would genuinely like a substantive answer rather than reassurance. In the ordinary, malfunction-free case, would enrolling actually get me anything like what I currently get from surviving into tomorrow, or would it just produce someone very similar to me while whatever it is that I actually care about simply ends on the operating table? And given that the malfunction cases seem to show those two things coming apart, should that change how I think about the ordinary case at all, or is my colleague basically right that I am drawing a line that does not track anything real?
