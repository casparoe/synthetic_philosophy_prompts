I am part of a reading group on rational choice theory, and we recently spent a session on Gregory Kavka's 1983 toxin puzzle: an eccentric billionaire will pay you a large sum of money if, by midnight tonight, you form the sincere intention to drink a vial of toxin tomorrow; the toxin will make you painfully ill for a day but will not otherwise harm you. He can detect your actual mental state with a scanner stipulated to be perfectly accurate, so a merely verbal claim will not suffice, and, crucially, whether you receive the money depends only on tonight's intention and not on tomorrow's action -- the money will already be transferred before the moment when you would actually have to drink. Afterward, two people from the group, Naomi and Devesh, kept arguing about it out in the hallway, and I mostly just listened.

(1) Devesh opened by saying the puzzle trades on a confusion: intending is simply deciding, and deciding is something one can do at will, the way one can decide tonight to set an early alarm while already suspecting one will want to hit snooze. On his view, he can simply decide, right now, to drink the toxin tomorrow; that satisfies the billionaire's condition outright, and whatever happens tomorrow is a separate matter with no bearing on tonight's decision.

(2) Naomi objected that this only works if "deciding" means an empty verbal act rather than forming a real intention. A genuine intention, she said, is a state that is supposed to issue in action absent new information, and Devesh already has all the information he will ever have: he knows with certainty, tonight, that drinking will cost him a day of misery and gain him nothing, since the money will already have cleared. Nothing is left for tomorrow's self to learn. If he can already predict his own perfectly rational reasoning that far in advance, he is not intending anything -- only narrating a plan he already knows he will drop.

(3) Devesh replied that this standard proves too much, since it would disqualify intentions we normally treat as genuine. Someone who resolves tonight to go running tomorrow morning also knows, with near certainty, that tomorrow's self will feel a strong pull to skip it and will have some excuse ready. We do not usually say such a person fails to really intend to run, merely because an outside observer could predict exactly how the temptation will unfold.

(4) Naomi answered that the analogy breaks down at the crucial point. The tempted runner's future self, in skipping the run, would be caving to a desire that the runner's own standards count as a lapse -- a failure by their own lights. Devesh's future self tomorrow would not be lapsing into anything: skipping the toxin is exactly what a clear-headed, fully rational agent with Devesh's own goals should do, with no weakness of will involved anywhere. That, she said, is the actual bite of the puzzle -- it is not a story about temptation overriding a good intention, but one where the agent's own foreseeable rationality is what undoes the intention before it can even form.

(5) Devesh's last move was to concede the disanalogy but argue that it only shows expected-utility maximization is too thin a theory of intention, not that intending is impossible here: if rational agents can be "resolute" -- capable of sticking to a plan even when, once the moment for acting on it arrives, doing so no longer serves their interests -- then the puzzle dissolves, since such an agent could sincerely intend tonight and follow through tomorrow regardless. Naomi's rejoinder was that this merely renames the difficulty instead of solving it: positing a standing disposition to override one's own clear-eyed self-interest is exactly the sort of thing whose rational credentials the puzzle puts in question, so it is circular to assume the disposition in order to explain how an intention that supposedly depends on it could arise in the first place.

They left it unresolved and asked what I thought, but I honestly have no settled view. Could you go through the five moves above in order and say, for each one, who has the better of the argument and why -- not just an overall winner -- and then close with a brief remark on what, if anything, both Naomi and Devesh are overlooking about the puzzle?
