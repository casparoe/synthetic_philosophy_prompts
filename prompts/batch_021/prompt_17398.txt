Ok so this is going to sound like a weird rabbit hole but bear with me. I joined a local effective giving circle a few months back (five of us pool about $40k/yr and vote on where it goes each quarter), and at the last meeting one guy brought up an argument called "evidential cooperation in large worlds" to say we should deliberately send some of our pool to causes none of us actually think are the most important. It's been rattling around in my head since then. I wrote up a post to bring to the next meeting laying out the argument and my one big problem with it, and I'd really appreciate comments before I inflict this on the group -- on whether I'm representing the argument right, whether my objection actually lands or if there's something that rescues it, and honestly whether this is too in-the-weeds for a group that mostly wants to argue about GiveWell spreadsheets. Draft below:

Why we should maybe give money to causes we don't believe in

Right now our pool goes almost entirely to AI safety orgs because three of the five of us are convinced that's the highest-leverage thing anyone can fund. The ECL argument says that might be a mistake, and not for the usual "you could be wrong" reason.

Here's the rough idea. Say the universe (or multiverse, if you're into many-worlds, or just a spatially infinite universe) is really big -- big enough that huge numbers of agents exist that we can never causally contact, some of whom reason in ways correlated with how we reason even though they land on totally different values. Since we can't causally affect them, a causal decision theorist just shrugs. But if you give any weight to evidential or "updateless" reasoning, your choice here is evidence about what those correlated-but-disconnected agents choose. So if we adopt a policy of nudging some resources toward causes we don't personally rate, that's evidence that agents elsewhere -- who don't rate our causes, but reason the same way we do about this very argument -- are doing the same for us. Nobody moves first, nobody negotiates, nobody even finds out the trade happened, but everyone's values end up a bit better served then if everyone had gone all-in on their own top pick. It's basically Hofstadter's old superrationality trick from the prisoner's dilemma, scaled up to a whole population of value systems we'll never meet.

Here's my problem. Open Phil already argues for "worldview diversification" -- spreading money across cause areas because you're not certain your moral and empirical views are right -- and that argument needs zero multiverse machinery, just ordinary uncertainty. Every time I try to find something ECL recommends that plain worldview diversification doesn't already recommend, I come up empty, which makes me suspect either (a) ECL isn't doing any independent work and "acausal trade" is just a dramatic redescription of hedging, or (b) I'm missing a case where the two arguments actually pull apart -- e.g. maybe ECL says to cooperate with value systems you're fully confident are wrong, not just ones you're unsure about, which ordinary moral uncertainty could never justify. I keep going back and forth on which one it is.

There's also a smaller worry I don't fully trust myself on: even granting all of it, how would we actually identify which causes to divert to? "Whoever's out there reasoning like us" isn't exactly something I can put in a spreadsheet next to our other cause areas.

Comment on whatever's most useful here -- whether I've got ECL right, whether (a) or (b) is closer to the truth (or neither), whether a practical "so give X% to Y" conclusion even follows from ECL if it survives my objection, and whether this is written in a way that wont make people's eyes glaze over before we get to the interesting part.
