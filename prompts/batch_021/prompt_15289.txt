I am assembling a reading packet for a graduate seminar on the philosophy of space and time, and I keep running into the same difficulty when I teach the hole argument: students grasp the basic diffeomorphism trick quickly enough, but the discussion usually stops right where it becomes interesting, at the point where the substantivalist tries to hold her ground rather than simply concede defeat. I would like a dialogue that picks up exactly there, so that students see the argument as a live exchange rather than a single knockdown blow followed by a footnote about "sophisticated substantivalism."

Stage the exchange between a manifold substantivalist and a relationalist who both accept the technical setup without dispute: a diffeomorphism that acts as the identity everywhere outside a matter-free "hole" region but varies smoothly inside it will carry any solution of Einstein's field equations to a second solution that agrees with the first on everything outside and prior to the hole, yet disagrees inside it. The substantivalist, committed to spacetime points as individuals whose identity does not depend on the fields defined over them, therefore appears stuck with an indeterminism that has no possible physical or observational correlate. Have her respond with the move that has come to be called sophisticated substantivalism in the literature (associated with, among others, Butterfield, Brighouse, and Hoefer): reject haecceitism about spacetime points — the thesis that a point keeps its identity across models independently of the qualitative role it plays — and so deny that the two diffeomorphic models describe distinct physical possibilities at all, while still insisting that points are genuine substances and not mere constructs out of matter and its relations. I want the relationalist to press her hard on whether that concession leaves anything standing of substantivalism's original appeal, or whether giving up primitive, transworld identity for spacetime points simply is relationalism (or what Earman and Norton called Leibniz equivalence) wearing a borrowed name; and I want the substantivalist to have a real answer, not a stipulation, to the question of what, if not that primitive identity, still makes her a substantivalist rather than a relationalist. Let the relationalist answer for herself in turn: why does denying spacetime points their own identity across models cost nothing, when we would balk at the analogous move for ordinary objects or persons?

Let the two of them genuinely engage each other's strongest moves rather than talk past one another, and let the dialogue end wherever it honestly leads — agreement, a stable division of labor, or a frank impasse over whether what remains of their disagreement is still about the physical world or has become a dispute about how philosophers ought to use the word "substance." A dozen or so exchanges apiece should be enough; I am less interested in a tidy resolution than in seeing the crux of the disagreement pinned down with some precision.
