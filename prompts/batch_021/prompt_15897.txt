As the philosopher on my teaching hospital's resource-allocation subcommittee, I've been asked to help settle our tie-breaking rule for scarce ECMO machines among patients who rank equally on the triage tiers. Our biostatistician wants to replace the coin flip with a lottery skewed toward whichever patient has the higher modeled expected survival benefit, however marginal, arguing that Harsanyi's aggregation theorem shows that any policy satisfying expected-utility rationality and the Pareto principle must maximize a weighted sum of patients' survival prospects, so an even-odds lottery leaves expected lives on the table. Several clinicians want to keep equal odds, insisting that patients within a tier are owed equal treatment as a matter of procedure, not merely equal odds in expectation, and warning that weighting by benefit estimates whose margins sit inside our models' error bars will smuggle back the very comorbidity-linked biases the tiering system was built to screen out. Please draft the short memo I'll bring to Friday's meeting: recommend a specific tie-breaking rule, state plainly what the appeal to the theorem does and doesn't establish, and engage honestly with the strongest objection to whichever rule you recommend.
