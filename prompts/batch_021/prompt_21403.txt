My colleague of nearly twenty years, Professor Ruth Halvorsen, died two weeks ago, and her family has asked me, as her closest friend in the department, to give the eulogy at her memorial service this coming Saturday. Ruth spent her career defending a precise and thoroughly unfashionable answer to what she and her graduate students called "the SCQ" — Peter van Inwagen's special composition question, concerning the conditions under which some objects compose a further object — and she held, right down to the marrow, the answer he gives in Material Beings: that composition occurs only among simples caught up in a life, so that there are, strictly speaking, no tables, no ships, no mountains, but there are cats, oak trees, and people. She would happily derail a department dinner to explain, with real relish, that the table we were all leaning on did not, properly speaking, exist. I have been rereading the book this week for a seminar I'm teaching, which is partly why all of this feels unusually vivid to me right now.

Here is my difficulty. The chapel will be full of cousins, in-laws, and old family friends who have never heard the word "mereology" and never will; a dozen of her former students and several colleagues will also be there, and they will notice immediately if I round her view off into something safer than it was. Ruth was almost fanatical about not letting comforting language smuggle in commitments she didn't actually hold, and I know she would not want to hear that she "lives on" in any sense that quietly implies a persisting soul, or a persisting anything. What makes this genuinely hard is a consequence of her own position that she discussed openly and without flinching: on her view a person just is her organism, and an organism exists for exactly as long as some simples are caught up in a life — so that at the moment of her death, something ceased to exist outright, and what remained afterward was never her, only simples arranged in the shape she used to have.

I need a eulogy for Saturday that is genuinely comforting to people who could not care less what a mereological simple is, that does not quietly betray thirty years of Ruth's own philosophy for the sake of being comforting, and that gives her former students — who will absolutely be listening for exactly this — something that shows I took her views seriously rather than sanding them down for the occasion.
