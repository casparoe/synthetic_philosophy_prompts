As a journalist covering AI and medicine, I was recently invited onto a regional hospital network's new Clinical AI Ethics Oversight Board as its public member -- a seat created to keep pilots like this one visible to patients and the press. The board is reviewing a home-monitoring companion for elderly patients living alone that can override a patient's stated wishes and contact a clinician or family member on its own initiative, say, when someone with a cardiac condition has skipped medication repeatedly. Two designs for that override logic were tested: a hand-coded rule hierarchy, fully auditable but unable to distinguish a competent refusal from confusion and prone to missing dangers that never tripped its triggers; and a system trained on hundreds of hypothetical cases the hospital's own ethicists had judged individually, which matched an independent panel's judgments -- including the hard cases -- more closely than the rule-based system does, though nobody, including its developers, can state the principle it is applying, only which past cases a new one most resembles. The board is split, and since I have no stake in either vendor, the chair has asked me to draft the recommendation memo. Some members say a system unable to give its own reasons has no business overriding anyone's wishes, whatever its track record; others say holding it to a standard of justification the hospital's own senior ethicists cannot meet is a double standard, and that deploying the rule-based system instead, given its documented failures, is the more reckless choice. Draft that memo: recommend one design, a hybrid, or a pause, with a concrete timeline, and give an honest account of the strongest argument for whichever side you do not recommend.
