This term I'm taking a seminar that pairs Sartre's chapter on bad faith with the analytic literature on self-deception, and one issue from lecture keeps nagging at me. Sartre rejects Freud's theory of repression on the grounds that positing a censor merely relocates the paradox of bad faith rather than solving it, since the censor would itself have to "know" what it represses in order to repress it. His own alternative treats bad faith as a single, "metastable" consciousness that manages to half-believe its own lie without ever splitting into two systems. It strikes me that Alfred Mele's deflationary account of self-deception -- which explains the phenomenon through ordinary motivationally biased evidence-processing, with no antecedent contradictory belief and no deceptive intention required -- might make Sartre's whole apparatus unnecessary. Write a dialogue between a committed Sartrean and a Mele-style deflationist debating exactly this. The Sartrean should have to explain why bad faith can't simply be redescribed as biased cognition, given that Sartre treats it as a "project" the agent is fully responsible for choosing -- part of what radical freedom is supposed to entail -- whereas biasing mechanisms sound like something that happens to a person rather than something chosen. The deflationist should turn Sartre's own regress argument back on him: doesn't the metastable-consciousness solution need its own kind of split in awareness, of just the sort he criticizes the censor for needing? Have the two of them respond to each other's actual moves rather than restate their opening positions, and don't force a tidy resolution -- let each concede one real cost of their own view.
