I'm writing a feature on how decision theorists handle problems of self-locating uncertainty, and I keep returning to Piccione and Rubinstein's absent-minded driver: the driver on a highway who cannot distinguish the first of two identical intersections from the second, and who must therefore commit to the same probability of exiting at each one. Every popular account illustrates the puzzle with that same highway, which will not help readers who have already encountered it. Could you devise a genuinely different example, from a domain with nothing to do with driving or route-finding, that preserves the puzzle's essential structure: a sequence of decision points indistinguishable from the inside, a single policy that must consequently apply at all of them, and payoffs arranged so that the policy it is optimal to commit to in advance is not the one that looks optimal once the agent is actually at one of those points reasoning about what to do next? Please supply your own concrete payoffs and probabilities, solve for both the advance-commitment strategy and the in-the-moment strategy, and show precisely where and why they diverge.
