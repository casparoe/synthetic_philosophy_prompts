Question: Pascal has that bit in the Pensées about how, since we couldn't make justice strong, we made strength count as justice instead ("not being able to make what is just strong, we have made what is strong just"). Explain what he's actually doing with this idea, and how it connects to what he's trying to accomplish in the Pensées as a whole.

My answer: Pascal's fragment on justice and force is one of the more political-looking bits of the Pensées, and it can feel kind of out of place next to all the stuff about the wager and the hidden God, but I think its doing similar work underneath. He points out that justice, unlike force, is always disputed - people never fully agree on what's actually just - while force is obvious and undeniable, whoever's strongest is strongest, no argument needed. Since we can't make justice strong (we can't get everyone to agree on it), we've effectively done the reverse and made strength count as justice. This is basically Pascal working out his own version of the social contract that Hobbes was building around the same time: since people can't agree on what's really just, they hand authority over to whoever has the power to keep the peace, and that arrangement then gets dressed up as legitimate so nobody has to keep fighting about it. Pascal also has that great line about truth on one side of the Pyrenees being error on the other, which he's borrowing from Montaigne, to show that laws and customs are basically arbitrary and just vary by country. Putting this together, Pascal's overall point is that there's no such thing as objective justice - its all just custom and force wearing a mask - and he uses that to knock down the reader's confidence in reason before making his pitch for Christianity later in the book.
