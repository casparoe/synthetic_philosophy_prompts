When a few of us are moving a heavy sofa together, or when my amateur band and I are working through a song, I notice something odd: if one person just drops out partway through without a word, the rest of us feel genuinely wronged rather than merely inconvenienced, and it feels like none of us could unilaterally end the whole thing — that seems to take all of us, even though nobody ever promised anything aloud.

I have since read a little philosophy on this question. Michael Bratman holds that a shared intention reduces to separate individual intentions plus mutual knowledge of them, sub-plans that mesh rather than clash, and mutual responsiveness in intention and action. On his view, whatever obligation shows up can be explained by an ordinary moral norm against exploiting expectations you have knowingly created in someone else — he likens this to Scanlon's idea that assurances alone, with no promise required, can ground obligations. Margaret Gilbert denies that this is enough: she argues joint action rests on an irreducible "joint commitment" binding the parties as a plural subject, which is precisely what explains why no single party can rescind it unilaterally and why the others are entitled to rebuke someone who tries.

Please argue that Gilbert is right on this specific point: the asymmetry between freely revisable personal intentions and a jointly held commitment that takes everyone's concurrence to dissolve cannot be reconstructed out of Bratman's individualist apparatus, Scanlon-style assurance norm included, so that joint action really does require something like a sui generis joint commitment.
