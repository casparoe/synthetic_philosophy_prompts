Last night a friend and I ended up in a two-hour, coffee-fueled argument about whether Grice's machinery for explaining conversational implicature can survive contact with large language models. It started when she pointed out that chatbots often seem to "imply" a refusal without ever quite stating it ("I can't help with that specific request, but I could try...") and I countered that this looks like textbook Gricean implicature: an apparent flouting of the Maxim of Quantity or Relation that generates an implicated meaning going beyond what is literally said. She pushed back hard, arguing that Grice's entire account of speaker meaning (what he called non-natural meaning, or meaning-NN, in his 1957 paper "Meaning") rests on a nested, reflexive structure of communicative intentions: a speaker means something by an utterance only if they intend it to produce a belief in an audience partly by means of the audience's recognition of that very intention. An LLM, she insisted, has no beliefs and no intentions in anything like the sense Grice's analysis requires, so strictly speaking there is no speaker meaning here at all, and therefore nothing that could count as implicature in his technical sense - at best we're anthropomorphizing a very sophisticated autocomplete. I wasn't convinced, since something implicature-shaped does seem to happen whenever a model reliably "flouts" a maxim to convey content it never states, but I couldn't construct a fully satisfying reply, and we ended the night unable to agree where Grice's own commitments would actually land him.

I'd like your help settling this properly, working strictly within the framework Grice laid out in "Logic and Conversation" and "Meaning" - the Cooperative Principle, the four maxims (Quantity, Quality, Relation, Manner), the associated distinctions between violating, opting out of, and flouting a maxim, and the intention-based analysis of speaker meaning. Reasoning only from those specific commitments, would Grice have to conclude that talk of "LLM implicature" is a category error, given how much weight his theory places on speaker intention? Or is there a plausible way of reconstructing the relevant intentions for a system like an LLM (through its designers, through some functional stand-in, or otherwise) that would let his apparatus apply more or less as written? And if the theory really can't stretch that far, what is the smallest modification to it - preserving as much of Grice's original picture as possible - that would let it accommodate machine-generated implicature? I'm less interested in a general verdict on "AI understanding" than in a close, textually grounded reconstruction of what Grice's actual commitments force him to say.
