Got into it with a coworker last week over whether our team should switch to trunk-based dev, and after about an hour of arguing back and forth, including trading actual percentage confidences a couple times, like "I'm 80% sure this cuts merge conflicts for a team our size" / "ok I'm more like 55% because of X", he pulled out Aumann's agreement theorem on me. His claim: since we're both reasonably smart, rational engineers, and we'd each laid out our full reasoning to the other, the fact that we still disagreed at the end means one of us basically has to be irrational, or is sitting on some evidence or consideration we're not admitting to ourselves. Worth mentioning, he's coming at this having watched trunk-based dev work great at his last startup, while I watched it cause a slow rolling disaster at mine, if that's even relevant. He sent me a couple old LessWrong posts about it, and I even skimmed the wikipedia page for the actual theorem, which does seem to say two Bayesians with the same prior who have common knowledge of each others probability estimate for something can't stay in disagreement. So it kind of feels like he's got me, even though it stings to admit.

Two things I'm hoping you can clear up. First, is that actually a solid argument, should "we both explained our full reasoning and still disagree" count as decent evidence that I'm the irrational one here (or that I'm hiding something from myself)? Second, assuming the theorem really does back him up, what would it practically take to structure an argument like ours so we actually hit "common knowledge" and converge, instead of just running out of steam after an hour and going back to our desks still disagreeing?
