Ok, I need an outside perspective on this because I've clearly lost all sense of proportion (occupational hazard of a philosophy PhD - everything turns into a case study eventually). My housemate and I agreed back in February, just over dinner, no formal arrangement or anything, that we'd cook together every Sunday night for the rest of the semester, alternating who picks the recipe and splitting the grocery bill. Its honestly been one of the better parts of my week. Two Sundays ago she texts me an hour before dinner: "hey, I don't think I want to keep doing Sunday dinners, gonna just do my own thing going forward, you don't have to either though!" I was way more annoyed than the actual stakes justify (it's dinner, I can cook alone, nobody died), and when I tried to explain why to her, the best I could get out was something like "that's not really a call you get to make on your own, that was a 'we' thing, you don't get to unilaterally end it AND announce that I'm released from it too, like you're speaking for both of us." Which, said out loud, sounds a little unhinged, since obviously nobody can force her to keep showing up to Sunday dinner.

But I can't quite shake the feeling that there's a real difference between (a) someone unilaterally changing their mind about their own future plans, which is obviously always fair game, and (b) someone unilaterally declaring that an arrangement the two of you built together is now over, even though from the outside (a) and (b) can look like exactly the same action. I brought it up at a department lunch and got a suspiciously even split between "yeah, there's something there" and "you're a grad student who has broken his own brain, it is dinner." So: is there an actual principled distinction between those two cases, one that would justify feeling wronged rather than merely disappointed, or am I just dressing up a completely mundane bit of housemate friction in fancy philosophical clothes?
