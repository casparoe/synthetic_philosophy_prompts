I'm working on a piece about the vogue for "computational metaphysics," and I keep returning to one specific episode. In 2013, the logicians Christoph Benzmüller and Bruno Woltzenlogel Paleo used automated and interactive theorem-proving software to formally verify Gödel's modal ontological argument, in the version refined by Dana Scott, confirming that its conclusion follows validly from its axioms. Headlines duly announced that a computer had "proved God exists," although checking validity says nothing about whether the axioms themselves are true. What got far less attention is a separate line of criticism, going back to Howard Sobel, showing that those same axioms entail modal collapse: the result that every true proposition turns out to be a necessary one, leaving no room in the world for contingency. C. Anthony Anderson later proposed revising Gödel's definition of the property of being God-like specifically to block Sobel's derivation.

For the piece, please argue for a fairly narrow thesis: that the 2013 verification was philosophically beside the point, and that modal collapse — not Kant's familiar complaint that existence is not a real predicate — is the argument's most serious liability, one that Anderson-style emendations only partly defuse. Explain concretely why machine-checking an inference's validity cannot bear on the truth of its premises, and engage with whether revising the notion of being God-like can block modal collapse without simply relocating the same cost elsewhere among the axioms.
