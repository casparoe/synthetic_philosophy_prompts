"The moral equality of combatants (MEC) is the traditional view, defended by Michael Walzer, that soldiers on both sides of a war have the same rights to kill and the same liability to be killed, no matter which side has the just cause. This is why Walzer keeps jus ad bellum and jus in bello separate: whether the war itself is just is a question for statesmen, but once the war has started, soldiers are judged only by whether they follow the rules of war (proportionality, discrimination, etc.), not by whether their side's cause is just. On this view, a soldier fighting for an aggressor state has the same permission to shoot at enemy soldiers, and the same liability to be shot at, as a soldier defending against the aggression.

McMahan rejects this. His argument is that moral liability to be killed in war tracks individual moral responsibility, not which side you're on. A combatant becomes liable to attack by contributing to an unjustified threat of harm. Since the unjust combatant's threat is unjustified (his side has no just cause), he is morally liable to be killed, while the just combatant, who is only resisting that threat, is not liable -- even though the two of them might be doing the exact same thing from the outside (aiming and firing a weapon at an enemy soldier). So there's a deep moral asymmetry between the two sides even though the law of armed conflict treats them symmetrically, and McMahan draws the further conclusion that the law itself ought to eventually be reformed to track this asymmetry, since the whole point of his argument is that unjust combatants aren't really entitled to combatant immunity.

Even so, McMahan can explain why we don't blame most individual unjust combatants very much: he argues that most soldiers who fight for unjust causes are justified in doing so given their epistemic situation -- they've been fed propaganda, they aren't in a position to independently verify whether their state's war is just, and they'd be imprisoned or shot for desertion if they refused. So although what they do is objectively wrong, they're justified given what they reasonably believed and the situation they were in, which is why we don't treat them like we'd treat murderers."
