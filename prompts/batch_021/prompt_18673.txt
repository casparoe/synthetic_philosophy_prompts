My older sister is getting married in a few weeks, and she has asked me — her younger sibling, and the only other philosopher in the family — to give the toast. She and her fiancé met as PhD students in the same metaphysics seminar, and the running joke of their four years together has been a genuine, unresolved disagreement about what van Inwagen calls the Special Composition Question: she is a committed universalist who holds that any two or more objects whatsoever compose a further object, while he leans toward van Inwagen's own answer, that composition occurs only among simples caught up in a life, so that strictly speaking there are no tables, cars, or wedding cakes, only particles arranged tablewise, carwise, or cakewise. Neither has managed to convert the other in four years, and the disagreement has become something like a shared hobby; at their engagement dinner he raised a glass "to the simples arranged fiancée-wise," which she still describes as the moment she knew.

I'm currently taking a seminar on metaontology, and we just finished a unit that bears directly on this: Eli Hirsch's argument that disputes like theirs are merely verbal, since both sides agree on every possible arrangement of particles and differ only in which sentences containing "exists" they are willing to assert, against Ted Sider's reply that this concedes too much, since some candidate meanings of the quantifier may really carve nature at its joints better than others, in which case there is a fact of the matter about who is right after all. I don't think it's my place to adjudicate that from the podium, and I'm honestly not persuaded either side has won — but I also don't want to flatten a real philosophical disagreement into a cheap "aren't they quirky" joke for the benefit of the people in the room who would know better.

About half the guests are people from their program who will recognize a reference to quantifier variance or "arranged x-wise" immediately; the other half are relatives who have only ever experienced this as the thing the two of them bicker about at holidays. Could you write me a five-minute toast that takes their composition dispute seriously as its throughline, including the further question of whether it's even a substantive dispute at all, while still functioning as a toast about a marriage and not a seminar on mereology?
