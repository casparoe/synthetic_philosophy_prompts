So this has been bugging me for weeks and I dont really have anyone left to hash it out with properly. My dad's father (my opa) was a German soldier in WW2 -- drafted in 1943 when he'd just turned 19, regular Wehrmacht, not SS, no war crimes accusations attached to him that anyone knows of. He fought on the eastern front for a couple years, then got shifted west and was captured by the Americans in early '45, and eventually emigrated to Canada afterward. He passed away this past spring, and while me and my cousins were clearing out his house we found his medals and a couple of old letters, which is what kicked off a whole argument between my aunt Teresa and my uncle Rich that I still can't stop turning over in my head.

Teresa's opening point was that opa was 19 and got drafted, and if he'd refused he'd have been shot or thrown in a camp himself, so morally he wasn't in the same category as the men who actually started the war -- he was just a kid trying to survive and keep the guys next to him alive, same as literally any soldier in any war ever. Rich pushed back that sure, but he still spent two years shooting at Soviet and Allied soldiers who were defending against a war Germany started, and no amount of "he had no choice" changes the fact that the actual killing was on the wrong side of it. Teresa said fine, but by that logic every drafted 19 year old in every unjust war in history is a killer, same as the generals who started it, which felt way too harsh to her to just accept. Rich said he's not saying opa is as guilty as Hitler -- obviously being lied to and forced into it matters for how much you blame somebody -- but "less blame" isn't the same thing as "he was allowed to do it," same way a guy who gets tricked into robbing a bank still isn't allowed to shoot the security guard, even if we'd go easier on him afterward for being tricked. Teresa's comeback was: then why does basically the entire actual rules-of-war system treat soldiers on both sides the same -- prisoners get equal treatment no matter which side they fought for, nobody gets tried as a criminal just for being a soldier, only for how they behaved once they were one -- and if this "both sides had an equal right to fight" thing is a total myth like Rich's saying, isn't the whole system every army and court on earth actually uses just built on a lie? Rich's answer was that maybe it kind of is a useful lie -- that we treat both sides the same on paper because that's the only way to get soldiers on either side to actually follow rules like not shooting prisoners, not because it reflects who really had the right to kill who in the first place.

Neither of them budged and we never really got anywhere before everyone had to head home. Could you go through their exchange move by move and say who actually had the better of it at each point, rather than just declaring an overall winner? And if there's some angle you think neither of them considered but should have, I'd like to hear that too. I cant claim to know anything about formal philosophy so please don't assume I'll follow technical terms -- just walk me through it plainly.
