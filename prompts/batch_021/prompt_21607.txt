I've been re-reading Hobbes's *Leviathan* alongside some old MIRI and LessWrong material on "program equilibrium," and the parallel between them has been nagging at me since a reading-group discussion last week. Hobbes's argument, in brief, is that in the state of nature, mutually self-interested parties cannot stabilize cooperation on the strength of promises alone, since whoever performs first simply hands the other an incentive to renege; as he puts it, covenants, without the sword, are but words, and of no strength to secure a man at all. On his view, only submission to a common coercive power, the sovereign, can make covenants binding. Notably, Hobbes does not think this problem gets solved even for sovereigns themselves: commonwealths remain in a permanent, if less catastrophic, state of nature with respect to one another, since there is no power above them capable of enforcing treaties between them.

Program equilibrium, introduced by Tennenholtz and later sharpened by researchers in the MIRI orbit working with provability logic, is a formal answer to a structurally similar problem that never invokes a sword at all. Instead of submitting strategies, players submit programs with mutual read-access to each other's source code, and the equilibrium question becomes whether either player would want to unilaterally swap programs given the other's. Under that setup, mutual cooperation in a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma can become genuinely stable: a program often nicknamed "FairBot," which searches for a bounded formal proof that its counterpart cooperates with it and cooperates only if it finds one, can be shown to cooperate with an identical copy of itself via an argument that turns on Löb's theorem. No repeated play, reputation, or third-party enforcer is needed here, just mutual, verified transparency about what the other party will actually do.

I would like your best reconstruction of how Hobbes would evaluate this. Would he treat verified source-code transparency as a genuine technological stand-in for the sword, something that could in principle let individuals escape the state of nature without a sovereign after all, or would he insist that it merely relocates the trust problem one level up, to trusting the transparency mechanism itself: that the code shown is the code that runs, that it has not been tampered with, that the formal system it reasons in is sound? And given his own view that sovereigns remain in a state of nature toward each other, do you think he would find program equilibrium more promising as a solution for cooperation between states or artificial agents that could plausibly expose verifiable commitments to one another, than as a solution for cooperation between ordinary individuals, whose word cannot be reduced to inspectable code? Please engage with his actual arguments about human nature, pride, and the impossibility of ever fully trusting a rival's profession of intent, rather than simply gesturing at "the state of nature" as a slogan.
