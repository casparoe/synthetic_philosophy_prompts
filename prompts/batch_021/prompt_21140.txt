So I spent about 35 years running an experimental physics lab -- lots of high-voltage equipment, so I got very familiar with safety interlocks and fault-detection circuits. Now that I'm retired I've fallen into reading philosophy for fun, and lately I'm stuck on this corner of metaphysics about "dispositions" (fragility, solubility, that sort of thing) and a problem called finks and masks that apparently gives the simple textbook analysis of them a lot of trouble.

The classic case (C.B. Martin's, I believe) is a "dead" wire hooked up to a gadget that senses the instant you touch it and makes the wire live just in time to shock you -- so it's disposed to shock you even though, up until contact, it isn't live at all. Funny enough, that's basically the mirror image of a ground-fault interrupter, which senses contact and does the opposite: kills a live wire the instant its touched, before it can hurt you -- we had those on every rack in the lab. Then theres "masking," the flip side -- a poison stays just as much a poison even when an antidote given beforehand stops it from ever actually killing anyone, so the disposition never gets a chance to show itself.

What I can't shake is whether the disposition is "real" the whole time in cases like this, or only real in the instant it does (or pointedly doesn't) show up. Could you write me a short story built around a character or device like that -- something whose true disposition, to betray, to protect, to fail, whatever you like, is finked or masked, so that neither the other characters nor the reader can be fully sure what was true of it the whole time. Don't turn it into a dialogue-shaped lecture on metaphysics, just let that uncertainty drive the plot.
