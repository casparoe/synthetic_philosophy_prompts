So Thomas Hobbes spent basically the rest of his life (like 20-odd years) in a public dispute with the mathematician John Wallis, because he was convinced he'd squared the circle and Wallis kept publically tearing his proofs apart. Write a dialogue between the two of them, but skip the play-by-play of the actual quadrature -- have them argue instead about the deeper disagreement underneath it: Hobbes's insistence that a real geometrical demonstration has to bottom out in construction (figures generated by the motion of points and lines, things you could in principle draw), which is also why he distrusts indivisibles and Wallis's more symbolic, algebra-heavy way of doing math, versus Wallis's view that those more abstract methods are legitimate and productive even when they outrun anything you could physically construct or picture. Make sure both of them actually land punches on the other's position instead of just restating their own.
