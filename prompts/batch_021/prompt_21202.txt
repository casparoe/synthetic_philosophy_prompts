I've spent the past few weeks reporting on a small but growing group of analytic epistemologists who treat the classical Nyāya–Buddhist dispute over the nature of perception as a live option in debates about concepts and universals, rather than as a historical curiosity. The more of the primary material I read, the more I wanted to see the actual dialectic staged rather than summarized, since every popular treatment I've found reduces it to "the world really is the way it looks" versus "it's all illusion anyway" -- a framing that badly mangles what the Buddhist side was actually arguing. I think a dramatized version would make a good boxed insert alongside the piece I'm writing, so I'm hoping you can draft it for me.

Could you write it as a formal vāda -- a public philosophical debate of the kind classical Indian royal courts staged, with an audience and a patron's favor genuinely at stake -- between a Naiyāyika epistemologist and a Buddhist epistemologist working in the Dignāga–Dharmakīrti lineage? Both are arguing to win, not to explain their views for a textbook. The Naiyāyika defends the standard Nyāya position: that perception (pratyakṣa) can be determinate (savikalpaka), presenting a particular as qualified by a real universal (sāmānya) via the inherence relation (samavāya), and that this determinate content is exactly what makes it possible to recognize the same kind across different particulars and to act successfully on that recognition. The Buddhist defends the rival view associated with that lineage: that only indeterminate (nirvikalpaka) awareness of the bare unique particular (svalakṣaṇa) is genuinely perceptual, that universals are never found in things but are constructed afterward through the exclusion of what a thing is not (apoha), and that Nyāya's inherence relation is a piece of metaphysical machinery that can't do the job it was hired for.

Have the Naiyāyika open by pressing the Buddhist on whether "exclusion of the other" can do its work without covertly presupposing the very general fact it claims to dispense with, and have the Buddhist's reply fold in something like the regress objection later pressed against inherence as a real connector: if samavāya ties a universal to a particular, what ties samavāya to either of them? Don't let the Naiyāyika simply concede that one -- classical Nyāya had a real answer to it, and I'd like to see it deployed. Carry the exchange another four or five moves apiece, with each side responding to what the other actually just said rather than restating its opening position, and end the piece unresolved -- a judge's noncommittal word, the debate breaking for the day, whatever device you like -- since that's closer to how this dispute actually stood for centuries than a clean winner would be.
