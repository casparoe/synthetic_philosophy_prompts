For a little over six years I've belonged to a small study-and-meditation group that grew out of the local rationalist meetup scene -- the sort of crowd that reads Scott Alexander and David Chapman's *Meaningness* and then decided to actually work through the *Mūlamadhyamakakārikā* verse by verse instead of treating "emptiness" as a vibe. The person who held the group together for most of that time, Miriam, a retired analytic philosopher who spent her last two decades on Madhyamaka, died three weeks ago, and her family has asked me to give the address at her memorial this coming Sunday. She was exacting about the tetralemma to the point of being a minor terror at potlucks: if anyone reached for what feels like the safe, sophisticated-sounding Buddhist move -- "well, in a sense she both exists and doesn't now" or "we simply can't say" -- she would point out, correctly, that Nāgārjuna closes off the third and fourth corners just as firmly as the first two, and that treating "both" or "neither" as a clever escape from the dilemma is exactly the reflex his arguments are built to defeat. Among her papers is a note, half joking, asking that nobody smuggle a self in through the back door by promising she persists in our memory or watches over us, and equally that nobody flatten her into bare non-existence, as though that weren't its own quiet reification.

I'd like you to draft that eulogy. The room will have perhaps forty people, roughly half of them dharma-study regulars and former philosophy colleagues who will notice immediately if the speech quietly picks one of the four corners for comfort's sake, and half family and old friends who have never heard the word "catuṣkoṭi" and are there to grieve someone they loved, not to audit an argument. The eulogy needs to do real work with the two-truths distinction -- finding something true and consoling to say at the level of conventional truth (saṃvṛti-satya) about grief, gratitude, and what visibly continues in the people she taught, without that consolation quietly resting on any of the four claims her own philosophy rules out. If you find that the two truths don't fully dissolve the tension -- that there's a genuine remainder where comfort and rigor pull apart -- I'd rather the eulogy acknowledge that honestly, in her spirit, than paper over it with the kind of "everything is interconnected" language she spent her life arguing against. About eight minutes, read aloud.
