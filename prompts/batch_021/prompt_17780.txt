I teach a graduate seminar, cross-listed with the psychology department, on the philosophical foundations of statistical inference, and much of our discussion this term has turned on how much of the replication crisis is really a symptom of frequentist methodology specifically, as opposed to something that would survive a wholesale switch to Bayesian methods. A psychology postdoc auditing the course offered his own recent practice as evidence for the Bayesian side of that debate: he had specified a prior for his key parameter before running an experiment, found that the resulting posterior left his hypothesis looking distinctly unpromising, decided on reflection that his original prior had actually been too uninformative given a closely related result in the literature, revised it accordingly, and reran the analysis, arriving at a posterior that supported his hypothesis comfortably. That is the version now heading toward publication. He maintains that since his revised prior is, if anything, more defensible than his original one, nothing methodologically suspect is going on here, in contrast to the sort of data-contingent flexibility that produces false positives under null hypothesis significance testing.

I could not say exactly why this bothered me at the time, beyond a sense that he had relocated a familiar problem rather than escaped it. Is he right that moving to Bayesian inference insulates a researcher from the kind of analytic flexibility that produced so many false positives in the first place, or is there something wrong with what he did, even though each step in his reasoning about the prior sounds reasonable taken on its own?
