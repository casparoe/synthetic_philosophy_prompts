Frank Ramsey occupies an odd double role in discussions I've followed on rationalist blogs and forums. On one hand, he's a founding figure of subjective expected utility theory, having argued in "Truth and Probability" (1926) that degrees of belief are best understood through betting behavior, against Keynes's rival view of probability as an objective logical relation. On the other hand, he's remembered for "Epilogue," a talk from the mid-1920s later printed as the closing chapter of The Foundations of Mathematics (1931), in which he refuses to be humbled by the universe's size or by the brevity of human life against deep time, insisting that value tracks the capacity for thought and feeling rather than sheer extent, and drawing a personal "map" of importance on which friendship and conversation loom large while the stellar vastness shrinks almost to nothing.

These two legacies pull against each other once you bring them into contact with present-day longtermism, whose case for treating existential risk from advanced AI as an overriding priority (via, e.g., Bostrom's "astronomical waste" argument) depends on exactly the kind of expected-value reasoning over vast, heavily-discounted quantities that Ramsey's own decision theory helped make respectable.

Working only from what you know of Ramsey's actual philosophical commitments, tell me how he would most plausibly have responded to the longtermist case for prioritizing AI existential risk. Would the Ramsey of "Truth and Probability" accept the expected-value calculus while rejecting its "astronomical stakes" framing, would the Ramsey of "Epilogue" reject the premises outright, or is there a more interesting position available to him that draws on both? Argue for one specific answer rather than simply cataloguing the options.
