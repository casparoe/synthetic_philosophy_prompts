For a longer piece I'm reporting on how probability theory buckles once an agent becomes uncertain not about the world but about "where" or "when" it is within some process, I kept running into an older cousin of the now much-discussed Sleeping Beauty problem: Piccione and Rubinstein's "absent-minded driver." In their setup, a driver sits in a pub planning her route home along a highway with two consecutive, visually identical exits; she must take the second one to get home. Taking the first by mistake sends her down a hazardous side road (payoff 0), missing both leaves her overnight at a motel at the highway's end (payoff 1), and finding the second gets her home (payoff 4). Because of her absent-mindedness, once she is actually driving she cannot tell, at any intersection she reaches, whether it is the first or the second, and she cannot even recall how many she has already passed.

What I can't get a real intuitive grip on is the clash between two seemingly sound pieces of reasoning about what she should do. Back in the pub, comparing her two available policies as wholes, "always continue" straightforwardly beats "always exit" — 1 is better than 0 — so that's the plan she settles on. Yet later, actually sitting at an intersection with nothing to distinguish it from the other, it's tempting to reason that she should split her credence evenly between being at the first or the second, in which case exiting right now has an expected payoff of 2, twice as good as the 1 she gets by sticking to her plan. Piccione and Rubinstein presented this as a genuine paradox generated by her imperfect recall; Aumann, Hart, and Perry replied that a sufficiently careful analysis makes the paradox dissolve; and I've since come across later papers constructing variants of the highway in which a real gap between what the literature calls "planning-optimal" and "action-optimal" behavior survives any amount of care. I'd love your help building actual intuition here, rather than just re-deriving the numbers: where exactly does the 50/50 reasoning at the intersection go wrong, if it does; why is it specifically the driver's imperfect recall that opens up room for this kind of conflict, given that Kuhn's theorem rules it out for ordinary decision problems with perfect recall; and how would you relate all of this to the halfer/thirder dispute over Sleeping Beauty, which a couple of the philosophers I've interviewed for this piece described as essentially the same disagreement in a different guise?
