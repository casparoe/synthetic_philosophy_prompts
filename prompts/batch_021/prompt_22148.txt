I'm a PhD student in philosophy (normally I work on phil of mind, so this is a bit out of my lane) and this term's history-of-ethics proseminar just spent two weeks on Augustine's two treatises on lying, De Mendacio and Contra Mendacium. It's wild stuff once you're actually in the text -- he ends up committed to the idea that you can't lie even to save an innocent person's life or protect someone's chastity, and he grades lies into eight tiers of badness while insisting every single tier is still a sin. After the second seminar, two people in my cohort, Leah and Owen, ended up arguing about it over drinks and it went on long enough that I wrote down the gist afterward, because I honestly couldn't tell who was winning.

(1) Leah opened by saying the whole system has a loophole baked into its own definition: Augustine says a lie is asserting something contrary to what's in your mind with intent to deceive, so if you mislead someone using only sentences you believe are true -- misleading implicature, technically-true non-answers, whatever -- you literally haven't lied by his own criteria. That means the guy hiding a friend from people trying to kill him can mislead them all he wants and come out clean, exactly in the case where the absolute prohibition is supposed to bite hardest. (2) Owen said that's not a loophole, it's the theory working as intended: Augustine explicitly allows staying silent or refusing to answer, since silence isn't an assertion, but that's a different animal from constructing a true sentence as a deception device -- and his whole fight with the Priscillianists, famous for "swear, forswear, but never give up the secret," was precisely about refusing any cleverness aimed at persecutors. (3) Leah answered that the Priscillianist case doesn't transfer, since "forswear" there means actual false oaths, i.e. ordinary lying with extra steps, and that Augustine never actually discusses, let alone condemns, someone who deceives via a merely misleading true statement -- so the loophole just never gets closed by him, unlike later Catholic casuists who at least argued explicitly about "mental reservation." (4) Owen's comeback was that this is an argument from silence, and that Augustine's actual rationale for why lying is wrong -- speech exists to reveal the mind, and lying perverts that function to plant a false belief in someone -- condemns engineered misleading truths for the same underlying reason it condemns outright falsehoods, whether or not he spells out that exact case. (5) Leah said fine, but then Augustine's position is either toothless (on the letter of his own definition) or it has to import some broader "don't deceive" principle from outside the text, which he cant really afford given how hard he insists elsewhere that motive and circumstance never turn a lie into a non-lie. (6) Owen's last move was that there's a third option: the real Augustinian principle is just narrower than either of them had been assuming -- "never assert what you take to be false," full stop -- and biting the bullet on letting misleading-true statements through, while still banning outright lies told for that very same protective purpose, is a coherent, if uncomfortable, place to land, not a hidden inconsistency.

Neither of them would budge and I go back and forth everytime I reread my notes on it. Could you go through this move by move -- (1) through (6) -- and say who's got the better of it at each stage, rather than just picking an overall winner? Then, stepping back, give me your own take on the question underneath their fight: is there actually a stable, principled Augustinian line between permissible silence/misleading-truths and forbidden lies, or does Leah's dilemma in (5) stick, so that something has to give? Also flag anything you think both of them are just missing.
