I work on the engineering side of a small DeFi protocol, and a few weeks ago we ended up in a standoff with a rival protocol over the terms of a shared liquidity bridge. Because on-chain governance actions are irrevocable once executed, each side realized that whoever got its preferred terms locked into a deployed contract first would leave the other with a choice between accepting a worse deal or paying real costs to unwind the mess — essentially Schelling's old point that the power to bind yourself credibly is itself a source of bargaining leverage. What actually happened is that both teams rushed proposals through governance within about a day of each other, before either had fully modeled the consequences, and we ended up with two irrevocable, mutually incompatible commitments on the books — an outcome strictly worse for both sides than if neither of us had been able to pre-commit at all. A friend who follows AI safety research looked at our postmortem and said we'd basically reproduced, in miniature and with dumb code instead of clever agents, something called a "commitment race," which he described as a real concern for how advanced AI systems might end up bargaining with one another. When I went looking for an explanation myself, though, I only found fairly dense discussions that assume familiarity with updateless decision theory and talk about agents wanting to commit at the earliest possible "logical time," which isn't background I have.

Could you unpack what's actually going on here without presupposing that background? I'm especially interested in: (1) why an ability that looks like an unambiguous good on the classical Schelling story — the power to bind yourself — turns into a trap once more than one party has access to it; (2) why the resulting pressure seems to favor committing early and fast, even before a situation has been properly thought through, rather than the slower, more deliberate commitments you'd naively expect to be the more credible ones; and (3) how "being updateless" fits into the picture — whether it's supposed to be a cure for the race or whether it's actually part of what generates the incentive to commit at the earliest possible moment, logical or otherwise. If it helps, I'd also be curious whether this is genuinely different from an everyday first-mover-advantage game like Chicken, or whether a "commitment race" is really just that same dynamic dressed up in fancier vocabulary.
