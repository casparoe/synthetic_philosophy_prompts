I'm assembling an anthology of philosophically minded speculative fiction, and I'd like a short story built around the Boltzmann brain problem in cosmology. Skip the familiar hand-wave about being a random fluctuation in the void. Ground the piece instead in the sharper argument, associated with Dyson, Kleban, and Susskind, that a universe with a positive cosmological constant settles into an eternal de Sitter phase whose thermal fluctuations will, given enough time, spontaneously assemble isolated, momentary brains complete with false memories far more often than they will ever produce a genuine, ordered history like ours. Center the story on a protagonist forced to take seriously the possibility that their own memories, including whatever led them to worry about this in the first place, are the last flicker of such a fluctuation, and let the narrative also carry the standard rejoinder that this conclusion is self-undermining: if it is true, nothing that seems to support it, including the physics itself, can be trusted. I want the story's unease to come from that specific bind rather than from generic existential atmosphere, and I'd like it to close on an earned resolution rather than trail off.
