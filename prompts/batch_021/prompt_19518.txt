I'm several chapters into a novel set among a circle of Cambridge-educated intellectuals in England in the early 1930s, and I've been stuck for a week on a dinner-party scene. Two of my characters -- one closely modeled on John Maynard Keynes, the other on Bertrand Russell, both men formed as young Apostles under the same G. E. Moore-influenced Cambridge ethics before drifting into rather different lives -- fall into a disagreement about what people are actually meant to do with themselves once machinery and compound interest have finally solved the problem of scarcity. I know Keynes sketched exactly this future around the time my scene is set, in "Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren," proposing that something like a fifteen-hour week might satisfy whatever in us still craves purposeful work, and that Russell covered similar territory a couple of years later in "In Praise of Idleness," arguing for a four-hour day on rather different grounds. Every draft of their exchange I've written so far comes out as two agreeable men taking turns extolling leisure, which I don't believe is right -- these feel to me like differently shaped arguments wearing the same optimistic conclusion.

Before I attempt the scene again, I would like a clearer sense of where the two essays actually pull apart rather than merely differing in tone. Both diagnose more or less the same historical moment and propose more or less the same cure, yet my suspicion is that Keynes frames the obstacle as one of inherited character -- whether a species bred for centuries to solve the economic problem can be taught, within a few generations, to want anything besides more of it -- while Russell frames that same persistence of long hours as an arrangement certain people have a standing interest in defending, which makes his remedy redistributive where Keynes's is closer to psychological. I would like to know whether that distinction survives contact with what the two essays actually argue, and how much of each man's inherited Cambridge ethics -- Keynes's debt to Moore's talk of valuable states of mind and personal affection, Russell's more public and less aestheticized sense of a life worth living -- is doing genuine argumentative work in each essay, rather than sitting decoratively on top of what is otherwise a shared economic forecast. Given that average wealth has grown roughly as Keynes expected while the promised leisure has stubbornly failed to arrive, whose diagnosis of what actually stands in the way seems more nearly correct, and why? As a closing flourish, you might also give me the single most ungenerous line one of my two characters could plausibly deliver against the other's whole position.
