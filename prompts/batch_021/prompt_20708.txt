I was recently added to my hospital's clinical ethics committee, and in only my second meeting we had to work through a real policy question that turned out to be a version of a puzzle philosophers have argued over for decades. A shipment of antivenom arrived short: enough for one bitten patient to receive a full course, or to be split into five partial doses across five other bitten patients, each of whom the toxicologists judged would survive on a fifth of the full amount. Two committee members, Dr. Reyes and Dr. Osei, argued at length about which policy to adopt, and I haven't been able to settle it in my own head since.

(1) Dr. Reyes, who has a philosophy background, argued that we owe the one patient nothing less than we owe the five together: there is no single sufferer whose loss is five times worse than his, only five separate people each losing exactly what he stands to lose. She said this echoes an argument made by a philosopher named Taurek, on whose view giving everyone an equal shot — say, flipping a coin between the two options — can be fairer than an automatic rule that favors whichever side has more names on it.

(2) Dr. Osei objected that taken seriously, this would commit the committee to being indifferent between saving one patient and saving a thousand, which no reasonable protocol could accept.

(3) Dr. Reyes replied that the thousand-patient case doesn't touch her argument, since intuitions about such lopsided scenarios are unreliable guides to the underlying principle — at that scale almost any rule feels compelling, including ones that don't survive scrutiny — so using the case against her begs the question.

(4) Dr. Osei tried another angle: even without comparing anyone's loss to anyone else's, a policy that left the five untreated couldn't be justified to any one of them, since the same vial could have honored more people's equally weighty claim to treatment at no extra cost — so numbers matter without anyone's suffering being added together.

(5) Dr. Reyes answered that "honoring more claims" does the same work as summing losses under a different name, since it still tells the lone patient that he alone must go untreated for no reason but that he happened to be outnumbered — exactly what she denies can ever justify a person's death.

Neither budged, and the committee tabled the question.
