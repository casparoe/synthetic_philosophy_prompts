I'm a novelist currently drafting a book narrated by a lapsed Jesuit seminarian who cannot quite let go of a doctrine he was taught before he left the order: Molinism, and in particular the idea of divine middle knowledge. To get his voice right I've spent the last few weeks reading around Molina's own attempt to square providence with libertarian free will — the tripartite scheme of natural knowledge (God's grasp of all necessary truths and every metaphysically possible world), middle knowledge (God's prevolitional grasp of the counterfactuals of creaturely freedom, i.e., what any possible free creature would do in any possible circumstance), and free knowledge (God's knowledge, once he has decreed which world to actualize, of what will in fact occur) — together with the standard objections, especially the grounding objection: the worry that these counterfactuals of freedom have no truth-maker, since they are supposed to hold true independently of both God's will and the creature's actual existence.

What keeps distracting me from the research, though, is how neatly the whole apparatus maps onto something about my own craft that I've never known how to describe precisely: my relation to the people I invent. I will sometimes say a character "surprised me" or "wouldn't have done that," as though I had merely consulted some prior fact about what she would freely do in a given scene rather than simply deciding it on the spot, while at other times I know perfectly well that I am just imposing an outcome for the plot's sake and dressing it up afterward as inevitable. Rather than a general summary of Molina, then, I'd find it far more useful if you could set out the most important implications of Molinism and middle knowledge, grounding objection included, for that authorial predicament — whether there is any coherent way to cash out the idea that a novelist "discovers" or is answerable to what a character would do prior to writing her, as opposed to merely stipulating it and calling the stipulation freedom, and what, if anything, in the theological debate should make me more or less comfortable resting on either description.
