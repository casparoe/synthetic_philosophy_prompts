After forty years working as a condensed-matter physicist, I retired three years ago and have since been attending a small philosophy reading group that meets through the continuing-education program at my old university. This term we have been working through the early chapters of David Lewis's *On the Plurality of Worlds*, and I confess that after a career spent worrying about symmetry breaking and phase transitions, the sheer confidence with which philosophers reason about non-actual concrete worlds still catches me off guard; it makes physicists' talk of the multiverse look almost timid by comparison.

What has occupied the group most is Lewis's case against genuine transworld identity — the view that one and the same individual can be strictly, numerically identical across two distinct possible worlds — and in favor of his own counterpart theory, on which no individual exists in more than one world at all. The argument that gave us the most trouble is not Kripke's familiar complaint that counterpart theory misdescribes what we care about when we say that Humphrey could have won the election; it is Lewis's own positive argument from what I've seen called the problem of accidental intrinsics. As I understand it, the worry is this: if I myself, one and the same individual, existed in another world with a rather different build than I actually have, then, since intrinsic properties are supposed to be had by a thing outright and not relative to anything else, the only way to avoid attributing incompatible builds to a single individual would be to say that each build is had only relative to a world. But that turns having-a-build into a disguised relation, and so seems to undermine its being a genuinely intrinsic property in the first place. One member of the group took this to settle the debate decisively in Lewis's favor. I am not convinced, if only because the counterpart relation doing the analogous work in Lewis's own theory is itself relativized to a world and to a similarity standard that he allows to shift with context, so it is not obvious to me that his view is any better positioned with respect to intrinsicality.

Could you set out, as rigorously as you can, the strongest argument for the position that the problem of accidental intrinsics gives us no good reason to prefer counterpart theory over a suitably relativized transworld identity view? I would especially like you to address why relativizing the having of an intrinsic property to a world need not turn it into an objectionable relation of exactly the kind Lewis's argument is supposed to rule out, and why counterpart theory does not simply relocate this same difficulty, rather than avoid it, by making cross-world similarity itself relative to a world and a standard of comparison. Please work the argument out yourself rather than just gesturing at the fact that some philosophers have resisted Lewis on this point.
