I've spent enough years reading LessWrong, Astral Codex Ten, and the wider rationalist blogosphere that phrases like "notice your confusion" and "take ideas seriously even when it's uncomfortable" function as something close to inherited reflexes for me. So I was delighted, and slightly unsettled, to recently learn just how literally Alan Turing lived by something like that ethic. About two-thirds of the way through "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" (1950), right after the more famous objections to the imitation game, Turing turns to telepathy: he calls the card-guessing statistics of his day "overwhelming," describes the resulting objection as "quite a strong one" against his own test, and, rather than dismiss it outright, proposes housing the contestants in what he calls a "telepathy-proof room." He even adds, almost in passing, that once you've accepted evidence like that, believing in ghosts is barely a further step. This wasn't quite the isolated eccentricity it looks like from here, either: Cambridge had a long, semi-respectable tradition of philosophers taking psychical research seriously, going back to the Society for Psychical Research founded in 1882 by a cluster of Cambridge scholars, and Turing, a Cambridge man himself, moved in circles that overlapped with it.

What makes this more than a quaint footnote, though, is what sits underneath it. Two years after Christopher Morcom -- Turing's closest friend, and by most accounts his first love -- died at eighteen, the young Turing sent Christopher's mother a short essay titled "Nature of Spirit," arguing that mind might survive the death of the body, leaning on the physics of the day the way he'd later lean on statistics. And there's a coda Turing never lived to read: the most celebrated British telepathy research of that period, card-guessing work very like S. G. Soal's, was shown decades after Turing's own death to rest on quietly doctored target sequences.

I'd like a short story that takes this whole tangle as its actual subject, rather than treating ESP or the Turing test as set dressing for something more familiar. The through-line I want kept intact is this: a rigorous, evidence-bound founder of machine intelligence who cannot quite bring himself to rule out some channel between minds that no test built purely on outward behavior could ever catch, for reasons that trace back to one specific grief, resting on evidence that would later quietly dissolve without his ever knowing it. Handle the biographical material as loosely as fiction requires -- write about Turing directly, invent a fictional stand-in in another decade, or set the whole thing in a present-day lab re-running its own version of the imitation game and stumbling into the same problem unaided. What I don't want is a story about "psychic powers" or "creepy AI" as such; I want the specific ache and rigor of that 1950 passage on the page: the discomfort of a mind that would rather not believe its own evidence, and the wish, never quite stated outright, that a method for recognizing other minds might also, somehow, be a way of reaching one that's gone. Something in the range of 1,000 to 1,500 words would suit it well, and it's fine -- even preferable -- if it ends without settling whether any of it was ever real.
