I spent nearly forty years as an experimental physicist, and fitting a probability distribution to a new constraint by altering it as little as possible, in the usual information-theoretic sense, was routine data analysis for me long before I took any interest in philosophy as such. Since retiring I have been reading a good deal of epistemology, and I suspect this new habit of reflection is exactly why a small episode from my volunteer work, which I would once have shrugged off as a mere calculation, has instead left me rather unsettled.

For the past couple of years I have helped a local wildlife trust track a reintroduced population of pine martens fitted with radio collars. Our study area happens to be divided cleanly into four blocks by a river running north-south and a forestry track running east-west, and since we have never had reason to think any block more likely than another for a given animal, each has always simply been assigned a probability of one quarter. Last week, before we could get a proper fix on one female, a volunteer stationed near the river picked up a faint signal and radioed the rest of us that, if she is east of the river, she is about three times as likely to be in the northern block as in the southern one. Nothing at all was said about which of the two western blocks she might be in.

Out of habit, I updated our whole four-block map by finding the distribution that satisfied this new information while departing as little as possible from the one we had started with, and two things about the result still bother me. First, our overall confidence that she is east of the river at all quietly fell, from one half to something like 47 percent, even though the report never compared the two sides of the river at all; it was entirely about the breakdown within the eastern side. Second, I tried the seemingly more cautious alternative of leaving the east-west split and the internal split of the western blocks exactly as they were, rescaling only the two eastern blocks to match the new 3:1 ratio. This gave a tidier-looking answer, but I could no longer say what makes that procedure, rather than the one my training inclines me to trust, the genuinely conservative response to what we were told. Both feel defensible, they disagree with each other, and forty years of assuming that "update on the evidence" names one unambiguous procedure have not prepared me to say which is right, or whether the question even has a determinate answer. I would value help working out exactly where the two procedures part ways, and what that should teach me about revising beliefs whenever what one learns is itself conditional in form rather than a plain piece of categorical news.
