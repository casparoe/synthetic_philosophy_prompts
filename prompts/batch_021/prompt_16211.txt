I don't have any formal training in philosophy, but over the past year I've worked my way through a fair amount of the literature on material constitution on my own, and I've started helping a friend prepare for their introductory metaphysics course by writing practice questions and going over their answers together. Since I can't fully trust my own judgment on how rigorous their reasoning actually is, I'd like an outside, careful assessment.

Here is the question I gave them:

A sculptor takes a lump of clay and, without adding or removing any clay, shapes it over the course of an afternoon into a statue. Call the lump, considered from the moment it takes on this shape, "Lumpl," and call the statue "Goliath." (a) Explain why Lumpl and Goliath seem to occupy exactly the same matter, in exactly the same place, at exactly the same time. (b) Give the argument, appealing to Leibniz's Law, for the conclusion that Lumpl and Goliath are nevertheless two distinct objects rather than one. (c) Explain how Lynne Rudder Baker's constitution view is supposed to accommodate this distinctness without treating Lumpl and Goliath as two separate things that merely happen to coincide. (d) State one serious difficulty facing Baker's view.

And here is what they wrote:

Lumpl and Goliath are made of all the same clay, arranged the same way, in the same place, at the same time, so at the moment the statue is finished there is no physical difference between them at all: every particle of clay in one is a particle of clay in the other. Despite this, many philosophers deny that Lumpl and Goliath are the very same object. The argument turns on Leibniz's Law: if two things are truly identical, they must share every property, including modal properties (the ways they could or couldn't have been). Lumpl could survive being squashed back into a ball — it would just be a ball-shaped lump instead of a statue-shaped one — but Goliath could not survive being squashed, since squashing it just is destroying the statue. So Lumpl has a property Goliath lacks, and by Leibniz's Law, whatever has a property the other one lacks cannot be identical to it. Hence Lumpl and Goliath must be two different objects, not one.

Baker's response is that this doesn't make Lumpl and Goliath two unrelated objects awkwardly crammed into the same space. Instead, Lumpl constitutes Goliath: a relation weaker than identity but much stronger than mere coincidence, comparable to the way a flag is related to the piece of cloth it's made from. What keeps this from being "spooky coincidence," on Baker's view, is that Lumpl and Goliath are really just different temporal parts of one and the same four-dimensional object, so during any stretch when their temporal parts overlap they automatically share all their categorical properties, and only diverge outside that stretch. This is how Baker accounts for the unity of the two without collapsing them into strict identity.

A serious problem for the view is that Lumpl and Goliath are made of exactly the same matter, arranged in exactly the same way, down to the level of the fundamental particles, so it is unclear what could possibly ground the fact that one of them can survive squashing and the other can't.
