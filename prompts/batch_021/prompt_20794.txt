Last night a friend and I -- she's finishing a dissertation in philosophy of science -- got into a long argument about theories of causation, and by the end she had convinced me of something I would like a second opinion on. Her claim was that James Woodward's interventionist theory improves on David Lewis's counterfactual analysis by dispensing with unactualized possibilities altogether. Rather than asking what would happen in the closest possible world where the cause is absent, she said, Woodward cashes causation out in terms of structural equations and a designated intervention variable, which she took to show that his theory is a non-modal, empirically grounded alternative to Lewis's, rather than merely a sophisticated variant of it. If that's right, though, I'm not sure how an interventionist is supposed to license causal claims about things nobody has ever intervened on, or could ever intervene on -- that continental drift shaped the present coastlines, say, or that a star's core temperature determined whether it went supernova. How would Woodward handle cases like these, if not by quietly reintroducing exactly the sort of unactualized possibilities my friend says his account avoids?
