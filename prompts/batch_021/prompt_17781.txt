A friend and I spent a long evening arguing about how the different things called "disjunctivism" in the philosophy of perception actually relate to one another, and we left disagreeing. My friend maintained that Duncan Pritchard's epistemological disjunctivism is really just the older metaphysical disjunctivism of Hinton, Snowdon, and McDowell wearing an epistemologist's hat: if perceptual knowledge rests on a factive, reflectively accessible reason that is simply unavailable in the matching hallucination case, then the two experiences must already differ in fundamental kind, not merely in epistemic standing. I countered that Pritchard designed his view to be detachable from any particular metaphysics of experience, so that one could accept a common-factor account of perceptual experience and still hold that the justification available in the good and bad cases differs disjunctively. Could you settle this by spelling out exactly what is claimed to differ "disjunctively" on each view (the intrinsic nature of the experience itself, versus the epistemic support it grounds), and by explaining whether epistemological disjunctivism really presupposes metaphysical disjunctivism about experience or can be motivated independently of it? I would also value your judgment on which of the two views, if either, really does the work of blocking the classical argument from illusion, and which is instead geared toward a related but different skeptical worry about justification.
