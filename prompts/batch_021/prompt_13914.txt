I'm about two-thirds into a novel where the narrator's mom, who has a slow-moving degenerative illness, point-blank refuses to hear her prognosis from her doctors, even though she's got real decisions riding on it — whether to keep pushing through an aggressive treatment or shift to hospice, whether to sell the family house now or wait it out, that sort of thing. My partner did a stats PhD and thinks this is a cheat, character-wise: he keeps bringing up something called Good's theorem, which apparently proves that for a rational Bayesian, looking at free evidence before you act can never make your expected outcome worse — only better or the same — so a fully rational version of my character should always want the prognosis, full stop. He says if I want to keep her refusing, I basically have to write her as having some flaw or blind spot, otherwise its just not believable.

I don't want to cave and slap a "flaw" label on her just to make the theorem happy, so before I go back to the draft I want to actually understand if he's right. Can you make the strongest possible argument that a person in her position could refuse the prognosis without that being a failure of rationality — not by just gesturing at "people are complicated" but by actually digging into which specific premise or setup-condition of Good's theorem her situation might be violating, and why that's not some minor technicality but the whole ballgame. If you honestly think there's no way around it and my partner's basically right, say so plainly too — I'd rather sort this out now than after my writing group tears the scene apart.
