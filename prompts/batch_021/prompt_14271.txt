Ok this is a bit of a weird one but stick with me. Last weekend I finally tried one of those sensory deprivation float tanks (a coworker's been recommending them forever for stress) and about half an hour in I had this genuinely unsettling moment where I completely lost track of my own body — like I couldn't tell you where my arms were, whether my legs were crossed, any of it, just this weird floaty "I'm still here" feeling with nothing underneath it. Kind of shook me more than I expected, ngl. When I got home I went down a research hole trying to figure out what even happened and stumbled on this thing called Avicenna's "flying man" argument (he's the medieval Persian philosopher, also goes by Ibn Sina), which is basically the same setup: he asks you to imagine a person created instantly as a full grown adult but suspended in mid-air, blindfolded, limbs spread so they don't even touch each other, no wind on the skin, nothing — zero bodily sensation of any kind. His point is that even in that state the person would still immediately know "I exist," without being able to point to any part of their body to prove it, and he uses that to argue the self (or soul) has to be something separate from the body, not just another one of its parts. Apparently people bring this up as a precursor to Descartes' cogito, except it's not built on doubt, its built on this weird immediate certainty that supposedly survives even when every scrap of bodily feeling is stripped away.

Anyway I cant stop turning this over and would honestly love a short story out of it instead of me just overthinking it alone at 1am. Doesn't have to be literally a float tank — could be an upload, deep anesthesia, a coma, some solo deep-space thing, whatever setting lets you sit inside a character's head the longest — but I want the actual philosophical nerve of Avicenna's argument to be doing real work in the story, not just set dressing. Like, really make me feel the pull between "yeah, obviously I'd still know I exist with zero body input" and the creepier possibility that the sense of self only holds together because it's quietly borrowing some last thread of bodily feeling, and once that's fully gone it doesn't work the way Avicenna thought it would. I don't need a tidy resolution, honestly I'd prefer it stayed a little unresolved — I just want it to actually grapple with that tension instead of being a generic "floating in the void" mood piece.
