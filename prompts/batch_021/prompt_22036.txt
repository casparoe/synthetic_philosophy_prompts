And here's the student's answer, copied verbatim:

"The SCQ asks under what conditions some objects compose a further object -- e.g., when do many small things add up to one bigger thing? Van Inwagen rejects 'moderate' answers such as Contact (the xs compose something iff they're touching) or Fastening (iff they're stuck together) via the vagueness argument: any such criterion would let composition come in borderline cases, so it would sometimes be indeterminate how many objects exist. But he thinks there can't be a vague number of objects -- number and existence don't admit of degrees -- so no criterion of that contact-or-fastening kind can be correct. Since van Inwagen's own answer (that the xs compose something iff their activity constitutes a life) doesn't produce this kind of borderline case, the vagueness argument establishes that composition occurs exactly when some xs constitute a life, which is why he ends up a restrictivist who thinks only organisms are composite objects.

Because almost nothing else -- tables, rocks, stars -- is alive, van Inwagen has to explain away our ordinary talk of such things, and he does this with a paraphrase strategy: instead of saying 'there is a table here,' he says 'there are particles arranged tablewise here.' Since this is the exact same move mereological nihilists make, only extended to everything rather than just non-living stuff, van Inwagen's view basically collapses into nihilism for all practical purposes -- the two positions only really disagree at the level of official ideology, not in what they say we should believe exists.

One influential objection, due to Markosian, targets the vagueness argument itself: a restricted answer to the SCQ doesn't have to be vague just because it lacks a tidy, informative criterion like Contact or Fastening. Composition facts could simply be brute -- true without being grounded in any further analysis -- and a brute fact isn't automatically a vague one. If that's right, van Inwagen was too quick to rule out all restricted answers other than his own."
