Long shot question, but it's been rattling around in my head since a LessWrong thread a while back about whether "blame" even survives taking determinism seriously -- the kind of thread that always ends up linking Dennett's stances or one of Yudkowsky's old free-will sequence posts. That's what actually got me to read P.F. Strawson's "Freedom and Resentment" properly instead of just knowing it by reputation, and the part that stuck with me is how much weight he puts on what he calls the "objective attitude": instead of resenting someone or holding them responsible, you just see their behavior as caused, and you manage/treat/avoid them accordingly, the way you'd deal with a malfunctioning machine or a small child. Strawsons whole argument is that we can flip into that stance toward particular people under particular conditions (kids, the severely mentally ill) but we couldn't sustain it universally, toward everyone, and -- this is the bit I actually care about -- we shouldn't even want to, because that would mean giving up on treating each other as full participants in relationships: no resentment, sure, but also no gratitude, no love, none of it.

Which got me thinking about Spinoza, who looks to me like he's arguing FOR exactly the thing Strawson says is off the table. The preface to Part III of the Ethics is basically a manifesto against people who curse, mock, or feel indignant at human vice instead of just understanding it causally, the way you'd understand a triangle, and the whole arc of the Ethics afterward is that the free/wise person, the more they understand things (including other people's actions) as following necessarily from prior causes, the less they hate, envy, or resent anyone -- that IS blessedness for him. So he'd already built an entire positive ethical ideal out of living inside something like the objective attitude, permanently, a good three centuries before Strawson showed up to argue that's neither possible nor desirable for creatures like us.

So here's the actual ask: if Spinoza somehow got to read "Freedom and Resentment" cover to cover, what would he say back? Don't give me the shallow version where Spinoza's a determinist so obviously he's on the "pessimist" side -- Strawson's own position isn't simple optimism either, so actually work out where Spinoza lands relative to both camps, not just relative to determinism in the abstract. Specifically, engage with Strawson's claim that the objective attitude can't be universalized without giving up the reciprocal, participant-level stuff (love, gratitude, friendship) that makes us regard each other as people at all -- does Spinoza's account of friendship among free men actually satisfy that, or does it quietly amount to a thinner, more uniform kind of benevolence than what Strawson has in mind, basically a benign solitude wearing the costume of friendship? Go wherever the actual argument leads, including "Strawson wins."
