The question she set was: "In the 1958 Hart–Fuller exchange, explain what each philosopher thought should happen to the German 'grudge informers' once the Nazi regime fell, and how each position grows out of his broader jurisprudential commitments. Then assess whether Fuller's 'internal morality of law' really undermines Hart's separability thesis, or whether Hart's own concessions (e.g., the minimum content of natural law) already accommodate it."

Here's one student's answer, copied more or less as submitted:

"Hart and Fuller agree on the bottom line in the grudge informer cases: people who denounced others to the Nazi authorities for offences like criticizing Hitler, and thereby got them imprisoned or worse, ought to be liable to punishment once the regime fell. Where they disagree is on how to reach that conclusion, and the disagreement tracks their larger positions in jurisprudence.

Hart, arguing as a positivist, accepts that the wartime statutes the informers invoked were valid law at the time, however odious — for Hart, whether a rule is law is a question of its social pedigree (roughly, whether it was made the way the system's rule of recognition specifies), not a question of its moral merit. Given that, he thinks the honest way to punish the informers is through a frankly retroactive statute passed after the war: openly admit we're inflicting a real cost (retroactive punishment, which offends the rule-of-law value of non-retroactivity) for the sake of a greater good, rather than pretend, as the German courts did in following Radbruch, that the Nazi statute was never really 'law' in the first place. For Hart, that move just papers over a hard moral choice by redefining a legal concept instead of owning it.

Fuller, in his reply, thinks Hart is too quick to grant that the Nazi enactments counted as law at all. His view is essentially a more technical version of Radbruch's: Radbruch had already argued that statutes crossing a threshold of injustice forfeit legal validity, and Fuller's contribution is mainly to spell out, through the eight canons of legality he later lists in The Morality of Law (things like generality, prospectivity, clarity, and congruence between the rules on the books and how officials actually behave), what 'crossing the threshold' more precisely amounts to. On this basis Fuller can say a regime that governs by secret, retroactive, mutually contradictory decrees enforced arbitrarily has failed as a legal system regardless of the content of any particular rule, so the informers can be tried under ordinary law without anyone having to pass a retroactive statute.

Still, I don't think this fully vindicates Fuller against Hart, because Hart had already conceded, in his 'minimum content of natural law,' that any functioning legal system will include rules against violence and some minimal protection of property and promises, given basic facts about human vulnerability and scarcity. Fuller's internal morality of law is really just a further list of features a system of rules must have to count as law, so once Hart admits one such list (the minimum content) he has no principled way to reject Fuller's list (the eight canons) as a difference in kind rather than degree. The real disagreement between them, in other words, is less about whether law and morality are conceptually connected — Hart already thinks they are, a bit — than about how long that list should be."
