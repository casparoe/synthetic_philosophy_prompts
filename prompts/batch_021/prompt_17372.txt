I'm working on a magazine feature about ideas ethicists have quietly borrowed from economics, and the one I can't stop turning over is Toby Ord's notion of "moral trade," laid out in a 2015 paper of that name. His argument is that people who disagree fundamentally about ethics can still strike deals that leave the world better by each person's own lights, in much the same way that two countries with no shared standard of value can still gain from trading according to comparative advantage. To make it concrete: a committed vegetarian who is indifferent to, say, a proposed wildlife cull might agree to tolerate it in exchange for a hunter, who sees nothing wrong with eating meat, cutting back on his own consumption anyway — each ends up with more of what they actually care about than unilateral action would have gotten them, without either side conceding a single point in the underlying disagreement.

What I haven't seen properly argued out is the objection that keeps nagging at me: that this isn't a moral free lunch at all but a kind of laundering operation, since agreeing to perform, permit, or subsidize something you sincerely regard as wrong — for a price — sits very uneasily with actually believing it is wrong, and since in practice such a market would mostly let whoever has the most to offer buy their way out of other people's moral objections. For a sidebar, I'd like to lay out both sides at their strongest rather than settle it myself. Could you write me a dialogue between two moral philosophers at a conference dinner — one an enthusiastic longtime defender of moral trade, the other convinced it is a respectable-sounding license for complicity — who actually engage with each other's best argument rather than talk past one another, and where neither is left with a clean, unanswerable final word?
