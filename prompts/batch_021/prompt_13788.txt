So this came up at my rationalist meetup last week after a talk on the classic money-pump argument for why coherent agents basically have to act like they're maximizing some utility function. Afterward two regulars, Ravi and Alex, got into a whole thing about what "utility" actually means, and I've been chewing on it since.

Ravi opened with: the whole revealed-preference move is kind of empty. If "preference" just means "whatever your choices reveal" and "utility" just means "the thing your preferences maximize," then saying "she did X because it maximized her utility" doesn't explain anything -- it's just redescribing the choice in fancier words.

Alex said that's not fair to the actual theory: revealed preference isn't "whatever you pick counts as utility-maximizing by fiat," it requires your choices across different situations to be jointly consistent (the WARP/SARP-type axioms), and people blow through those all the time, like the classic reversals between how people price a gamble and how they choose between gambles. So it's a real, falsifiable claim, and it does get falsified.

Ravi's comeback was: fine, consistency is falsifiable, but that's a smaller win than it sounds. All that buys you is "your choices can be represented by some ordinal ranking" -- it doesn't get you to "utility" in the sense people actually mean when they say a vacation gave them a ton of utility, or use it to justify some tradeoff. For that you need cardinal, comparable amounts, not just an ordering.

Alex replied thats literally what the Von Neumann-Morgenstern theorem gives you: consistent choices over lotteries hand you a cardinal utility function, unique up to a positive affine transform, and that's not just relabeling -- you can back out someone's utility number from a couple gambles and then use it to correctly predict a totally different gamble. That's a real prediction, and it can fail.

Ravi shot back that this is exactly where it does fail, constantly -- Allais-paradox-type violations of independence are robust and endlessly replicated -- and whenever they show up, the move is never "ok, utility maximization is wrong," it's "patch the utility function" (probability weighting, reference points, loss aversion, etc). A theory that's unfalsifiable in practice is exactly what you'd get if every failure just gets absorbed as a patch instead of counting against it.

Alex's last word was that patching a theory when you run into anomalies isn't automatically a cheap rescue, sometimes that's just what good science looks like, and that Ravi still hadn't given a principled line between "legitimate refinement" (prospect theory) and "saving the theory by definition."

Neither of them backed down and I honestly don't know this stuff well enough to referee it myself.
