The question was: "In Nick Bostrom's framework for anthropic reasoning, the Self-Sampling Assumption (SSA) and the Self-Indication Assumption (SIA) give different verdicts on the Doomsday Argument and on the 'presumptuous philosopher' case. In roughly 200-250 words: (a) state SSA and SIA precisely enough to show how they differ; (b) explain why the Doomsday Argument's force depends on SSA-style reasoning, and why adopting SIA is usually said to block it; (c) explain how the presumptuous philosopher case arises as a cost specifically of adopting SIA, using a toy example of two cosmological theories that imply very different total numbers of observers; and (d) briefly assess whether SSA's dependence on a reference class is a comparably serious cost." Priya's answer, copied exactly as she wrote it, was: "SSA says you should reason as if you were a random draw from all the observers who actually exist within your 'reference class' -- the set of observers relevantly like you. SIA instead says you should weight hypotheses by how many observers each predicts, since finding yourself existing at all is more likely under a hypothesis with lots of observers; put another way, SSA samples over observer-moments inside a fixed reference class, while SIA samples over observers across every possible world, reference class aside. The Doomsday Argument is SSA-style reasoning applied to birth rank: if you imagine yourself as a random pick among everyone who will ever be born, you're statistically more likely to be born late in a short-lived species than late in a long-lived one, so pure self-location pushes up the odds that doom comes soon. SIA blocks exactly this move, because it independently favors hypotheses with bigger populations, and that preference exactly cancels the shift the Doomsday Argument depends on. The presumptuous philosopher is what that same preference costs you when there's no birth-rank information at all: given two theories, T1 and T2, that are empirically tied except that T2 implies enormously more observers overall, SIA already tells you to be almost certain T2 is right before physicists even run the deciding experiment, purely because more observers exist under T2 -- which amounts to getting a physics result for free. SSA doesn't have that problem, but it isn't free either, since it has to specify a reference class -- and that turns out to be just as arbitrary a cost as SIA's, since SIA needs one too, just drawn around a bigger pool of observers instead of a narrower one."
