After a long career as an experimental physicist, I've spent my retirement sitting in on a philosophy discussion group at the local university, and last week's session on Harsanyi's aggregation theorem produced an exchange between two of the regulars, Owen and Priya, that the group never quite resolved.

(1) Owen opened by claiming the theorem essentially forces utilitarianism on any rational group: if every member and the group itself are expected-utility maximizers evaluating the same prospects, and the group's ranking respects unanimity (Pareto), then the group's utility function must be a positive-weighted sum of the members' utilities -- about as close, he said, as ethics ever gets to a mathematical proof of utilitarianism.

(2) Priya objected that this oversells the result. Each person's von Neumann-Morgenstern utility function is only defined up to its own arbitrary zero-point and unit, so the "weights" the theorem hands you are only as meaningful as whatever scale you'd already chosen for each person -- the theorem itself is silent on that choice, and is perfectly compatible with treating one person's interests as a thousand times more important than everyone else's. Calling the result "utilitarianism," she said, quietly assumes the equal weighting the theorem never actually supplies.

(3) Owen conceded the point about weights but called it a technicality: Bentham never fixed an exchange rate between two people's pleasures either, and the theorem still proves something substantive on its own terms -- that the social welfare function has to be linear and additive in the individuals' utilities, with no structural room for, say, a rule that gives independent extra priority to whoever is worse off.

(4) Priya answered that the deeper problem isn't the weights but an old objection, due to the economist Peter Diamond, to exactly this kind of construction. Harsanyi's Pareto condition is stated ex ante, over prospects, so the resulting rule is indifferent between tossing a coin to decide who gets some scarce good and simply handing it to one person outright, whenever both options happen to yield the same expected sum of utility -- even though the coin toss is intuitively fairer, since it gives each person an equal chance. That kind of fairness, she said, is invisible from the theorem's ex ante vantage point, no matter how the weights are fixed.

(5) Owen replied that this only shows that process fairness and expected-utility Pareto are different values, not that the theorem or its premises are mistaken: if you want the coin toss for its own sake, you're importing a consideration the axioms were never meant to capture, so Priya's objection is really a complaint about utilitarianism rather than about Harsanyi's mathematics.

Priya wasn't persuaded, and the group moved on before either could press further. Could you go through the exchange move by move?
