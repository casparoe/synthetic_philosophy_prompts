I've followed the scalable oversight literature on the Alignment Forum ever since Irving, Christiano, and Amodei's original 2018 debate paper, and these days I sit on the small research-strategy committee at the alignment nonprofit where I work. We've spent the last three years, and roughly half our technical budget, on a debate-based oversight agenda: training two copies of a model to argue opposing sides of a question in front of a much weaker judge, on the theory that the honest debater should win in equilibrium because the judge only has to verify locally checkable steps rather than solve the object-level problem itself. We're now deciding whether to renew this bet for another funding cycle or reallocate most of it to weak-to-strong generalization and interpretability work instead, and the committee is genuinely split down the middle.

One camp, including our most senior researcher, wants to double down: debate is still the only scalable-oversight proposal with a real theoretical pedigree (the analogy to interactive proof systems, and the newer "doubly-efficient" variants that use cross-examination to recover soundness guarantees even when debaters vastly outclass the judge in compute), and our own small internal replications of the recent LLM debate experiments show judges, human and automated, do somewhat better with cross-examination than with a single persuasive consultant arguing one side. The other camp thinks this misses the point: the obfuscated-arguments problem shows that a sufficiently sophisticated dishonest debater can bury a flaw such that locating it costs the judge far more computation than hiding it cost the debater, and they suspect this isn't an engineering wrinkle but a symptom of something deeper — that for exactly the highest-stakes claims we would want debate to police, such as whether a model is being deceptive, the "atomic" claims the argument tree is supposed to bottom out in may themselves be beyond what any judge we could afford to run is able to verify. They want to fund a serious adversarial stress test, using a model deliberately trained to construct obfuscated arguments, before committing another funding cycle to the agenda as currently conceived.

Could you draft the decision memo I'll circulate to the committee ahead of our renewal vote in three weeks? It should recommend one concrete course of action — full renewal, reallocation, or something more conditional such as a gated stress test with a hard deadline and pre-registered failure criteria — give the reasoning behind it, and engage honestly with the strongest version of whichever side's case you end up recommending against, rather than caricaturing it.
