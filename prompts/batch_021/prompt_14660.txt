I sit on my hospital's clinical ethics committee. Our epilepsy surgery program has brought us a policy question rather than a single case: when adult patients are being worked up for corpus callosotomy to control severe, drug-resistant atonic ("drop") seizures, should the informed-consent process say anything about the philosophical status of the operation -- specifically, that severing the corpus callosum is the very intervention behind the classic split-brain studies, and that philosophers have never agreed on how to describe what happens to the unity of a patient's mind afterward? The question became concrete last month when a patient's brother, midway through a philosophy PhD, told the pre-surgical team that no consent form could really cover what his sister was agreeing to. Citing Nagel, he argued that it may simply be indeterminate after the operation whether one subject of experience is present or two; citing Puccetti, he pressed the more unsettling possibility that the surgery would not create a second locus of consciousness so much as unmask one that, on that view, was already there, merely well-coordinated with the first.

The attending neuropsychologist who fielded this wants committee guidance she can actually use, not a one-off reassurance. Roughly half the committee thinks none of this belongs anywhere near a consent conversation: callosotomy patients are extensively documented to behave as single, well-integrated agents in ordinary life, with the classic dissociations only appearing under contrived, lateralized laboratory presentation, so raising Nagel and Puccetti with an anxious surgical candidate would be importing an unresolved academic dispute with no predictive bearing on her actual future, purely at the cost of making an already hard decision more frightening. The other half thinks that objection moves too fast: informed consent is supposed to track what is genuinely unsettled and bears on the choice being made, not merely what shows up on a symptom checklist, and "we do not currently have a stable answer to how many subjects of experience this operation leaves you as" looks like exactly the kind of fact a reasonable patient would want named, rather than quietly managed on her behalf by clinicians who have already decided it doesn't matter.

Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate before next month's meeting. It should take a clear position rather than just describing the disagreement, specify concretely what the consent process should (or should not) include going forward and who is responsible for delivering it, propose a date or trigger for revisiting the policy, and engage seriously with the strongest version of whichever side it doesn't adopt, rather than dismissing it as either fearmongering or philosophical hair-splitting.
