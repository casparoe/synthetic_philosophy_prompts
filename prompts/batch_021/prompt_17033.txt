I'm several drafts into a novel that follows one man from a self-absorbed youth to a chastened, generous old age, and some background reading has left me with a metaphysical puzzle I can't quite resolve on my own. Lewis's "problem of temporary intrinsics" holds that endurantism -- the view that a persisting thing is wholly present at every moment it exists -- has trouble accommodating an object's having incompatible intrinsic properties at different times; the standard case is shape, since nothing is simply bent and simply straight at once. Perdurantism handles this easily by assigning the incompatible properties to different temporal parts of one four-dimensional whole. Please argue that this consideration gives us little reason to prefer a perdurantist picture of persons over an endurantist one when what changes is character rather than shape, since traits like "cruel" or "generous" aren't intrinsic properties of the kind Lewis's argument requires, but are already dispositional -- a matter of how someone tends to act across a range of circumstances -- in a way shape is not. I've also seen it argued that dispositions such as fragility count as genuinely intrinsic all the same, which would undercut my disanalogy -- please take that objection seriously, and tell me how much of the original argument survives it.
