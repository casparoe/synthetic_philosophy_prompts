I sit on my university's data-security incident-response committee, and we are about thirty-six hours into a live situation that has forced me to think hard about something I had previously only encountered as a decision-theory thought experiment on the rationalist blogs I've followed for years. A criminal group has encrypted several research servers and is separately threatening to publish, by Friday morning, roughly a decade of unpublished data from one of our labs, along with a batch of current students' financial-aid records that got swept up in the same server image, unless we pay a sum that is, frankly, trivial next to the university's budget. This is the second such incident in eighteen months; last time, a previous committee paid quietly and never adopted a public policy, and no one currently serving is confident that decision was ever revisited on its merits.

Half the committee wants to use this meeting to adopt a standing no-payment policy, on the usual deterrence logic that institutions known to pay get targeted again while institutions known to refuse get targeted less. The other half points out, reasonably, that the specific data now at risk cannot be recreated at any price, that we have no actual evidence this particular group tracks institutional payment history rather than working down a list of soft targets more or less indiscriminately, and that refusing to pay buys us nothing if the attackers can't read our policy off us in the first place. One member, who shares my habit of following this stuff online, keeps insisting that the right question isn't "what should we do, now that we're the ones being extorted," but something closer to what she calls an updateless framing: asking what policy we would have wanted to commit to before any attacker chose whether to target us, on the grounds that how we actually act now is the only thing any such commitment could ever consist in. I find that framing intuitive in the individual cases I've seen it applied to -- Parfit's hitchhiker, counterfactual mugging, and the like -- but I can't tell whether it survives the move from a single agent to a standing committee with rotating membership, divided constituencies, and real uncertainty about whether we're even the kind of target whose disposition anyone is in a position to read.

Please draft the memo I'll circulate to the full committee before Thursday's emergency session. It should give a concrete recommendation both for the live decision, which has to be made before the attackers' deadline, and for the standing policy going forward; it should say plainly what you make of my colleague's updateless framing, including whether the disanalogies with the individual thought experiments actually undercut it here; and whichever way the recommendation comes out, it should engage honestly with the strongest version of the argument it's rejecting, rather than resting on a deterrence slogan on one side or an appeal to the data's irreplaceability on the other.
