My grandmother died ten days ago, and I've been asked to give the eulogy at her funeral this Saturday. I'm a junior in high school and have never written anything like this before, which would be daunting enough on its own, but what's actually kept me up at night is something she told me privately about a year before she got sick. She had sung in her church choir and never missed Sunday Mass for something like forty years, and everyone in my family assumes she died exactly as certain in her faith as she always seemed from the outside. But she confided in me that for a long stretch of years, praying had stopped feeling like talking to anyone at all, that she kept showing up and kneeling down out of loyalty more than conviction, and that she genuinely didn't know whether that meant there was nothing on the other end or whether it was just supposed to feel that way sometimes. She never told my parents or her other grandchildren any of this. I don't want to betray what she told me by standing up on Saturday and implying she went out sure of everything, but I also don't want to tell a room full of people who loved her that she died feeling abandoned by God, full stop, because I don't think that's true to how she actually held it either.

After she told me this, I went looking for whether anyone had written about it properly, and I ended up reading about what philosophers call the problem of divine hiddenness, especially the version J.L. Schellenberg turns into an actual argument against God's existence: roughly, that a God who really loved us would make sure that anyone capable of a relationship with him, and not actively resisting one, could always find their way to belief, so the existence of sincere, non-resistant people who still don't feel God's presence is itself evidence against such a God existing at all. What's caught my attention, though, is less the atheist conclusion than the way theists who take the problem seriously have replied to it, and how differently they do it: some argue God keeps a kind of distance on purpose so that love and belief stay free rather than being forced on us by overwhelming evidence, while others argue the deeper issue is that God only makes himself available through the kind of evidence that asks something of you morally, so that anyone looking for proof the way you'd look for proof of anything else will come up empty by design. I don't think either quite describes grandma's situation, since she wasn't after a philosophy paper's worth of proof. She just wanted the feeling back, and it didn't come.

Could you help me write the actual eulogy, around five minutes long, using all of this as background rather than content? I don't want to mention Schellenberg or "divine hiddenness" by name, or make the eulogy sound like a philosophy essay, but I do want the language underneath it to be shaped by these distinctions: it needs to be honest that her sense of God's presence went quiet toward the end, without collapsing that into either "she died an unbeliever" or "she's watching over us now, all her doubts resolved," since I'm fairly sure she would have rejected both of those as more certain than she ever let herself be. It also has to work for a room where my uncle hasn't set foot in a church since he was eighteen and my mom still prays the rosary every night, without flattening either of their reactions in order to make it land for the other.
