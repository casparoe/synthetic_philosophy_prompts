My partner and I recently went through expanded carrier screening before trying to conceive, and learned that we are both carriers for cystic fibrosis, which means any child we conceive naturally has a one-in-four chance of being affected. Our genetic counselor explained that IVF with preimplantation genetic diagnosis would let us test several embryos and transfer only an unaffected one. In reading around this decision, I ran into Parfit's non-identity problem and realized our situation is close to a textbook case of it: whichever path we take, a different child results — a different combination of egg and sperm if we conceive naturally, an entirely different embryo if we use PGD. So if we conceived naturally and our child turned out to be affected, it looks like we could not have wronged that particular child by skipping PGD, since PGD was never going to make things better for him — it would only have resulted in a different, unaffected child existing in his place.

I mentioned this to two friends from a philosophy reading group I have belonged to for years, Dana and Priya, both of whom know this literature far better than I do, and the two of them split immediately into camps that neither was willing to abandon. Dana defended what she called the "bite the bullet" position associated with writers like David Boonin: to wrong someone is to make them worse off than some available alternative would have made them, and since no alternative existed that would have been better for our actual child, nothing we did could count as a wrong done to him, however uncomfortable that conclusion feels. Priya thought this was too quick, and pointed instead to the sort of non-comparative or consent-based accounts of harm developed by writers like Elizabeth Harman and Seana Shiffrin, on which an act can wrong someone by imposing a serious, avoidable burden even if no alternative existence would have been better for that very person. Neither of them managed to persuade the other before the meeting broke up, and I have been turning the disagreement over ever since.

I would like to see the argument worked out properly rather than half-remembered from one evening. Could you write a dialogue in which Dana and Priya pick the disagreement back up over coffee after the meeting, working through a case structurally like ours? I would like both of them to be equally sharp, and to actually respond to each other's strongest points rather than restate their opening positions: Dana should have to say something about why her view does not also excuse any decision to impose a serious, foreseeable, avoidable burden as long as a different person is born instead, and Priya should have to say something about why her view does not collapse into the claim that having any child at all, rather than waiting for a different, better-off child one could have had, is thereby wrong. Ideally, by the end, at least one of them has had to genuinely refine or qualify her position in response to the other, rather than the conversation simply ending in a draw.
