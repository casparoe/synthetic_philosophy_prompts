I was elected to my local school board this past fall -- partly, if I'm honest, because my daughter starts ninth-grade biology next year and I wanted a say in what she'd be taught -- and at our next meeting we are due to vote on a proposal, backed by a vocal group of parents, to add a "critical analysis of evolution" unit to the curriculum that would present a young earth, a global flood, and separately created "kinds" as live scientific alternatives to common descent and deep time, rather than as matters of faith properly left outside the science classroom. I'm religious myself, and I don't think the parents pushing this are cranks or acting in bad faith; I just don't think what they are proposing amounts to good science education, and I would like to be able to explain why on grounds that go beyond "because the scientists say so."

While drafting my remarks for the meeting, I went back to Galileo's 1615 letter to the Grand Duchess Christina, since the fight he was having with churchmen who wanted a literal reading of certain psalms and the Book of Joshua enforced against Copernican astronomy struck me as having roughly the same shape as the one in front of my board, even though the science involved is quite different in character -- his a matter of mathematical astronomy, ours a cumulative, inferential historical science. What I would like from you is a genuine reconstruction, not a slogan: reasoning strictly from the principles Galileo actually lays out in that letter -- his use of Augustine, his distinction between what is merely probable and what is properly demonstrated, his views on when and how scripture may need reinterpretation -- how do you think he would have advised a body like mine to handle this proposal? I am especially interested in which of his arguments carry over cleanly to the evolution case, which ones strain or break down given the disanalogies between his science and this one, and any places where you suspect he would have counseled more caution than his modern admirers in the evolution debate usually credit him with.
