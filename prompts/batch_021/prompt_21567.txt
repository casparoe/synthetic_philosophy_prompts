So I went down a total rabbit hole this weekend after a coworker brought up the "Sleeping Beauty problem" during the retro for a gnarly bug we'd had. Quick version of the puzzle in case it's useful: Beauty is put to sleep on Sunday, a fair coin gets flipped, and if it lands heads she's woken up once, on Monday, and that's the whole experiment. If it lands tails, she's woken up Monday AND Tuesday, but her memory of the Monday waking gets wiped before Tuesday so she genuinely can't tell the two apart from the inside. Every time she wakes up she's asked "what's your credence that the coin landed heads?" Halfers say it should stay 1/2, since she hasn't learned any new info about the coin itself. Thirders say 1/3, because she has to account for there being twice as many "waking up" experiences on the tails branch. I read a bunch of posts on this over the weekend and now I'm stuck between camps and honestly a little lightheaded, ngl.

Heres why its not purely academic for me: the bug in question was basically a real-life Sleeping Beauty setup. Our job orchestration layer would occasionally fork/duplicate a worker after a timeout, so you'd end up with an "original" and one or more "clones," all running off identical starting state, with no way for any individual instance to tell from the inside whether it's the original or a clone. We patched it with idempotency keys so it stopped mattering in practice, but it left me wondering what the "correct" internal reasoning would even be for an instance that did need to bet on its own status -- and that's apparently exactly the kind of thing the thirder/halfer fight is about (self-locating belief, I think is the term of art).

Could you lay out the most important implications of the thirder vs. halfer debate for how an agent should reason about self-locating credence when it might be one of several duplicated copies of itself? I'm especially curious whether the "right" answer changes depending on details like how the duplication actually happens or what the copies are being asked to bet on, whether the classic Dutch-book/betting arguments really favor one side over the other, and whether this is genuinely still an open disagreement among philosophers or there's something close to a consensus that I just haven't come across yet.
