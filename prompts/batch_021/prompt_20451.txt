I've been reading about information cascades and rational herding, the sequential-choice models due to Banerjee and to Bikhchandani, Hirshleifer, and Welch, in which agents rationally set aside their own private signal and imitate whoever chose before them once the public record of past choices is informative enough. In the standard numerical illustration, a cascade can be triggered after as few as two agents in a row happen to pick the same option, after which every later agent should rationally pile on regardless of their own signal. Since this herding is the product of individually rational Bayesian updating rather than panic or conformity pressure, it seems to me that it ought to describe a genuinely stable equilibrium: once the crowd has coordinated on an action, nobody has any private incentive to break from it. And yet real fads, investment bubbles, and even expert consensus seem to unravel quite suddenly, apparently dislodged by a comparatively small piece of news. Doesn't the sheer suddenness of these collapses suggest that real-world herding has more to do with psychological panic than with the sober Bayesian mechanism these cascade models are supposed to describe?
