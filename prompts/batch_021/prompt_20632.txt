My dissertation advisor is retiring after thirty years spent defending David Lewis's account of convention -- that a convention is an arbitrary regularity in behavior which persists on nothing more than common knowledge and mutual expectation, requiring neither explicit agreement nor outside enforcement -- against critics, in the tradition of Margaret Gilbert, who argue this leaves no room for real obligation: on Lewis's picture, breaking a convention should be a mere surprise, though in practice it often feels like a wrong. I'm giving the toast at her retirement dinner, where roughly half the room works in philosophy or game theory and will catch every technical reference, and the rest is family, who would not know a coordination equilibrium from a Schelling point. Write a five-minute toast built around one substantial joke that turns on the fact that this very dinner -- the toasts, the raised glasses, the order of speakers -- is itself a Lewis-style convention, arbitrary in its particulars yet now something we would all feel faintly betrayed to see dropped. The joke should reward the specialists without turning the toast into a seminar, and the speech as a whole should land, for everyone else, as affection rather than argument.
