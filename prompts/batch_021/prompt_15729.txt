I'm working on a feature about the science of animal minds, and one consequence of the research seems to be underappreciated in most of the coverage I've come across. The 2012 Cambridge Declaration on Consciousness held that a mammalian neocortex isn't necessary for conscious experience, pointing to octopuses and birds as creatures that clearly have some form of it despite lacking one. If the neocortex is beside the point, then the property actually doing the work must simply be the sheer number of neurons an animal has -- comfortably covering octopuses, at roughly half a billion neurons, but ruling out honeybees, whose brains hold well under a million. Yet researchers such as Lars Chittka keep pointing to bees' flexible learning and apparent play behavior, and others have argued that the insect brain's "central complex" is functionally analogous to the vertebrate structures thought to underwrite basic sentience. Why do serious neuroscientists keep entertaining consciousness claims for bees, given how few neurons they have to spare, rather than conceding they're simply very sophisticated biological machines running on instinct?
