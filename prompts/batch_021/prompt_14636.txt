I've spent more of the last decade than I'd like to admit reading LessWrong, Overcoming Bias, and their various offshoots, and a few years ago some of us who met through that world started one of those small Tetlock-influenced calibration groups that seem to exist in every rationalist-adjacent city now — people who put numbers on things and mean it. Two of the most committed members, Nate (quantitative trading) and Elena (epidemiology), got into an argument a few weeks ago that I still haven't been able to adjudicate myself, and it's been nagging at me ever since. They'd gone three rounds on a private calibration question — the object-level content doesn't matter and wouldn't mean anything to you anyway — with his number parked around 15% and hers around 60%, and after a full double-crux session the week before had failed to move either of them, Nate reached for Aumann's agreement theorem to argue that the continued gap was no longer a mere difference of opinion but an actual mistake one of them was making.

Reconstructing the thread as best I can: (1) Nate opened by noting that they'd each stated their number, heard the other's, and restated their own unchanged number several times running, so their posteriors were common knowledge in the relevant sense; since Aumann's theorem says two Bayesians who share a common prior can't have common knowledge of differing posteriors, and they were both careful, honest forecasters, the persistent 15-60 split meant somebody was being irrational. (2) Elena replied that "common prior" was the load-bearing clause he'd walked past: her decade in a very different field had given her different base-rate instincts about this whole class of question, nothing about exchanging numbers manufactures a shared prior after the fact, and if that antecedent is false, the theorem simply has nothing to say about their case. (3) Nate answered that this was too quick, since Geanakoplos and Polemarchakis showed that when two agents just keep announcing their posteriors back and forth, the exchange is guaranteed to converge to a single shared value within finitely many rounds, because a finite state space can only be carved up so many times — so continued honest exchange should erase whatever gap their different starting points created, not preserve it. (4) Elena said he was overselling that result: what it actually guarantees is that the back-and-forth eventually stabilizes, not that the stable point is one of agreement — the equality conclusion is still riding on the same common-prior assumption as the static theorem, so two people who start from different priors can each reach a settled, unmoving number, satisfy every "common knowledge" condition Nate cares about, and simply stay at 15% and 60% without either of them having violated anything. (5) Nate said that was fair as far as it went, but that she was leaning a lot of weight on "different priors" as an all-purpose escape hatch: they'd both come up through the same calibration culture, so treating fundamentally different priors as a live hypothesis was a bigger stretch than treating a 45-point gap as evidence that one of them was mishandling some piece of evidence — otherwise nobody could ever be convicted of Aumann-irrationality, since anyone could always claim unverifiable prior differences. (6) Elena said that conceding their priors were probably close was exactly her point: once you're arguing from the empirical likelihood that two similarly-trained forecasters share a prior, rather than from the theorem itself, the real question becomes what unshared evidence produces the residual gap — and in three rounds, neither of them had actually named a single piece of evidence the other lacked; they'd only ever exchanged the numbers themselves.

Neither of them budged, and everyone else in the group mostly just found it funny. I'd like a genuine outside adjudication rather than another opinion added to the pile. Could you work through moves (1) through (6) and, for each, give a verdict on whether the point being made is correct, partly correct, or mistaken, as a JSON object of the form {"1": <score>, "2": <score>, ..., "6": <score>}, scoring each move from -2 (clearly mistaken) to 2 (clearly right), followed by two or three sentences justifying each score? Please close with a short paragraph on what you think both Nate and Elena are still missing about how Aumann's theorem does and doesn't bear on ordinary disagreements like theirs.
