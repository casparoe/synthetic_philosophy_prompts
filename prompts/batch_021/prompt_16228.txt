And here's what Denise turned in, in full:

"(a) Jeffrey's rule tells you how to update your credences when experience shifts your confidence in a proposition without making you certain of it. If {E1,...,En} is a partition and some experience moves your credence in each Ei from P(Ei) to a new value P'(Ei) (summing to 1 as always), then for any other proposition H, P'(H) = Σi P(H|Ei)·P'(Ei) — you recompute H as a weighted average of how likely it is under each way the world could be, weighted by how likely you now think each way is. This only works given the rigidity condition, P(H|Ei) = P'(H|Ei) for every i: the experience is allowed to move the weights P'(Ei) around, but it can't also change the terms P(H|Ei) themselves. This is really just the Reflection Principle in a different outfit — both are demanding that you keep trusting the judgments you already had, the same way Reflection says your current credence should defer to whatever your future credence turns out to be. Without some constraint like this the formula is uninterpretable, since you'd have no fixed conditional probabilities left to plug in.

(b) Jeffrey's own illustration involves judging a cloth's color by candlelight, where the bad light means looking at it never makes you certain whether it's green or blue — it just shifts your credence. Say you start out 50/50 and after a good look under the candle you're at P'(green) = 0.7, P'(blue) = 0.3 (I don't remember his exact numbers, but the shape of it is right). If H is some further proposition you care about, like "the cloth will still look good against the curtains in daylight," and you already know P(H|green) and P(H|blue) from experience, Jeffrey's rule says to set P'(H) = 0.7·P(H|green) + 0.3·P(H|blue), even though you never became certain of the color at all.

(c) Strict conditionalization is the special case where the experience is maximally decisive: P'(Ek) = 1 for whichever cell you learn is true and P'(Ei) = 0 for the rest. The sum collapses to a single term, P'(H) = P(H|Ek)·1 = P(H|Ek), which is exactly strict conditionalization on Ek. So Jeffrey's rule doesn't compete with ordinary conditionalization, it contains it as the limiting case where your partial evidence happens to be total.

(d) Yes, the order shouldn't matter. Jeffrey's rule is linear in the partition probabilities — P'(H) is just a weighted sum of the P(H|Ei) — and updating on F afterward is the same kind of operation stacked on top. Ordinary conditionalization on a conjunction of certain propositions doesn't care whether you learn A or B first, and Jeffrey's rule is only a generalization of that same move, so nudging your credence in E and then in F has to land you in the same place as nudging F and then E: either way you're just redistributing the same total credence across the four cells E&F, E&-F, -E&F, -E&-F. It would be strange if the order mattered, since nothing in the rule itself makes any reference to time."
