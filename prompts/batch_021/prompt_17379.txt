I'm about two-thirds of the way through drafting my third novel, and I've hit a snag with my writing group that I haven't been able to think my way out of. The book runs two interleaved timelines: present-day chapters follow Nora, a retired archivist cataloguing a dead diplomat's private papers, and shorter chapters made up of letters written thirty years earlier by a young dissident who signs herself only "Wren" and who was, according to the official record every other character accepts, killed in a border crossing that went wrong. For most of the novel, the people around Nora — her estranged son, a journalist trying to get her on the record, an old comrade of Wren's who turns up uninvited — treat her and Wren as two entirely separate women, one living and unremarkable, one dead and half-legendary. The hinge of my third act is that Nora is Wren: she faked the border-crossing death decades ago and has spent the rest of her life quietly being someone else, so that everyone who thought they were dealing with "just Nora" has, without knowing it, been dealing with Wren the entire time.

One of my beta readers — she has a philosophy background from her undergraduate years, before she moved into translation, which I think shows in how she's phrased this — says the reveal doesn't actually hold up once you look at it closely. Her argument is that if Nora and Wren are one person, then they were one person on page one, before anybody had found out anything, so no new fact comes into being at the moment of the reveal; strictly speaking, nobody in the story learns a new truth about the world, they simply receive information in a different order than the reader does. On her reading, the gasp I'm engineering in the third act is really just a mechanical effect of narration — withholding a label and then releasing it — rather than any character discovering something real, which she thinks makes the emotional weight I'm hanging on that scene somewhat unearned. I want to push back, since restructuring around her objection would mean giving up the two-timeline shape the whole book is built on, but every time I try to explain why the discovery is real and not just a trick of sequencing, I end up restating my conclusion instead of arguing for it. Is there an actual flaw in her reasoning, or has she put her finger on something I need to take more seriously than I have been?
