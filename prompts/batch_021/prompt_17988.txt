Over the last few months I've gotten mildly obsessed with prediction markets as a side project: I wrote a small bot that scans Manifold and Polymarket for contracts that look mispriced relative to my own estimates, mostly for fun rather than profit. That rabbit hole eventually led me to Robert Aumann's "agreeing to disagree" result and the Milgrom–Stokey no-trade theorem, neither of which I'd ever encountered despite years of writing trading-adjacent code. Both seem to say, roughly, that fully rational agents reasoning from the same starting point shouldn't be able to knowingly disagree, and shouldn't want to make speculative trades with each other at all — which is a strange thing to learn right as I'm building a tool whose entire premise is that such trades are worth making. I wrote up my own attempt to work through the puzzle, mostly to pin down my own thinking, and I'd like a second opinion before I keep citing it to my (extremely patient) friends. Here's roughly what I have:

Markets Shouldn't Work (and Yet)

Aumann's 1976 result is the foundation: if two Bayesian agents share a common prior, and their posterior probabilities for some event are common knowledge — not just known to each other, but known to be known, and known to be known to be known, and so on without end — then those posteriors have to be identical. You cannot rationally "agree to disagree" under those conditions, because the mere fact that the other person holds a different number is itself evidence you're obligated to fold into your own estimate, and vice versa, and this mutual updating converges on a single shared number before either of you has said anything substantive.

Milgrom and Stokey pushed this into markets in 1982. If it's common knowledge that a proposed trade is voluntary for both sides — each party believes it's advantageous, and each knows the other believes this — then rational traders with a common prior are in exactly the situation above. The offer to trade is itself informative: if I'm willing to sell you a contract at a given price, and you know I'm rational and know what you know, my willingness should make you more suspicious rather than more eager, and symmetrically for me. This unwinds any purported gain from trading on private information alone. Pure speculation should not occur; only trades motivated by something other than differing information (differing risk tolerance, say) survive.

This seems obviously false of real markets, prediction markets most of all, whose entire premise is betting against other people's private beliefs, and where volume is enormous. (There's a related but separate paradox, Grossman and Stiglitz's, about how perfectly efficient prices would destroy anyone's incentive to pay for information in the first place — but that's not quite the puzzle I'm after here.) The standard escapes seem to be: (1) real traders don't actually share a common prior, so the argument never gets off the ground; (2) people trade for non-informational reasons — hedging, liquidity, taxes, entertainment; (3) "common knowledge" in the full, infinitely-iterated sense the theorems need never actually holds between two humans, so the result is true but simply doesn't bind us.

None of these feels fully satisfying to me. Escape (1) seems to prove too much: if differing priors are always a legitimate, cost-free way out, it's hard to see what's left of the intuition that persistent disagreement between smart, well-informed people is sometimes itself evidence that somebody is making a mistake — an intuition I'm not ready to give up. Robin Hanson, who has spent much of his career designing and advocating for prediction markets, has a paper arguing that uncommon priors require "origin disputes": that two agents who agree about the causal history of how each of them arrived at their beliefs should, if fully rational, end up at the same prior. That seems to bite hard against escape (1), but I came away from it unsure whether Hanson thinks this makes all persistent disagreement irrational, or leaves room for some honest kind. Escape (3), meanwhile, seems to prove too little: it renders the whole apparatus unfalsifiable, since any observed trade can always be blamed on some unspecified failure of common knowledge at some unspecified level. Where I keep landing is something like: prediction markets are epistemically useful not because traders satisfy the conditions of these theorems, but precisely because they don't — the market's value comes from a persistent, mutually-known-to-be-imperfect form of disagreement, not from anything like Aumannian convergence. I can't tell if that's a real position with a name I haven't come across, or just me talking my way out of a result I find viscerally uncomfortable.

I'd appreciate comments on a few things in particular: whether I've actually stated the two theorems correctly, or I'm quietly fudging the common-knowledge condition somewhere to make the puzzle come out cleaner than it should; whether there's a standard treatment of this tension in the philosophy-of-economics or social-epistemology literature that I'm reinventing badly rather than engaging with; whether my closing suggestion about the source of markets' epistemic value actually holds together as a position or is more hand-wave than argument; and, if you have a view, whether this is worth developing into something longer or is a well-worn point I'm arriving at late.
