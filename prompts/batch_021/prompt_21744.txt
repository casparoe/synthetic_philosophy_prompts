Something came up at a barbecue two weekends ago that's been stuck in my head since, even though I've got zero philosophy background (im a backend engineer, closest I get to philosophy at work is arguing whether a null check counts as defensive programming or just paranoia). My friend Raf quit drinking cold turkey about three years ago, after it wrecked a relationship and nearly got him fired, and he's stayed sober through a divorce and a layoff since, which everyone agrees is genuinely hard. Raf wasn't even there that night, but two other friends of mine, Dana and Theo, got into a surprisingly intense back-and-forth about how much credit he actually deserves for staying sober, and I honestly can't tell who's right.

It went roughly like this. (1) Dana said Raf deserves a huge amount of credit, maybe more than for almost anything else he's done, because he kept actively choosing not to drink, over and over, when caving would've been so much easier. (2) Theo pushed back that the very ability to make that choice isn't something Raf is responsible for either — it's downstream of his temperament, his upbringing, who happened to be around to support him, brain chemistry, whatever, none of which he picked, so crediting him for it is basically like crediting him for being tall. (3) Dana came back with two things: first, that this proves way too much, since by that logic nobody could ever be praised for anything, which seems obviously false; and second, that Raf still visibly chose, moment to moment, not to pick up a bottle, and isn't that in-the-moment choosing exactly what we mean by credit anyway? (4) Theo said fine, bite the bullet on the first part — praise and blame are still useful going forward for encouraging good behavior, but "deserving" it in some deeper sense is an illusion — and on the second part, that pushing things down to "the moment" doesn't actually help, because whatever Raf did in that moment was still just the output of whatever state and character he happened to carry into it, which he didn't choose any more than the longer-term stuff either. Chase it back as far as you like, Theo said, and you'll never land on a point where he made himself from scratch.

Neither of them budged, and I walked away basically agreeing with whoever had spoken most recently, which probably just means I don't understand the argument well enough yet. It also gave me a weird sense of deja vu, like Theo was reciting somebody's actual, named position in philosophy rather than just riffing over beers. Could you go turn by turn through (1) to (4) and say who's got the better of it at each step, not just who wins overall — and flag anywhere one of them is leaning on a premise that's shakier than it sounds, or skipping a move the other side should've made?
