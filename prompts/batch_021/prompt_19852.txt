Two colleagues on our department's graduate admissions committee independently scored the same borderline applicant's file on our usual 1-10 scale, each blind to the other's number. Renata gave it an 8, based mainly on a strong personal statement, while Devon gave it a 3, citing a methodological weakness he believed the letters suggested. Both are experienced readers with comparable track records, and neither had any prior reason to think the other careless or biased. The following exchange ensued when they compared notes.

(1) Renata said that since they were equally qualified readers who had seen the identical file, the five-point gap was itself a reason for each of them to move toward the other's number rather than simply keep their own.

(2) Devon answered that this cannot be right as a general policy: if one of their two scores is in fact the accurate read of the file, averaging only guarantees that the committee ends up further from it, and the bare fact of disagreement does not tell you which score that is.

(3) Renata replied that this is exactly why splitting the difference is called for: neither of them can show, from the inside, that their own score is the accurate one, and if either of them could show this, they would not be describing each other as equally qualified readers in the first place.

(4) Devon said he was not claiming to be the more careful reader in general -- only that, on this file, he had reread the third letter twice and found that it identified a specific weakness in the candidate's proposed methodology that Renata's summary had not mentioned. That, he said, is a concrete, first-order reason to weight his score more heavily here, and not the sort of unfalsifiable claim to superior judgment that Renata could just as easily make about herself.

They left the meeting still five points apart. Please go through moves (1) through (4) and say who has the better of each individual exchange, rather than simply declaring an overall winner. Then offer your own verdict: how should Renata and Devon have handled their disagreement, and is there a consideration about how epistemic peers should handle disagreement that neither of them raises?
