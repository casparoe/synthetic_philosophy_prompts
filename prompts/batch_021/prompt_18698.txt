I write about science and ideas for a living, and I've spent the past few weeks reporting a piece on the odd grammar pundits fall into on election night, hedging between sentences like 'if the western counties haven't reported yet, the race is closer than it looks' and 'if the western counties hadn't been so slow to report, we'd have called this hours ago.' That project has left me newly alert to conditional sentences, which is probably why I couldn't stop turning over an argument two friends of mine, Kai and Renata, had at dinner last month, sparked by something that had actually happened in their house.

Kai and Renata share a house with famously bad wiring, and they already knew, from experience, that the ground-floor circuit blows if the kitchen is drawing full power -- oven and kettle both going -- while anyone upstairs also has a space heater running. On the night of their housewarming, the circuit duly blew mid-toast, killing the lights and the fridge -- and with it several days of party food -- for six hours, until a neighbor turned up a spare fuse. Comparing notes afterward, everyone worked out that their housemate Deb had been the only one with a heater on that night; the other housemates were all downstairs at the party with their own heaters off. Weeks later, over dessert, Kai, who is finishing a PhD in semantics, pointed out that two sentences about that night -- one a counterfactual, the other what he called a plain indicative conditional -- pull in opposite directions even though they seem to be two ways of asking the same thing: (A) 'If Deb hadn't tripped the circuit, someone else's heater would have,' which struck both of them as obviously false, since no one else's heater was even on, and (B) 'If Deb didn't trip the circuit, someone else's heater did,' which struck both of them as obviously true, given that they knew for certain the circuit had blown and knew it could only blow that way. Renata's first move was that the two sentences simply aren't competing for the same job: (B), she said, isn't really a claim about how the evening could have gone differently at all -- it's a piece of reasoning from what they already knew had happened, working backward from the blown fuse to its cause -- whereas (A) genuinely does ask you to rewind the tape and picture Deb behaving differently, and in that rewound scene there's no reason for anybody else's heater to switch itself on. Kai accepted the distinction but pushed back: if (B) isn't really about what would have happened, only about what they were entitled to infer from their evidence, then its truth seems to depend on whose evidence is in play -- someone who had merely heard a rumor that the lights went out, rather than having sat in the dark themselves, wouldn't be entitled to assert (B) with the same confidence, which makes it a strange kind of 'true' for an ordinary-looking declarative sentence to have. Renata answered that this is simply what sentences like (B) are like, and not a defect: plenty of everyday claims are tied to the speaker's evidence without being any less assertable for it, and the real mistake is assuming (A) and (B) answer one shared question, when they actually answer two different ones, so neither undermines the other. Kai wasn't persuaded, and ended by pointing out that sentences shaped like (B) get used constantly in forecasting, law, and diagnosis -- 'if the smoke alarm wasn't faulty, something else on that floor started the fire' -- in contexts where speakers clearly mean to be tracking the actual world rather than confessing their own state of mind, so relabeling (B) as evidence-relative rather than world-directed just renames the puzzle instead of dissolving it.

Neither of them budged, and it's been nagging at me since, well past the point where I can justify it as research. Could you work through the exchange move by move -- Kai's opening observation, Renata's first reply, Kai's follow-up, and Renata's answer to that -- and say who has the better of it at each stage, rather than just handing down an overall winner? I'd also like to know whether there's a way of reading (A) and (B) on which they both come out true, or both come out false, that neither of them considered -- and if not, what larger point about conditionals you think the two of them are missing.
