"The hole argument traces back to Einstein, who ran into it around 1913-1915 while working out general relativity with Grossmann. It actually held him up for a couple of years -- he briefly convinced himself that generally covariant field equations couldn't be right because of it, before talking himself out of the worry with what's called the point-coincidence argument (roughly, that only coincidences of point-events, like where two worldlines cross, are physically meaningful, so the two solutions agree on everything observable). That let him go back to generally covariant equations and finish GR in late 1915. Earman and Norton picked the argument back up in 1987 as a challenge to substantivalism. The setup: take a chunk of spacetime H that's empty of matter, and a solution (M, g) to the field equations. Because GR is generally covariant, you can relabel the coordinates just inside H, leaving everything outside H alone, and this gives you a new metric g* that's also a solution to the same equations. If you're a substantivalist -- you think the points of the manifold have their own identity independent of what fields live on them -- then (M,g) and (M,g*) count as two different physical possibilities, since the same points end up with different metric properties. But both possibilities agree on absolutely everything up to and including the boundary of H, so nothing about the earlier state of the world fixes which of the two happens inside H. That means, if substantivalism is true, GR turns out to be radically indeterministic, for no real physical reason -- just because of how we're individuating points. Substantivalists have tried to escape this with a move called sophisticated substantivalism, which was actually Earman and Norton's own proposed way of saving substantivalism: you keep the manifold as real but deny that diffeomorphic models represent distinct physical possibilities, similar to how relationalists already treat shifted/symmetric descriptions as physically equivalent."
