"Leibniz's Principle of the Identity of Indiscernibles (PII) says that if two things share literally all the same properties, they must actually be one and the same thing - there can't be two genuinely distinct objects that are completely alike in every way. (This is different from the 'indiscernibility of identicals,' which just says a thing shares all its properties with itself - that direction is trivial and nobody denies it; PII is the interesting, controversial direction, and the two get mixed up a lot.) Max Black attacks PII with a thought experiment: imagine a universe containing nothing at all except two spheres, and the spheres are exactly alike in every way - same size, same mass, same material - arranged perfectly symmetrically, so there's nothing else in the universe you could use as a landmark to tell them apart. He writes it as a dialogue between two people arguing back and forth. His point is that we can clearly describe and conceive of this situation, and in it there are two spheres, not one, even though there's no property sphere A has that sphere B lacks. So PII can't be a necessary truth - at best it might happen to be true of our actual world, but logic alone doesn't guarantee it.

One objection - which Black actually raises and settles himself within the dialogue - is that the spheres aren't fully indiscernible after all, because each one is some fixed distance from the other, and nothing is that same distance from itself, so there IS technically a relation that tells them apart, just not a quality. Black has the PII-defending character use this to show the principle survives just fine once you let relations count as properties, which is basically why most later objections to Black's argument don't hold up."
