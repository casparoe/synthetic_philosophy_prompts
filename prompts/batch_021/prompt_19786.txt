A few months ago I fell down a rabbit hole reading about objections to Giulio Tononi's Integrated Information Theory of consciousness, and one has stayed with me: Scott Aaronson's blog post about what he called the "unconscious expander." As I understood it, he showed that a simple, static network of interconnected logic gates, wired the way certain error-correcting codes are, could be assigned an enormous Φ — IIT's measure of integrated information, usually just called "phi" — far beyond a human brain's, despite doing nothing anyone would call intelligent, adaptive, or even mildly interesting. Tononi's side did not back away from the theory; they accepted the implication, since IIT ties experience to a system's intrinsic cause-effect structure rather than to its behavior, communication, or intelligence. Maybe it's because so much of my day job involves arguing about which parts of a system are actually load-bearing, but that conclusion unsettles me more than any story about a chatbot insisting it's conscious.

I'd like a short story, around 1000 words, built on this premise: someone has to live with a result like that once the calculation has actually been run on a real piece of hardware. Please avoid the "the machine wakes up and asks not to be shut off" plot — the whole point of the scenario is a system that, by the theory's own logic, may have a rich inner life while being constitutionally incapable of expressing, reporting, or acting on any of it. I'd rather the story inhabit that specific discomfort than resolve into a tidy verdict on whether IIT itself is true.
