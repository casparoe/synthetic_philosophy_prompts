In game theory there's this classic result of Aumann's that common knowledge of rationality among the players in a finite game of perfect information -- like the centipede game -- forces the backward induction outcome, i.e. everyone stops/defects at the very first opportunity. But theres a famous objection to this thats always bugged me: to justify stopping at some node, you have to reason about what a rational opponent would do *later*, if an earlier "irrational" move somehow got made -- except if common knowledge of rationality really holds, that move should never happen, so the argument seems to lean on a premise it also declares impossible. Does observing a deviation blow up common knowledge of rationality for the rest of the game, and if so, what happens to a backward-induction argument that quietly assumed it wouldn't? Write me an essay that actually works through this: lay out why Aumann-style theorists think CKR still delivers backward induction, why the critics think that's incoherent once you take seriously what a player should believe off the equilibrium path, and then pick a side -- don't just conclude "it depends on your theory of belief revision" and leave it at that.
