I spent close to four decades as a field entomologist, a good part of it watching colonies of ants and paper wasps go about their business, and it is only in retirement that I have begun reading systematically in the philosophy of biology. One passage that has stayed with me is Darwin's own treatment, in the Origin, of what he treated as a serious difficulty for his theory: the existence of elaborately adapted, entirely sterile worker castes in social insects. Since a sterile worker leaves no offspring of her own, he could not explain her adaptations by selection acting on her individually, so he resolved the problem by proposing that selection could act instead on the family, or on the community as a whole, favoring a lineage on account of traits that appear only in members who never themselves reproduce.

It occurred to me recently to wonder how Darwin might have positioned himself in the twentieth-century debates that grew directly out of this very puzzle, conducted in a conceptual vocabulary he never possessed. On one side stands Hamilton's formalization of inclusive fitness and kin selection, together with his suggestion that the unusual genetics of bees, ants, and wasps — where full sisters can end up more closely related to one another than a mother is to her own daughters — helps explain why sterile worker castes evolved so readily in precisely the insects Darwin was puzzling over; not far off stands Dawkins's gene's-eye view, on which the organism is little more than a disposable "vehicle" and the gene the real beneficiary of selection. On the other side stands the multi-level selection theory associated with Sober and Wilson, who insist that selection acting on groups is a distinct causal process in its own right, and not simply a relabeling of selection on individuals or genes. When I raised the question over dinner with two former colleagues, one of them dismissed it as hopelessly anachronistic; I am not so sure, given that Darwin's own instinct, faced with essentially this problem, was already to relocate the relevant unit upward, toward the family or community, rather than downward, toward some hereditary particle.

Given that instinct, which side of the modern dispute do you think Darwin would ultimately have found more congenial, and why? I would be especially interested in whether his reasoning about the sterile castes commits him, on reflection, more deeply to one camp than a first glance suggests, and in whether there is anything in the terms of the present-day debate — the very idea of a gene as a "self-interested" unit, for instance — that you suspect he would have resisted altogether.
