Question 3 (10 pts): "Coherence arguments" hold that an agent whose preferences violate the axioms behind expected utility theory can be exploited by a sequence of trades that leaves it strictly worse off, and some people use this to argue that advanced AI systems will end up acting like expected-utility maximizers. (b) [5 pts] Rohin Shah has argued that this kind of coherence argument doesn't, on its own, establish that an advanced AI will be "goal-directed" in the sense that actually matters for AI risk. Explain his point, using an example.

Student answer: "(a) Say an agent strictly prefers A to B, B to C, and C to A. A money-pump exploits this: give it C, then offer to trade C for B plus a small fee (accepted, since B > C), then offer B for A plus a fee (accepted, A > B), then offer to trade A back for C plus a fee (accepted, C > A). It's now back where it started, minus three fees, and you can loop this forever to drain arbitrary amounts of money from it. This is basically a Dutch Book argument: your preferences have to satisfy the four VNM axioms -- completeness, transitivity, continuity, and the sure-thing principle -- or a bookie can set things up so they're guaranteed to profit off you no matter what happens. (b) Shah's point is that a coherence theorem only shows an agent's behavior CAN be represented as maximizing some utility function, and pretty much any behavior can be given that kind of representation after the fact -- his example is a thermostat, which you could describe as "maximizing" a function that's highest at the target temperature, but which obviously isn't pursuing an ambitious goal across new situations the way a dangerous AI would need to. So getting from "has coherent preferences" to "will ruthlessly pursue some real-world objective" takes an extra argument that coherence alone doesn't hand you."
