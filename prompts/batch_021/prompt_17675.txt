I'm about two-thirds into a novel with a subplot set in Prague around 1610, and one of my side characters is a loosley-fictionalized Kepler -- mathematician, casts horoscopes for the court to help keep the household fed, the whole deal. There's a scene (don't ask about the structure, its a time-slip thing and my writing group already side-eyes me for it) where a modern character shows him her phone: one of those astrology apps that calculates your exact natal chart down to the minute from your real birth data and then still tells you "Scorpios are secretly soft" or whatever. I don't want him to react like the two cartoon versions of this I keep running into in other people's books and shows -- either delighted medieval mystic, or proto-scientist rolling his eyes at silly fortune-telling -- because from everything I've read, that's not actually where the real guy landed.

From what I understand he spent real effort trying to thread a needle between the astrologers and the debunkers of his own day (there's a whole treatise, Tertius Interveniens, that's basically him telling both camps to calm down), defending a version of astrology grounded in his own harmonic theory of planetary aspects while dismissing most of the zodiac-sign personality stuff as baseless superstition -- all while being the exact same guy who cared enormously that the underlying planetary data actually be accurate, to the point of spending years of his life on the Rudolphine Tables partly to fix that problem. Could you reconstruct that actual, specific position for me (not "some people back then believed X," his own reasons) and then extend it, in his voice if you can manage it, to how he'd genuinely respond to an app like that -- finally handed better data than he ever had, in service of exactly the kind of generic sign-based reasoning he always thought was nonsense?
