So a couple of people in my philosophy reading group got into a whole side-argument last week about Frege, the founder-of-modern-logic guy. We'd just learned that late in life (1924ish, the year before he died) he kept a private diary that only surfaced after his death, and it's full of hard-right, antisemitic stuff -- wanting Jews stripped of political rights, contempt for the Weimar parliament, wanting a strongman to fix Germany. Apparently even Michael Dummett, who did more than anyone to build Frege's reputation as the father of analytic philosophy, admitted decades later that finding out about it genuinely rattled him.

(1) Owen's take: this is just biography and beside the point. Frege's actual contributions -- the sense/reference split, the definiton of number, the whole campaign against psychologism -- stand or fall on logical grounds alone. A proof doesn't get worse because the guy who found it was a bigot, and if we start tossing every philosopher with ugly personal views out of the canon there's nothing left.

(2) Nadia agreed that's true of any specific argument (Basic Law V collapses either way, diary or no diary), but said there's a real, non-coincidental link underneath: Frege spent his career insisting logical truth is objective, not up for grabs by individual minds or majority opinion, and that same impulse -- distrust of the "merely subjective," wanting one correct order that isn't decided by counting heads -- shows up again in his contempt for parliamentary democracy and his wish for a leader who just discerns the right answer instead of voting on it. Same cast of mind, not two unrelated facts in separate drawers.

(3) Owen called that a pun on the word "objective" dressed up as an argument: plenty of committed anti-psychologists (Bolzano, Husserl) were nowhere near authoritarian, and plenty of authoritarians had zero view on logic, so the "rhyme" carries no real evidential weight.

(4) Nadia's comeback: it doesn't need to hold across a whole population to be a legit observation about *this* particular thinker. Noticing that the same vocabulary and temperament recur across one person's logic and their politics is a totally normal move in intellectual history -- it's exactly how people read Heidegger's jargon in light of his Nazism -- and nobody calls that move a pun.
