For a novel I am drafting, I want a grieving character to reason her way out of time itself, in the spirit of Borges's essay "A New Refutation of Time." Borges builds his case there by extending two borrowed arguments: Berkeley's denial that matter exists apart from being perceived, and Hume's account of the self as nothing but a loose, discontinuous bundle of perceptions with no substance underneath. If neither matter nor the self needs a persisting substance behind its appearances, Borges reasons, then time should not need one either, so that each present moment stands on its own, with nothing properly called "duration" binding it to the next. Please compare Borges's refutation of time with the Berkeleyan and Humean idealism it draws on. In what specific ways is his argument more radical than anything Berkeley or Hume themselves were willing to propose, and how should I read the essay's closing reversal, in which Borges seems to concede that he cannot, after all, stop feeling himself and the world to be stubbornly real and situated in time? Please justify your reading by engaging with the actual structure of the argument, not just its mood.
