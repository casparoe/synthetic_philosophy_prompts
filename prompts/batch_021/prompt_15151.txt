Been down a rabbit hole this week re-reading old Distill circuits posts alongside some philosophy of mind stuff, and I've got a question that a friend and I couldn't settle over drinks (he's very into the Dennett side of things, I'm less sure). When Anthropic says they "found" a Golden Gate Bridge feature in Claude via an SAE, or when a probe pulls a board-state representation out of Othello-GPT's residual stream (turns out it's linear after all if you use "my color/their color" instead of "black/white", per Neel Nanda's follow-up to the original paper), or when people invoke the linear representation hypothesis to explain why activation steering vectors work -- is that discovering a representation that was really there all along, the way a neuroscientist discovers that grid cells represent position? Or is "representation" doing the same job Dennett's intentional stance does: a convenient gloss that predicts behavior well without there being a principled fact about whether the network really represents anything?

What makes this thornier than the standard 90s eliminativism-vs-representationalism fight is that we've now got very concrete, architecture-specific reasons to suspect the whole framing might be confused. Superposition and polysemanticity mean loads of "features" are smeared across neurons in ways that make the feature/neuron split look kind of arbitrary to begin with. Probing results are notoriously sensitive to how expressive you let the probe be -- a strong enough probe will "find" a representation of almost anything, including stuff that plausibly isn't used downstream at all. And causal-intervention methods like activation patching or distributed alignment search only tell you a representation is "real" relative to a causal abstraction you had to pick in advance, which feels uncomfortably close to begging the question. People on LW/AF toss around "the model represents X" as unproblematic shorthand constantly, but I don't think its actually obvious what would have to be true for that sentence to be correct rather than just a useful fiction.

So: can you write me an essay that actually takes a stand on this instead of retreating to "it depends what you mean by representation"? Lay out the strongest case for interpretability realism (representations as discovered, not invented) and the strongest deflationist/instrumentalist case, engage properly with the superposition, probe-expressiveness, and causal-abstraction complications above rather than waving them away, and then tell me where you actually land and why -- including whether your verdict changes depending on whether we're talking about a single neuron, a whole circuit, or an SAE-derived feature direction.
