I've been part of an AI safety reading group where we worked through Yudkowsky's old writeup on "coherent extrapolated volition," and around the same time my metaethics seminar covered Peter Railton's account of a person's good from his "Moral Realism" paper -- basically, that what's good for you is whatever a fully-informed, idealized version of you (one who knows all the relevant facts and can vividly imagine their consequences) would want for the actual, non-idealized you. It struck me that these two moves are doing something very similar, grounding normativity in extrapolated/idealized desires rather than the messy actual ones, just at wildly different scales: Railton for a single person, CEV for humanity as a whole.

Could you compare the two in some depth, rather than just noting they're both "idealized preference" views? I'm curious especially about: where the idealization procedures actually diverge (CEV's talk of extrapolating what we'd want if we "knew more, thought faster, and were more the people we wished we were" sounds like it's idealizing values/character and not just correcting for missing facts, wheras Railton keeps the idealized advisor's fundamental values fixed and only pumps in information and imaginative vividness); whether aggregating across a whole population, rather then idealizing one person, introduces genuinely new problems for CEV or just multiplies Railton's existing ones; and whether the standard objections to full-information theories of well-being (e.g. that your idealized self might basically be a different person with different cares, so why should it get "authority" over you, or that the outcome depends on the arbitrary order you feed in the idealizing information) carry over to CEV, and whether doing this for an entire civilization makes those problems worse or somehow easier to dodge. I don't want a neutral "here are the similarities and differences" -- actually stake out a view on which of the two projects is in better philosophical shape, and defend it.
