A friend and I nearly missed our dinner reservation last night because we could not let go of an argument about the centipede game. She maintained that Robert Aumann's result on common knowledge of rationality settles the matter decisively: if it is common knowledge among the players that each is rational, then a population of such players could never climb even the first rung of the ladder, and the intuition that they would both do better by continuing for a while is really just a comment on human psychology, irrelevant to what ideally rational agents would do. I objected that the proof only goes through by treating rationality as a property a player satisfies at every node of the tree, including nodes that, on the very assumption that rationality is common knowledge, could never actually be reached. So once a player finds herself at, say, the sixth node, wondering what to do, is it not the case that the very assumption behind the backward-induction prediction has already been falsified by the fact of her being there? And if so, what is she now entitled to infer about her opponent's future play, given that "common knowledge of rationality" has, in some sense, just broken down along that branch of the tree?

Neither of us could state cleanly whether this is a genuine gap in the argument or one that has already been closed. I have a hazy memory of theorists such as Binmore or Reny pressing exactly this kind of worry, and of Aumann having some reply that turns on a more careful, dispositional or counterfactual notion of rationality, but I could not reconstruct it precisely enough to change my friend's mind, and she was equally unable to state Aumann's actual argument beyond "common knowledge of rationality implies backward induction." Could you lay out, with some precision, what is really at stake here: what notion of rationality Aumann's theorem needs in order to go through, why that notion strikes critics as question-begging (or otherwise suspect) once it is applied to nodes that are off the backward-induction path, and whether the dispute has a settled resolution or is still regarded as a live tension in the epistemic foundations of game theory?
