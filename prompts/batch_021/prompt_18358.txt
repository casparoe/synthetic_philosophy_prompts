I've been reading through Descartes's correspondence for a seminar on early modern philosophy of mind, and I've run into a passage that sits awkwardly with everything I thought I understood about his view of animals. The textbook story, based mainly on Part V of the Discourse on the Method, is that non-human animals are elaborate automata: whatever they do, however skillfully, is produced entirely by the arrangement of their bodily organs, with no accompanying inner life, and the tell-tale sign of this is their total lack of genuine language — as opposed to the natural, instinct-driven cries and gestures that even the dullest human beings, and deaf-mutes using signs, can supplement with real speech, but that no animal ever manages.

In a letter to Henry More dated 5 February 1649, though, Descartes runs through essentially this same argument and then adds a qualification that I can't fit into that picture. Having said that the absence of speech is his chief reason for denying thought to animals, he immediately draws a line between thought, on one side, and life and sensation, on the other. Life, he says, is nothing more than the heat present in the heart; and of sensation he says: "I don't deny sensation, in so far as that depends on a part of the body." He then draws the moral that his view is meant less to spare animals than to spare human beings from guilt, since it removes any suspicion of wrongdoing in killing or eating them.

What I can't work out is what to make of this. Elsewhere, in the Meditations and the Principles, Descartes treats "thought" (cogitatio) as a broad umbrella that explicitly covers sensing and feeling right alongside pure intellection and willing, so that on his official picture, to sense something just is to think, in his technical sense of the word — which seems to leave no room for a category of merely bodily "sensation" once thought has been subtracted from a creature. So which reading of the letter is right: (a) is Descartes quietly granting that animals do have some kind of inner, felt experience, short of reason or self-awareness, which would make the popular idea that he denied animals could feel anything at all into something of a caricature; or (b) is "sensation… depending on a bodily organ" just his name for a purely mechanical process — organs reacting to stimuli and triggering suitable behavior — that carries no felt quality whatsoever, so that the concession to animals is merely verbal and the traditional "unfeeling machine" picture is left untouched? And if the answer is (b), how is that meant to square with his own official classification of sensing as a species of thought? I'd like to understand what's actually doing the work in this distinction, and how much it should change how we read Descartes's considered position on whether animals can suffer.
