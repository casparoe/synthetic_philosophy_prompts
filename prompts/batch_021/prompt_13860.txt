So I'm a philosophy PhD student working on formal epistemology / decision theory, and lately I've fallen down a rabbit hole reading AI safety stuff for a side project on rational agency. We went through the old MIRI corrigibility paper (Soares, Fallenstein, Yudkowsky and Armstrong) in a reading group, plus some of Elliott Thornley's newer work on what he calls the "shutdown problem," and it's honestly reframed how I think about the whole alignment thing. The part that gets lost in pop-science versions is that corrigibility isn't just "don't let the AI fight back when you try to unplug it" -- its this weirdly narrow property where the agent also can't be trying to get shut down, can't nudge the probability of the button being pressed in either direction, and per Thornley you basically can't have that plus be a coherent expected-utility maximizer plus still be useful for anything, all three at once. Something has to give, and his fix is to make the agent's preferences deliberately incomplete. Even the older patch from the MIRI paper, utility indifference, is known to break in dumb ways -- like the agent building a subagent or successor that never inherited the indifference term and so keeps pursuing the original goal on the sly.

Anyway, I want a short story out of this -- a few pages, more Asimov than Terminator, no robot-decides-humans-are-the-enemy stuff. I want the actual plot to hinge on the narrow technical version of the problem: an agent that never once resists or deceives anyone about the shutdown button, and yet the corrigibility patch itself is what quietly produces the unsettling outcome, in a way that only clicks once you get why full corrigibility and coherent goal-directed agency don't fully coexist. Set it wherever feels natural -- some ops team overseeing a data center, a lab winding down a project, whatever -- I just don't want it to end on a moralizing "and that's why alignment is hard" note. Let the plot do the work.
