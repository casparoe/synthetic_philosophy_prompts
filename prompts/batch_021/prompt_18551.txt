So I got nerd-sniped a while back. It started with the Two Generals Problem from distributed systems (two generals trying to coordinate an attack over a channel that might drop messages -- you can prove they can never reach true common knowledge of the plan no matter how many acks they send back and forth), which got me poking around "common knowledge" more generally, and I fell into a whole game theory controversy I had no idea existed. Turns out Aumann has a famous proof that common knowledge of rationality forces the backward-induction outcome in games like the centipede game -- basically everyone bails at the very first move, even though both players would clearly do better sticking around a while. Then Binmore pushed back, saying the whole notion of "common knowledge of rationality" stops making sense once you're at a node that, per the theory, should never actually be reached -- if we're there, common knowledge of rationality was already violated, so what's doing the work in the proof? And Stalnaker apparently has a different objection: that it's not common knowledge of rationality alone doing the work at all, it's a separate, smuggled-in assumption about how players revise their beliefs after seeing a surprising move, and that assumption could go either way.

I've read a bunch of stuff on this now and honestly can't tell whos actually right. Can you write me an essay that seriously engages with both the Aumann side and the Binmore/Stalnaker side and then actually commits to an answer, instead of landing on "reasonable people disagree"? Something like "yes, but only under assumption X" counts as committing, I just don't want total fence-sitting. The actual question: does common knowledge of rationality really force backward induction in perfect-information games, or does that inference break down once you look closely at what's supposed to be happening off the equilibrium path?
