For a dissertation chapter on hard choices, I want to pressure-test the parity thesis against its strongest rival before I commit to a view. Write a dialogue, set during the Q&A after a colloquium talk, between two philosophers who agree that a choice like the one between a career as a clarinetist and a career as a lawyer resists description as "better than," "worse than," or "equally as good as" its alternative, but who disagree sharply about what follows from this.

The first philosopher argues, in the spirit of Ruth Chang, that such cases reveal a fourth, sui generis value relation, "parity," since the trichotomy of better/worse/equal cannot survive the small-improvement argument. The second argues, in the spirit of John Broome, that positing a new relation is unmotivated, because the same phenomenon is already explained, more economically, by vagueness in the "better than" relation.

Center the exchange on the small-improvement argument itself: have the vagueness theorist try to show that a merely vague "better than" accommodates the case just as well as parity does, and have the parity theorist press back on whatever the vagueness view struggles hardest to explain. Neither should simply restate their opening position; each turn should engage with what the other just said, and neither should get the better of the exchange by default. Keep it to a page or so.
