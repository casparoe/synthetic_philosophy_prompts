I recently read Cicero's essay "On Old Age," in which he has the elderly Cato argue that almost everything people hold against getting old — being pushed out of important work, physical decline, a mind that supposedly isn't what it used to be — really comes down to the habits and character a person built up over a lifetime, rather than being an automatic result of the years themselves. He even has Cato, portrayed as sharp and active well into his eighties, insist that judgment and practical wisdom tend to improve with age rather than fade, and he makes a point of noting that the Roman senate took its very name from the word for "old man," since political judgment was traditionally seen as an old man's domain. This made me think about recent controversies over aging leaders: Dianne Feinstein's visible decline before she died in office, Mitch McConnell's on-camera freezing episodes, and the way Joe Biden's age and sharpness became a defining issue of the 2024 campaign, all of which revived talk of age limits or mental fitness tests for high office. Based on Cicero's actual reasoning in that essay, rather than the easy assumption that he'd simply say "respect your elders," would he end up defending officials like these, or does his own logic actually point toward something more like case-by-case scrutiny of who is still fit to serve, regardless of age?
