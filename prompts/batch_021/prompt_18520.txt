Having spent a career in evolutionary ecology before turning to philosophy in retirement, I find myself reading Hegel's Philosophy of Right through an occupational lens. In the Addition to §324, Hegel argues that periodic war preserves the "ethical health" of nations, comparing its function to the way turbulence keeps a body of water from the stagnation that a prolonged calm would produce — an analogy any student of disturbance ecology will recognize. Kant, in Perpetual Peace, seems to grant a structurally similar premise: nature's "guarantee" of peace works through the same unsociable sociability he describes in his essay on universal history, using war itself to scatter peoples across the globe, force them into lawful relations, and eventually make commerce — and with it peace — advantageous. Yet Kant treats this mechanism as a ladder to be discarded once states federate, while Hegel treats recurring conflict as a permanent feature of any world made up of genuinely sovereign states. Compare the two arguments and pinpoint exactly where this shared premise — that antagonism performs necessary historical work — forks into such different verdicts on whether lasting peace is even a coherent goal. Whose position is more consistent with its own account of sovereignty, and whose reasoning, if either, is doing real philosophical work rather than resting on the organic metaphor? Justify your answer.
