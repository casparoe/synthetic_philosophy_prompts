I run a tiny podcast about governance and mechanism design — started out mostly interviewing people from the DAO/crypto governance world, but lately I keep steering it toward "real" political philosophy because half of what we reinvent over there turns out to be some 200-year-old argument with worse vocabulary. Next episode I'm talking to a constitutional theorist whose new book defends judicial review (courts striking down laws as unconstitutional) as basically a Ulysses contract: the idea being that "We the People," in a sober founding moment, tied our own hands against the passions of future majorities, the same way Ulysses had his crew lash him to the mast so he could survive the Sirens' song. I've done enough reading to know this "precommitment" framing is a real, seriously defended position, and also that there's a solid tradition of people (Waldron, and I think even Elster himself in his later stuff) picking at the analogy pretty hard. I don't want to just lob her the softball "why is judicial review good, actually" and let her recite the book jacket for 40 minutes.

Could you draft me 8-10 interview questions that actually dig into the mechanics of the Ulysses analogy rather then just restating it? At minimum I want: one question that presses on the fact that Ulysses bound himself, while a constitution binds a totally different set of people decades or centuries later who never consented to anything (the "dead hand" problem); one built on the Waldron-ish point that reasonable disagreement about what rights even require isn't like being tempted by a Siren song, so it's not obvious there's a "sober self" being protected at all; and one that uses the uglier historical case of gag rules -- like the constitutional compromises over slavery, which arguably functioned as exactly this kind of agenda-clearing precommitment -- to ask whether "precommitment" is doing any real moral work here or just relabeling whatever deal got cut. Since a chunk of my audience is from the crypto side, I'd also like one question bringing up the 2016 DAO hack and hard fork as a modern case of a community overriding a supposedly immutable, "code is law" precommitment the second it got inconvenient, and asking what that should do to our confidence in constitutional rigidity being a good thing in itself. For each of these, throw in a quick follow-up keyed to whatever answer she's most likely to give to wriggle out of it.
