I'm a PhD student in cognitive science working on how people update beliefs under uncertainty, and this semester I've been sitting in on a formal epistemology seminar that spent several weeks on Dutch book arguments: the proofs that an agent whose credences at a single moment violate the probability axioms can be talked into a set of bets that guarantees a loss no matter what happens. I found these arguments genuinely persuasive, but they concern coherence within a single moment, and a situation in my own life has me wondering whether something similar is supposed to hold across time — a question I don't have the tools to answer on my own.

I recently accepted an offer from a large strategy consulting firm whose two-week "immersion" onboarding is something of a running joke among incoming analysts: partner talks structured as personal conversion stories, small-group "reflection" sessions every evening, and a closing ceremony where new hires say out loud what the firm's mission means to them. Everyone I know who has gone through it reports the same outcome, but the clearest case is a friend who spent business school loudly mocking the firm's fees and its talk of "transformational impact": by the end of the second week, she genuinely, unironically believed the fees were justified and the mission roughly as important as advertised. When I mentioned that I was dreading going in as the resident skeptic, she said: "You already know how this ends. Why spend two weeks being the annoying holdout when you're going to come out a believer anyway? Just adopt the belief now and skip the discomfort."

I don't have a clean answer to her. If I really am close to certain about what I'll believe two weeks from now, there does seem to be some pressure on what I believe today to already match it — which feels like the same kind of coherence the Dutch book arguments were pushing on, just stretched across time. But it also seems obviously wrong that I'd be rationally required to adopt a belief now just because some process is reliable at instilling it later, regardless of whether that process has anything to do with the belief's actually being true. I'd like help working out whether my friend's argument actually goes through. If it doesn't, I want to understand what exactly blocks it, without undermining the very reasoning that made the original Dutch book arguments compelling to me in the first place.
