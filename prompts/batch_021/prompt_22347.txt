Every rebellion has its leader, a man who by his boldness becomes the head of the movement and lends it a face, and even those who follow him are, in following, made to stand forth as themselves, each answerable for the side he has chosen. But the levelling of our age has no such head, and can have none: the man who set himself at the head of it, and thereby distinguished himself, would in that very moment escape being levelled, and become instead a leader — which is precisely what levelling cannot suffer. Levelling is therefore something which every individual can help forward, each in his own small circle, and which no one, not even the press, can be said to direct; it is not a conspiracy but an abstraction, and it required only that communal life should first have grown too thin to hold the individual in anything concrete, for the press to conjure out of that thinness its proper spirit: an unreal whole, at once everything and nothing, which we call the public.
