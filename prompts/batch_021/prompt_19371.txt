I've been working through Berkeley's *Querist* (1735–37) for a chapter on early modern theories of money, and I keep running into a disagreement in the secondary literature that I'd rather see argued out than summarized. Berkeley repeatedly suggests that money need not be a commodity with intrinsic worth at all: its "true idea," he says at one point, may be nothing more than that of a "ticket" or "counter," useful only for reckoning and transferring "power" — which he glosses as the power to command the industry of others. Yet the same tract worries constantly about credit issued without adequate backing, and ties Berkeley's proposed national bank to the land and industry of the nation rather than to specie. One reading of this, developed at length by Caffentzis, treats Berkeley's monetary nominalism as continuous with his immaterialism and his sign-based account of language: money, like a word, functions through convention and use, so nothing in Berkeley's own commitments rules out a purely fiat currency backed by nothing but public confidence. A rival reading holds that this over-intellectualizes a pamphlet written for Ireland's specie shortage, and that Berkeley's "power" is doing real, non-conventional work: a ticket must answer to the nation's actual productive capacity, on pain of the very speculative collapse he had already diagnosed in his 1721 essay on the South Sea Bubble.

Write a dialogue of eight to twelve exchanges between a proponent of each reading. Each side should press the other on its weakest point: the nominalist owes an account of what "backing" could even mean once money's value is granted to be conventional, while the pragmatist owes an account of what, in Berkeley's own commitments, sets a principled limit on paper issue if not an appeal to something non-conventional. Both interlocutors should be equally well versed in the text, and every turn should respond to the specific objection just raised rather than restating a prepared position.
