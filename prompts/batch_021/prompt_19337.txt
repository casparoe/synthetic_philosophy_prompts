I spent the better part of four decades as a condensed-matter physicist working on continuous phase transitions and renormalization-group methods, and since retiring I have been trying to fill some obvious gaps in my education by reading philosophy, currently working through a philosophy-of-mind anthology with a former colleague who has taken up the same hobby. We have just finished Hilary Putnam's papers on functionalism and the multiple realizability of mental states: the argument that a state such as pain can be instantiated in human neurons, in the very differently organised nervous system of an octopus, or (as later writers were fond of adding) in silicon-based hardware, and that this variability shows psychological kinds cannot be identical with any specific physical or neural kind, only with an abstract functional or causal-role kind that is indifferent to its implementing substrate. Putnam's fellow functionalist Jerry Fodor later generalised the point into the claim that the "special sciences" traffic in higher-level kinds and laws that are explanatorily autonomous from, and irreducible to, the physics of their underlying realizers.

Reading this, I could not help thinking of something much closer to my own former work: the phenomenon of universality in the theory of critical phenomena. A fluid at its liquid–vapour critical point and a uniaxial ferromagnet at its Curie point are built from utterly different microscopic constituents and forces, yet close to the critical point both flow under the renormalization group to the very same fixed point and end up with identical critical exponents (they belong, as it happens, to the same universality class as the three-dimensional Ising model): the microscopic details are, in the technical sense, "irrelevant," and only a couple of gross features — essentially the symmetry and dimensionality of the order parameter — fix the macroscopic behaviour. My colleague and I spent an entire afternoon disputing whether this is genuinely the same phenomenon as Putnam's multiple realizability, dressed in different clothes, or only a superficially similar one.

I would value your help settling the matter. Please compare Putnam-style multiple realizability, and the functionalist and Fodorian theses it is meant to support, with renormalization-group universality, as two accounts of why a higher-level kind can be explanatorily autonomous from its lower-level realizers. Where do the two genuinely run in parallel, and where do they come apart? In particular, universality classes come with a precise, principled criterion — fixed points, together with a clean division of perturbations into relevant and irrelevant ones — for exactly which microscopic differences wash out and which do not, whereas multiple realizability seems to offer nothing nearly so principled for deciding when two physical states count as "the same" functional state. Does that disanalogy matter as much as it seems to, or am I overrating the tidiness of my own field? I would be grateful for your honest, well-justified judgment as to which of the two — functionalism in philosophy of mind, or universality in statistical physics — rests on the firmer conceptual foundation.
