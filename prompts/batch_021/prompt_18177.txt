I'm a PhD candidate in philosophy working on formal social choice theory -- my current seminar paper is on Bayesian models of social aggregation -- and that background has landed me an odd assignment on my university's graduate research grant committee, which I joined this year as the student representative. Each cycle the committee awards roughly a dozen $12,000 grants out of around forty applications, and when two or more proposals land in an exact tie on the panel's composite score, the standing rule breaks the tie in favor of whichever applicant received the award least recently (or never). At our last meeting, a newer member proposed scrapping that rule in favor of an explicit weighted lottery among tied proposals, arguing this is the only honest way to treat applicants the panel has itself certified as equally deserving. Our chair, who has a background in welfare economics, pushed back with what she called "basically Harsanyi's aggregation theorem": since the committee's ranking of funding lotteries is transitive, satisfies the expected-utility axioms, and agrees with every panelist's preference whenever they're unanimous, the theorem guarantees that ranking is representable as maximizing a weighted sum of applicants' expected scientific value -- and by that measure, a coin flip and the status-quo tiebreaker come out worth exactly the same, so there is no principled reason to switch. The dissenting member, without knowing it, ended up channeling something very like the objection Peter Diamond raised against Harsanyi back in 1967: that treating two people with equal claims alike requires giving each an equal chance, not merely an equal expected payoff, and that a framework unable to distinguish a fair coin flip from a rule that happens to yield the same numbers has exposed a blind spot rather than settled anything.

Since I'm the one who introduced the theorem into the discussion, I've been asked to write the memo the committee will vote on before finalizing this cycle's awards. Please draft it. It should recommend one specific tie-break protocol -- the lottery, the status quo, or a third option, if you think a better one exists -- rather than calling for further study; it should explain clearly enough for non-specialists why Harsanyi's theorem does or does not actually settle the dispute; and it should present the losing side's case in its strongest form before explaining why it ultimately doesn't prevail. If you know how the wider ex-ante/ex-post literature this dispute belongs to has tried to resolve it, feel free to draw on that, but the memo should stand on its own reasoning rather than read as a literature survey.
