I'm a third-year cognitive science student in a seminar on philosophy of mind, where we recently worked through Clark and Chalmers's "The Extended Mind," the Otto-and-notebook case, and the standard replies to it: Adams and Aizawa's charge that the argument trades on a coupling-constitution fallacy, and Rupert's argument that biological memory and notebook-consultation aren't functionally alike enough to satisfy the parity principle even once it's granted in the abstract. A few of us have kept arguing about all this since Tuesday's class, which is partly why I'd like an outside opinion before I put anything in writing: I'm also the student member of my university's Examination Accommodations Appeals Board, and a case has come up on this week's agenda that reads as though it were lifted from the syllabus.

A student with a documented memory impairment from a traumatic brain injury (confirmed by neuropsychological assessment on file with Disability Services) already receives extra time and a quiet room, but has now requested something more specific: permission to consult, during an otherwise closed-book exam, a set of handwritten notes she says are continuous with a running notebook she has relied on all year for ordinary coursework, precisely because her memory doesn't reliably retain material otherwise. Citing Clark and Chalmers by name, her submission argues that the notes satisfy the very features the two of them use to distinguish real extended memory from mere lookup: she wrote and reviewed every page herself, she consults notes like these constantly and without deliberation in every other academic context, and she takes what's written there on trust rather than checking it against anything else. Two board members are unmoved. One thinks that even granting all of this, functional similarity to memory can't be what settles the case, since coupling reliably with a resource isn't the same as that resource being part of one's memory in the sense an exam is entitled to care about — essentially Adams and Aizawa's worry that coupling is being mistaken for constitution. The other points out, more practically, that our accommodations policy only guarantees adjustments that don't "fundamentally alter what the assessment measures," and thinks the case turns on a question philosophy of mind alone can't answer: what this particular closed-book exam is actually supposed to measure.

Could you draft the short memo I've been asked to prepare for Thursday's meeting? It should give the board a concrete recommendation — grant, deny, or grant subject to specific conditions and a review point — and it should take both the coupling-constitution worry and the "what does this exam measure" objection seriously on their own terms, rather than resolving the case by simply declaring the extended mind thesis true or false.
