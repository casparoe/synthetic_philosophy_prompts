After a career of roughly forty years as an experimental physicist working mostly on acoustics and wave phenomena, I retired a few years ago and have finally had the leisure to read philosophy properly, rather than in the stray evening here and there. A small reading group of former colleagues and I have been working slowly through Schopenhauer's The World as Will and Representation, and we have become rather stuck on a stretch of Book Three, section 52 — the long final section on music — which none of us can agree how to interpret, despite, or perhaps because of, our scientific backgrounds.

Schopenhauer's central claim in that section is that music differs from the other arts in a crucial respect: painting, sculpture, and poetry all present copies of the Platonic Ideas, the "grades" at which the will objectifies itself in nature, whereas music bypasses the Ideas altogether and offers instead, in his words, "a copy of the will itself," so that, as he puts it, the other arts "speak only of the shadow, but music of the essence." Having established this, however, he immediately complicates matters by drawing a fairly elaborate structural analogy between the voices of harmony and precisely the hierarchy of nature that music was just said to skip over: the bass, on his account, corresponds to inert, inorganic matter; the middle, filling voices correspond to the plant and animal kingdoms; and the melody proper — the single sustained voice that wanders through dissonance before resolving back to its keynote — corresponds to the deliberate, self-conscious striving of human beings.

What none of us in the group can settle is how seriously this second move is meant to be taken, and how it is supposed to sit alongside the first. If music genuinely bypasses the Ideas and copies the will directly, it seems odd that its internal architecture should then be mapped, register by register, onto exactly the hierarchy of Ideas he describes elsewhere in the book — the analogy seems almost to smuggle representation back in through the side door. Is this bass-to-melody correspondence generally read as doing real metaphysical work in his system, perhaps grounded in some principled rationale about the acoustically "crude," overtone-rich character of low tones as against the comparative purity of a single melodic line, or is it better treated as a looser illustrative flourish that he allows himself once the main argument is already secured? I would be glad of help thinking through what is actually going on in this part of the text, and whether the tension I believe I am seeing between the two claims is real, or simply a misreading on my part.
