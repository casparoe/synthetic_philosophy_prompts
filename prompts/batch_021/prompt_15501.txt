I'm a junior at my high school. Between an AP Literature unit on tragedy — we read Sophie's Choice and parts of Antigone — and my work on the school's Ethics Bowl team, I've become a bit obsessed with a question our coach raised almost as an aside: whether "genuine" moral dilemmas, in the strict philosophical sense, are even possible. She explained that a dilemma only counts as "genuine" if an agent is really and inescapably obligated to do two things that cannot both be done, with neither obligation simply overridden or cancelled by the other, and mentioned that some philosophers think this notion falls apart once you spell out its logical commitments. Looking into it afterward, I found that the argument seems to hinge on two principles: the "agglomeration principle" (if you ought to do A and separately ought to do B, then you ought to do A-and-B together) and "ought implies can" (you can only be obligated to do what is actually possible for you to do). Apparently Ruth Barcan Marcus and Bernard Williams resist this, pointing to the guilt, or "remainder," that agents in cases like Agamemnon's or Sartre's student (the one torn between caring for his mother and joining the Resistance) feel afterward as proof that a real obligation was left unmet, while others, more in the spirit of Alan Donagan, treat that guilt as a sign that the agent's moral code contained an error somewhere rather than as evidence of a genuinely unresolvable conflict.

I'd like to work through the strongest version of the argument that genuine moral dilemmas of this kind are impossible, partly because I suspect it's the more defensible position and partly because I want to be ready for it if my teammates push back with the Williams/Barcan Marcus line during a scrimmage. Could you argue, as rigorously as you can, that no agent can ever be simultaneously and inescapably bound by two obligations that cannot jointly be satisfied? Please work directly with the agglomeration and "ought implies can" principles instead of just asserting the conclusion, and be sure to explain away the residual guilt that Williams treats as decisive evidence against this view, ideally by engaging with the Agamemnon and Sartre cases directly rather than staying purely in the abstract.
