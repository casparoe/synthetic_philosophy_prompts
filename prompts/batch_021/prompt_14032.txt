Two weeks ago I served on a civil jury in a case that required the plaintiff to establish four separate elements (duty, breach, causation, and damages) by a preponderance of the evidence. During deliberation, most of us were willing to say, informally, that we were perhaps 80 to 90 percent confident in each element on its own. One juror with an engineering background pointed out that if these figures are taken at face value and simply multiplied together, the probability that all four elements hold simultaneously drops to somewhere near fifty percent, arguably right at, or below, the very threshold we had been instructed to apply. In other words, each of us could be individually confident of every element and yet, strictly speaking, not confident of the conjunction that the verdict actually required. It was a strange, slightly vertiginous moment, and in the end we returned a verdict for the plaintiff regardless, effectively tabling the objection rather than resolving it.

Since then the question has stayed with me, and some reading has led me back to two puzzles I had half forgotten from a college epistemology course. There is the lottery paradox, where it seems rational to believe of any single ticket that it will lose even though one is certain some ticket will win, and there is the preface paradox, where an author reasonably believes every sentence she has written, yet also, in her preface, reasonably believes that at least one of those sentences is false. A friend who stayed closer to philosophy than I did tells me the two are usually treated quite differently in the literature: the preface case, on her account, is often defused simply by denying that rational belief is closed under conjunction, while the lottery case is treated as posing a much deeper threat to what she called the "Lockean thesis" -- the idea that sufficiently high probability can by itself ground outright belief.

I would like your help working through this properly rather than simply taking her word for it. Specifically, how similar in structure are the two paradoxes actually, and is there a principled basis for treating them so asymmetrically, or is that asymmetry more a matter of philosophical convention than of anything intrinsic to the two cases? And, returning to my jury room, does either paradox, or the standard move of rejecting conjunction-closure, offer a good diagnosis of what was happening with our elementwise confidence estimates, or was our situation different enough, with subjective, testimony-based estimates rather than known objective frequencies, that neither puzzle quite applies?
