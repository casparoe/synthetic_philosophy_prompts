I'm working on a long-form piece for our ideas section on philosophical theories of what makes a life worth living, and for the past couple of weeks I've been stuck in the closing chapters of G. E. Moore's Principia Ethica. Moore's famous claim there is that nothing we can conceive is more valuable than personal affection between people and the contemplation of what is beautiful -- more valuable, he insists, than pleasure by itself or true belief by itself. That conclusion is easy enough to summarize for readers; what I can't yet explain clearly is the machinery Moore uses to argue for it, which rarely makes it into the popular summaries (most people remember only the "naturalistic fallacy" from his opening chapter and stop there).

What I'd like help with, specifically, is his notion of "organic unities." Moore holds that the value of a whole need not equal the sum of the values of its parts, and he leans on this to explain why consciously appreciating a friend's character, or a painting's beauty, is worth so much more than either the bare fact of the friend's or painting's good qualities, or the bare feeling involved, taken in isolation. I follow the slogan, but I don't have a real feel for why this particular combination -- true cognition plus a fitting emotional response -- should generate such an enormous surplus of value rather than a modest one. I'd also like to see how this fits with the separate thought experiment Moore runs elsewhere in the book, where he asks us to imagine a world of exquisite beauty and a world of sheer ugliness, neither one ever observed by anyone, and asks which state of affairs is better. My hunch is that this "isolation" test is doing different work -- establishing that beauty has some value at all, independently of anyone's pleasure -- rather than explaining why conscious appreciation of it is worth so much more than the beauty or the feeling alone. Tell me if that hunch is right.

Finally, a colleague who works on early analytic philosophy insists that Moore's whole argument about organic unities and the supremacy of beauty and affection is, in principle, separable from his far more famous claim that "good" names a simple, unanalyzable, non-natural property. I'd like to know whether she's right -- could someone reject Moore's metaethics wholesale and still accept his substantive conclusions about the ideal, or does the argument for the ideal quietly presuppose the non-naturalism after all? I'd like to get this right before it goes to print.
