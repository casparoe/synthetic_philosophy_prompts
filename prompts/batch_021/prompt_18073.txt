Over several reporting trips to countries where I don't speak the language, I have relied on local fixers to interpret interviews for me, and something about that arrangement has bothered me ever since a trip a few years back. My fixer at the time was translating an interview with a local official when I caught a single word I recognized — I speak enough of the language to pick out stray words, if not follow a sentence — that did not seem to fit at all with what he had just relayed to me in English. When I gently pressed him on it afterward, he had a plausible explanation, and I let the matter drop, but it left me wondering how many similarly unlucky moments there might have been over the years, ones I simply never happened to catch.

What nags at me now, having moved from filing dispatches to writing about ideas for a living, is the structural version of that worry. Whenever I have some independent way of checking a translation — a document I can read myself, a later answer that only makes sense if an earlier one was rendered accurately, a fact I can verify through other channels — the fixer's account holds up. But almost everything I learn through a fixer is, by definition, exactly the part I have no independent way of checking; that is the entire reason I needed one in the first place. It occurs to me that a fixer who was, consciously or not, feeding me whatever version of events best matched what I already seemed to expect or want to hear, rather than what was actually said, would look completely indistinguishable from an honest one on every occasion I am actually in a position to check — precisely because those are the occasions where his honesty was never strictly necessary to catch him out. The two would diverge only in the cases I am structurally unable to verify, which are exactly the cases I most need to get right.

I put this to a couple of journalist friends with similar experience, and the best answer on offer was some version of "you develop a feel for who to trust," which does not satisfy me as a response to the sharper version of the puzzle. So I would like a real answer: is there any principled way — some pattern of questions, some form of cross-checking, some test internal to the situation itself — that could give me justified confidence in a fixer's honesty specifically in the cases where I have no independent access to the truth? Or is this simply a case where that particular kind of confidence cannot be earned at all, no matter how carefully I go about it, and can only ever be extended on faith?
