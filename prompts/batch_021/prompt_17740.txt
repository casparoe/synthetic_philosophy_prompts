I'm reporting a longer piece on the hidden metaphysics behind everyday predicates like "fragile," "soluble," and "poisonous" — the kind of topic I assumed would be a footnote to elementary logic until a metaphysician I interviewed insisted it was one of the more stubbornly contested corners of analytic philosophy. Digging in, I've spent the last couple of weeks working through the finks-and-masks literature that grew out of C. B. Martin's objections to the old conditional analysis of dispositions, and what strikes me is how resilient the puzzle turns out to be. Martin's "electro-fink" — a device that switches a dead wire live the instant something touches it, so that "if touched, it would conduct" comes out true of a wire that is not actually live — looked at first like the kind of counterexample a clever restriction could patch, and David Lewis's reformed analysis, which requires the disposition's causal basis to survive intact through the onset of the stimulus, does handle it reasonably well. But masking cases seem to be a different animal: a perfectly fragile glass that has been bubble-wrapped, or a poison rendered harmless by a co-administered antidote, leaves the causal basis completely untouched, and yet the object plainly would not manifest the disposition if triggered. Lewis's fix does not obviously touch these at all, which is apparently why some philosophers think the whole project of analyzing dispositions via conditionals is a dead end no matter how many epicycles you add, while others insist the right patch is still findable.

I'd like you to dramatize that disagreement rather than summarize it for me. Write a dialogue between two philosophers who run into each other after a workshop session on this material: one who has spent years defending a conditional analysis of dispositions and is committed to repairing it against whatever counterexample comes up next, and one who has concluded that finks and masks show dispositions, or powers, must be treated as metaphysically primitive rather than reduced to conditionals at all. Rather than a rehearsal of settled talking points, I want them to actually test each other in real time: have the primitivist invent a new fink- or mask-style case tailored precisely to whatever patch the reductionist has just proposed, and have the reductionist either absorb it, bite a bullet, or concede a genuine limitation, so that the exchange has real forward motion. Keep both sides sharp and give neither of them the last, unanswerable word — I want readers who already know this literature to feel that both characters are arguing about as well as the position can be argued.
