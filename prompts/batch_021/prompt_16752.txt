I'm preparing an essay for the International Philosophy Olympiad, and one passage I keep returning to is from the end of Section VII ("Of the Idea of Necessary Connexion") of Hume's An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding. Hume writes:

"We may define a cause to be an object followed by another, and where all the objects, similar to the first, are followed by objects similar to the second. Or, in other words, where, if the first object had not been, the second never had existed."

He treats these as two ways of saying the same thing, but they don't look equivalent to me at all: the first just describes a regular pattern, while the second is about what would have happened in a scenario that never actually occurred. My teacher mentioned in passing that this exact slippage is where the modern split between counterfactual theories of causation (like David Lewis's) and interventionist theories (like James Woodward's) really comes from, but she didn't have time to explain how. Could you walk me through it: why the two clauses in the passage aren't really equivalent, what goes wrong when you try to build a whole theory of causation out of the second clause alone (I've heard "preemption" and "backtracking counterfactuals" mentioned as the standard problem cases, though I don't fully understand them), and why some philosophers concluded that appealing to interventions, rather than to regularities or bare counterfactual dependence, was the better way to fix Hume's account? I'd like to actually understand the argument well enough to use it in the essay, not just collect the vocabulary.
