I'm putting together a session for my graduate seminar on formal epistemology on I. J. Good's 1967 theorem that a cost-free experiment can never lower, and can only raise or leave unchanged, the expected utility of a rational Bayesian agent who conditionalizes on its outcome and then chooses optimally in light of it. Colleagues of mine like to cite the result as a tidy vindication of Carnap's old principle of total evidence — the maxim that one should always avail oneself of all the evidence one has before acting — but the proof leans on some fairly delicate stage-setting: the information has to be free to acquire and to process, the decision problem itself (the set of available acts, the possible states, and the utility attached to each act–state pairing) has to stay fixed once the evidence comes in, and the agent has to update by strict conditionalization and then actually choose the optimal act rather than some other one. I want my students to feel the bite of these restrictions rather than just recite the theorem, and I would like your help designing the case I will put to them.

Could you construct one carefully worked-out example of a decision situation in which a single rational agent — not a market, an insurer, or any other population of interacting agents — appears, on first inspection, to be made worse off in expectation by learning something true that costs her nothing to learn? I would rather you not reach for the familiar move of locating the harm in other agents' reactions once the information becomes public, of the kind that comes up in discussions of the Hirshleifer effect on insurance and asset markets, since that changes the decision problem in a different way than the one I have in mind. Once you have set out the example, please say exactly which premise of Good's theorem it is actually trading on: is it a genuine violation of one of the theorem's conditions, or merely an appearance of one that dissolves once the agent's decision problem and utility function are specified with enough care?
