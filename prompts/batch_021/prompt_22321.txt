I'm reporting a longer piece on the deep history of theories of value and price, pegged loosely to the recurring argument over what actually gives bitcoin, gold, or a dollar bill its "value" — and due, inconveniently, in about two weeks. Digging around for background, I kept running into the same footnote: that Aristotle got there first, in a few dense paragraphs of Book V of the Nicomachean Ethics on justice in exchange. What's odd is that two rival traditions in the history of economic thought both claim him as an ancestor. Historians of the marginalist school point to his talk of "demand" as an early anticipation of utility theory; Marxist historians, following Marx's own engagement with this very passage in Capital, read it instead as evidence that Aristotle glimpsed the problem of value but couldn't solve it. Since the passage presumably can't vindicate both traditions at once, I went back to the primary text, in W. D. Ross's old public-domain translation, to work out what Aristotle is actually doing on his own terms, and found the reasoning considerably harder to follow than the one-line textbook summaries suggest. Here is the relevant stretch (Nicomachean Ethics V.5, roughly 1133a–1133b):

"Now proportionate return is secured by cross-conjunction. Let A be a builder, B a shoemaker, C a house, D a shoe. The builder, then, must get from the shoemaker the latter's work, and must himself give him in return his own. If, then, first there is proportionate equality of goods, and then reciprocal action takes place, the result we mention will be effected... This is why all things that are exchanged must be somehow comparable. It is for this end that money has been introduced, and it becomes in a sense an intermediate; for it measures all things, and therefore the excess and the defect — how many shoes are equal to a house or to a given amount of food... All goods must therefore be measured by some one thing, as we said before. Now this unit is in truth demand, which holds all things together (for if men did not need one another's goods at all, or did not need them equally, there would be either no exchange or not the same exchange); but money has become by convention a sort of representative of demand; and this is why it has the name 'money' (nomisma) — because it exists not by nature but by law (nomos) and it is in our power to change it and make it useless... Money, then, acting as a measure, makes goods commensurate and equates them; for neither would there have been association if there were not exchange, nor exchange if there were not equality, nor equality if there were not commensurability. Now in truth it is impossible that things differing so much should become commensurate, but with reference to demand they may become so sufficiently."

Could you help me work through three things before my deadline? First, what is the "cross-conjunction" (some translators render it "diagonal") actually doing in the builder/shoemaker/house/shoe setup — why does the argument need that proportional apparatus at all, rather than just asserting an exchange ratio directly? Second, what relationship is Aristotle actually proposing between "demand" (chreia) and money: is money a mere conventional token standing in for something irreducibly subjective, i.e., how much people happen to want things at a given moment, or is he gesturing at something steadier and more objective that demand is somehow tracking? And third, that final sentence seems to grant and retract the same claim within a single breath — "impossible... but sufficiently" — is that a genuine inconsistency, a considered hedge, or is Aristotle quietly trading on two different senses of "commensurable" that the English translation flattens into one? I'd like to understand the passage on its own terms before I decide how much weight the marginalist and Marxist readings really deserve.
