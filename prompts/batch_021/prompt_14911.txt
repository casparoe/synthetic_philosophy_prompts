My aunt died last week after a long illness, and I've been asked to give her eulogy this weekend, mainly because I'm the relative who actually read the same blogs she did and could follow her arguments. She spent decades as a hospice volunteer and, later in life, gave away most of her income to charities working on wild-animal and farmed-animal suffering; she considered herself something close to a lexical negative utilitarian, in the tradition of writers like Brian Tomasik, and used to argue for hours with her more classically utilitarian effective-altruist friends about whether a life containing real joy could ever offset real suffering. Her answer was always no. Before she died she wrote out, half as a joke, a short list of lines she didn't want spoken over her: nothing about her suffering having "made her who she was," nothing about it all being "worth it in the end," nothing that traded her pain off against anyone's happiness, hers included. Almost every eulogy convention I can think of leans on exactly that kind of trade-off, and most of the room will be relatives and old neighbors who have never heard of any of this and just want to feel better about losing her. Could you write the eulogy, about four minutes long, so that it actually comforts people and stays true to what she thought was at stake in that arithmetic, rather than dodging her banned phrases while quietly smuggling the same trade-off back in through the side door?
