Here's the question as it was given to the students: "Money-pump arguments are standardly used to show that rational preference must be transitive, and diachronic Dutch Book arguments are standardly used to show that rational credence must be updated by conditionalization. (a) Explain the structural feature the two arguments share. (b) A classic objection to the diachronic Dutch Book argument holds that it doesn't actually show that failing to conditionalize is irrational. Explain the objection, and explain how it parallels the point that 'sophisticated' and 'resolute' choosers can each avoid the money pump without their preferences ever becoming transitive."

And here's the student's answer, verbatum: "(a) Both arguments have the same skeleton: you get exploited by a sequence of trades or bets that each look like a good deal by your own lights at the moment you're offered them, but the sequence as a whole is stacked so you're guaranteed to end up worse off no matter what happens. In the money pump, the stacking comes from a preference cycle (A over B, B over C, C over A): a bookie starts you off holding C, sells you B for C plus a small fee (you take the deal, since you prefer B to C), then sells you A for B plus a fee, then sells you C for A plus a fee, and you're back holding C, just poorer. In the Dutch book (first set up by Ramsey and de Finetti, who extended their coherence argument to cover updating over time), the stacking comes from a mismatch between your current conditional credence in H given E and the different credence you've announced you'll adopt in H if E actually comes in -- a bookie can build a pair of bets right now, before E is even settled, that lock in a loss for you however things go once you update as planned. Either way the exploiter has to know your dispositions in advance, and the trick only works on someone who evaluates each offer on its own instead of as part of a package. (b) The objection is that the Dutch book only bites if we assume you're the sort of agent who'll definitely go through with the later trade -- that you'll mechanically take whatever looks fair to you at t2 even though doing so completes a sequence that's bad for you overall. But nothing forces you to be that naive, and this is exactly the sophisticated/resolute move against the money pump: a sophisticated chooser leaves her cyclic ranking exactly as it is, but reasons backward through the decision tree and refuses the very first trade because she can see where the whole sequence ends up; a resolute chooser instead commits up front to a plan for the whole sequence and just sticks to it later, even though the later node, taken on its own, would tempt her to keep going. Either way you dodge the pump without your preferences ever becoming transitive. Carry this over to credences: an agent doesn't have to conditionalize in general to dodge the Dutch book, she just has to be savvy enough to decline the second bet once she notices it's part of a rigged sequence. So really, once you allow for a sufficiently savvy agent, the Dutch book shows nothing at all about conditionalization being specially required by rationality -- it only ever catches naive agents, and since any updating rule at all can be rescued from its own version of the pump the same way, there's no principled reason left to prefer conditionalization over some other rule."
