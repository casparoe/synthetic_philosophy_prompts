Our paper's standards editor, Carol, is retiring in a few weeks after 34 years of catching every fudged stat and suspiciously smoothed-over quote before it went to print, and I somehow got voluteered to give the toast at her send-off dinner. It's going to be a weird room: half the guests are political reporters and editors who'll clock every reference, the other half is her actual family, including a sister flying in from Ohio who has never read a word of political theory in her life. I've been buried in Hannah Arendt's "Truth and Politics" and "Lying in Politics" essays for an unrelated piece on AI-generated disinformation, and I can't stop thinking about her argument that factual truth -- as opposed to philosophical or mathematical truth -- is actually the more fragile of the two, precisely because it has no self-evidence of its own and depends entirely on witnesses and record-keepers to survive, which is exactly why organized political lying goes after the factual record rather than bothering with logic.

Here's the actual problem I need help with: Carol has told people, more than once, that she will walk out of her own party if anyone stands up and calls her a "guardian of truth" or some hero fighting misinformation -- she thinks that kind of talk is precisely the sentimentalizing, image-making instinct she spent three decades quietly editing out of other people's copy. So I need a five-minute toast that actually puts Arendt's ideas to work -- the fragility of factual truth, the way she ties lying to the same human freedom that makes action possible in the first place, the unglamorous but load-bearing role of people like Carol in keeping a shared factual world available for the rest of us to argue over -- without ever tipping into the kind of hagiography she'd hate. It needs to land as an in-joke for the newsroom crowd and as a warm, followable tribute for the family, and it can't, under any circumstances, make her sound like a saint. Can you write it?
