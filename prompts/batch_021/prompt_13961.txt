I sit on my hospital's AI oversight committee, and last month we closed the review of an incident in which a diagnostic-support algorithm contributed to a missed sepsis diagnosis. We could trace the error through the vendor's model, the alert thresholds our informatics team had configured, and the attending physician's decision to defer to a "low risk" score, yet the ethicist advising our committee kept insisting that what we were facing was a genuine responsibility gap -- not merely a case of nobody having looked hard enough for the culprit -- and that this was itself distinct from the further question of who, if anyone, the patient's family could rightly hold accountable. I have been asked to give the opening remarks, about five minutes, at our hospital's first symposium on AI in clinical care, to a room that will be mostly clinicians and administrators with only a handful of ethicists and lawyers among them. Please write those remarks. They should take the idea of a responsibility gap seriously enough that it does not collapse into "nobody's fault," and seriously enough that it is not quietly doing the vendor's public-relations work for it, while still leaving the clinicians in the room -- who will be back on the floor deferring to these tools tomorrow morning -- with something usable about how they ought to act.
