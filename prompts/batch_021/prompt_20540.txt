I got appointed to my state's parole board back in the spring, and this week I've got my first genuinely hard case, which is messing with my head partly because I've been doing alot of meditation myself lately (started after a rough year, nothing dramatic, just needed something). The guy up for parole, Ray, mid-40s, has served 25 years for a murder he committed at 19. Setting aside that he's been a model prisoner, what's actually thrown the board is his statement: he's been a serious Buddhist practitioner for something like fifteen years now and runs the meditation group inside, and instead of the usual "I'm not the same man anymore" speech, he made an actual metaphysical argument — that on the teaching of anatta there was never a continuous self there to begin with, so this isn't really a case of him having changed, it's that treating him as identical to "the person who did that" is a category error, on the order of punishing someone for a crime their grandfather committed.

Two of my colleagues think this is either sophistry meant to dodge accountability, or, if we take it seriously, way too dangerous a precedent to set (anyone could say this). A third thinks Ray is basically just restating what Parfit and other reductionists about personal identity have argued anyway, so it's unfair to hold his being more articulate about it against him. I'm stuck in the middle, partly because I vaguely remember that Buddhist teaching doesn't actually let you off the hook this easily — something about karma still operating across the "stream" of causes even without any fixed self riding along in it — but I can't reconstruct the argument well enough to bring it to the table myself. Could you write the memo I'm circulating to the rest of the board before Thursday's hearing? I need it to land on an actual recommendation (grant, deny, or some concrete third option with a timeframe, like deferring pending a specific evaluation), and it needs to take Ray's argument seriously on its own terms rather than either dismissing it as wordplay or treating "it's philosophically respectable" as if that settles the practical question.
