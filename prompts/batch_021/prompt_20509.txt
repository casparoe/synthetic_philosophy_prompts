Compare the common prior assumption—often called the Harsanyi doctrine—with the more general framework of Bayesian subjectivism about credences. Ordinary Bayesian rationality requires only that an agent's beliefs be probabilistically coherent; it places no constraint on where a prior comes from, or on whether it matches the priors of other agents reasoning about the same world. In which ways, then, is the common prior assumption a distinctive and more demanding commitment, and what could justify imposing it on agents whose beliefs did not arise from a shared informational starting point? Taking Aumann's agreement theorem as your central test case, defend a verdict on whether genuine, persistent disagreement between ideally rational Bayesian agents is possible.
