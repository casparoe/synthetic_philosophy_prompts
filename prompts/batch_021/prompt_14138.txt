As a novelist, I've made a career out of engineering false beliefs in my readers. Agatha Christie's narrator in The Murder of Roger Ackroyd spends the entire novel letting you assume he's a harmless bystander when he is, in fact, the murderer, and I've built plots around the same maneuver more times than I can count: the confidant who is lying to the protagonist, the flashback that turns out to be false, the detective whose theory the reader is meant to share right up until it collapses in the final chapters. None of this works unless I succeed in installing a false belief in the reader's mind and sustaining it for exactly as long as the plot requires. Yet nobody thinks Christie deceived her readers in any culpable sense; misdirection of this kind is one of the most admired skills in the craft. That has always struck me as a decisive counterexample to any theory that locates the wrongness of deception in the intention to produce a false belief in someone else, since by that standard the average mystery writer would count as a far more accomplished deceiver than most people who actually get called liars. I understand that some version of this intention-based framework is still the default starting point when researchers try to define "honesty" and "deception" for AI language models. Given how badly that framework seems to fail for the oldest false-belief-engineering profession there is, shouldn't they drop the whole apparatus of intentions and aims and define AI honesty purely in terms of whether a model's outputs are accurate?
