We're reading the "mill" passage from Leibniz's Monadology -- the bit where he says that even if you blew up a machine capable of thought and perception to the size of a mill and walked around inside it, you'd only ever find parts pushing on other parts, never anything that looks like a perception -- right alongside Frankish and Dennett on illusionism. A couple students already sense there's some kind of tension between the two but can't quite articulate it, and I brought it up with a colleague over lunch and we just went back and forth without really landing anywhere either.

Here's the crux of it: illusionists claim that phenomenal consciousness, the ineffable "something it's like" quality Leibniz is implicitly leaning on, isn't actually real -- it's an introspective misrepresentation produced by an entirely mechanizable system that portrays itself as having these special, non-functional properties when it doesn't. So suppose Leibniz got pulled into a room with Frankish and actually had to engage with that move instead of just restating the mill thought experiment louder. Does he have real resources to resist it, given his own committments about the unity of monads, perception as representation, and appetition, or does he end up having to concede that the mill argument only ever showed that consciousness *seems* irreducible from the inside -- which the illusionist was happy to grant all along?
