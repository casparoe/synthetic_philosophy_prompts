I'm taking a seminar this term on early American moral thought, and for my final paper I've been working closely with Part Two of Franklin's Autobiography, where he lays out his project of arriving at moral perfection: the thirteen virtues, tracked day by day in a little ruled book with a black mark for every lapse. Franklin is candid that the project only partly succeeded. He reports persistent failure with Order in particular, which he blames on his good memory and on the irregular demands of his printing business, and he wryly remarks that even satisfaction at having conquered pride would itself amount to a new species of pride. I'd like your help thinking through how Franklin would respond to a challenge that only emerged two centuries later: the situationist critique associated with Gilbert Harman and John Doris, which appeals to evidence such as the Hartshorne-May cheating studies, Milgram's obedience experiments, and the Darley-Batson "Good Samaritan" study to argue that the kind of stable, cross-situational character traits his whole method presupposes may not really exist, and that conduct is shaped far more by situational cues than by settled disposition. Does his own testimony about Order already give him the resources to answer this challenge, or does the situationist evidence cut against something his project genuinely requires?
