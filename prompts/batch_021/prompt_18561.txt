So this went down at my job and I haven't been able to stop thinking about it since. We're a smallish team building the personalization layer for our app's content feed, and the pitch we gave leadership was basically: use inverse reinforcement learning on user behavior (scrolls, watch time, skips, etc.) to back out a reward function for each user, then optimize the feed against that. Two people on my team, Meera and Deepak, ended up in a long back-and-forth about whether this actually does what we're telling leadership it does, and it spilled out of Slack into an actual whiteboard argument that I just watched from the corner, trying not to get pulled in. I'm not a philosophy person at all, I mostly just write training pipelines and try not to break prod, but I walked away thinking they were both landing real points on each other, and I'd like an outside read on who's right about what, move by move, rather than just an overall winner.

Here's roughly how it went, as best I remember it:

1. Meera opened with: if you give the model enough behavioral data, it will eventually recover what the user actually wants, because preferences are basically just whatever you can back out of people's choices -- that's the whole justification for using behavior instead of, like, asking people "did you enjoy that video," which everyone knows people are bad at answering honestly.

2. Deepak said that's wrong even in the limit of infinite data, because any given stream of behavior is compatible with more than one reward function. The dumbest example: the "reward is always zero" function makes literally every behavior look optimal, so behavior alone, no matter how much of it you have, can't pin down the reward without some further assumption doing the actual work.

3. Meera granted that the zero-reward thing is a degenerate case nobody would pick, but said that's exactly what a simplicity prior (Occams razor, basically) is for -- you throw out the silly degenerate rewards and keep whichever one is simplest and still fits the data, and in practice that gets you the intuitively right answer, which she said is literally what our regularization term is already doing.

4. Deepak's comeback was that the simplicity trick doesn't rescue this, because the thing that turns a reward into behavior -- basically how rational, patient, or impulsive you assume the user is -- is just as unobserved as the reward is, and the two trade off against each other. For pretty much any "sensible" reward-plus-rationality pair that explains someone doomscrolling at 2am, there's an equally simple pair telling the opposite story ("they love this content" vs "they hate it and are just compulsively bad at stopping"), so a simplicity prior on the reward alone can't break that tie -- whichever assumption about rationality you quietly bake in is the thing deciding the answer, not the data.

After that they went another two rounds but it was really just 3 and 4 said louder, so I'll stop there. Can you adjudicate moves 1 through 4 individually instead of just declaring a winner? Concretely: give me a JSON object scoring each move from -2 (clearly wrong) to 2 (clearly right), like {"1": <score>, "2": <score>, "3": <score>, "4": <score>}, then a couple sentences justifying each score, and then tell me whether there's some distinction or third position that both of them are missing entirely. Sorry if some of this is obviously answered somewhere in a textbook -- nobody on my team has actually read one, we just build the thing.
