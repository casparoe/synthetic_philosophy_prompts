Over the past month I have been auditing a graduate seminar on the metaphysics of dispositions, and a good deal of our discussion has circled around the trouble that finks and masks cause for the "simple conditional analysis" of dispositional predicates — the idea, tracing back to Gilbert Ryle's hypothetical treatment of dispositional words, that to say an object is disposed to give response r to stimulus s is just to say that it would give r if it underwent s. C. B. Martin's electro-fink is the standard illustration of how this fails on one side: a device that alters a wire's live-or-dead status at the very instant a test is applied, so that the relevant counterfactual comes out true, or false, quite independently of any standing disposition the wire had beforehand. David Lewis's "reformed" conditional analysis, which requires that the object retain, throughout the counterfactual scenario, whatever intrinsic causal basis grounds the disposition, was explicitly designed to handle cases like this — but the masking and antidote cases that Mark Johnston and Alexander Bird raised soon afterward are usually presented as a structurally different problem that this same fix does not obviously solve, since a mask can block the manifestation downstream of an entirely unaltered basis, rather than tampering with the basis itself.

This stopped being a purely seminar-room puzzle for me last week, when an electrician came out to my building after a wiring fault and, in trying to explain why a cable that had tested "dead" twice in a row could still deliver a shock on the third attempt, described something that struck me as an uncanny echo of Martin's example. It sent me back to the readings with a nagging worry: I can restate the official distinction between finks and masks perfectly well, but I am not sure I could produce it myself in a genuinely new case, which makes me suspect I understand it less well than I would like to.

So I would like you to help me test that. Give me one original example of a finkish disposition and one original example of a masked, or antidote-type, disposition, drawn from anywhere other than wiring, poisons, or fragile glass, since those are the cases I already have memorized. For each example, explain exactly why the simple conditional analysis misclassifies the object's disposition, and whether Lewis's basis-retention fix is enough to rescue that particular case, and why or why not. Then, drawing on both examples together, tell me which of the two problems, finks or masks, strikes you as the deeper obstacle for anyone who still hopes to analyze dispositions conditionally, rather than following Bird in treating at least some dispositions as metaphysically fundamental in their own right.
