I'm a PhD student in Islamic intellectual history, currently drafting a dissertation chapter that puts al-Ghazālī's occasionalism into conversation with later European occasionalists such as Malebranche. Before I get to that comparative work, my advisor keeps pushing me to make sure I actually have a firm grip on the internal Arabic debate on its own terms, rather than flattening it into the one-line version everyone reaches for ("Ghazālī says God does everything, Ibn Rushd says things have natures"). I've been working through Marmura's translation of the Tahāfut al-Falāsifa alongside van den Bergh's translation of Ibn Rushd's rebuttal for a reading group on classical theories of causation, and I suspect thinking it through in dialogue form would serve me better than writing yet another summary.

Could you write a dialogue between al-Ghazālī and Ibn Rushd on natural causation, staged as an imagined exchange (I'm well aware Ibn Rushd was writing decades after Ghazālī's death, responding point by point to the Tahāfut al-Falāsifa in his own Tahāfut al-Tahāfut)? Use the fire-and-cotton case as the anchor, but please push past the cliché version of the disagreement. Specifically, I'd like the dialogue to preserve the fact that Ghazālī's argument, in what I believe is the seventeenth discussion, is officially dialectical — aimed at denying that the philosophers can demonstrate a necessary connection between cause and effect — with "habit" (ʿāda) offered only secondarily as his own positive alternative, rather than having him assert full-blown occasionalism from the first line. On the other side, I want Ibn Rushd to press what strikes me as his strongest card: that denying real, necessitating natures doesn't just make room for miracles but threatens to make demonstrative, Aristotelian knowledge of nature impossible altogether, since nothing would then block any substance from producing any effect whatsoever. I'd also like Ghazālī to bring in the Qur'anic case of Abraham unburned by the fire, and Ibn Rushd to actually engage it rather than sidestep it — there seems to me to be an interesting reply available about what "miraculous" could even mean if things had no stable natures to begin with, and I would rather the two of them arrive at that move themselves in the exchange than have it handed to them in advance.

Please don't let either side win by default or come off as the weaker sketch of a position: I want each of them applying real pressure to the other's actual argument rather than talking past one another, and I am perfectly happy for it to end in a stand-off rather than a tidy resolution. If it fits naturally near the end, a brief gesture toward how this whole disagreement differs from Hume's later denial of necessary connection would be a nice touch, though I don't want that comparison to take over the piece.
