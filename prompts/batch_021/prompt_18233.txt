So I was up way too late on an on-call shift last night (nothing was actually broken, just some alert that pages me every 20 minutes for no reason) and I fell into a Wikipedia hole about decision theory, which is definitely not my background -- I just write backend services for a living. I landed on this old puzzle called "Death in Damascus" and I honestly can't stop turning it over. Setup, if I'm getting it right: a guy is in Damascus and runs into Death, who more or less tells him "I'm coming for you tomorrow." The guy panics and has exactly two moves -- stay put in Damascus, or flee to Aleppo. The catch is that Death is basically a perfect predictor and has already arranged to be wherever the guy ends up, because Death predicted the choice ahead of time -- predicted it, not caused it, theres no backwards-causation weirdness going on, Death just always guesses right.

The part that actually broke my brain a little is what that does to the guy's decision-making. Apparently the second he leans toward "stay in Damascus," staying now looks like the doomed move (since that's what got predicted), so "flee to Aleppo" looks better -- except as soon as he leans that way, fleeing becomes the doomed one instead, and it just flips back and forth with no place to land. Some comment thread called this a failure of "ratifiability," which I gather roughly means an action you'd still be glad you picked once you knew for sure you were picking it. Atleast that's my best understanding as a total outsider to this stuff.

I don't want a history lecture on it, I want to actually feel why this is a real, general problem and not just a spooky campfire story about a guy and a hooded figure. So two asks: first, invent a brand new example with the exact same bones -- a predictor that's correlated with, but doesn't cause, your choice, and no action that survives you actually settling on it -- except set somewhere completely mundane and ideally software-flavored, since that's the world I live in (a fraud-detection model, a matchmaking algorithm, a CI pipeline, whatever fits best, just not another person fleeing between two cities). Second, once you've built it, be honest about whether the standard patches people reach for -- this ratifiability requirement, switching to evidential decision theory, or that "updateless" decision theory thing I keep half-seeing referenced on rationalist blogs -- actually make the instability disappear in your version, or whether they just quietly relocate it somewhere else. And if this turns out to be a thoroughly solved problem and I'm just late to the party, that's a totally fine answer too, I just want to understand why people ever thought it was hard in the first place.
