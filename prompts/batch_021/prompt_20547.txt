I'm a retired field biologist -- spent something like 35 years studying territorial contests in reef fish, which meant I picked up a lot more evolutionary game theory than I ever expected to need. One of the tidier results from that world is the "bourgeois" strategy -- owner keeps the territory, intruder backs off -- which is stable once everybody plays it, even though the mirror-image rule ("intruder always wins") would have been just as stable if history had happened to settle on that one instead. Since retiring I've been filling the gap with philosophy, mainly through a little discussion group that meets at the library once a month, and I about fell out of my chair when I hit the arbitrariness condition in David Lewis's book Convention, because its the exact same idea wearing different clothes. Which is maybe why an argument two other people in the group got into last session, over, of all things, restaurant tipping, has been rattling around in my head ever since.

It went roughly like this. (1) Walt said tipping is a clean textbook case of a Lewis convention: its arbitrary in his technical sense (baking a living wage into the menu price would coordinate everyone just as well), and it's self-perpetuating because everyone conforms, everyone expects everyone else to conform, and that's common knowledge among American diners. (2) Sonia said not so fast: Lewis is explicit that a convention solves what he calls a coordination problem, and a coordination problem requires something like "coincidence of interest" -- people being close to indifferent about which of several equilibria gets picked, the way nobody cares whether we all drive on the left or the right so long as we agree. Tipping isn't like that, she said: servers, diners, and owners have real, unequal stakes in whether we land on "tip generously" versus "pay staff properly and skip the theater," so it fails the very condition that makes something a coordination problem for Lewis in the first place. (3) Walt came back that Lewis himself admits almost every real coordination problem is "impure," mixed with some conflict, and only needs coincidence of interest to predominate, not be total -- and what really does the work in the definition is the conditional part: given that everyone else currently tips, each diner still prefers to tip too, and given that diners tip, each server prefers the tipping system to unilaterally opting out of it. That conditional preference holds right now, he said, no matter how the equilibrium got picked in the first place. (4) Sonia said that concedes too much -- almost any entrenched, self-sustaining arrangement passes that test, including ones everybody would call exploitative, so if that's sufficient, "convention" stops picking out anything distinctive, and Lewis's talk of coincidence of interest and arbitrariness has to be doing real work that Walt's move quietly throws away.

They both turned to me afterward since I'm the one who suggested the book, but I've only been doing this a year and couldn't honestly tell you who has Lewis on their side. Could you go through moves (1) through (4) and adjudicate each one on its own terms -- basically right, basically wrong, or onto something real but overstated -- against what the definition actually requires (general conformity plus expectation of conformity, common knowledge, the coincidence-of-interest condition, the conditional-preference-for-conformity condition, and the arbitrariness condition)? And then tell me, on balance, whether tipping counts as a genuine Lewis convention or whether Sonia has put her finger on a real gap in the framework that goes well beyond this one example.
