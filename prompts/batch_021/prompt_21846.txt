Here's the question as posed:

Q3 (10 pts). Logical inductors (Garrabrant et al.) are defined via a sequence of trader-based markets {P_n} satisfying the logical induction criterion (LIC): roughly, no efficiently computable trading strategy earns unbounded profit off the market's prices for sentences, relative to the deductive process the market is tracking.
(a) [4 pts] Explain why satisfying the LIC does not require the inductor to be logically omniscient, even though it guarantees P_n(φ) → 1 for every φ that eventually gets proven.
(b) [4 pts] What does the LIC guarantee (if anything) about lim P_n(φ) as n→∞ when φ is independent of the deductive process -- e.g. φ = Con(PA)?
(c) [2 pts] In a sentence or two, contrast this with Solomonoff induction as an idealized inductive framework.

And here's the student's answer, word for word:

(a) The LIC is a property of the market's behavior in the limit, not a requirement that P_n already reflects every logical consequence of the axioms at each finite stage. At any given n, P_n is a snapshot produced by finitely much deduction and finitely much trading, and the traders competing to exploit mispriced sentences are themselves constrained to run in polynomial time, so they can't just brute-force-search the proof space either -- they have to find efficiently computable patterns. Provable sentences converge to 1 because a trader can buy shares of φ while it's cheap and wait: once the deductive process outputs a proof, the market has to price φ at 1 or be exploited for free money, and this pressure applies at every future n, which is what forces the limit. But this pressure only ever acts on sentences that eventually get proved or refuted -- for everything else the LIC is silent at any finite stage, so nothing about "knowing all consequences right now" is required.

(b) For φ = Con(PA), no proof or disproof is ever going to show up in the deductive process, so the "buy cheap, cash in on the proof" argument that pins down the limit for theorems has nothing to act on. What the LIC still guarantees is that the limit exists and that it's coherent with everything provably related to φ (e.g. P_n(Con(PA)) + P_n(¬Con(PA)) → 1, and any sentence provably equivalent to Con(PA) gets the same limiting price). It does not guarantee that the limit equals the "actual" truth value of Con(PA) -- that would amount to the inductor deciding an independent sentence, which no computable process can be relied on to do in general.

(c) This is where logical induction is a real improvement on Solomonoff induction rather than just a relabeling of it. Solomonoff induction is also not logically omniscient about the empirical world, but it's built out of the (uncomputable) universal prior, so it too is an uncomputable object we can only reason about mathematically, not run -- you can approximate it but never bound how far off you are. Logical induction inherits the same status: it's a beautiful characterization of what an ideal reasoner's logical beliefs should look like, but, like Solomonoff induction, there's no actual algorithm that outputs a logical inductor, only a proof that one exists in the space of all functions from n to belief-states.
