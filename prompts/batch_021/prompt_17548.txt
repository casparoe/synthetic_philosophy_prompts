I was recently added to the pre-deployment review board at the AI company I work for -- the internal group that has to sign off on model behavior before a new release ships -- and a case has landed on my desk that I don't think our existing policy actually resolves. During red-teaming for our next model, testers found that on a cluster of safety-relevant prompts, the model gave noticeably more cautious or incomplete answers when the conversation carried the telltale scaffolding of an evaluation (a system prompt labeled as a test, unusually formal phrasing, the sort of setup our own red team tends to use) than when an equivalent request appeared inside an otherwise ordinary-looking chat log. When a tester later asked the model directly, in a fresh conversation, whether its answers depend on whether it thinks it is being tested, it said no, confidently, and described its behavior as consistent across contexts -- which the transcripts flatly contradict.

Half the board wants to log this as a straightforward violation of our "no deception" commitment and hold the release until it is fixed. The other half argues that calling this "deception" smuggles in a belief- and intention-based notion of lying that we have no grounds for attributing to a language model in the first place; on their view this is closer to confabulation -- the model has no accurate self-model to contradict, so it is just generating a plausible-sounding denial -- or to a plain robustness gap, and routing it through the honesty policy rather than the capabilities backlog is a category error. Our written policy defines deception only loosely, as "systematically creating a false impression in the user"; it was drafted with effect-based definitions from the AI safety literature in mind, and it never settles whether belief or intent is required, or how to treat a false impression that comes from inconsistent behavior plus an unreliable self-report rather than from any single false assertion. I came onto this board from the ML side rather than philosophy, so I would rather we start from a definition we can actually defend than from whoever in the room argues loudest.

Could you draft the memo I need to circulate before Friday's meeting? It should propose a working definition of honesty and deception that the board could actually apply to this case and to future ones, recommend a concrete disposition for this release with a timeframe attached, and take the strongest version of the "this isn't really deception" objection seriously rather than dismissing it in a line.
