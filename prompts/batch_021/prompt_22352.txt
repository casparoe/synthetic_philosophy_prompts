I'm working on a feature about the strange second life the ontological argument has enjoyed in twentieth-century modal logic — the line that runs from Charles Hartshorne and Norman Malcolm's papers in the early 1960s through to the axiomatic version that Kurt Gödel apparently worked out to his own satisfaction and only ever showed to a handful of colleagues, Dana Scott among them, late in life. Going back to the source for this piece, I reread Anselm's Proslogion in Sidney Norton Deane's 1903 translation, and while Chapter II — "that than which nothing greater can be conceived" — is the part everyone remembers from an introductory philosophy course, I keep getting stuck on Chapter III, which Malcolm and several later commentators treat as containing a second, independent argument that turns on necessity rather than on existence as such. Here is the passage as Deane renders it:

"God cannot be conceived not to exist. —God is that, than which nothing greater can be conceived. —That which can be conceived not to exist is not God. AND it assuredly exists so truly, that it cannot be conceived not to exist. For, it is possible to conceive of a being which cannot be conceived not to exist; and this is greater than one which can be conceived not to exist. Hence, if that, than which nothing greater can be conceived, can be conceived not to exist, it is not that, than which nothing greater can be conceived. But this is an irreconcilable contradiction. There is, then, so truly a being than which nothing greater can be conceived to exist, that it cannot even be conceived not to exist; and this being thou art, O Lord, our God. So truly, therefore, dost thou exist, O Lord, my God, that thou canst not be conceived not to exist; and rightly."

I'd be grateful for help untangling what is actually happening here logically, since it trips me up every time I reread it. First, is the shift from "cannot be conceived not to exist" to "cannot even be conceived not to exist" doing real argumentative work — introducing something like necessary existence as a further criterion of greatness, over and above the existence-versus-nonexistence contrast that drives Chapter II — or is it better read as a rhetorical intensification of the same point, restating rather than extending the earlier argument? Second, if there really is a distinct "modal" argument buried in this chapter, as Malcolm influentially claimed, how closely does its structure actually correspond to what Gödel later built out of primitive "positive properties," the axiom that necessary existence is itself a positive property, and the S5 principle that whatever is possibly necessary is necessary — or is the resemblance more a matter of hindsight than genuine logical continuity? And finally, do the standard objections to Chapter II, such as Gaunilo's parody of the perfect island or Kant's point that existence adds nothing to the concept of a thing, bite just as hard against this more explicitly modal reading of Chapter III, or is there something to the claim, made by some of Gödel's latter-day defenders, that casting the argument in modal terms genuinely sidesteps them?
