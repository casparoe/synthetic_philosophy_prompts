A few of us in my philosophy of mind reading group finally got around to Turing's 1950 "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" paper this week, and the section where he brings up extrasensory perception as an objection to the imitation game kind of derailed the whole meeting. As far as we could tell he's not just mentioning ESP to wave it off — he actually says the statistical evidence for telepathy at the time seemed pretty much overwhelming and couldnt just be dismissed, and then tries to patch the imitation game by suggesting you'd need to run it in a "telepathy-proof room" so a psychic human contestant couldn't cheat by reading the machine's answers or the interrogator's mind.

Two people in the group took this to mean the whole paper is kind of broken by its own admission: if Turing is granting that the test's validity depends on ruling out psychic powers in the first place, and the only fix he's got is some physical shield against a paranormal signal nobody even understands, then doesn't that mean the imitation game was never actually a clean test of machine thought to begin with, just one that happens to work only in a world where ESP is fenced off? That feels like a pretty serious problem to just be sitting in the middle of the founding paper of AI without anyone treating it as a big deal. Am I missing something about what job the ESP section is actually doing in Turing's larger argument, or is this a real hole that mostly gets ignored because it's a little embarrassing?
