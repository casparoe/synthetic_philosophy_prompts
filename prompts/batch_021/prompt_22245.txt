In my school's philosophy club, we've been working through classic metaphysics puzzles, and this week's was Leibniz's principle of the identity of indiscernibles -- the claim that there cannot be two distinct things sharing literally all the same properties -- alongside Max Black's thought experiment describing a universe that contains nothing but two exactly similar iron spheres, alike in every intrinsic quality and separated by some fixed distance. Black argues that ordinary relational properties can't rescue the principle here, since whatever relational property one sphere has, such as being some distance from an iron sphere of a given size, the other sphere has the very same property, so neither one is singled out. Our faculty advisor mentioned, though, that some philosophers think a different kind of relation can still tell the spheres apart: one that nothing bears to itself, like the very distance relation holding between them, even when no quality or one-sided relation can do the job. I believe this move is sometimes called "weak discernibility," and I gather a version of it also comes up in arguments about whether quantum particles like electrons really count as strictly identical. I can't decide whether this actually rescues Leibniz's principle, or whether it secretly assumes the spheres are already distinct just to get the relation to hold "between" them in the first place. Could you write an essay that lays out this specific disagreement, weighs the strongest arguments on each side, and defends a clear, justified verdict on whether this kind of relational move really does save the identity of indiscernibles from Black's counterexample?
