Over dinner last night, a friend and I spent two hours arguing about whether the preface paradox actually adds anything to the lottery paradox, or whether Makinson's 1965 case is just Kyburg's 1961 lottery wearing different clothes: swap "ticket" for "sentence in the book," she says, and you get the same structure — a large set of individually well-supported, rationally accepted propositions whose conjunction is rationally rejected. I argued the differences are not cosmetic: the lottery's probabilities are precise, known in advance, and generated by a symmetric chance setup in which no ticket is epistemically special, whereas an author's confidence in each sentence of her book is qualitative, drawn from uneven evidence, and her belief that not everything in it is true is a higher-order judgment about her own fallibility rather than a fact read off a known distribution. Neither of us gave ground. I want an actual verdict, not a survey of positions: compare the two paradoxes closely enough to say whether they get the same diagnosis and the same fix, and specifically, whether rejecting agglomeration for acceptance — accepting P and accepting Q without thereby being committed to accepting their conjunction, roughly Kyburg's own way out of the lottery — handles the preface case equally well, or whether the disanalogies I've pointed to mean it can't. Tell me which of us had the better argument, and why.
