I've been teaching the early modern philosophy of mind unit this week, and we spent a whole class on Descartes' bête-machine doctrine -- the idea that animals are just extremely ingenious clockwork, with no rational soul at all, because (per the Discourse and his letters) they can't use language flexibly to respond to whatever comes up the way we can, and because reason is supposed to be a "universal instrument" while animal "skills" are always narrow and specialized, like organs built for one job. Half my students were pretty disturbed to learn that this doctrine is sometimes linked to real vivisections carried out by Cartesian-influenced natural philosophers, who apparently treated a dog's yelping under the knife as no more meaningful than a spring creaking -- its a famous anecdote (it comes down to us via Port-Royal sources), though I always caveat that I'm honestly fuzzy on how much of it is reliable history versus later polemical exaggeration against Descartes. What got people even more worked up, though, was when I mentioned that there's a live scholarly dispute over whether Descartes even denied that animals feel anything at all -- some readers (Cottingham, for one) think he only denied they think/reason/have an immortal soul, and that he actually allowed animals bodily sensations of a sort. So it's genuinely unclear which "Descartes" the doctrine's critics are even attacking.

Anyway, here's what I'd like from you: write me a short story (a page or two is plenty) centered on a 17th-century natural philosopher, a committed Cartesian, who's in the middle of some kind of experiment on a live animal -- a dog is fine -- and finds his mechanist convictions genuinely tested by what unfolds. Don't make it a simple "dogma shatters, he learns his lesson" morality tale; I'd rather you actually sit inside the ambiguity of the doctrine itself, the way the scholars do. You could even play with the narration a bit -- is it even coherent to narrate "from the dog's point of view," if the Cartesian view might be right that there's nobody home in there? Invent whatever names, places, and details you need; I'm not looking for historical accuracy so much as a story that dramatizes the actual philosophical bind rather than just assuming the answer is obvious.
