I write about the history and philosophy of science for a science-and-ideas magazine, and two years ago a long piece I did on the invention of modern logic led a small, mostly volunteer-run society for the philosophy of language to invite me onto its advisory board. Since the 1970s the society has attached Frege's name to its early-career prize in logic and philosophy of language, and until this year I had assumed the choice was about as uncontroversial as naming a lecture hall after Euclid. Then a doctoral student on the board circulated Frege's 1924 political diary, which sat unremarked among his papers and only became widely discussed among philosophers decades after his death. In his own hand, Frege records contempt for the Weimar Republic, hostility to parliamentary democracy, and antisemitism explicit enough that Michael Dummett -- the philosopher most responsible for Frege's twentieth-century reputation in the English-speaking world -- wrote candidly, years later, about how hard he found it to square with the man whose logic he had spent his career defending.

The board is now split ahead of next month's vote. One faction wants the prize renamed, on the grounds that the profession does not, in practice, let "the logic holds regardless of the logician" excuse other figures from the consequences of their politics, and that a name on a plaque honors the man, not merely the distinction between sense and reference. A few of them go further and argue the connection isn't only biographical: Frege's lifelong hostility to psychologism, his insistence that a thought is true or false absolutely and for everyone, independent of who is doing the thinking, rhymes uncomfortably with the political absolutism of the diary, and they want that resemblance addressed rather than waved away. The other faction, mostly working logicians rather than historians, thinks Frege is close to a limit case for "separating the work from the person" precisely because his contribution is formal: the cogency of the Begriffsschrift or of "On Sense and Reference" doesn't depend even slightly on the character of the man who wrote them, in a way that a political philosopher's or a novelist's work arguably would. As the board member with no institutional stake in either camp, I have been asked to draft the memo that will circulate before the vote.

Please write that memo. It should reach one concrete recommendation -- keep the name, retire it, or some third option such as keeping it alongside a permanent statement of context -- with a realistic timeline for carrying it out. It should engage seriously enough with the strongest argument on the side you don't take that its proponents would recognize their own position in your account of it, rather than a strawman. And since ours will not be the last award in the field named after a twentieth-century thinker with an ugly private archive, it should close with a brief, general principle the society could actually apply the next time this happens, rather than one that would only work for Frege.
