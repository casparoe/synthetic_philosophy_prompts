I spent nearly forty years as an observational astrophysicist before retiring, and it is only in the last couple of years, with more time on my hands, that I have started reading philosophy of science with any real care, rather than absorbing Popper at second hand through what colleagues said about him at seminars. Lately I have become somewhat preoccupied with the long-running dispute over whether string theory and multiverse cosmology still deserve to be called science. I read George Ellis and Joe Silk's 2014 piece in Nature urging physicists to "defend the integrity of physics" against theories that yield no testable predictions, and that led me to Richard Dawid's work on what he calls "non-empirical theory confirmation": his claim that a theory can rationally earn credence from things like the absence of any viable competitor, unexpected coherence among its different parts, and the track record of structurally similar theories in the past, all without a single new observation. Sean Carroll has argued along similar lines that falsifiability was always the wrong idol for scientists to worship, and that it is well past time to retire it as the mark of genuine science. My own sympathies lie with Ellis and Silk on this; it feels to me like exactly the kind of move a field makes when it wants the prestige of science without paying the empirical dues. But I have learned, from a couple of recent arguments with old colleagues at dinner, not to trust that reaction on a question I have only just begun to study properly.

So I would like you to argue the other side. Please make the strongest case you can that Popperian falsifiability is the wrong criterion for demarcating science from non-science, and that Dawid and Carroll are right to treat these non-empirical, structural virtues of a theory as legitimate grounds for scientific belief even in the absence of new testable predictions. I would like the argument to engage with the actual content of Dawid's reasoning rather than simply gesturing at Kuhn's paradigms or Lakatos's research programmes in the abstract, and I would like it to confront directly what strikes me as the obvious objection: that this move risks admitting almost any sufficiently elegant piece of metaphysics into the fold of science, with no principled way back out. If you can see a way to grant Dawid and Carroll their central point while still resisting that slide, I would be glad to hear it as well.
