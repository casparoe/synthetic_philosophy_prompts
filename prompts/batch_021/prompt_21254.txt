Student answer:

Strawson's aim in "Freedom and Resentment" is to reorient the free will debate away from the abstract question of whether determinism is compatible with moral responsibility and toward the structure of our actual interpersonal practices. He labels the two traditional camps "optimists" and "pessimists." Optimists are compatibilists who think our practices of praising, blaming, and punishing can be justified independently of the truth of determinism, typically by appeal to their social utility. Pessimists are incompatibilists who insist that this kind of external, consequentialist justification leaves out something essential — desert — and who conclude that if determinism is true, our practices of holding responsible are, at bottom, unjustified.

Strawson thinks both sides mislocate the issue by asking whether the practice as a whole can be given an independent rational grounding. His alternative is to describe the practice in terms of the reactive attitudes: resentment and gratitude, our personal responses to the ill will or good will others display toward us; their vicarious, impersonal counterparts, such as indignation and disapprobation, felt on behalf of third parties; and self-reactive attitudes, such as guilt and remorse. Holding someone responsible is, on this view, mainly a matter of occupying the participant stance toward them, as a fellow member of the moral community capable of displaying or withholding good will. The objective stance, by contrast, is the one we take toward someone no longer regarded as a possible object of these attitudes — someone to be managed or treated rather than engaged with, as when we deal with a young child or a person who is severely mentally ill.

Strawson uses this apparatus to answer the pessimist and correct the optimist at once. Against the pessimist, he argues that the truth or falsity of determinism is simply the wrong kind of consideration to dislodge the participant stance, since our commitment to it does not rest on a belief that agents possess some contra-causal power that a deterministic universe would rule out; it is a framework within which we live, not a hypothesis we entertain, and one we could not abandon wholesale without forfeiting the entire web of adult human relationships that the reactive attitudes make possible. Against the optimist, he argues that the utilitarian story is not so much false as beside the point, since it treats as an open, external question something that is actually internal to and constitutive of the practice. In this sense, Strawson's argument culminates in essentially the same place as his son Galen Strawson's later Basic Argument, which shows that because no one can be the ultimate cause of their own character, backward-looking desert of the sort the pessimist demands is impossible in principle; this is precisely why Strawson redirects the justificatory burden away from desert and onto our naturally occurring reactive attitudes.

The most influential objection, developed by Derk Pereboom, grants Strawson's naturalism its descriptive point but denies that it settles the normative question. Even if the reactive attitudes are natural and practically ineliminable, Pereboom argues, resentment and indignation, as we ordinarily feel them, presuppose that their target is basically deserving of blame just in virtue of what he did, and not merely because blaming him would have good effects. If, as the hard incompatibilist maintains, no one is ever basically deserving in this sense, these attitudes are not just unjustified in some abstract theoretical sense but actually inappropriate, however natural they may be to feel. Strawson does anticipate something like this worry, since he distinguishes cases in which we suspend a reactive attitude toward a particular act, because it was an accident or the agent was coerced, from cases in which we suspend the participant stance toward the agent as a whole; and in the essay's later pages he treats the wholesale expansion of the latter as the direction our thinking should take as we grow more sophisticated about the causal history of character, so that indignation and resentment are gradually superseded by clinical management even in paradigm cases of wrongdoing. If that is right, Pereboom's proposal is less a departure from Strawson than a completion of a trajectory already present in his text, and the objection loses much of its force as a criticism, though it still usefully shows that Strawson's own reasons for expecting this outcome are naturalistic rather than desert-based.
