Ok this is going to take a sec to set up but bear with me. At the decision theory reading group I'm part of, two of the regulars, Theo and Sasha, got into it over Kavka's toxin puzzle and I keep turning it over in my head. Quick recap in case its not fresh: an eccentric billionaire will pay you a million dollars tonight if, by midnight, you *intend* to drink a nasty-but-harmless toxin tomorrow at noon. He's got a reliable way of detecting the intention itself, not whether you actually follow through, and drinking the toxin gets you nothing extra since the money's already yours by then.

Theo's opening move: the puzzle basically dissolves once you take an FDT/UDT-style stance. Don't ask "what should I do at noon tomorrow," ask "what policy, decided once, gets the most utility over the whole scenario." The policy "drink" wins, so you just adopt it -- same way a good updateless agent pays the driver in Parfit's hitchhiker even after being safely dropped off in town. No local incentive at noon should override a commitment made from the earlier vantage point; that's the whole point of being updateless.

Sasha pushed back: fine for a stipulated agent whose "decisions" are just policy outputs with no seperate psychology, but actual intentions (she brought up Bratman here) aren't free-floating policy choices -- forming one requires actually believing you'll go through with the act, and real people don't get to just declare themselves updateless and thereby rewrite what they'll believe about their own future compliance. Kavka's question was whether rational intention-formation is possible here, not whether a sufficiently stubborn person could grit their teeth through it.

Theo's reply: that begs the question by assuming CDT-style psychology -- where only the local, in-the-moment incentive counts -- is the only kind rational agents can have. People keep promises against local incentive all the time (that's literally what a commitment device is), and that disposition is exactly what the billionaire's test is picking up on. Calling it "irrational" just because it doesn't maximize at the final node smuggles in the very theory under dispute.

Sasha's comeback: sure, humans can be dispositionally stubborn, but Kavka's puzzle is specifically about whether a fully rational agent -- one who always does what they have most reason to do at the time -- can form the intention, and by hypothesis such an agent has zero reason to drink at noon. If "rational" just means "whatever policy pays off ex ante," she said, then he's redefined rationality to win the argument, not shown the original puzzle was confused.

Neither of them budged and the group moved on to bar food before it got resolved. Can you go through this move by move -- who's actually got the better of each point -- and flag anything you think both of them are missing? Doesn't have to end in a strict "winner," I'm more interested in which specific claims hold up.
