And here's my classmate's answer, copied word for word:

(a) The theorem says that any voting rule which takes in each voter's strict ranking of the candidates and outputs a single winner has to be a dictatorship, provided that: there are at least three candidates who could possibly win, the rule is defined for every possible profile of preferences (it can't just refuse to work for some rankings), and the rule is Pareto efficient, i.e. it never picks a candidate when every voter prefers some other candidate to it. If a rule meets all of that and still isn't a dictatorship, it has to be manipulable somewhere: some voter, at some profile, does better by lying about her ranking than by reporting it honestly.

(b) Three voters, candidates A, B, C, Borda with 2/1/0 and alphabetical tiebreaks. Sincere ballots: V1: A>B>C, V2: C>B>A, V3: C>B>A. Scores come out to A=2, B=3, C=4, so C wins, which happens to be V1's least favorite outcome of the three. V1 can't make A win outright, so instead she buries C by reporting B>A>C rather than her sincere ranking. New scores: A=1, B=4, C=4, a tie that goes to B alphabetically. Since V1 prefers B to C, she comes out ahead by lying rather than voting sincerely -- exactly the kind of manipulability the theorem says has to be lurking somewhere for a non-dictatorial rule like this one.

(c) Two ways people try to dodge the theorem: first, restrict preferences to be "single-peaked" (say everyone's opinions line up along one left-right axis) and just use the median voter's favorite as the outcome -- that's strategy-proof and non-dictatorial, but only because you've given up letting people have literally any preferences at all. Second, randomize: pick one voter completely at random and hand her whatever she names as her favorite. Nobody has any reason to lie, because if you're the one picked, your best move is obviously to name your actual favorite, and if you're not picked, what you said didn't matter anyway -- so honesty always does at least as well as lying. What you give up here is determinism: the exact same set of preferences can produce different winners depending on who the lottery happens to pick.
