Here's the question as given on the exam:

"Nozick argues that no 'patterned' principle of distributive justice -- one that says a distribution is just only when it matches some pattern, such as strict equality or Rawls's difference principle -- can be maintained over time without continual interference in people's lives. Using the Wilt Chamberlain example, reconstruct this argument in your own words, and explain exactly what the example is supposed to establish about the relationship between liberty and patterned distributions. (20 points)"

And here's the student's answer, copied exactly as submitted:

"Nozick's entitlement theory says a distribution of holdings is just if it came about through the right kind of history: just acquisition of previously unowned things, plus just transfer (gift, trade, sale) between people who already hold justly. If everyone's current holdings can be traced back through a chain of transfers like that to legitimate original acquisitions, the resulting distribution is just, no matter what it looks like -- it doesn't have to be equal, or proportional to need or merit, or anything else.

The Wilt Chamberlain example is supposed to show why this historical, unpatterned approach is the right one. Start with any distribution D1 that you like, on whatever patterned theory you favor -- say, everyone has an equal $1,000. Now a million people each freely choose to pay $1 to watch Wilt Chamberlain play basketball, on the understanding that the dollar goes straight to him. By the end of the season Wilt has $1,000,000 and everyone else has $999. Call this D2. D2 is wildly unequal and doesn't match the original pattern, but every step from D1 to D2 was a voluntary transfer nobody objects to. So if D1 was just, D2 has to be just too. Since this kind of thing will keep happening as long as people are free to do what they want with their own holdings, the only way to keep a patterned distribution intact over time is for someone to keep stepping in and redistributing, which is really just a nicer way of describing continuous forced transfers. That's the sense in which liberty upsets patterns: any patterned principle at all, egalitarian or Rawlsian or otherwise, ends up requiring the state to constantly interfere with peaceful exchanges to drag the distribution back into the approved shape. And because Wilt earned that money fair and square through a talent a million people were freely willing to pay for, it would be a further injustice to confiscate it from him after the fact just to restore the old pattern.

So the example basically works as a reductio: patterned theories of distributive justice are committed to something nobody should accept, namely constant coercive interference with people's lives, so we should reject patterning as the criterion of justice in favor of the historical, entitlement-based view. This is also why redistributive taxation can never be justified on Nozick's view -- once you trace holdings back to legitimate transfers, any later attempt by the state to reshape the distribution, for any reason, treats people's labor as a pool of resources for others to allocate, which is exactly the sort of thing entitlement theory is designed to rule out."
