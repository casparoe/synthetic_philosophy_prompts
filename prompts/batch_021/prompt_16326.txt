A couple of my old colleagues from way back, Ray and Meredith, still get coffee with me most Friday's now that we're all retired (Ray was a physiologist, Meredith did population genetics, and I was mostly a geologist, so I've got no horse in this race biologically speaking). Last week they got into it about my neighbor's kid, who's ten and on track to end up around 5'2" as an adult. His endocrinologist ran the usual tests, growth hormone came back completely normal, and the official diagnosis was "idiopathic short stature" -- meaning nobody actually knows why he's short, he just is. The parents are trying to decide whether to put him on daily growth hormone injections anyway, and it turned into a full-on argument between Ray and Meredith over whether that would be treatment or enhancement, which is funny timing because I've been on a bit of a philosophy kick since I retired and had literally just been reading about this exact distinction in medical ethics.

Ray's opening shot was that it's obviously enhancement: the kid's hormone axis tests normal, so nothing is malfunctioning, and giving him more hormone just shifts a working system to a different point on its own normal curve. Meredith countered with Christopher Boorse's old line that health just is statistically species-typical functioning for your reference class, so landing way down in the bottom percentiles for adult male height already counts as a departure from normal functioning, whatever his hormone levels say. Ray shot back that this proves too much, since plenty of statistically rare traits -- red hair, being left-handed, off-the-charts IQ -- obviously aren't diseases, which is exactly why Boorse ties disease to some specific part or process working below its species-typical contribution to survival and reproduction, not to how rare the end result is; and if the kid's GH axis is fine and he's just unlucky on the polygenic height lottery, that's no different, dysfunction-wise, from someone who's just naturally very tall. Meredith conceded the rarity point but said that's precisly the force of "idiopathic": nobody can positively rule out some undetected defect further down the pathway -- growth-plate sensitivity, IGF-1 signaling, whatever -- so we're in no position to confidently say nothing's malfunctioning. Ray replied that this just flips the burden of proof: we don't normally call a trait diseased merely because science hasn't nailed down its full mechanism yet, or every unusually gifted athlete would count as having some undiagnosed condition too.

Neither of them budged, and they landed on me to referee over a second round of coffee, which is a bit absurd given that my entire career was spent looking at rock strata, not kids. Could you go through Ray and Meredith's exchange move by move and say who's got the better of each individual point, not just who wins overall? I've only been reading this stuff for about a year, so also feel free to correct me if I've garbled Boorse or the treatment/enhancement literature somewhere in retelling this -- just don't let them off the hook with a "both sides have some validity" non-answer.
