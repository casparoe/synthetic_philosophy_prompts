The question was:

"Explain the core disagreement between Humeans and anti-Humeans about the metaphysics of laws of nature. Your answer should address (a) Lewis's Best System Analysis, (b) at least one anti-Humean alternative, and (c) one major objection facing each side."

And here's the student's answer:

"Humeans and anti-Humeans disagree about what grounds the laws of nature. For Humeans like David Lewis, there are no necessary connections between distinct existences - the world is just a vast mosaic of local, particular facts, and the laws are simply whichever generalizations come out as axioms of the deductive system that best balances simplicity and strength in describing that mosaic. On this view laws don't 'govern' anything over and above the pattern of events; they're just a convenient summary of regularities that happen to hold. Lewis calls this view 'Humean Supervenience' because, in keeping with Hume's empiricism, he thinks we can only ever have knowledge of the regularities themselves, not of any hidden necessary connections behind them.

Anti-Humeans reject this picture. Armstrong, for instance, argues that laws consist in relations of natural necessitation between universals, symbolized N(F,G), which necessitate that all Fs are Gs. Because this is a genuine necessitation relation rather than a mere regularity, Armstrong's view implies that our laws could not have been otherwise - once N(F,G) holds it holds necessarily, so the corresponding regularity is guaranteed in every world with those laws.

A standard objection to Lewis is the circularity worry: the best-system laws are supposed to explain why the regularities hold, but they're defined in terms of those very regularities, which seems to get the direction of explanation backwards. Armstrong avoids this since the necessitation relation is explanatorily prior to the regularity, but he pays for it by positing a relation between universals that we have no direct epistemic access to."
