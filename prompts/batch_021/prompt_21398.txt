I'm a software engineer, not a philosopher, but a few of us at work run an informal lunchtime discussion group, and we've spent the last couple of weeks on free will after someone circulated a video on Harry Frankfurt's attack on the "principle of alternate possibilities" — the idea that you're only morally responsible for something if you could have done otherwise. Two of the more philosophically well-read members, Jess and Owen, ended up in a long back-and-forth on our Slack channel about the standard version of Frankfurt's case, and the rest of us mostly just watched it happen. I'd like a neutral outside opinion on who actually had the better of it, because both of them are still convinced they won.

The case they were arguing about goes like this: Black has rigged a device that will force Jones to shoot the victim if, and only if, Jones shows some sign of deciding not to; as it happens, Jones decides to shoot entirely on his own, the device never engages, and Frankfurt's conclusion is that Jones is responsible even though, because of the device, he couldn't have done otherwise. (1) Owen opened by arguing that this settles the matter: since the device never actually fires, the causal history of Jones's decision is exactly like an ordinary case of unimpeded deliberation, so whatever grounds his responsibility has to be a fact about that actual sequence of events, not about possibilities that were never realized. (2) Jess pushed back that the whole case depends on Black having a reliable "prior sign" — something in Jones's brain or behavior that tips Black off before the decision is final — and that this sign is either a guaranteed predictor of the decision, in which case the case has quietly smuggled in determinism and can't show what Frankfurt needs it to show for an undetermined, libertarian-style agent, or it's an imperfect predictor, in which case there's a real stretch of time, however brief, during which Jones could still have decided otherwise. (3) Owen granted that some such "flicker" of alternative possibility might survive on the second horn, but said it's the wrong kind of alternative to matter, since Jones has no control over whether the flicker occurs — it's more like an involuntary hiccup in the deliberative process than a real choice point — so it can't be the sort of robust, agent-controlled alternative a defender of the principle actually needs. (4) Jess replied that this move concedes too much by stipulation: Owen gets to keep narrowing what counts as a "robust" alternative until the principle is true by definition, which doesn't refute the original idea so much as relocate the argument to what robustness requires — and that relocation is itself a win for her side, since it grants that some notion of a genuinely available alternative is doing real work after all. (5) Owen's last message pointed to a further-refined version of the case in which a backup deterministic process runs alongside Jones's own undetermined deliberation and only takes over at the very last instant, with no prior sign required at all, and he said this was built precisely to route around objections like Jess's, so at best she'd found a flaw in an outdated version of the argument. Jess hadn't replied by the time the thread went quiet, and that's where it still sits.
