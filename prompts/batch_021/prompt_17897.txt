I've managed to schedule mine with a philosopher who works on Nagarjuna's use of the catuskoti (the "tetralemma," or fourfold negation) in the Mulamadhyamakakarika, and who defends a paraconsistent, dialetheist reading of it in the spirit of Garfield and Priest's "Nagarjuna and the Limits of Thought" — on her view, at the level of ultimate truth some of the four horns of a given tetralemma can be jointly true (or jointly false) without this counting as a defect in the position. In seminar we spent two sessions on the obvious worry that this makes Nagarjuna's central claim — that all phenomena lack intrinsic nature (svabhava) — self-refuting, since that very claim would need to be exempt from its own scope to avoid being just one more empty, non-assertoric utterance. From the secondary literature I've read on her work, her standard move whenever this comes up is to fall back on Nagarjuna's line from the Vigrahavyavartani that he "has no thesis to defend," so that the demand for consistency never gets a grip on the position in the first place.

Could you help me draft eight to ten interview questions, arranged so that the "no thesis" reply gets progressively harder for her to redeploy? I'd like at least one question on each of the following: whether reaching for a paraconsistent logic to make room for the fourth corner is not already a substantive logical commitment that the "no thesis" defense can't absorb; whether the catuskoti is doing the same kind of work when Nagarjuna applies it to causation or motion as when it's applied to whether the Tathagata exists after death, where it looks more like a rejection of a loaded question's presuppositions than a genuine four-way exhaustive disjunction; and whether a dialetheist Madhyamaka can avoid sliding into trivialism — if some contradictions come out true at the ultimate level, what principled reason is there for not letting all of them through? For each question, please add a short follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give — I'd guess "emptiness is also empty" gets invoked more than once, so at least one follow-up should target that move head-on. Close the sequence with a question asking what would have to be true for her to concede that invoking the tetralemma here functions as a way of dodging the objection rather than as a genuine result about the limits of conceptual thought.
