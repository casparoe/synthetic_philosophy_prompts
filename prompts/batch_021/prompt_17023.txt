"Two-dimensional semantics is often invoked to explain how a sentence like 'Water is H2O' can be both necessary and knowable only a posteriori. (a) Briefly say which philosophers were most responsible for developing the two-dimensional framework, and what problem in Kripke's work it was meant to address. (b) Explain the distinction between the primary and secondary intension of 'Water is H2O', and show how each one contributes to explaining its necessary a posteriori status. (c) Chalmers and Stalnaker both make use of the two-dimensional apparatus, but disagree about how much epistemic/metaphysical weight to put on the primary intension - briefly characterize that disagreement."

And here's the student's answer:

"(a) Two-dimensional semantics was introduced by Kripke, in response to the puzzle he raised in Naming and Necessity about how a statement could be necessarily true yet only knowable through empirical investigation. The framework represents an expression's meaning along two dimensions: one tracking which world plays the role of the context of utterance, and the other tracking which world plays the role of the counterfactual circumstance of evaluation.

(b) For 'Water is H2O,' the primary intension is the diagonal of this two-dimensional matrix: for each world considered as actual, it asks whether the watery stuff of that world would turn out, on investigation, to be H2O. Since it's epistemically possible that the stuff filling our oceans and lakes could have turned out to be XYZ instead, this proposition is contingent - true at some epistemically possible scenarios and false at others - and that contingency is what accounts for the sentence's a posteriori status, since it takes empirical inquiry to find out which scenario we're actually in. The secondary intension, by contrast, holds fixed the actual-world referents of 'water' and 'H2O': since both are rigid designators for the same substance once we've fixed which world is actual, it asks whether H2O = H2O across every counterfactual world, which comes out true no matter what, and that's why the sentence is necessary. In this sense the primary intension behaves a bit like a Fregean sense for the sentence, though Chalmers is careful not to lean on that comparison too heavily.

(c) Stalnaker and Chalmers both rely on this apparatus, but Chalmers is the more deflationary of the two: he treats the diagonal mainly as a pragmatic bookkeeping device for tracking common ground between speakers who might associate different secondary intensions with the same sentence. Stalnaker, by contrast, treats primary intensions as tracking a robust space of conceptually possible scenarios that grounds a genuinely a priori layer of meaning, which is what licenses the conceivability-to-possibility arguments he later runs against materialism."
