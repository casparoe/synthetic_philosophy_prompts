I have spent a long time working through the LessWrong sequences and the wider rationalist blogosphere's running argument about probability -- Jaynes and the Bayesians on one side, various defenders of frequentist statistics on the other -- and I recently tried to work out where the propensity interpretation actually belongs on that map, since it keeps turning up as a supposed "third option" alongside frequentism and Bayesian subjectivism. As I understand the history, Popper introduced propensities largely to give quantum mechanics and other single-case events -- the chance that this particular atom decays in the next hour, say -- an objective probability that frequentism, tied as it is to long-run or hypothetical infinite sequences, was thought unable to provide. The longer I sit with this, though, the less I can locate a substantive difference: whenever anyone actually wants a numerical value for a propensity, the only way to get one seems to be by appeal to frequencies across many repetitions of "the same" setup, or to a symmetry argument that itself only makes sense against a background of imagined repeated trials. My tentative conclusion has therefore been that propensity theory is little more than hypothetical frequentism restated in causal vocabulary, and that insisting on it as a genuinely distinct third interpretation is a matter of philosophical bookkeeping rather than substance.

This came up recently when a friend from my local rationalist meetup, who did a philosophy of science minor, mentioned in passing that there is a specific technical result -- she called it "Humphreys' paradox" -- that is standardly presented as a problem for the propensity interpretation in particular. If my deflationary view is correct, this struck me as odd, since the paradox ought to be a general problem for any objectivist account of chance, and I would have expected it to land just as heavily on ordinary frequentist statistics -- confidence intervals, p-values, and the like -- as on propensity theory. Could you help me sort out where I am going wrong here, if indeed I am: is there a principled reason to keep propensity and frequency apart as interpretations of probability, and does Humphreys' paradox actually generalize across objectivist interpretations in the way I would expect, or does it depend on some commitment specific to propensity theory that frequentism does not share?
