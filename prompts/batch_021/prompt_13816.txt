Last weekend a friend of mine who is finishing a doctorate in Buddhist philosophy and I spent most of a dinner arguing about how to read Nāgārjuna's catuṣkoṭi, and neither of us gave any ground. He defends, more or less, Garfield and Priest's position in "Nāgārjuna and the Limits of Thought": that when the Mūlamadhyamakakārikā rejects all four corners regarding a thing's svabhāva, this expresses genuine, true contradictions arising at the limits of thought — on the model of the Inclosure Schema, alongside cases like Russell's paradox and Kant's antinomies — and that paraconsistent logic is what lets Nāgārjuna assert this without the whole system exploding into triviality. I ran the Tillemans-style objection that Madhyamikas are best read as quietists holding no thesis whatsoever, so that flatly asserting "these are true contradictions" just is a way of occupying the very third corner — both true and false — that those same passages also reject. He answered that these are "limit" contradictions rather than ordinary ones and so are not subject to the same rejection; I said that just relabels the problem rather than solving it; we went another round or two along those lines and gave up somewhere around midnight.

This is not purely academic for me, because I help run a graduate reading group on cross-traditional philosophy, and next month we are hosting a scholar known for defending essentially my friend's line, who will give a talk followed by a Q&A. She argues that the catuṣkoṭi is a genuine, formalizable case of true contradiction, rather than a scope-of-negation trick or a purely therapeutic device with no logical commitments, and I would like to go into that session better prepared than I was at dinner.

Could you draft eight to ten questions for the session that go beyond having her simply restate the dialetheist reading? I would like at least one question that presses the quietism-versus-assertion worry directly — does labeling the rejected pairs "true contradictions" itself amount to a first-order thesis, and does calling them "limit" contradictions actually block that charge or just relocate it — one that raises the "classical recapture" problem, namely that if the catuṣkoṭi is formalized with something like Anderson and Belnap's First Degree Entailment, which invalidates modus ponens, it is unclear how that squares with Nāgārjuna's evident reliance on ordinary reductio argument elsewhere in the text, and one that asks her to weigh the dialetheist reading directly against the rival move of reading the four corners' negations as operating at different scopes (paryudāsa versus prasajya-pratiṣedha), so that the appearance of contradiction dissolves without revising classical logic at all. For each of these, please add a short follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give, and close with a question asking what, short of simply relocating the problem to a higher limit, would actually count as evidence against her reading.
