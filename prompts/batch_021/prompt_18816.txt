A friend and I spent much of a long evening arguing about the Doomsday Argument without either of us persuading the other. I was defending the basic inference: if one treats one's birth rank as a random sample drawn from everyone who will ever live, finding oneself relatively early in that sequence should shift one's credence toward a smaller total population, and thus a nearer end for humanity, than one would otherwise expect. My friend objected that this inference depends on an arbitrary choice of reference class, and that once one properly credits the fact that one's own existence is itself evidence favoring hypotheses on which more observers exist, the shift disappears entirely. Neither of us was willing to concede, and each suspected that the other's preferred fix merely relocated the difficulty rather than resolving it.

Write a dialogue between two philosophers, equally sharp and well-versed in anthropic reasoning, in which one defends a doomsday-style update using the Self-Sampling Assumption and the other resists it using the Self-Indication Assumption. Have them press each other on the specific cost of their own position: the reference class problem for the first, the Presumptuous Philosopher for the second. Each should respond to the other's actual arguments rather than restating their own, and the dialogue should end without forcing a resolution that the arguments themselves do not support.
