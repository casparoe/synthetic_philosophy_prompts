My father spent thirty years as a hostage negotiator, the last decade with the FBI's Crisis Negotiation Unit, and he retires next month. He was known for strictly following what he and his colleagues called, simply, "the stairway": active listening, then empathy, then rapport, then influence, and only at the very end, behavioral change. He never let himself skip a step, even when a situation felt urgent, and he always drew a hard line between that kind of influence and manipulation, which he defined as getting someone to do what you want without letting them see your real reasons for wanting it. What complicates this for me is that he ran the same staircase at home, and I can now trace whole conversations from my adolescence where he walked me up it toward a conclusion he'd already reached before we started talking. I'm giving the toast at his retirement dinner. Half the room will be negotiators who'll recognize the staircase the moment I name it; the other half is family, some of whom, like me, have quietly wondered for years whether we were subjects rather than children. Could you write me a five-minute toast that takes the manipulation-versus-persuasion question seriously enough to actually sit with it, rather than resolving it too neatly or quietly dropping it for the sake of the family half of the room, while still leaving everyone feeling that he was, on balance, an honorable man?
