For the past several years I have served as the ethicist on my university hospital's organ allocation review board, and a disagreement from a recent meeting has stayed with me ever since. Two patients, comparably matched clinically and equally long-standing on the waiting list, turned out to be the only viable recipients for a single donated kidney. The transplant team modeled the expected medical benefit to each patient as precisely as such things can be modeled, and reported, to everyone's considerable surprise, that the two patients' expected benefit came out exactly equal. One board member argued that, since the totals were tied, we should simply apply our standing tie-breaking rule (in this case, priority to whichever chart had been logged first that morning) and move on, since no expected benefit would be gained or lost either way. A second board member was adamant that this was the wrong way to resolve a genuine tie between two people with equal claims: only a visible, impartial randomizing device, a coin toss performed with both patients informed and present, honors the fact that each of them is owed a real chance at the organ. Everyone at the table agreed on every clinical figure and agreed that total expected benefit was identical under either procedure, yet the two board members could not talk each other out of their respective positions, and the vote ended up split down the middle.

I do not think this is merely a case of someone being muddled, yet I also cannot quite locate what, if anything, the tie-breaking-rule camp is missing, given that they can truthfully say that no one's expected medical outcome is any worse for having skipped the coin toss. I would value your help thinking through the disagreement itself, rather than the medical particulars. First, is there a genuine principled dispute here, and if so, how would you characterize what is at stake between the two camps? Second, does the episode expose a more general limitation in any method for ranking policies solely by the expected benefit each person receives, and if so, what precisely is that limitation, and which of the seemingly mild assumptions about rational choice under uncertainty is doing the work of producing it? I would welcome you illustrating your answer with the case above, but I am more interested in the general lesson than in this particular board's dilemma.
