Ok so I had this whole argument with my friend Mara last night about Sor Juana's Respuesta a Sor Filotea (I'm sitting in on a seminar on early modern women writers, she just reads this stuff for fun, which honestly should of been my first warning that I'd lose) and I want a second opinion because neither of us backed down. Mara's position is that the Respuesta is basically a proto-feminist manifesto -- that underneath all the modesty topoi and flattery, Sor Juana is really arguing something like "women have the same rational capacities as men and deserve education on that basis," full stop. My position is that this over-modernizes her: almost every actual argument she makes for why she (and women generally) should be allowed to study is built entirely out of Church-internal materials -- the parade of learned women from scripture and history (Deborah, the Queen of Sheba, the pagan Sibyls, Jerome's pupils Paula and Eustochium), the claim that you basically can't understand theology properly without also knowing logic, rhetoric, physics, arithmetic, geometry and history, since scripture touches on all of those, and the move where she rereads Paul's line about women keeping silent in church as being about public preaching specifically and not private study, backed up by Paul elsewhere telling older women to teach younger ones. So my read is that her defense of women's education is less a general principle about intellectual equality and more a technically skilled, almost legal-style brief that wins the case using the opposing side's own rulebook -- impressive, but not quite the same move as a feminist argument from equal rational nature.

Neither of us was going to budge so now you're the tiebreaker. Does my "immanent/scriptural brief" reading actually hold up as a fair characterization of how the essay argues, or is Mara's "proto-feminist" reading better supported by the text than I'm giving it credit for -- like is there stuff I'm conveniently ignoring, the autobiographical bits about her wanting to disguise herself as a boy to get into university, cutting her own hair off as a kid as a punishment for not learning fast enough, that kind of thing, that pushes the essay past mere internal-to-orthodoxy argument? Don't just split the difference and tell me we're both a little right -- actually say which reading the text supports better, and call out anything in my summary above that's flat wrong or oversimplified.
