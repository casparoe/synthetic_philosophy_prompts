In my seminar on the epistemology of perception this term, we spent a session on Stewart Cohen's "problem of easy knowledge" and the closely related bootstrapping worry that besets dogmatist and phenomenal-conservative views of perceptual justification. On these views, an experience as of P can justify belief that P without the subject needing any prior, independent assurance that her perceptual faculties are reliable. Cohen (and, in a related vein, Jonathan Vogel) pointed out that this seemingly modest assumption licenses a suspiciously cheap route to a substantive empirical conclusion: from a string of basic, non-inferentially justified beliefs of the form "it perceptually seems that P, and P," one can infer, instance by instance, that one's faculties got it right on that occasion, and then generalize by ordinary enumerative induction to "my perceptual faculties are reliable" — all without ever stepping outside the very faculty whose credentials are supposedly being established. My students kept returning to Vogel's example of a car's fuel gauge: checking the gauge's reading against an independent look into the tank, again and again, seems like a perfectly legitimate way to establish the gauge's reliability, whereas doing the analogous thing using only the gauge's own outputs (or a second instrument wired to the same sensor) strikes everyone as illegitimate bootstrapping. What split the room was whether our perceptual faculties are relevantly more like the "independent-check" case or the "wired-to-the-same-sensor" case, and whether the dogmatist has any principled way of insisting on the former.

I'd like you to write a dialogue between two philosophers standing beside that car, gas cap open, gauge reading "full." One is a committed dogmatist who is willing to bite the bullet and accept that the bootstrapping inference to "my faculties are reliable" yields perfectly good, if unglamorous, knowledge; the other thinks the fuel-gauge case shows exactly why that inference must be illegitimate, and presses the dogmatist to say what relevant difference, if any, separates the two cases. Have them work through the disanalogies people typically float — for instance, that we possess independent background knowledge of how gauges and instruments in general come to be reliable, but no comparable outside vantage point on our own basic faculties, or that the "independent checks" in the perceptual case are not really independent of what they are supposed to be testing — and have each push the other on whether their opponent's position, applied consistently, would make it too easy to secure memory, introspection, or testimony against doubt as well. Neither character should simply concede or be a pushover for the other; I want real engagement with the strongest form of each position, developed through genuine back-and-forth rather than parallel speeches, and it's fine if it ends without a tidy resolution.
