In "On Denoting," Russell argues that denoting phrases such as "the present King of France" are not genuine referring expressions. Using the sentence "The present King of France is bald," explain: (a) how Russell's theory translates this sentence into its underlying logical form; (b) what truth value Russell assigns to the sentence, and why; and (c) how Russell's distinction between the primary and secondary occurrence of a denoting phrase resolves the apparent ambiguity in "The present King of France is not bald."

And here is my classmate's answer:

"Russell's theory treats 'the present King of France' as what he calls an incomplete symbol: it has no meaning on its own, but a sentence containing it can be paraphrased into a longer sentence that doesn't mention it at all. On his analysis, 'The present King of France is bald' really asserts three things at once: that there is at least one entity that is presently King of France (existence), that there is at most one such entity (uniqueness), and that this entity is bald (predication). Because the existence clause isn't satisfied — there is no present King of France — the sentence can't be verified, and since the description carries an existential commitment that isn't met, the sentence ends up lacking a determinate truth value: it's neither true nor false, since there's nothing for the predicate 'bald' to be truly or falsely applied to. This is exactly the move that lets Russell avoid the puzzle that troubled Meinong, who thought we needed to posit subsistent non-existent objects like 'the King of France' in order for sentences about them to be meaningful at all; Russell's incomplete-symbols approach paraphrases such sentences away instead of positing any of those shadowy entities.

Russell also uses this apparatus to explain why 'The present King of France is not bald' doesn't violate the law of excluded middle, even though there's no king to place on either side of the bald/not-bald divide. He distinguishes two readings depending on the scope, or 'occurrence,' of the description relative to the negation. On the primary occurrence reading, the negation has wide scope over the whole existential claim, so the sentence means 'it is not the case that there is a unique present King of France who is bald' — and this comes out true, precisely because there's no king at all, bald or otherwise. On the secondary occurrence reading, the negation instead falls narrowly on the predicate 'bald' inside the existential claim, so the sentence asserts 'there is a unique present King of France, and he is not bald' — and this comes out false, since it still requires a king to exist. Distinguishing these two occurrences is what lets Russell say that one reading of the negated sentence is true and the other is false, so excluded middle is preserved after all; there's no genuine third truth-value here, just an unnoticed ambiguity of scope."
