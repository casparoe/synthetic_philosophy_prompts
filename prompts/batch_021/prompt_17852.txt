Q: H publicly condemns his colleagues for plagiarism, while occasionally plagiarizing himself. Some hold that H's hypocrisy strips him of the standing to blame his colleagues, even if what he says about plagiarism is true. (a) Explain the best argument for this view. (b) Give one reason to doubt that "lacking standing" is really the right diagnosis of what's wrong with H's blaming, as opposed to some other complaint we might have about him.

Student answer: H's hypocrisy doesn't make "plagiarism is wrong" any less true - pointing out that H does it too is a classic tu quoque, and it doesn't actually rebut the charge against his colleagues. What the standing view says is that hypocrisy can still make it inappropriate for H specifically to be the one voicing that charge. Blaming someone implicitly presupposes a kind of parity between blamer and blamed - roughly, "I hold myself to this too" - and by exempting himself while judging others, H claims an authority over the norm he hasn't earned. That's a distinct unfairness from whatever the colleagues did wrong. So there are two seperate questions: whether plagiarism is wrong (yes), and whether H is well-positioned to press that charge against these people (arguably not) - and conflating the two is exactly the mistake the tu quoque objection makes. Since H does the very thing he condemns, he lacks standing on this view, and that would hold even if H never explicitly claimed any special exemption, because merely being inconsistent between your stated standards and your conduct already is a kind of unfair self-exemption, whether you announce it or not. For (b): maybe the real problem with H isn't that he's lost some special permission to blame, but just that he's less credible or seems insincere, which would be a reason to discount what he says, not a reason to think he has no standing to say it.
