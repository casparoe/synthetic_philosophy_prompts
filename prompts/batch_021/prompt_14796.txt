I've spent the last few months, for a dissertation chapter on pre-Popperian demarcation criteria, working through Kepler's astrological writings: the early tract *De Fundamentis Astrologiae Certioribus*, the 1610 *Tertius Interveniens*, and Book IV of the *Harmonices Mundi*. What strikes me most is how much energy Kepler spends mocking the "superstitious" apparatus of the astrological tradition he inherited — the twelve houses, planetary rulerships and dignities, the conventional significations of the zodiacal signs, and the whole predictive machinery for reading an individual's fate out of a nativity. Yet he does not reject astrology; he replaces all of that apparatus with his own. He licenses only a restricted set of "aspects," admitting just those angular configurations that correspond to regular polygons constructible by straightedge and compass, and he grounds their effects in a picture on which the earth, and by extension the newly formed human soul, possesses an instinctive, non-rational geometrical faculty that resonates with these harmonic configurations of light and thereby shapes temperament and weather, without (he insists) compelling any particular fated outcome.

Kepler clearly thought this put astrology on "more certain foundations" than what came before, but every time I try to state, on his own terms, the principle that licenses keeping the aspects and the earth's soul while discarding the houses and rulerships, I end up with something that looks question-begging to me. For a paper I'm workshopping, I'd like you to argue for the following thesis: that Kepler's line between his own reformed, harmony-based astrology and the "vulgar" astrology he criticized is not principled, because the very grounds on which he dismisses the astrological tradition — its lack of any independent physical mechanism, its reliance on conventional rather than demonstrated correspondences, its indifference to disconfirming instances — apply with equal force to his own theory of aspects and to the soul he attributes to the earth. I'd like the argument to take Kepler's own self-understanding seriously rather than resting on an anachronistic "but it isn't falsifiable" dismissal, and to briefly state, and then answer, the strongest reply a sympathetic Keplerian scholar could offer on his behalf — presumably one that leans on the constructibility criterion as itself a non-arbitrary, geometrically principled standard.
