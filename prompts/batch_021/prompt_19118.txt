I'm a retired experimental physicist -- spent my career on rare-event physics, the kind of work where 95% of the job is proving the bump in your data is background and not the thing you're hoping to see. Since retiring I've fallen into philosophy pretty hard, and there's a little reading group I joined that's been slowly working through Sidgwick's Methods of Ethics. A few weeks back I went down a rabbit hole after learning that this same Sidgwick was the founding president of the Society for Psychical Research and stayed heavily involved in it, alongside his wife Eleanor (who by most accounts had the sharper statistical head of the two), right up until he died almost twenty years later -- running honest-to-god empirical investigations into telepathy, apparitions and mediums: census-taking on ordinary hallucinations, controlled thought-transference tests on the Creery sisters (if I've got that name right), that whole world. My first reaction was "huh, weird footnote," but the more I read the less it looks like a footnote and more like the same project as the ethics, just run through a different apparatus.

Here's the case I want to make, still in note form rather than a proper essay: Sidgwick's ethics leaves him stuck with what he calls the dualism of practical reason -- egoism and utilitarianism are both rationally self-evident to him, they conflict in plenty of real cases, and nothing in pure reason picks a winner between them. The one clean way out is if there's some kind of cosmic bookkeeping (an afterlife, a moral order to the universe) that makes self-interest and duty coincide once you zoom out far enough. So on my reading he's not out there chasing mediums as some eccentric side hobby -- he's trying to find, empirically, the missing premise his own ethical theory needs. And here's the bit that really gets me, coming from where I sit: his actual method is recognizably a scientist's. The Census of Hallucinations is basically a base-rate argument (do coincidental "someone appeared to me right when they died" cases outrun what a chance model of everyday hallucinations predicts), and the telepathy experiments are constantly trying to rule out signalling -- the 1880s version of controlling for a confound. He never gets the result he needs, and he stays agnostic till the end. My tentative thesis is that this negative result is actually a point in his favor, evidence he didnt let twenty years of wanting an answer bend his standards of evidence, rather than a point against the ethics. Though I can hear the other side too: a philosopher whose moral theory needs two decades of ghost-hunting to prop it up has maybe thereby shown you the theory needed propping up.

So, three things I'd love your take on: (1) am I overselling the link between the psychical research and the dualism of practical reason, or is that a fairly standard way of reading Sidgwick among people who actually work on him; (2) is my "triumph of method over motive" framing defensible, or does it let him off too easy -- doesn't his lifelong agnosticism just leave the dualism exactly as unresolved as it started, which would cut against the ethics either way; and (3) is there a sharper way to state what's really at stake between my reading and the "this undermines the ethics" reading, because I feel like I'm circling a distinction I can't quite pin down.
