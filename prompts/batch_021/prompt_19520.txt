Last night a friend and I spent nearly three hours arguing about Bas van Fraassen's Reflection Principle, and neither of us came away persuaded. I'd brought it up because of some reading from a formal epistemology seminar I'm auditing; my friend, who did her graduate work in decision theory, was skeptical of the whole framework.

The Reflection Principle says, roughly, that your current credence in a proposition, conditional on knowing that your future credence in it will be some value x, should itself be x: you're supposed to treat your own anticipated future opinion the way you'd treat the testimony of a trusted expert, on pain of being exploitable by a diachronic Dutch book if you don't. Her contention was that this is really just David Lewis's Principal Principle wearing a disguise: swap "future credence" for "objective chance" as the authority you defer to, and you get the same underlying deference schema, so Reflection has no independent rationale beyond what the Principal Principle already supplies. I pushed back, pointing out that the two principles answer to different counterexamples and different metaphysical commitments: van Fraassen eventually had to restrict Reflection to future selves you have independent reason to trust as more rational or better-informed, on pain of absurdity in cases — his own example is Ulysses anticipating the Sirens' song — where you expect to be manipulated into irrationality, whereas Lewis had to carve out an "admissibility" clause and later propose a revised "New Principle" to survive the problem of chances that get undermined by how the future actually turns out. Neither of us could settle whether these are two structurally distinct patches on two structurally distinct problems, or whether she's right that it's the same fix applied twice under different names.

I'd like your help settling this properly. Please compare the Reflection Principle and the Principal Principle in enough technical detail that the comparison does real work, rather than resting at the level of the rough glosses above. Then take a side on the question we couldn't resolve: is Reflection best understood as a special case of a more general norm of deference to an "expert" credence function, one that would also cover the Principal Principle as a further instance, or do the two principles answer genuinely different questions — an agent's rational relation to her own future opinion, versus her rational relation to an objective, world-given feature like chance? I'd also like your judgment on which principle's defensive qualification — van Fraassen's retreat to trusted future selves, or Lewis's admissibility clause and its descendants — gives up more of its principle's original motivating force, and I'd like you to defend that verdict rather than simply cataloguing the moves available on each side.
