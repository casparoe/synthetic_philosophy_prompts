I'm several chapters into a novel set in Cambridge in the early 1890s, whose central figure is an ageing moral philosophy don closely modelled on Henry Sidgwick. By day he lectures on why reason alone cannot show that duty and self-interest ultimately coincide; on several evenings a month he sits in darkened rooms with fellow members of the Society for Psychical Research, timing raps, cold draughts, and the other stock effects produced by paid mediums. I am working toward a scene in which a former pupil, now a journalist with a grudge, publishes a piece accusing the don of a subtle kind of intellectual cowardice: having satisfied himself that morality is only fully rational if the universe somehow guarantees that virtue and happiness coincide, and having already lost the ordinary religious faith that once vouched for such a guarantee, he went looking in the séance room for what his own arguments could no longer supply him elsewhere. I want my character to have either a genuine answer to that charge or a genuine capitulation to it, and I'd rather not put words in his mouth until I've worked out what I actually think happened with the real man he's drawn from.

So, setting the novel aside: I'd like an essay on the historical Sidgwick rather than my fictional version of him. In the concluding chapter of The Methods of Ethics, Sidgwick argued that practical reason cannot, unaided, close the gap between the standpoint of rational egoism and that of impartial, universal benevolence — a conflict discussed in the secondary literature as the "dualism of practical reason" — and that only some further postulate, in the neighbourhood of a moral governance of the world that ensures duty and self-interest ultimately coincide, could fully secure the rational authority of morality. This was not a postulate he could simply help himself to: in 1869, well before any of this, he had already resigned his Trinity fellowship rather than go on subscribing to the Thirty-Nine Articles. He then spent the rest of his life as a founding and repeat president of the Society for Psychical Research, patiently investigating telepathy, mediumship, and apparitions, and just as patiently reporting, case after case, that the evidence fell short of proof.

Was that research best understood as a natural, even admirable extension of the same rigorous, evidence-first temperament that had produced the Methods — refusing, now on the question of a future life, to assume anything that could not be independently shown — or was it in real tension with that temperament: a decades-long exercise in motivated searching for a conclusion his ethical theory needed but his own standards of evidence would not let him simply posit? Weigh both readings seriously, including what a defender of Sidgwick's consistency could say about his famously candid admissions of failure, before committing to a verdict of your own. I would rather have a specific, defended position than a judicious "a bit of both," and rather have your own reasoning than a survey of the kind I could already compile from an encyclopedia entry.
