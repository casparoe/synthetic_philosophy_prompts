I've been following a long thread on an ACX open thread (spilling over, as these things do, into a LessWrong comment section) about what DeepMind's AlphaProof and AlphaGeometry systems actually "understand" when they translate an IMO problem into Lean and then search their way to a formally verified, silver-medal-standard proof. Predictably, someone invoked Lucas and Penrose within about ten replies, and someone else immediately started quoting Feferman's objections to the whole mind-versus-machine argument back at them. That sent me back to Gödel's own texts, and what struck me is how much more careful he was than either side of that later argument. His 1951 Gibbs lecture doesn't claim that minds beat machines; it only argues that either the human mind's mathematical powers outrun those of any given finite machine, or there exist mathematical propositions absolutely undecidable for the human mind as well — and, in a remark Hao Wang later made a great deal of, Gödel explicitly left open the possibility that the mind is itself a machine whose program is too complicated for it ever to prove complete facts about its own workings. This is, of course, also the man who was about as thoroughgoing a Platonist as the field has ever produced, happy to say that the axioms of set theory force themselves on us as true in a way not fundamentally unlike sense perception.

What I'd like from you is a careful reconstruction of what Gödel himself — not Lucas, not Penrose, not a generic stand-in for "the Platonist" — would probably have said if someone had shown him AlphaProof. Does a system that finds valid Lean proofs of hard olympiad problems through brute-force search, without anything anyone is prepared to call insight, actually bear on his disjunction at all, and if it does, which horn does it push toward, given his own caution about machines that can't fully know their own programs? Since Lean's underlying logic is itself a formal system to which incompleteness applies, is there a genuinely Gödelian reason to think AlphaProof must be limited in a way a mathematician's grasp of an axiom's truth is not — or is that just restating his disjunction and calling it an answer? And does watching mathematics get done this way, successfully, by search over derivability rather than anything resembling perception of objects, sit comfortably with his own Platonism, or does it hand ammunition to a nominalist of, say, Hartry Field's stripe? I'd rather you sit with whatever tension turns up in Gödel's actual position than smooth it over on his behalf — and if you think the question itself is somehow confused, I'd genuinely like to hear that too.
