I've spent the last couple of weeks bouncing between an old Scott Alexander post and a stack of papers on higher-order evidence, and I think I've found an analogy worth writing up as a short essay for my blog — but I'd like a sanity check on the philosophy before I commit to a full draft. The germ of the idea is this: Alexander's essay on "epistemic learned helplessness" describes adopting a standing policy of discounting how persuasive an argument feels, on the grounds that he has repeatedly watched himself get talked into positions he later recognized as false by arguments that seemed airtight at the time. I want to argue that this policy, taken at face value, puts him in exactly the position that Sophie Horowitz's Non-Akrasia Constraint rules out as irrational: believing some claim P on the strength of a given argument while simultaneously believing that arguments with that degree of felt persuasiveness are, in general, poor evidence for their conclusions. My first move is to rescue him using Alex Worsnip's distinction between structural and substantive rationality — on this reading, Alexander is structurally akratic but substantively fine, since he is correctly tracking the fact that felt persuasiveness is a poor guide to truth in domains crowded with clever cranks. My second move is to bring in Maria Lasonen-Aarnio's defense of "stubbornness" toward misleading higher-order evidence as a rival diagnosis, and to ask whether her picture is actually incompatible with Worsnip's or just a different vocabulary for the same underlying phenomenon.

What follows are notes rather than finished prose, since I would rather get reactions before doing the work of turning this into full sentences:

1. Open with a stripped-down version of David Christensen's drug case as the toy model of the structure I am after, before turning to Alexander's messier real-world version.
2. Lay out Horowitz's constraint and show why Alexander's stated policy looks akratic on its face.
3. Raise, and then try to defuse, the objection that "epistemic learned helplessness" is really just chronic suspension of judgment about P rather than continued belief in P alongside distrust of that very belief, which would make the whole akrasia framing a category error rather than a genuine instance of the phenomenon.
4. Bring in Worsnip and then Lasonen-Aarnio as competing ways of vindicating Alexander's policy, conditional on point 3 being resolved in favor of treating it as genuine akrasia rather than mere suspension of judgment.

I would be grateful if you could tell me, first, whether I have represented Horowitz, Worsnip, and Lasonen-Aarnio accurately enough to build an argument on; second, whether the objection in point 3 is actually fatal to the whole project rather than something I can defuse in a paragraph or two; and third, whether the analogy is doing real philosophical work or is closer to a pun on the word "evidence" dressed up in technical vocabulary.
