For my AP Seminar research project this year, I have been reading about the history of infographics, which is how I first encountered Otto Neurath's Isotype project and the more ambitious philosophical claims he built around it. What caught my attention is that Neurath did not merely want an attractive way to illustrate statistics. In pieces such as "International Picture Language" and "Universal Jargon and Terminology," he appears to argue that a carefully standardized picture language could serve as a genuinely universal, cross-linguistic medium for the kind of unified, physicalist science the Vienna Circle cared about — one that would avoid both the vagueness of ordinary language and the elitism of a fully formalized logical symbolism that only specialists could read. Famously, Isotype shows a larger quantity by repeating a fixed-size icon rather than by enlarging it, so that a chart never implies more precision than the underlying data actually has. At the same time, he seems to concede, almost in the same breath, that his pictograms still need verbal captions and keys to be understood at all, and I cannot quite tell how strong the "universal language" claim was ever really meant to be. This is the tension I would like to see dramatized.

Could you write a dialogue of roughly a dozen exchanges between Neurath, defending his picture-language project along the lines he actually argued for it, and a fellow logical empiricist who shares his physicalism and his contempt for "pseudo-rational" metaphysical language but doubts that any picture-based sign system can ever dispense with a verbal syntax? Feel free to imagine them talking informally, perhaps over coffee after a Vienna Circle meeting, though the setting matters far less to me than the substance. I would like the skeptic to press Neurath specifically on how Isotype is supposed to handle things that sentences manage easily, such as negation, generality (the difference between "some" and "all"), and conditional relationships, none of which repeated icons obviously handle. Please keep both characters equally sharp: Neurath should not simply concede the point, and the skeptic should not be raising objections that she herself has no serious answer to. If possible, I would like the dialogue to end with at least some clarity about whether the disagreement is a substantive one about what a "language" even is and whether pictures could ever be genuine rivals to words, or whether it is really just a disagreement of degree about how much work natural language still has to do underneath the pictures.
