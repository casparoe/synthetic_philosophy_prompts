I'm teaching a graduate seminar on rational choice this term, and we recently spent two sessions on money-pump arguments: the family of arguments trying to show that an agent whose preferences are cyclic (A > B > C > A) or incomplete can be turned into a "money pump," exploited through a sequence of trades — each one made willingly, each an improvement by the agent's own lights at the moment it's made — that leaves the agent strictly worse off than where it started while some clever bookie collects a fee at every step. The textbook moral is that rational preferences must be transitive (and, on some versions, complete) on pain of exploitability. What generated the most disagreement in class, though, wasn't the basic argument but the assorted strategies philosophers have proposed for how an agent with cyclic or incomplete preferences might resist the pump without simply capitulating and adopting a fully ordered preference relation. I promised the seminar I'd get an outside, unsentimental opinion on how well these actually hold up, so I'd like your help.

Here are the seven strategies we discussed, each stated roughly the way a defender of intransitive or incomplete preferences might put it:

1. Sophisticated choice — at each node in the decision tree, correctly anticipate what you would choose at every later node, and never take a step whose foreseeable continuation you would not endorse.
2. Resolute choice, as McClennen calls it — commit at the outset to a plan for the whole tree and carry it out even where a later step, considered in isolation, looks locally unattractive.
3. Wide framing — insist on evaluating whole bundles or entire life-histories rather than a series of local pairwise trades, so the cyclic-looking preferences are never actually pitted against one another in sequence.
4. A tie-breaking or "status quo" rule for indifference or Chang-style parity — when a further trade would only carry you to an alternative you rank as merely equal or on a par, default to keeping what you already have unless offered a strict gain net of any fee.
5. Temkin-style rejection of an underlying ranking — hold that some comparisons are "essentially comparative," so that failures of transitivity across different pairwise contexts needn't reflect any incoherence in the agent's values.
6. Bite the bullet — concede that such an agent really can be pumped by a sufficiently well-informed adversary, but deny that mere exploitability is, by itself, proof of irrationality.
7. For incompleteness specifically, a caution or "admissibility" rule — never choose an option that is dominated relative to some ordering in your set of permissible representors, even without ever settling on one complete ordering.

Could you sort these seven strategies into tiers S, A, B, C, and D, where S means the strategy genuinely defuses the money-pump argument's charge of irrationality on its own terms, and D means it's little more than a technical patch that avoids the letter of the pump while leaving the underlying rationality complaint untouched? Please give your answer as a JSON object whose keys are "S", "A", "B", "C", and "D" and whose values are lists of the relevant strategy numbers (1–7), e.g. `{"S": [2], "A": [1, 4], "B": [], "C": [3, 5], "D": [6, 7]}`; leave a tier's list empty if no strategy belongs there. You're welcome to append a short parenthetical justification after each number, but the tier placement itself is what I'm really after.
