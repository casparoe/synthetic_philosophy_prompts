Student's answer:

"The result targets what's usually called Lewis's Thesis: that the probability of an indicative conditional 'if A, C' should equal the conditional probability P(C|A), i.e., P(A > C) = P(C|A). This looks intuitively right because it matches the Ramsey test — to evaluate 'if A, C' you provisionally add A to your stock of beliefs and see how confident you are in C — and it matches ordinary practice: if someone asks how likely it is that a flight gets cancelled given that the storm hits, we answer by imagining the storm has hit and reporting our confidence in a cancellation, which just is a conditional probability.

The triviality argument works by requiring the thesis to hold not just for our actual credences P, but for every credence function we'd have after conditionalizing on new evidence — otherwise the equation would just be an accident of our current opinions rather than a fact about what conditionals mean. In particular it has to hold after conditionalizing on C and after conditionalizing on ¬C. Conditionalizing on C, the right-hand side becomes P(C | A&C), which is 1 whenever P(A&C) > 0; conditionalizing on ¬C, it becomes P(C | A&¬C), which is 0 whenever P(A&¬C) > 0. So the thesis forces P(A > C | C) = 1 and P(A > C | ¬C) = 0. By the law of total probability, P(A > C) = P(A>C|C)P(C) + P(A>C|¬C)P(¬C) = P(C). Combined with the original thesis, this gives P(C|A) = P(A > C) = P(C) — A and C come out probabilistically independent, for any A and C whatsoever, which is absurd, since e.g. whether it's cloudy is obviously relevant to whether it rains. So the thesis can't be true in general.

Lewis took this to show that indicative conditionals don't have truth conditions after all, siding here with Adams's view that 'if'-sentences merely express degrees of confidence rather than propositions. This forced Lewis to give up his own possible-worlds account of conditionals as a general theory, since that account assigns 'if A, C' a truth value at every world."
