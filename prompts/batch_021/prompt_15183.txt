I spent about thirty years as a molecular biologist before I retired, and I remember exactly where I was sitting when the ENCODE consortium dropped their big 2012 Nature paper claiming that something like 80% of the human genome is "functional" -- and I remember the fight that broke out right afterward just as clearly, with people like Dan Graur writing absolutely scathing rebuttals arguing that most of that 80% is just biochemical noise (a bit of transcription here, some protein binding there) rather than anything evolution actually built for a reason. At the time I sort of nodded along without thinking too hard about why the two camps couldn't just agree on what "functional" even meant. Now that I've retired and started reading philosophy of science for fun (my local library runs a little discussion group, mostly other retired academics, and we've been chewing on this lately), I've realized the fight is basically a rerun of a much older argument between two camps of philosophers of biology: the "causal role" camp following Cummins (1975), who say a part's function is just whatever causal contribution it makes to some capacity of the system you happen to be interested in explaining right now, no history required; and the "etiological" or "selected effects" camp following Wright (1973) and later Neander, who say a trait's real function is whatever effect explains why natural selection kept it around, so a stretch of DNA that's merely biochemically active without ever having been selected for that activity isn't functional in any interesting sense at all.

The more I read the more stuck I get, because both views seem to capture something real. The causal-role view matches how working biologists and doctors actually talk day to day -- nobody re-derives the selective history of the pancreas every time they want to explain insulin -- but it apparently has trouble with "malfunction": if a valve stops doing what it usually does, Cummins' own view seems to say it simply no longer has that function anymore, rather than that its failing at a function it still possesses, which feels backwards. The etiological view handles malfunction nicely, but then it struggles with cases like a brand-new beneficial mutation that hasn't been "tested" by selection yet (does it have zero function until enough generations pass?), or classic exaptations like feathers, which were apparently selected early on for insulation and only later got recruited for flight -- so which effect gets to be "the" function, the old one or the new one?

Could you write me an essay that actually wades into this, using the ENCODE fight as your main illustration alongside at least one or two of the other puzzle cases above, lays out the strongest version of each side's case, and then actually commits to an answer -- something like "causal-role is basically right and here's why the malfunction worry doesn't really work," or "etiological is basically right and here's how it handles the hard cases," or some other specific, principled position -- rather than just landing on "well, both notions are useful in different contexts," which I already believe and would like you to move past.
