In my seminar on classical Islamic philosophy, we've just spent two weeks on al-Ghazālī's occasionalism: his argument, against the philosophers, that fire has no power in itself to burn cotton, that what we call cause and effect is really only God's unbroken habit (ʿāda), and that He can suspend that habit at will, as in a miracle. My grandmother, who taught Ashʿari theology for forty years and died earlier this week, held this as more than a classroom position. Before she grew too weak to say so, she asked that nobody stand up at her funeral and say the cancer "took her" or that her body "finally gave out," as though illness were an agent doing something to her in its own right, rather than merely the occasion on which God, as always, acted directly. I'm giving that eulogy tomorrow, to a room split between people from her mosque who will catch every reference and cousins and coworkers who have never heard the word "occasionalism" and just want to grieve her. Please write it -- a short eulogy, five minutes or so. It has to comfort the second group without smuggling in the causal language she rejected, and it has to find an honest way to credit her patience and generosity to her, as things she actually did, even though her own theology, through the doctrine of kasb, made "she chose this" a much thinner claim than it ordinarily sounds. I don't fully share her view myself, but it's her eulogy, not mine, and I'd like to get it right.
