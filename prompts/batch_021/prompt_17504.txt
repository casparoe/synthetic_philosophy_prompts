I'm about two-thirds into a novel that follows the friendship between Rousseau and Diderot from their Encyclopédie days down to the ugly break in the late 1750s, and I've hit the scene I keep putting off. In my version, the real hinge of the split isn't the "only the wicked man lives alone" line everyone always brings up (though thats in there too, as the thing that finally cracks it open) but the theatre question underneath it -- whether Geneva should get a playhouse. I want to actually stage that argument between them as a real argument, not Rousseau ranting while Diderot rolls his eyes off-page: Diderot genuinely making the case that drama educates the sentiments and does real moral work, Rousseau genuinely making the republican case against ever letting a theatre into a small, equal, virtuous city -- and each of them actually landing blows on the other instead of the two of them just talking past one another.

Could you write that scene for me as a dialogue -- just the exchange, no stage directions or set dressing needed, maybe 700-1000 words? I'd like it to use the real substance of Rousseau's side from the Lettre à d'Alembert (actors as skilled counterfeiters of feeling who sell their own persons, the idleness and luxury and inequality a theatre imports into a small republic, the preference for the open civic festival where everyone participates and nobody's merely a spectator) against what Diderot actually thought theatre was for -- the bourgeois drama stuff, and the "paradox of the actor" idea, even though he only wrote that down properly later in life, so feel free to let him reach for it a little early if it sharpens the argument. I don't want a generic "is fiction good or bad for you" chat, since that's easy to fake -- I want it to feel like their actual disagreement, where neither one is obviously winning, because I genuinely haven't decided which way I want the reader to lean by the end of the chapter.
