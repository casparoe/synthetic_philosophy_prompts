I'm a backend engineer with no formal philosophy training, but I got nerd-sniped a few weeks ago by something a friend in a philosophy PhD program mentioned over dinner: Kripke's "quus" example from his book on Wittgenstein and rule-following. The setup is that you're asked to compute "68 + 57" for the first time in your life and get 125, but a skeptic points out that nothing about your past use of "+" rules out that you always meant "quus" rather than "plus," where quus agrees with plus on every pair of numbers you've actually added before but is stipulated to return 5 whenever either number is 57 or larger. I haven't been able to stop mapping this onto things from work — a function that passes every test in the suite while remaining compatible with infinitely many other implementations that would only diverge on inputs nobody thought to try, or the silent branch that only shows up once some value crosses a bit-width boundary. Apparently the point isn't really about arithmetic: Kripke uses it to argue, via a reading of Wittgenstein's remarks on rule-following, that no fact about anyone's past behavior, dispositions, or mental states can fix the meaning of any word at all, which turns it into a full-blown skeptical paradox about meaning.

What I'd like from you is an essay, maybe 800–1000 words, that treats this as a live controversy rather than settled trivia. Lay out the skeptical argument precisely enough that I could reconstruct it for a colleague using my overflow-bug analogy, then take seriously the two main families of response: "straight" solutions that try to locate some actual fact that fixes meaning (the obvious candidate being your disposition to answer one way rather than another), and Kripke's own "skeptical solution," which gives up on truth-conditions altogether and instead tries to rescue meaning-talk through a community's practice of endorsing and correcting uses of a word. I'm especially interested in whether the standard objections to each side actually land: the complaint that dispositions are finite and can misfire, so they can't underwrite a normative fact about what I ought to say, versus the complaint that the communitarian move seems to strand a solitary rule-follower — Robinson Crusoe alone on his island — with no meaning at all, which looks like the wrong result. You don't need to adjudicate whether Kripke is even reading Wittgenstein correctly (I gather that's its own separate fight); just take "Kripkenstein's" argument on its own philosophical merits. Don't hedge into a "both sides have a point" ending — tell me whether you think there's a genuine fact of the matter about what any of us mean by our words, and defend that verdict against the strongest version of the opposing view.
