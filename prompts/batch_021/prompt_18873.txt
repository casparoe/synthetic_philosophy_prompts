My mother had Alzheimer's, and for a long stretch of it she was completely convinced her mind was fine - not lying, not really in denial either, just genuinely unaware that anything had changed, which apparantly is a real documented feature of the disease itself (the doctors called it anosognosia) rather than plain stubbornness. I'm a retired biochemist (spent thirty-odd years doing enzyme kinetics, of all things), and since retiring I've finally started reading some proper philosophy - working through an epistemology anthology a friend from my old book club lent me, mostly stuff about knowledge, belief, and how sure we can ever really be of things. It's slow going but I'm enjoying being a beginner at something again, and it's got me turning this old worry about my mother over in my head in a way I hadn't before.

What strikes me now is that it can't have happened overnight - it must have crept in so gradually, in steps too small to notice day to day, that on any given morning she'd have felt exactly as sharp as the morning before, right up until she was clearly not sharp at all. I'm about the age now that she was when it started, and I catch myself doing the standard reassuring thing: "I feel sharp, my memory's fine, surely I'd notice if something were going wrong." I said as much to my wife and she told me, essentially, not to be so silly, of course I'd notice. But the more I sit with that, the less comfort I actually get from it, because my mother presumably felt that exact same certainty at every stage, including the later ones where she was already pretty far gone. If I'd think "I'm fine, I'd notice" at every single step along a gradual slide, then the fact that I currently think it doesn't seem to tell me anything about whether it's actually true right now. Is my wife right that I'm just overthinking a non-issue here, or is there something genuinely tricky going on, and if so, how should I actually think about it - is there any better ground to stand on than just trusting my own sense that I'm fine?
