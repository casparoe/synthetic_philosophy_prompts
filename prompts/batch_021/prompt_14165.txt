"The grudge informer cases came up after WWII, when German courts had to deal with people who'd used Nazi-era laws to get rid of enemies or family members. Hart, as a legal positivist, held that law and morality are separate — a rule counts as valid law if it was enacted according to the system's own rules, no matter how evil its content is. So Hart thought the Nazi statute the informer relied on really was law at the time, and if postwar courts wanted to punish her anyway, the honest way to do it was to pass a new law making her act retroactively criminal, rather than pretend the old statute had never been law at all. Fuller disagreed. He argued that law has an 'inner morality' made up of eight principles — things like generality, publicity, and consistency between the rules as declared and how they're actually enforced — and that the Nazi regime violated these so badly that its enactments don't really count as law. So for Fuller there's no retroactivity problem, because there was never a valid law protecting the informer's actions to begin with. This inner morality is basically the same thing as the 'minimum content of natural law' that Hart himself later grants in The Concept of Law — both are just saying that any real legal system has to have some baseline of moral content, like rules against violence, or it stops functioning as law. So even though they disagree about how to handle the informer case itself, Hart and Fuller basically end up agreeing that law has to have some moral minimum built into it."
