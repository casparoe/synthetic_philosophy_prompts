So I'm interning this summer at a small health-economics consultancy that does cost-effectiveness analysis for public health programs, and this week I've basically been staring at QALY spreadsheets trying to figure out whether its even legitimate to treat one QALY as worth the same amount regardless of whose life it's attached to. When I brought this up with the senior analyst on my team — basically, "aren't we just assuming we can compare how much a year of health means to totally different people?" — she waved it off with "that's an old problem, Harsanyi basically solved it decades ago," which sent me down a rabbit hole all weekend instead of doing my actual assigned reading.

Here's where I've gotten to. Harsanyi's aggregation theorem says that if individual preferences and "social" preferences both satisfy the expected-utility axioms, and the social ranking respects unanimity (if everyone's indifferent between two options, so is society), then social welfare has to be some weighted sum of individual utility functions. Fine so far. But then I found a bunch of secondary reading (mostly citing Sen, and apparently a guy named Weymark wrote the paper everyone treats as the last word on it) arguing that this doesn't actually buy you interpersonal comparability, because each person's utility function in that theorem is only pinned down up to their own arbitrary zero point and scale — the theorem itself is completely silent on how to line up "one unit" of my utility with "one unit" of yours. So going from "some weighted sum" to "the equal-weighted, utilitarian sum" requires sneaking in exactly the interpersonal comparison that was supposedly still up for grabs. My roommate, who's an econ PhD student, thinks I'm being unfair to Harsanyi here, and that the actual comparability work is supposed to come from his earlier "impartial observer" paper, not the aggregation theorem by itself, and that the two are meant to be read as one package.

I'd like you to actually build the skeptical case here as rigorously as you can, partly because I want to be able to hold my own with my roommate about it: argue that Harsanyi's aggregation theorem, whether taken alone or together with the impartial-observer/equiprobability model, does not establish that interpersonal utility comparisons are meaningful, and that presenting it as having "solved" the comparability problem is at best a sleight of hand. I don't want the lazy version of this argument (something like "well, feelings are subjective, so who's to say" — I could've come up with that myself); I want you to actually pin down the exact point in the formal setup where an unearned comparability assumption gets smuggled in, and explain why the impartial-observer move doesn't rescue it.
