The following is a question from an upper-level exam in a philosophy of action course, together with one student's answer to it.

Question: Reconstruct Galen Strawson's Basic Argument for the conclusion that no one is ever truly or ultimately morally responsible for what they do. Your answer should explain (a) why Strawson takes the argument to succeed independently of whether determinism is true, and (b) how the argument's conclusion bears on the compatibilist tradition represented by P. F. Strawson's "Freedom and Resentment."

Student answer: "Strawson's Basic Argument turns on the idea that being truly responsible for an action requires being responsible for the mental states—the intentions, values, and springs of action—from which the action issued. But one could be responsible for those mental states only if one had chosen them in light of some prior set of values or preferences, and one could be responsible for that prior set only by having chosen it in light of some still-earlier set, and so on. Since this regress cannot terminate in a self that made itself from nothing, no one can ever satisfy the condition on genuine responsibility, and Strawson concludes that free will itself is an illusion.

Crucially, Strawson thinks the argument bites regardless of whether determinism is true. If our actions are causally determined by prior states of the world, we obviously did not choose the ultimate causes of our character. But indeterminism does not help either, since an action that issues from a random, undetermined event in the brain is not thereby an action for which we are more responsible—if anything, mere chance seems to undermine responsibility even further. This is what distinguishes the Basic Argument from a garden-variety hard-determinist argument: it does not rest on the truth of the causal-determination premise at all.

This connects naturally to the elder Strawson's work. In "Freedom and Resentment," P. F. Strawson likewise concluded that our reactive attitudes—resentment, gratitude, indignation—cannot be rationally sustained once we see that their target's character is ultimately the product of factors outside his control, so that, like his son after him, he held that true moral responsibility has no coherent metaphysical foundation."
