So I'm about three-quarters through a hard SF novel that ends trillions of years in the future, basically at the heat death of the universe, and while researching what de Sitter space actually does forever (it doesnt just go dark and sit there, it keeps quantum-fluctuating at some absurdly tiny temperature forever) I fell into the "Boltzmann brain" rabbit hole and now my actual chapter isn't getting written. The basic idea, if I've got it right: if the universe really does expand forever under a cosmological constant, then given infinite time, random thermal fluctuations will occasionally assemble a fully conscious brain, complete with fake memories of a whole life and cosmic history, straight out of the vacuum -- and because "forever" is so long, these freak brains end up vastly outnumbering every observer who ever came about the normal way (stars, planets, evolution), since that kind of observer could only ever form during one finite early window anyway.

What's messing with me is that this isn't just a cute thought experiment -- physicists actually treat it as a live problem for certain cosmological models. There was a paper in the early 2000s, I think by Dyson and a couple of others (may be misremembering), that first made a stink about it, and I've seen people like Sean Carroll argue that a theory predicting you're almost certainly one of these freak fluctuating brains is somehow "cognitively unstable," since the good evidence you'd cite for believing anything, including the theory itself, is exactly the kind of thing a Boltzmann brain hallucinates having. Apparently some cosmologists even use "does this measure avoid predicting we're almost certainly freak brains" as an actual filter for choosing between candidate mathematical measures over the eternally inflating multiverse, which feels backwards to me but is apparently a real move people make. There's also an older wrinkle where Boltzmann himself floated something similar to explain why our region of the universe has low entropy at all, and the standard rebuttal is that a small fluctuation (just a brain, or just a solar system) is astronomically likelier than a big one (a whole orderly galaxy with a real billion-year history) -- so his own fix backfires the same way. (That old bit is just context for how I got here; the thing I actually need settled is the future, de Sitter version.)

I want a chapter-ending scene where a physicist character, watching the last stars go out, has this realization out loud, and I need to actually understand the debate to write it honestly instead of just gesturing at "spooky physics."
