"Gödel's argument for an afterlife appears in letters to his mother, who had grown skeptical about religion as she got older, and he tries to win her back over using philosophical rather than religious language. His key premise is that the world is fundamentally rational rather than 'thoroughly chaotic and meaningless,' and that a rational world would not spend decades letting a mind develop a rich character and body of experience only to annihilate the whole thing at death — that would be a kind of cosmic waste that doesn't fit with the world being rationally ordered. So he takes some form of personal survival to be the more reasonable position, and treats the modern assumption against an afterlife as more of a prejudice of the age than a conclusion anyone actually argued for. This fits with how much he admired Leibniz, including the monadology.

This connects directly to his ontological proof, which he published in 1970: using modal logic, he defines 'positive properties' and shows that a being having all of them must necessarily exist, following basically the same strategy Anselm and Leibniz used to try to derive God's existence from the concept of a perfect being alone. Since Gödel counts immortality of the soul as one of the positive properties, the necessary existence of this being guarantees personal immortality as a theorem of the proof itself, which is presumably why he felt comfortable defending both claims out of the same rationalist outlook."
