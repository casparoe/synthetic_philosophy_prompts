I've been working on a long-form piece about the St. Petersburg paradox and, more specifically, about how unsettled its "resolution" still is almost three centuries after Daniel Bernoulli first tackled it for the St. Petersburg Academy of Sciences. What's struck me most while reporting this piece is how many genuinely distinct diagnoses of the puzzle are on offer, with surprisingly little agreement about which of them actually gets at the root of the problem rather than just relabeling it. Besides Bernoulli's own appeal to a diminishing marginal utility of wealth, there is Gabriel Cramer's earlier square-root-utility proposal, which Bernoulli cites approvingly; Buffon's attempt to define a threshold of "moral impossibility" below which minuscule probabilities can simply be disregarded; Karl Menger's 1934 "super-Petersburg" games, which show that a merely diminishing (rather than strictly bounded) utility function doesn't actually block the paradox from recurring in a worse form; the claim, sometimes made on the basis of cumulative prospect theory, that probability weighting rather than utility curvature is doing the real work; Ole Peters's more recent argument that the whole framework of expectation values is the culprit, and that the game should instead be evaluated by its time-average growth rate; the deflationary observation that no real counterparty has an infinite bankroll, so the game as stated is simply unrealizable; and, most radically, the position that expected-value (or expected-utility) maximization was never the right decision norm for a case like this to begin with.
