Thomas Hobbes is not someone I would have associated with mathematics -- as a software engineer with no background in philosophy, I mainly knew him as the author of Leviathan and the idea of the social contract. But I recently learned, from a book on the history of mathematics, that Hobbes spent a substantial part of his later life insisting, against repeated and detailed correction, that he had solved classical problems like squaring the circle. His main antagonist was John Wallis, the Savilian Professor of Geometry at Oxford, who published one exacting refutation of Hobbes's constructions after another; Hobbes never conceded a single point, and instead responded with pamphlet after pamphlet accusing Wallis and the university mathematicians of pedantry and bad faith, continuing this, by all accounts, until his death. What strikes me as genuinely puzzling is that this is the same Hobbes who, elsewhere, holds geometry up as the one branch of knowledge in which human beings can attain real certainty -- precisely, it seems, because we ourselves stipulate the definitions and constructions involved, rather than merely observing an external world we didn't make -- and who wants to build his civil philosophy (his account of sovereignty and the commonwealth) on that same method, on the assumption that sufficiently careful, self-authored definitions can yield comparably certain conclusions about politics.

I'd like you to write me an essay that takes this tension seriously as a philosophical problem rather than a mere biographical footnote. The organizing question is: given Hobbes's own prolonged and total failure at the one discipline he treated as the paradigm of certainty, how much confidence should we place in his larger claim that political philosophy can and should be built on a geometrical foundation? You might argue that the Wallis affair is ultimately beside the point -- a matter of one stubborn man's ego rather than any flaw in the underlying epistemology -- or you might argue that it is symptomatic of a deeper problem with Hobbes's whole approach, one that would only bite harder once you move from geometrical to political definitions. Weigh both readings seriously, but don't leave the matter unresolved: end with a clear, defended verdict on how much of Hobbes's geometrical ambition for political philosophy survives this episode. A conditional verdict (something like, "the argument holds for narrowly definitional sciences but collapses once applied to politics") is fine, as long as you actually commit to it.
