I've spent the last few weeks preparing a graduate seminar on the philosophy of emotion, and a disagreement broke out after last week's session that the group never quite resolved. We had just worked through Radford's original puzzle about how one can be moved to pity by Anna Karenina's fate while knowing perfectly well that she never existed, along with Walton's make-believe answer to it and the rival "thought theory" -- associated with Lamarque and, in a rather different form, Carroll -- on which what matters for emotion is vividly entertaining a thought, not holding an existence-belief. Two of my students, Owen and Naomi, kept the argument going on the walk back to their offices, and since I would rather not referee my own students, I would value an outside assessment of how their exchange actually goes, move by move.

(1) Owen defended Walton's view: what we feel toward fictional characters and events is not real fear, pity, or grief, but "quasi-emotion" -- a make-believe analogue generated within a game of pretense. His evidence was behavioral: if we genuinely believed the killer on screen were a threat, we would call the police or leave the theater; since we do neither, whatever we feel lacks the motivational profile constitutive of genuine fear. (2) Naomi objected that this proves too little. Someone standing on a glass-floored observation deck who correctly believes the floor is safe, and who does nothing evasive, can still feel unmistakable, visceral fear of the height; inaction shows only that a self-protective belief is absent or outweighed, not that the feeling itself is unreal. And if unmotivated fear can be genuine there, she said, the thought theorists are right that vividly entertaining a thought, with no existence-belief at all, can be enough to cause a real emotion. (3) Owen replied that the analogy fails: the glass-floor case still involves a real height, real gravity, and a real, if well-managed, possibility of falling, so some actual danger is being tracked, merely discounted; a fictional killer tracks nothing at all. Thought theory, he said, owes an account of why entertaining a thought about a wholly nonexistent threat should produce a real emotion, when every other uncontroversial case of fear-without-action still answers to some genuine feature of the subject's situation. (4) Naomi answered with nightmares, and with the case of deliberately imagining a loved one's death: both produce unmistakably real distress while tracking nothing actual whatsoever, which is exactly the mechanism thought theory posits. If Owen wants to insist that only reality-tracking cases count as real emotion, she said, he now owes an explanation of why nightmare terror doesn't count either -- and that is a much bigger bullet to bite than anything her view asks of him.

Neither of them budged, and I don't think either was simply confused; both were drawing on real resources in the literature. Could you go through their exchange and adjudicate it move by move?
