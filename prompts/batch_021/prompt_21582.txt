After a long career in experimental physics, I've spent my retirement working through the philosophy of language, and I've become rather preoccupied with what's often nicknamed "Kripkenstein" — the rule-following paradox that Kripke develops out of his reading of Wittgenstein (a reading that some Wittgenstein scholars consider more Kripke's invention than Wittgenstein's own view). The argument, as I understand it, is that no fact about my past behavior or mental states, however exhaustively specified, can settle whether, in adding two numbers I have never summed before, I am really applying "plus" or some deviant function — "quus" — that agrees with addition on every case I have actually computed but diverges beyond it. It strikes me as a cousin of the underdetermination of a physical law by finite data, except that here the target is the determinacy of meaning itself, which makes it much harder for me to shrug off.

I'd like you to write a short story that treats this paradox as a live threat rather than a seminar exercise. Give me a character, in any setting you like, who is forced by some concrete turn of events to confront the possibility that there was never a fact about which rule they had actually been following all along — and make the stakes real and non-academic: something the character stands to lose, not merely a puzzle to solve. Please don't just restate the quus example itself; find the story's own situation for it. And let the ending commit to some actual position on the paradox — Kripke's "sceptical solution," a dispositionalist reply, or a view of your own — rather than trailing off into unresolved mystification.
