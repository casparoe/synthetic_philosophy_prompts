I'm an ML engineer at a small agricultural robotics startup, and a debate that broke out between two of my coworkers over lunch last week has been bugging me ever since. Our current product is a strawberry-picking arm whose vision model is trained to fire a "pick" signal on ripe strawberries. It performs well in our test greenhouse, but during a field pilot it also started firing reliably on red plastic end-caps used on some of the drip-irrigation lines, which happen to be roughly the same size, color, and shape as a ripe strawberry. My colleague Marcus called this a straightforward bug: the model is supposed to represent "ripe strawberry," and the end-caps aren't strawberries. My colleague Aditi, who minored in philosophy and has been dying to use it at work, said it wasn't that simple and started talking about something she called "teleosemantics." I've since skimmed a bit about it on my own, but I'd like an outside, neutral read on their actual back-and-forth, which I've reconstructed below as faithfully as I can, because I don't think either of them was simply wrong.

(1) Marcus opened with the obvious take: the model malfunctioned, since it was built to detect strawberries and end-caps aren't strawberries. (2) Aditi replied that nothing in training ever forced the network to distinguish "ripe strawberry" from "red round thing in that size range," since every strawberry it was ever shown during training was also a red round thing in that size range, and the loss function never once penalized it for failing to reject a red round non-strawberry; so maybe the model's real content was always the more disjunctive property, in which case firing on end-caps isn't an error at all, just the network doing exactly what it has always done. (3) Marcus objected that this move proves too much, since you can always redescribe any detector's target more disjunctively after the fact; he brought up something he half-remembered about frogs snapping at BB pellets shot past them, and said we still want to say the frog's snapping circuit is "for" catching flies, with pellets merely fooling it, rather than concluding its real content was "small dark moving thing" all along — so a similar move should save "strawberry" as our model's real content too. (4) Aditi said the frog case doesn't transfer cleanly: we call the pellet a misfire for the frog only because catching flies specifically is what kept the frog's ancestors alive and got that exact circuit copied forward, generation after generation, whereas nothing analogous ever selected our network for strawberries specifically over red-round-things in general, since the loss function rewarded both identically; without a comparable history favoring strawberry-detection as such, she said, there's no principled basis for calling the end-cap detections errors rather than successes. (5) Marcus countered that we do have such a history: earlier in development, whenever the model fired on scraps of red plastic during testing, the team docked it and retrained on harder negatives — doesn't that count, by Aditi's own standard, as selecting for "strawberry" and against "red-round-thing-in-general," giving the model exactly the kind of history she says is required? (6) Aditi answered that this history is real but too narrow to do that work, since the team only ever retrained against whatever confounders they happened to notice at the time (mostly red autumn leaves blown into frame), never against anything cap-shaped — so by her own logic, end-caps were simply never among the things the model's history selected against, and remain part of its content whether we like it or not.

Could you go through this move by move and say who's right about what at each numbered step, rather than just declaring an overall winner? I'd also be curious whether there's some consideration neither of them raised that should actually bear on this.
