I spent almost forty years as an experimental physicist doing optics and lens design, and now that I'm retired I've traded journal articles for philosophy books in the afternoons. Lately I've been stuck on the old "argument from illusion" in philosophy of perception, and it bugs me every time because the stock example is always the stick that looks bent when you stick it in water, or the coin that looks elliptical from an angle. As an optics guy I know exactly what's happening in those cases - it's just refraction and perspective, completely lawful and unmysterious - so it always seemed a bit much to me that philosophers use them to argue we never directly perceive physical objects at all, only some intermediary "sense-datum," or that we need some elaborate disjunctivist story on which illusion and veridical perception don't even share a common underlying nature. I mentioned this to a friend who did a philosophy PhD and he just shrugged and said "well, some naive realists think illusion is easy and its only hallucination that's the real problem," which sounded right to me, but he couldn't really explain why, and now I can't stop chewing on it.

So here's what I'd like you to do: argue, as forcefully as you can, for the position that the traditional argument from illusion (bent sticks, elliptical coins, and the like) gives naive/direct realists no trouble at all, because a direct realist can simply insist that objects genuinely do look different from different positions and under different viewing conditions - no sense-data, no shared "common factor," no disjunctivism required. Only full-blown HALLUCINATION, where there is no object out there at all, should force any move toward disjunctivism, on this view. Please don't just summarize the standard debate for me, I've already read the SEP-type overviews - actually build the strongest version of the argument, and don't let yourself off easy by only using the perspectival cases I mentioned above; take on harder illusion cases too, like double vision or the Muller-Lyer illusion, where its much less obvious that "it just looks different from here" can do all the work.
