A friend and I spent a long evening last week arguing about "commitment races" — the idea, familiar from Schelling-style bargaining theory and more recently from discussions in AI safety, that an agent can gain decisive leverage in a negotiation simply by locking itself irrevocably into a hard-line position before its counterpart manages to do the same, in the spirit of the classic game-theoretic image of winning a round of chicken by visibly tearing out your own steering wheel so that the other driver has no rational option left but to swerve. Push that same logic onto two bargaining superintelligences, or two civilizations, or two agents trying to strike an acausal deal across possible worlds, and you get a genuinely bleak picture: everyone is incentivized to commit as fast and as irreversibly as possible, before they've had time to think matters through, since deliberating carefully is itself a competitive disadvantage. My friend found the whole thing simply depressing; I insisted there was something almost farcical about it once you sit with the mechanics, but when actually pressed to produce something funny, I came up with nothing.

So: write me a joke about commitment races that would land specifically with someone who knows this literature, where the humor turns on the real mechanics of the problem — the race to commit before you're ready, the advantage of being unable to reconsider, the risk of two premature commitments colliding catastrophically — rather than being a generic joke about robots, negotiation, or stubbornness that merely namedrops the term. Extra credit if it also gestures at the recursive bind whereby even the decision of when to stop deliberating and commit is itself something you'd, ideally, have wanted to commit to in advance.
