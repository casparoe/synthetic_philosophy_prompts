Kavka's toxin puzzle keeps getting name-dropped whenever decision theory comes up on the rationalist blogs and forums I've been reading for years, usually in the same breath as Newcomb's problem and Parfit's hitchhiker, but I realized recently that I've never actually seen anyone work out what follows from it rather than merely gesture at it. The setup, as I understand it: an eccentric billionaire tells you that if, by midnight tonight, you form the genuine intention to drink a toxin tomorrow afternoon -- one that will make you painfully sick for a day but leave no lasting harm -- she will have a million dollars wired to your account tomorrow morning, well before you are due to drink anything. You don't have to actually go through with drinking it to keep the money; the intention alone, formed tonight, is what earns the payout. Kavka's worry is that this intention looks impossible to form rationally: by noon tomorrow you will already have the million dollars no matter what, so drinking the toxin then would be pure self-inflicted misery with no offsetting benefit, and it isn't obvious how you can honestly intend, tonight, to do something you can already foresee your perfectly rational future self will have no reason to do. I gather this puzzle grew out of Kavka's other work on the ethics of nuclear deterrence, where the same structure appears in asking whether a country can rationally intend to retaliate once deterrence has already failed and retaliation no longer serves any purpose -- which suggests the toy example is meant to isolate a much more general problem about intentions.

Please describe what you take to be the most important implications of the toxin puzzle. I would rather have your own considered view than a neutral survey of the standard replies (Gauthier's constrained maximization, McClennen's resolute choice, and so on). In particular, I want to know what the puzzle implies about whether rationality has to be assessed at the level of policies or standing dispositions rather than individual momentary choices, and whether making that move -- which strikes me as basically what functional or updateless decision theories do when they handle Parfit's hitchhiker -- actually dissolves the puzzle or just relocates the same difficulty into the question of why an agent who already has the toxin in hand at noon tomorrow would still go through with executing the policy rather than quietly defecting from it. Finally, I'd like to know what, if anything, the puzzle implies for how much confidence we should place in our own sincere resolutions and promises in ordinary life, given how many of them share exactly this structure, where the benefit of having made the commitment is secured well before the moment at which actually keeping it starts to cost us anything.
