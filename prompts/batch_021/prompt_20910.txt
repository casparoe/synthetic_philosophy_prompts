I'm currently taking a graduate seminar on modality and possible-worlds semantics, and this week we moved from Kripke's discussion of transworld identity in *Naming and Necessity* to David Lewis's counterpart-theoretic alternative. In section, several of us got stuck on how a committed Lewisian is actually supposed to answer Kripke's well-known objection involving Hubert Humphrey: the worry that Humphrey himself has no stake in whether some merely resembling individual wins an election in another world, so that paraphrasing "Humphrey could have won" in terms of a counterpart's victory seems to change the subject rather than capture what we meant in the first place. Our TA's answer—that the counterpart relation is fixed by context to track exactly the resemblance we care about—struck a few of us as question-begging rather than a real reply, and I'd like to think the exchange through more carefully before I have to write a short paper on it.

Would you write a dialogue between a counterpart theorist (broadly Lewisian) and a transworld identity theorist (broadly Kripkean/Plantingian, willing to invoke individual essences to secure genuine identity across worlds) who run into each other after a colloquium talk and end up arguing this out, using the Humphrey case as their running example throughout? I'd like the counterpart theorist to give the strongest available reply to the Humphrey objection, and I'd like the transworld identity theorist to then press at least one further problem that is specific to counterpart theory rather than a restatement of Humphrey—for instance, the awkwardness of an individual having several equally good counterparts in a single other world, which makes talk of contingent identity shaky, or the cost of anti-haecceitism, i.e., of denying that two qualitatively identical worlds could differ only in which individual is which. Please have each side genuinely engage with the other's specific point rather than restate their opening position, keep both sides equally sharp, and let the dialogue end with real disagreement still on the table rather than a tidy concession.
