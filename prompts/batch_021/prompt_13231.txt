I'm taking a course on formal epistemology this semester, and we've recently spent several weeks on the Bayesian approach to confirmation, the general idea being that a piece of evidence supports a hypothesis to the extent that learning it should rationally raise your confidence in that hypothesis. Something that happened with my family over winter break has been nagging at me ever since, because it seems to test that idea directly, even though it has nothing to do with science.

For as long as anyone in my family can remember, we have watered our vegetable garden on Sundays at exactly 4 p.m., even though nothing about the plants, the soil, or the local weather requires that particular hour; it is simply what we have always done, and no one still living knows why. Over break, my great-aunt, who has spent months going through old letters and parish records, arrived at dinner with a theory: she believes our great-great-grandfather managed a small textile mill, and that mill towns in that county customarily rang the mill bell at exactly 4 p.m. every Sunday as a signal for workers' families to start their evening chores before the work week began again, so our family simply kept watering at that hour long after the mill and the bell were gone. She was delighted, because, as she put it, her theory "gets" the exact hour we still water at, and she takes this as real evidence that she has correctly identified our ancestor's occupation.

I objected that I already knew about the 4 p.m. watering perfectly well before she ever proposed the mill theory, and that nothing about my day-to-day experience of the garden changed in the slightest once she told me about it, so I don't see how a fact I was already completely certain of can now turn around and count as evidence for her story. She answered that my standard would make it impossible for any historian ever to get credit for anything, since accounting for already-settled, familiar facts is exactly what a good historical explanation is supposed to do. When I put the disagreement to a couple of classmates from my seminar, they split straight down the middle, which hasn't helped. I would like to know whether her theory's success in accounting for something I was already sure of really does count in its favor, or whether my instinct that it shouldn't is tracking something genuine.
