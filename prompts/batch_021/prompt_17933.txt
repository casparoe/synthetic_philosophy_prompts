So I fell down a bit of a rabbit hole this week because a friend who's doing a philosophy PhD mentioned something called "the causal exclusion argument" (I think the guy who's mainly associated with it is Jaegwon Kim?) and now I can't stop poking at it. From what I've pieced together, the setup is something like this: my belief that it's raining causes me to grab an umbrella, but that belief is also realized by / supervenes on some physical brain state, and physics is supposedly "causally closed," so that brain state is already fully sufficient on its own to cause my arm to move. If we don't want to say the umbrella-grabbing is bizarrely overdetermined by two independent complete causes every single time anyone does anything, it looks like the mental property's causal contribution gets "excluded" — the physical state is doing all the actual work and the belief is just riding along, epiphenomenal, unless your willing to just say the belief literally IS that brain state (which people don't love doing, because presumably the same belief could be realized by a totally different physical state in a different brain, or an alien, or whatever — "multiple realizability").

Here's my hang-up as someone who writes software for a living: this sounds exactly like levels of abstraction to me, and I don't get why its supposed to be a genuine puzzle instead of a total non-issue. "The service went down because of a null pointer exception" and "the service went down because of this exact pattern of voltages across a few billion transistors" are both true at the same time, and the second one is arguably more fundamental and physically sufficient all by itself, but nobody concludes from that that the null-pointer explanation is somehow fake, or that my code doesn't "really" cause the crash. It's just two levels of description of one event. So why doesn't that same move immediately dissolve Kim's argument for the mental case too? Is there some real disanalogy between the mind/brain case and the software/hardware case that blocks this response, or have philosophers already made basically this exact move and it has a name I should go look up? I'd love an answer that actually digs into why people think this is hard, rather than a quick surface-level summary I could've gotten off the Wikipedia page.
