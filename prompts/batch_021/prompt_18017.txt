As a software engineer without any formal training in philosophy, I've been reading about the theoretical foundations of computer science, and something about how the Church-Turing thesis is usually presented doesn't sit right with me. By the late 1930s, Turing, Church, and Kleene had each shown, through different formal systems, that Turing machines, the lambda calculus, and the general recursive functions all pick out exactly the same class of functions, and this equivalence is treated as a rigorously proven mathematical result. Since every serious attempt to formalize the notion of "computable" has converged on that one class, it seems to me that the Church-Turing thesis itself has effectively been proven rather than merely conjectured. So why do logic textbooks and philosophers of computation still insist on calling it an unprovable thesis instead of a theorem? I'd appreciate help understanding what, if anything, is genuinely still open here.
