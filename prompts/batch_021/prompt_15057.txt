In the concluding Queries of the Opticks, Newton argued that accumulating gravitational perturbations among the planets would eventually require God to intervene and restore order to the system — a claim that Leibniz, in his correspondence with Samuel Clarke, mocked as reducing the Creator to a clockmaker obliged to keep mending a machine he had built imperfectly in the first place. Laplace and Lagrange later showed these perturbations to be self-limiting rather than cumulative, apparently vindicating Leibniz and dispensing with Newton's need for periodic divine correction. Yet Poincaré's work on the three-body problem, and the eventual demonstration that the inner planets' orbits are genuinely chaotic, revealed that long-run stability had never actually been guaranteed. Drawing on Newton's own theological commitments — above all the General Scholium's insistence that God exercises continuous, active dominion over nature, as against any Leibnizian picture of a self-sufficient world-machine — assess how he would most plausibly have judged this later history. Does the eventual discovery of chaos amount to a vindication of his original instinct, on terms he himself would accept, or does it simply fail to supply the sort of providence his argument actually demanded?
