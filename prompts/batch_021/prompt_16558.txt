I'm a physics PhD student running Monte Carlo simulations of the two-dimensional Ising model for my dissertation on critical phenomena, and something about the results has been nagging at me philosophically. No matter how large I make the simulated lattice, quantities like the magnetic susceptibility never actually diverge; they show a smooth, finite peak near the critical temperature that grows taller and narrower as the lattice grows, but stays finite for any size I can actually run. The genuine singularity that formally defines a phase transition only appears in the mathematical limit of infinite system size -- an idealization that not even a real, finite-particle magnet ever technically reaches. This has left me wondering whether phase transitions are real features of the physical systems I study, or just artifacts of a convenient fiction imposed on systems that never quite have them. I gather this is a stock example in debates about reduction and emergence in philosophy of science, but I don't know that literature well enough to place it myself. Could you lay out, at a fairly technical level, why the necessity of the thermodynamic limit is treated as philosophically significant here -- specifically, the strongest argument that it makes phase transitions genuinely emergent rather than merely reducible in principle, and the best reductionist rejoinder to that argument?
