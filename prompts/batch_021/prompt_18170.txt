I spent about thirty-five years as a physical chemist before I retired, and these days I've traded lab notebooks for a pretty undisciplined tour through philosophy and history of science, which is how I ended up down a Newton rabbit hole a few weeks back. Like most scientists I always had this comfortable picture of Newton as basically a modern-minded empiricist who happened to have some old-fashioned religious furniture in the attic — churchgoing was just what you did in the 1600s, and it had nothing to do with the actual physics. Then I picked up Rob Iliffe's book on Newton's religious life and that picture fell apart pretty fast. The man secretly rejected the Trinity his whole adult life, which I guess makes sense given its basically illegal to say out loud back then, spent decades trying to reconstruct the floor plan of Solomon's Temple, and worked out from the book of Daniel that the world wouldn't end before around 2060. That's not a hobby, that's basically a second career — by some counts he wrote more on theology and Biblical chronology then he ever did on physics and math combined.

What's been bugging me since (my old postdoc, who I still email every week, thinks I'm nuts for caring about this) is whether any of that theological stuff actually did work in the physics, or whether it's just a curiosity that happened to live in the same head as the Principia. The bit that really got me is the Leibniz-Clarke letters, where Leibniz accuses Newton of basically treating space as God's sense-organ, and of needing God to keep nudging the planets back into line every so often like a mechanic servicing a car that shouldn't need servicing. That doesn't sound like idle religiosity to me — it sounds like the theology is doing real work in how Newton thought about absolute space and why the solar system stays stable over the long run. But apparently plenty of historians think that's overreading things, and that Newton-the-physicist and Newton-the-secret-heretic are best kept in separate boxes.

So I'd like you to actually adjudicate this for me instead of just laying out "both sides" and leaving it there. Write me an essay on whether Newton's natural philosophy should be understood as, in some real sense, a theological project — with his heterodox religious commitments doing genuine explanatory work in the physics — or whether that unity is mostly illusory and the two bodies of work are better treated separately. Go through the strongest considerations each way, but don't leave me with a "more research is needed" shrug at the end. Tell me which way you'd come down and defend it — a hedged verdict like "load-bearing for his concept of absolute space, but incidental to the inverse-square law itself" is fine, so long as you actually commit to something like that rather than just refusing to choose.
