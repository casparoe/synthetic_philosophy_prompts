The question: Philosophers standardly treat lying as requiring an assertion, the speaker's belief that the assertion is false, and, on most accounts, an intention to deceive the hearer. Harry Frankfurt argues that "bullshitting" is a distinct phenomenon from lying: the bullshitter, unlike the liar, is simply indifferent to the truth-value of what they say. Using these distinctions, evaluate the following claim: "A large language model that states a fabricated legal citation with total confidence is not lying, because lying requires a belief the model doesn't have; it's bullshitting, since the model was never tracking truth to begin with. But that doesn't mean no one has done anything deceptive." A full answer says (a) why the fabrication fails the standard definition of lying, (b) what would make it count as bullshit in Frankfurt's specific sense, and (c) who, if anyone, can be said to have deceived the user, and why.

The student's answer: "The model can't be lying in the strict sense, because lying requires believing that what you're asserting is false, and a language model has no beliefs to begin with -- it has weights and activations, not doxastic states. So the second condition of the standard definition is never satisfied, whatever else is going on.

This fits the concept of bullshit much better than it fits lying. As Bok defines it, an utterance counts as bullshit when it is produced with reckless disregard for whether it is true, and that is exactly what is happening here: the model isn't trying to represent the world accurately or inaccurately, it's simply producing the most statistically plausible continuation of the prompt, with nothing that functions as a check on truth anywhere in that process.

That said, someone can still be responsible for deceiving the user. Deception is broader than lying: unlike lying, it doesn't require the deceiver to believe that what's conveyed is false, only that the hearer ends up with a false belief as a result of it. Since the user ends up believing a citation exists that doesn't, the model has deceived them, even though it hasn't lied. More interestingly, the people who marketed the system as a reliable research tool without flagging its fabrication rate have plausibly deceived users too, in the more familiar sense of the word."
