Okay so I'm about two-thirds into a hard-SF novel and I've hit a snag with a scene I really want to land. My protagonist, a cosmologist named Mireille, interupts a seminar right before her colleagues are about to run a decisive experiment choosing between two rival cosmological models — and she announces that she can already tell them which one is true, not from any data, just from the bare fact that she exists and is having this particular thought at all. I got the idea from reading around anthropic reasoning this past month (Bostrom's observer-selection stuff, the Doomsday Argument, the whole Self-Sampling vs. Self-Indication Assumption business), and I was pretty sure I'd found my source material: the "presumptuous philosopher" thought experiment is basically this scene already. From what I can piece together, Bostrom sets it up so the philosopher barges in on the physicists and, using the Self-Indication Assumption, correctly scoops them using pure self-locating probability — and that the point of the thought experiment is to show SIA is basically vindicated, that you really can get real cosmological knowledge for free just by reasoning hard enough about your own existence.

I mentioned this to my partner, who did philosophy at uni, and she gave me a look like I'd gotten the whole thing backwards, but she couldn't remember the details well enough to explain why. So: is that actually the lesson philosophers draw from the presumptuous philosopher case, or am I about to write a scene that's going to make any philosopher in my readership wince? And relatedly, for the scene itself — should my physicist characters be furious with Mireille, or forced to grudgingly admit she's got a point?
