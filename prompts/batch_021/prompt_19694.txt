I am several drafts into a novel in which a lightly fictionalized Leibniz finds himself, through a plot device I have not yet settled on, in conversation with a present-day decision theorist who is trying to explain the concept of "acausal trade." For anyone unfamiliar with the term: it is an idea discussed among certain contemporary decision theorists, according to which two agents who can never causally influence one another — because they occupy causally disconnected branches of a multiverse, say, or because one exists only as a simulation run by the other, or because they are separated by an unbridgeable stretch of time — can still act to their mutual benefit, simply by each correctly predicting how the other reasons and choosing accordingly, so that their behavior ends up perfectly coordinated even though no message, signal, or causal chain ever passes between them.

My difficulty is that I do not want my Leibniz to respond with a knowing smile and a one-line reference to pre-established harmony, as though the two ideas were obviously the same trick wearing different costumes. He held, after all, that monads are windowless and cannot act upon one another causally, and yet the whole of creation unfolds in perfect mutual correspondence purely because God coordinated everything in advance when he selected this world, from among infinitely many possible ones, as the best to create. At a glance, that looks like exactly the same feat — coordination without causal contact — but I suspect it falls apart under scrutiny, since Leibniz's arrangement depends on a benevolent, all-seeing designer having fixed the whole system beforehand, and on every possible world but one remaining a mere idea in God's understanding rather than anything actual, whereas the decision theorists have no designer at all and are typically imagining causally disconnected worlds that are all equally real. Drawing on Leibniz's actual commitments — the principle of sufficient reason, the identity of indiscernibles, his account of contingency and of possible worlds — could you work out, in some detail, whether he would end up welcoming acausal trade as a secular vindication of his own system, dismissing it as a superficial imitation of it, or arriving at some third verdict more interesting than either? I would also value your own opinion on which of those outcomes holds up best once the analogy is pushed as hard as it will go.
