I'm working on a story about how transplant centers break ties for scarce organs, and I sat in on an ethics committee meeting that has stayed with me. Two patients had identical clinical scores and identical waiting times, giving them equally strong claims to the single kidney that had just become available. One committee member argued for settling the matter with a coin flip, since that seemed like the only way to honor their equal claims; the committee's staff statistician disagreed, pointing out that if you assign a utility of 1 to receiving the kidney and 0 to not receiving it, then giving it to patient A outright, giving it to patient B outright, and flipping a coin all yield exactly the same expected welfare across the two patients — so, he argued, the coin flip is an empty ritual, and the surgeon might as well decide by gut feeling, since the numbers come out identical either way. I couldn't locate the flaw in his arithmetic, yet handing the kidney to whichever patient the surgeon happens to prefer still strikes me as clearly worse than flipping a coin. Is the statistician right that, once you're reasoning in terms of expected welfare, the coin flip adds nothing of genuine ethical value?
