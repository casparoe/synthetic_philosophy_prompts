I teach philosophy at my university and currently chair an ad hoc Faculty Senate committee. We are weighing a proposal from our economics and computer science departments: a two-year pilot of Robin Hanson's 'futarchy' mechanism, applied to one decision -- which of two competing general-education curricula to adopt. The Senate would first vote on a welfare metric -- five-year graduation rate, weighted for first-generation students -- and then let a campus-wide prediction market settle the curriculum choice, since whichever option traders price as more likely to raise that metric is adopted automatically. Supporters call this a clean separation of contested values, fixed by the vote, from empirical prediction, settled by the market; other committee members object that fixing the metric already settles most of the contested values by itself, and that a market of at most a few hundred realistic traders, resolving five years out, is thin enough for an interested party to move. Draft the memo I will circulate before our vote: recommend approving, rejecting, or narrowing the pilot; specify any design safeguards that recommendation depends on; and take the strongest objection to your own recommendation seriously.
