So I'm about two-thirds through a novel where the POV character, a former bomb disposal tech named Mara who just rotated home from a bad tour, starts seeing threat where there isn't any -- a coiled garden hose reads as a wire for a second, a kid's backpack left on a bus seat reads as a device, before it resolves into what it actually is. I don't want this to be "she's paranoid and misjudging stuff she sees perfectly clearly" -- I want the fear itself to be reaching into the seeing, so the backpack briefly *looks* rigged to her, not just gets misjudged as maybe-rigged after the fact. Trying to figure out how psychologically defensible that actually is, I fell down a rabbit hole on "cognitive penetration of perception" and got stuck between two camps: Fodor and Pylyshyn's side, which says this sort of thing is basically impossible because early vision is modular and walled off from what you believe (their go-to move is that the Müller-Lyer illusion doesn't go away even once you know the two lines are the same length, which is suppose to show perception can't be touched by belief) -- versus people like Siegel and Macpherson, who want to carve out room for high-level "seeing-as," like seeing something as dangerous or a face as hostile, being penetrable even if low-level stuff like line length isn't.

Could you actually build the case for that second, narrower claim, instead of just laying out both sides for me? I want a real argument for why the persistence of illusions like Müller-Lyer doesn't settle things against my character -- i.e. why you can grant that early/low-level vision is encapsulated and still hold that fear or suspicion can genuinely change what her high-level threat-perception delivers, rather than merely what she's attending to, or how she judges an experience whose content was already fixed a beat earlier. If you can, take on the two obvious deflationary moves head-on: "she's just extra vigilant, attending to different features of the same scene" and "she's perceiving the backpack completely normally, and it's only the split-second judgment layered on top that's warped." Those are exactly the objections a sharp beta reader (or a philosopher from the other camp) would throw at me, so I want the argument to actually survive them, not dance around them.
