I spent several years on an equities desk, and three months ago I joined a small firm that operates validator nodes for proof-of-stake blockchains: clients lock up coins with us and receive newly minted coins back, roughly in proportion to how much they stake and for how long. I've been working through Aristotle's Politics Book I for an evening reading group, and his treatment of interest has been nagging at me ever since. He calls it the most unnatural branch of wealth-getting because interest — tokos, literally "offspring" — is, in his words, "money born of money," coin producing more coin with no good or service changing hands in between. Staking rewards seem to fit that description at least as well as ordinary interest does, and arguably better, since a lender at least forgoes the use of their funds and bears some risk of default, whereas several proof-of-stake protocols mint rewards on a near-fixed schedule regardless of what the staker actually risks. Proof-of-work mining, whatever its other problems, at least involves expending real energy toward producing something, which looks closer to the kind of bounded, effortful acquisition Aristotle treats as natural. Reasoning strictly from what he actually argues about money and exchange, rather than from the general slogan that money shouldn't make more money, would Aristotle have to condemn proof-of-stake more severely than proof-of-work, or does that comparison quietly depend on features of ancient metal coinage that don't survive the move to a purely programmable asset?
