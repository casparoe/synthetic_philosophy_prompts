"Using the distinction Bentham draws in An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation between the principle of utility and its two rivals — the principle of asceticism and the principle of sympathy and antipathy — reconstruct the argument he makes against legally punishing consensual 'irregularities of the venereal appetite' between adults. Then evaluate whether this argument sits comfortably with the rest of his hedonistic framework, in particular the calculus he uses to measure the value of pleasures and pains."

This week we're peer-grading each other's responses against a rubric she handed out, and I've been assigned a classmate's answer. Before I write up my comments I'd like a second opinion. Here is what they wrote:

"Bentham thought the law had no real business punishing sexual acts between consenting adults just because most people happened to find them disgusting. In the second chapter of An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation, he lays out three possible standards for judging conduct: the principle of utility, which asks whether an action increases or diminishes the happiness of the people affected by it; the principle of asceticism, which treats pleasure as bad simply for being pleasure and so condemns it no matter the consequences; and the principle of sympathy and antipathy, which just ratifies whatever a person happens to feel approval or disgust toward, without asking about consequences at all. Bentham treats both rivals as illegitimate, since neither one is actually measuring anything — they just dress up personal feeling as if it were a moral finding.

When Bentham turns to what he calls 'paederasty' and other sexual 'irregularities,' he argues that the laws against them, which in England could carry the death penalty, are not really justified by utility, because two people acting in private with mutual consent are not making anyone else worse off. Instead, he says, these laws are really enforcing the principle of sympathy and antipathy: legislators and the public feel antipathy toward the acts, and that antipathy gets treated as though it were proof of harm. This is essentially Bentham's harm principle, later popularized by Mill, and it was a strikingly radical position for the 1780s — decades before anyone else in England was willing to make this argument in print. Bentham seems to have understood exactly how radical it was, which is presumably why he chose not to publish the essay during his lifetime.

Where I think the argument runs into some trouble is with Bentham's own calculus for measuring pleasures and pains, specifically the dimension he calls fecundity: the likelihood that a given pleasure will go on to produce further pleasures of the same kind. Because sex between two men or two women cannot lead to children, it should score lower on fecundity than procreative sex, so Bentham's own bookkeeping seems to rate it as producing systematically less value even before antipathy is factored in at all. That looks like a genuine tension in his position, not just a case of people misapplying an otherwise consistent standard."
