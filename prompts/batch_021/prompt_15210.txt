I'm outlining a near-future novel in which governments and corporations have delegated much of their negotiating to autonomous AI systems, which finalize binding commitments -- trade terms, ceasefires, personal contracts -- phrased in ordinary promissory language ("we hereby undertake to...") that no human individually drafted or reviewed. In a pivotal scene, a character who has spent her career studying Hume is asked whether such an AI-issued commitment actually obligates the humans on whose behalf it was made, and I'd like her reasoning to track his real position rather than a generic take on AI ethics. Hume denied that a promise is something the will alone can create; he held that our sense of being obligated is the product of an artificial convention, adopted because society benefits from people being able to rely on one another, and kept alive by ordinary sentiments such as the desire for a good reputation and resentment toward those who break faith. Given that account, how would you expect him to judge a "promise" issued by a system with no reputation to protect and none of the sentiments he thought sustained the convention: would the human principal still be bound, on his terms, simply because the convention was invoked in her name, or does his argument imply that something essential to promising has dropped out?
