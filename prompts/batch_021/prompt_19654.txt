I'm about two-thirds into a novel where the emotional core is a family trying to decide whether to consent to organ donation after their son is declared brain dead following a motorcycle crash, and lately I've been hosting these little "research dinners" where I invite people who actually know the relevant stuff so I dont botch the details. Last week that was Maya, who spent almost a decade as an organ-procurement coordinator, and Devon, who she brought along and who's ABD in a bioethics program. I basically just wanted a few good quotes I could steal for dialogue, but the two of them ended up in a genuine argument over dessert that I think is more interesting than anything I could've scripted myself, so I want to give it to you close to verbatim rather than my own tidy summary of who "won."

(1) Maya opened by saying brain death just is death, full stop — it's the law in all fifty states, not some polite fiction hospitals invented so they can harvest organs. (2) Devon pushed back with something he'd read about kids and adults kept on ventilators for months or sometimes years after a brain-death diagnosis, still healing wounds, fighting off infections, even going through puberty in one case that got a lot of press — if the body is still integrating itself into one functioning organism, he asked, what exactly is supposed to have died? (3) Maya's answer was that all of that is just the vent and the drugs doing the brain's old job for it, and that left alone, most of those bodies go into cardiac arrest within a week or two anyway — its outsourced integration, not real integration. (4) Devon said that proves too much, since plenty of patients everyone agrees are alive — quadriplegics on ventilators, ECMO patients — are just as dependent on machines to keep integrating, so "needs a machine" cant be the line that matters. (5) At that point Maya switched to circulatory death instead: when organs are taken after "circulatory death" now, she said, doctors only wait a few minutes after the heart stops before declaring death, which isn't remotely irreversible in any strict sense — so if brain death is a legal fudge, circulatory death is a much more obvious one, and nobody complains about that one. (6) Devon said that was exactly his point and not the gotcha she thought it was — both criteria, he said, quietly swap "irreversible" for merely "permanent" (meaning: nobody's going to try restarting it), which is a policy choice wearing a lab coat, and he'd rather everyone just admit "legally dead" and "biologically dead" can come apart than keep pretending they're the same fact. Maya's comeback, not really a numbered move so much as a mic drop, was that if you actually let yourself believe that, you're saying hospitals take organs from people who are still biologically alive, and no transplant program survives that sentence getting said out loud.
