A few of us in a small reading group recently worked through Émilie du Châtelet's Discourse on Happiness, and two people ended up in a real argument over her claim that being "susceptible to illusions" is one of the necessary ingredients of a happy life.

Nadia said this amounts to prescribing self-deception, and that it sits oddly with someone who spent her life translating Newton and doing careful physics: if you notice that the pleasure of the opera depends on not thinking about the machinery backstage, the honest response is to accept the plainer pleasure, not train yourself to look away.

Marcus answered that du Châtelet never tells anyone to believe something they already know to be false -- you can't just decide to believe a claim you take to be untrue -- she's only recommending that people stop poking around for the mechanism behind a pleasure that's doing nobody any harm. That's not self-deception, just declining to investigate further, the same way you don't ruin a magic trick by demanding to see how it's done.

Nadia replied that du Châtelet goes further than simply not investigating: her own advice is to "thicken the varnish" that illusion lays over things, which sounds like actively building up a distorted picture, not just leaving well enough alone.

Marcus said that's fair, but maybe the varnish she has in mind is only ever the emotional shading passions give to things -- how enchanting the opera feels -- never a factual or philosophical claim, so none of it touches her commitment to truth as a scientist; those are simply different departments of a life.

Neither of them would budge, and they asked me to settle it, which I don't feel qualified to do. Could you go through the exchange move by move and say who has the better argument at each stage -- not just who wins overall -- and whether there's a coherent version of du Châtelet's position that survives the exchange?
