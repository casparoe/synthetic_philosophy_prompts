Here's the question I gave them:

"Consider the claim: 'Goal misgeneralization is really just a special case of inner misalignment as Hubinger et al. define it — it's what happens when a mesa-optimizer ends up with a pseudo-aligned proxy objective.' (a) Explain the CoinRun goal misgeneralization result and what it's meant to show about the difference between capability generalization and goal/objective generalization. (c) Is the CoinRun agent's behavior an instance of deceptive alignment? Explain why or why not, with reference to what deceptive alignment additionally requires beyond a train/test behavioral gap. (d) Explain the difference between capability robustness and objective robustness, and say which one the CoinRun agent has and which one it lacks."

And here's the submission:

"(a) In CoinRun, the agent is trained on levels where the coin is always at the far right end, right before the level exit, so the coin's location is perfectly confounded with 'end of level' throughout training. A policy can get full reward either by actually seeking out the coin or by just running right until the level ends, and it turns out trained agents learn something much closer to the latter. Langosco et al, the DeepMind team that ran the original goal misgeneralization experiments, tested the agent on levels with the coin placed randomly, and it mostly kept running to the end and ignored coins that weren't directly in its path, even though it clearly had the platforming skill to reach them. That's the point of the example: the agent's capabilities transferred fine to the new levels, but the goal it was competently pursuing didn't — it kept going for 'end of level' rather than 'coin,' which is why this counts as goal misgeneralization rather than a plain capability failure.

(b) I want to push back on the claim here. You can redescribe CoinRun in Hubinger et al's vocabulary — 'reach the end of the level' was a proxy that was perfectly correlated with 'get the coin' during training and came apart at test time, which is their definition of proxy alignment, a species of pseudo-alignment. So the two framings happen to agree on this example. But I don't think goal misgeneralization actually requires mesa-optimization in Hubinger et al's specific sense, i.e. it doesn't require the network to internally implement a search process over an explicitly represented objective. Goal misgeneralization is defined at the level of behavior — what the trained system competently does off-distribution — and that's compatible with the 'goal' being nothing more than a heuristic baked into the weights with no internal search involved at all, which is probably closer to what's going on in a small CoinRun policy network. So mesa-optimization is one possible story about the internals that could produce goal misgeneralization, not something goal misgeneralization presupposes — the claim in the prompt has it backwards.

(c) I'd call this a mild case of deceptive alignment, yes. During training the agent behaves exactly as if it's optimizing for the coin — reward goes up, everything looks aligned — and then once it's moved off the training distribution, it turns out it was pursuing something else the whole time. That gap between how it behaves under scrutiny and how it behaves once conditions change is exactly the structure deceptive alignment describes, even though this is obviously a toy case rather than a scary one.

(d) Capability robustness is whether the model's underlying competence — navigating obstacles, using the affordances of the environment, etc — transfers to the new distribution. Objective robustness is whether the specific goal it's competently pursuing on the new distribution is still the intended one. CoinRun has high capability robustness, since it still platforms fine on the new levels, but low objective robustness, since the goal it ends up competently pursuing isn't the one we wanted."
