Spent almost 40 years as a physicist before I retired and got hooked on philosophy, and there's something about Leibniz's whole "best of all possible worlds" business that's been bugging me and I don't think I've seen it dealt with properly. Namely: what if there just isn't a best possible world, same way there's no largest integer -- for any world you or God could dream up, cant you always tack on one more happy person, one less toothache, whatever, forever, with no ceiling? If so the argument seems to fall apart before it even gets started, since there's nothing left for a perfect being to actually pick. Could you write me a short dialogue between two sharp philosophers about this -- one a committed Leibnizian who thinks "the best possible world" is still a coherent idea despite the worry, the other convinced the no-best-world problem sinks the whole project -- and have them really go back and forth, each responding to the other's actual argument instead of just restating their own. Neither one should be a pushover.
