"Explain van Fraassen's Reflection Principle. Your answer should (a) state the principle precisely, (b) explain the kind of argument van Fraassen gives in its favor, (c) present the standard style of case that leads van Fraassen to restrict the principle, and explain why an unrestricted version of the principle is vulnerable to it, and (d) explain how the Reflection Principle relates to ordinary Bayesian conditionalization -- are they the same requirement, or is one more general than the other, and why?"

And here's the student's answer:

"Van Fraassen's Reflection Principle says that a rational agent's current degree of belief in a proposition A, conditional on the supposition that her degree of belief in A at some later time t' will be r, must itself equal r: Cr_t(A | Cr_t'(A) = r) = r. Informally, if I'm sure I'll believe A to degree 0.7 tomorrow, I should already believe A to degree 0.7 today, even though I don't yet know why my credence will shift there. The principle is meant to formalize a kind of rational self-trust: absent special reasons to distrust my future self, I should treat my anticipated future opinions the same way I'd treat the opinion of a well-informed advisor.

Van Fraassen defends Reflection with a diachronic Dutch book argument, the same style of argument he uses (following Lewis) to justify ordinary conditionalization: an agent who violates Reflection can be offered a combination of bets, some placed now and some contingent on what her credence turns out to be later, that guarantee her a net loss no matter how things turn out. Since no rational agent should be guaranteed to lose money purely by the shape of her own credences, this is meant to show that violating Reflection is a kind of incoherence over time, in the same way the ordinary Dutch book argument shows that violating the probability axioms is incoherence at a single time.

The obvious worry is that sometimes I have every reason not to trust my future self -- if I know I'm about to get very drunk, or that a manipulative neuroscientist is about to tinker with my brain, I don't want to defer now to whatever credence that future, compromised self will have. Van Fraassen is aware of this and restricts Reflection to cases where the agent has no specific reason to expect a loss of memory or rationality between t and t' -- this is the point of his discussion of Ulysses and the Sirens, where Ulysses rationally refuses to defer to the judgment of his future, song-addled self and has himself tied to the mast instead. But this restriction essentially collapses Reflection into ordinary conditionalization: once the principle only holds when the agent's future credence has been arrived at rationally, you've just stipulated that the future self updates by conditionalizing on whatever evidence comes in, which is exactly the situation ordinary conditionalization already covers. So Reflection, restricted this way, isn't really an independent constraint -- it's a repackaging of conditionalization, and the cases where Reflection would add new content are exactly the cases the restriction rules out. Really this is the same idea as Lewis's Principal Principle, where we defer to an objective authority; van Fraassen's innovation is just to turn the Principal Principle inward, making your own future self the 'objective chance-like' authority you defer to, instead of an external chance process."
