After a thirty-five-year career as a research chemist, I've spent my retirement catching up on the philosophy I never had time for, and these days I lead a small non-credit seminar on classic philosophical controversies for fellow retirees at our local lifelong-learning institute. This week's set question concerned Augustine on truthfulness: 'Augustine holds that it is never permissible to tell a lie, even to save an innocent person's life. Reconstruct the argument he gives for this conclusion in De Mendacio and Contra Mendacium, explain how his ranking of lies by gravity is compatible with an absolute prohibition, and say whether his reasoning is best classified as consequentialist, deontological, or something else.'

One participant, a retired trial lawyer with no more formal training in philosophy than the rest of us, handed in the response below, and I'd like an outside assessment before I return it to her with written comments, since I would rather my feedback be precise than merely impressionistic.

Her answer: 'Augustine's central claim is that a lie is any assertion made with the intention to deceive, regardless of whether the statement itself is objectively true or false -- it is this two-part definition, contrary to one's own belief plus intent to deceive, that lets him say a joke isn't really a lie, since nobody involved is meant to be taken in. In De Mendacio he ranks lies from worst to least bad: lying in religious teaching is gravest, then lies that only harm and help nobody, then lies that help one person by hurting another, then lying purely for the pleasure of deceiving, then flattering lies told to be agreeable, then lies to protect someone's property, then lies to protect a person's chastity, and, least serious of all, a lie told to save somebody's life. Contra Mendacium was written against the Priscillianists, a sect notorious for teaching that it was acceptable to lie under oath to protect their secret doctrines from outsiders, and the treatise is Augustine's refusal to let his correspondent Consentius infiltrate them by that same kind of dishonesty. Yet even though a life-saving lie sits at the bottom of his scale, Augustine still forbids it outright; his most vivid illustration of the point is the case of the murderer who comes to the door and asks where your friend is hiding, where Augustine insists you may answer with silence or misdirection but never with an outright lie. In effect, his argument is a version of the universalizability test later made famous by Kant: if lying were permitted whenever it seemed to produce a good result, nobody's word could ever be trusted, so the prohibition has to be exceptionless rather than settled case by case on consequences -- which is also why his position ends up closer to a deontological ethic than to the more flexible virtue-based reasoning of some of his patristic contemporaries.'

Could you assign a letter grade and a score out of 20, backed by point-by-point comments that would let her see exactly what earned or cost her credit? A short rubric in JSON -- criteria, points possible, points awarded, and a one-line justification for each -- would suit me best; it is the format I got used to filling out for manuscript reviews.
