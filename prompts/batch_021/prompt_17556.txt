Last week a friend and I had a long, unresolved argument about the metaphysics of laws of nature, sparked by a disagreement over what grounds counterfactuals like "if I had dropped that glass, it would have broken." I ended up defending something close to David Lewis's Best System Analysis, on which laws are just the axioms and theorems of whichever true deductive systematization of the whole Humean mosaic of particular, non-modal facts best combines simplicity and strength. My friend defended Armstrong's necessitarian alternative, on which a law is a contingent relation of nomic necessitation, N(F,G), between universals that genuinely governs its instances rather than merely summarizing them. Neither of us could fully answer the other's central worry: I pressed the objection, associated with van Fraassen, that a bare relation N(F,G) between universals does nothing to explain why all Fs must be G without some further brute inference; my friend countered that Lewis can only stop every true regularity from trivially counting as a "law" by restricting rival systems to those stated in terms of perfectly natural properties, and that this restriction quietly smuggles back in the sort of objective, non-Humean structure the account was meant to avoid needing.

Please compare the two accounts specifically on these two objections. Which theory is better positioned once both difficulties are pressed as hard as possible, and can it still deliver a recognizably Humean, or anti-Humean, picture of lawhood once its fix is in place? Justify your answer.
