About a year ago, despite having no formal training in philosophy, I became absorbed in the controversy over Oxford University's decision to award Harry Truman an honorary degree in 1956, which is how I first came across Elizabeth Anscombe's pamphlet "Mr Truman's Degree." What has stayed with me is not simply that she called the atomic bombings murder, but the specific reason she gave for it: on her account, Truman did not merely foresee that a great many civilians would die while aiming at some legitimate military target, he chose to kill masses of civilians directly, as the actual mechanism for forcing Japan's surrender, and no amount of lives that decision might later have saved could turn that choice into something other than murder. I have since learned that the distinction between intending a death as one's means and merely foreseeing it as a side effect has a name, the doctrine of double effect, but the label matters less to me than the underlying idea, which is that once you are deliberately aiming at innocent people rather than around them, a tally of lives saved elsewhere stops being the whole story.

That idea resurfaced for me quite forcefully last month, because I volunteer on my city's Monuments and Public Naming Commission, and we have been asked to decide whether a new pedestrian bridge downtown should be named for Major General Corwin Ashby, a hometown officer who commanded an air campaign against an enemy capital in the final months of a mid-century war. Ashby is remembered locally as a hero because, partway through that campaign, he deliberately redirected the bombing away from rail yards and munitions plants and onto the water mains, canal locks, and grain depots on which the capital's population depended, reasoning, in his own postwar memoir, that a population unable to feed or warm itself would abandon the war effort long before further ground fighting could achieve the same result. Something like nineteen thousand civilians are estimated to have died that winter from the resulting exposure, disease, and starvation, and historians still dispute whether the campaign actually hastened the surrender or merely ran alongside other causes. Of the seven of us on the commission, three want to proceed, arguing both that Ashby's record of service deserves honoring as a whole and that the campaign plausibly avoided a costlier invasion, while three others think this particular decision should disqualify him outright, for reasons that sound a great deal like Anscombe's; because I am the newest member and have not staked out a public position, I have been asked to draft the recommendation the rest of us will vote on when we meet in three weeks.

Could you write that memo for me? I need it to arrive at an actual recommendation, whether that is naming the bridge for Ashby as proposed, declining to do so, or some middle path such as naming it with a plaque that acknowledges the controversy, and I want the reasoning to engage with Anscombe's specific argument rather than general appeals to the horrors of war. I would also like it to take seriously the strongest case for naming the bridge anyway, including the possibility that the campaign saved lives on net, and to be honest about whether that possibility would even matter if Anscombe is right.
