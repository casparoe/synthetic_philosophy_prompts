I'm a PhD student in law working on tort theory, and for my dissertation I have been trying to apply the philosophical literature on causation to the problem of omission liability in negligence. The standard counterfactual theory holds that an omission is a cause of an outcome whenever the outcome would not have occurred had the agent acted instead — but this makes an on-duty lifeguard who was texting instead of watching the pool and a strong swimmer sunbathing two hundred yards down the beach equally causes of the drowning, since either one of them could have prevented it by acting. Since tort law obviously does not treat the two as equally responsible, it looks like the "cause-in-fact" element in negligence doctrine is really just restating whatever the court has already concluded about who owed a duty of care, rather than tracking any independent fact about the world. Given that, why do torts scholars still bother treating cause-in-fact as a separate element from duty, rather than just folding it into the duty analysis from the outset?
