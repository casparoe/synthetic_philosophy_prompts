My partner's family is Xhosa, from South Africa, and over the last few months I've been reading a bit of Tutu and this contemporary philosopher, Thaddeus Metz, who writes about ubuntu as an actual ethical theory rather than just a nice-sounding phrase, and I stumbled into that older argument between Menkiti and Gyekye about whether the community basically *makes* you a person (Menkiti's stronger view, more or less) or whether some individual rights survive no matter what the community decides (Gyekye's more moderate line). Purely by coincidence, almost that exact disagreement has now landed on my desk at work.

I sit on my hospital's clinical ethics committee. We have a patient — competent, no capacity concerns per the psych consult, fully able to make his own medical decisions — who needs a high-risk cardiac procedure and has told the surgical team, calmly and repeatedly, that he doesn't want to be the one who consents. He wants the decision made collectively by his family and two elders he named, because to him thats not an individual decision to make alone. Our consent policy is written entirely around one competent adult's autonomous choice (or a single legally named proxy if he lacked capacity) and just has no box for "competent patient voluntarily hands the decision to a collective." Half the committee thinks refusing to honor that request would itself be disrespecting his autonomy — it's his call how decisions about him get made. The other half, including our patient advocate, is worried we'd be laundering ordinary family pressure through a nice cultural label, and points out that the two elders are both older men, while a niece who'd be directly affected by the outcome isn't part of that circle and has quietly told a nurse she disagrees with where this is heading.

We meet Wednesday and I have to circulate something beforehand. Can you draft the actual memo — who consents and how, concretely, plus a timeframe for revisiting things if the family isn't unanimous by then — rather than just gesture at "respect his cultural values" and move on? I definately don't want it to mention the patient advocate's worry once and then drop it, since I don't think even Gyekye would wave that away.
