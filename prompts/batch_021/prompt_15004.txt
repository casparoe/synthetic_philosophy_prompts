A close friend of mine — a mathematician remarrying at sixty-two, some years after being widowed — has spent the last few years absorbed in Émilie du Châtelet, to the point of learning French well enough to read the Discours sur le bonheur in the original and joining a small reading group built around her work. She has asked me to give the toast, on one condition: that it actually engage with du Châtelet's real views on happiness rather than sanding them down into greeting-card sentiment, which is a harder brief than it sounds. Du Châtelet treats passionate love as one good among several rather than the point of a life, expects it to cool with time, insists we need our illusions rather than being cured of them, and argues that study is in the end the steadiest source of happiness because it depends on no one else and doesn't fade with age — none of which is standard wedding material. A handful of guests are from that reading group and will recognize exactly what's being drawn on, so any distortion will land badly with them; everyone else is family and old friends who just want something lovely said about a woman they love. Please write the toast: about five minutes long, warm enough for the second group, honest enough for the first, with du Châtelet's actual position doing real work in it rather than being merely name-dropped.
