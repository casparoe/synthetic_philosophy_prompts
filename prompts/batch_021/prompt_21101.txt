My friend Elena and I had a long argument over dinner last week about her sister Marisol, whose husband died a little over a year ago. Marisol is, by every account, still very much in the grip of it: she cries most days, has trouble concentrating at work, keeps returning to the last months of his illness, and some mornings reportedly needs nearly an hour just to make herself get out of bed. Marisol's grief counselor recently mentioned, gently, that at this point her grief technically meets the criteria for something called Prolonged Grief Disorder -- apparently a fairly new, distinct diagnosis, separate from ordinary bereavement and from depression, reserved for grief that remains this intense and disabling more than a year out. Elena, who was about to fly out to spend a few days with her sister, found the suggestion almost insulting on Marisol's behalf.

Her position is that grief this consuming, after thirty years of marriage, is not a malfunction of anything: it is exactly what an intact, loving mind is supposed to produce when its whole world is taken away, and attaching a clinical label to it just because a certain number of months have passed treats an honest response to catastrophe as though it were a broken thermostat. I disagreed. I don't see why the fact that Marisol's reaction is understandable, even fitting, given what she's lost, should settle whether something in her is currently failing to work the way it's meant to. If, fourteen months on, she genuinely cannot function properly -- can't sleep, can't concentrate, can't really show up for her own kids -- it seems beside the point how sympathetic the cause is; that, to me, is exactly the sort of thing medicine exists to name and treat. We went back and forth for the better part of an hour without either of us budging, and I came away thinking we weren't only disagreeing about Marisol, but about something prior and more general: what it actually takes for a condition to count as a disease or disorder at all, as opposed to a painful but perfectly healthy way of being.

I'd like your honest assessment of who has the more defensible general position here, and why. More fundamentally, is "is this really a disorder" a question with a fact of the matter that's independent of what each of us thinks grief is for, or does it ultimately come down to a disagreement in values that no amount of arguing back and forth could ever settle? I'd rather you work through the strongest version of both sides than just conclude that we're each half right.
