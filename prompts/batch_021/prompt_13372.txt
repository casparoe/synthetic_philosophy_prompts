My daughter was born six weeks ago, and next month I'm giving the opening remarks at a small workshop on long-term technological risk that the institute I work for is hosting. Roughly half the room will be researchers who already know John von Neumann's 1955 essay "Can We Survive Technology?", in which he argued that technological change had made the consequences of human action commensurate with the size of the planet itself, that "for progress there is no cure," and that our only real safeguard was ongoing, case-by-case judgment sustained by "patience, flexibility, intelligence" rather than any final fix. The other half of the room -- funders, journalists, and family -- will never have heard of the essay. Von Neumann wrote it the same year he was diagnosed with the cancer that killed him less than two years later, which makes his refusal to promise either a solution or a catastrophe feel less like rhetorical caution and more like a man speaking under actual time pressure. Before my daughter existed, I could treat that argument as a heuristic for a research agenda; now it reads like a description of the world she is going to grow up in. Please write me about eight minutes of remarks that put von Neumann's actual argument, not just the poignant biographical fact, to real use: legible to the guests who don't know the essay, not condescending to the researchers who do, and honest enough to refuse both the comfort of "we've always muddled through" and the theater of doom, since a careless reading of his essay could support either one.
