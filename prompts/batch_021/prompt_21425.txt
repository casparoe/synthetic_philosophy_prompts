So I spent something like thirty-five years as a field biologist, mostly doing beetle taxonomy, and if theres one thing guaranteed to start a fight among systematists it's the "species problem" -- whether species are real natural kinds carved out by evolution or just convenient bins we impose on a continuum, and which of the dozen-odd species concepts floating around (biological, phylogenetic, morphological, take your pick) is the "correct" one. For years my stock answer to any grad student who got worked up about this was "relax, it's a merely verbal dispute -- everybody agrees on the facts on the ground, they're just using the word 'species' differently." Now that I'm retired and have time to actually read philosophy properly, I've been going through Eli Hirsch's stuff on "quantifier variance," and it's messing with my tidy little answer.

As far as I can tell, Hirsch isn't just making the point I used to make about species -- that in a given dispute people happen to attach different meanings to a word and are talking past each other. He seems to be after something much bigger: that there's no privledged, "one true" meaning of "exists" (or of quantifiers generally), even once every other term in the dispute has been pinned down as precisely as you like -- so that whole families of ontological disputes (composition, persistence, you name it) turn out verbal in a deeper sense that doesn't just reduce to the everyday "you're equivocating" diagnosis. What I can't get a grip on is where the extra oomph in his view is supposed to come from, compared to, say, Chalmers' more workaday recipe of checking whether a dispute survives full precisification of the terms involved. Could you lay out clearly what actually distinguishes "this particular dispute happens to be merely verbal" from Hirsch's full-blown quantifier variance thesis, and why the second is supposed to be the far more radical claim? I vaguely recall there's a guy, Sider maybe, who thinks Hirsch is just wrong that there's no privileged quantifier meaning -- feel free to bring him in too if it helps sharpen the contrast.
