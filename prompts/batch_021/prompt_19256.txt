As it happens, I'm TAing a free will seminar this term, and the first short-answer question on the take-home midterm turns out to bear directly on that very dispute, which leaves me a little wary of grading it while the debate is still fresh in my mind. Could you assess the following answer on its own merits?

Question: State Strawson's Basic Argument for the conclusion that no one is ever ultimately morally responsible for what they do. Explain why Strawson holds that the argument's force does not depend on whether determinism or indeterminism is true. Then briefly assess whether Robert Kane's appeal to indeterministic "self-forming actions" gives a satisfactory answer to the regress step of the argument.

Student's answer: "Strawson's argument runs as follows. (1) We do what we do because of the way we are, mentally speaking. (2) So, to be truly responsible for what we do, we would have to be truly responsible for being the way we are, in the relevant respects. (3) But we cannot be truly responsible for being that way, because this would require having intentionally made ourselves that way, and any such act of self-making would itself have had to flow from a way we already were — which only pushes the question back a stage, starting a regress that can never reach a starting point we are responsible for, since nothing can be its own ultimate cause. (4) So we can never be ultimately responsible for anything we do.

Strawson takes this conclusion to hold whether or not determinism is true, since the problem is not causal necessitation as such but the impossibility of the regress ever terminating in something we authored. Indeterminism does not help either, essentially because of the causal closure of the physical: any indeterministic 'gap' in the causal chain leading to an action would have to be a gap in the physical order, and since the physical order is causally closed, such a gap could not really influence what we do, so it could not supply any responsibility-conferring input from us.

Kane tries to stop the regress by locating indeterminism in rare 'self-forming actions': moments of effort between competing motives in which, following Chisholm's picture of agent-causation, the self steps in as an uncaused cause to settle which motive wins, thereby authoring a piece of character not simply inherited from the past. If this worked, the regress would stop. But Strawson would reply that whichever motive wins either reflects the agent's already-existing character, so the regress continues, or is not so explained, so it is a matter of mere luck — and either way, Kane's move fails to secure the kind of responsibility he wants."
