A friend of mine — a second-year law student — and I spent nearly the whole of dinner last week, and then another half hour on her doorstep, arguing about a case from her torts seminar: a lifeguard who dozed off and failed to notice a swimmer drowning. I wanted to say, quite straightforwardly, that the lifeguard's failure to act was a cause of the swimmer's death. She pointed out the awkward consequence that philosophers have worried about for decades: if an omission counts as a cause whenever the outcome counterfactually depended on it, then every person who could in principle have swum out and saved the man — every able-bodied stranger sunbathing further down the beach, indeed anyone anywhere who could conceivably have intervened in time — turns out to be just as much a "cause" of his death as the lifeguard was. We went back and forth for a long time on whether there is any principled way to single out the lifeguard's omission as specially causal, rather than merely specially blameworthy, without quietly importing some independent normative notion — a duty, a role, a reasonable expectation — that ends up doing all the real work while the vocabulary of causation just goes along for the ride. Neither of us budged, and in retrospect I don't think either of us was right.

I'd like you to write a short story, somewhere between 800 and 1,500 words, that dramatizes this puzzle without collapsing into a philosophy seminar with character names attached. You might center it on an insurance investigator, a coroner, a detective, or a disciplinary panel trying to establish who is "really" responsible for a death that resulted from someone's failure to act, and who finds that responsibility keeps fanning out further the more carefully they look — until they are forced to decide, if only implicitly, whether that fanning-out is a genuine discovery about how causation works or a sign that they were asking a causal question when they should have been asking a normative one. The story should take a real position on that question and earn it through what happens in the plot, rather than through any character simply announcing a conclusion.
