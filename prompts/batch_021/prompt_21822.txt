In my school's philosophy elective, we recently read excerpts from Henry Sidgwick's The Methods of Ethics, and I became interested in what he calls the "dualism of practical reason": the problem that both rational egoism and universal benevolence (utilitarianism) present themselves as self-evident axioms of practical reason, yet in cases where the two conflict there seems to be no further principle available to decide between them. Curious to learn more about him, I looked into his biography and was surprised to discover that this same rigorous, skeptical moral philosopher was a founding member and first president of the Society for Psychical Research, devoting enormous energy to investigating things like telepathy and mediumship. When I brought this up with a friend from class, we initially assumed it was just an unrelated personal eccentricity, the philosophical equivalent of a strict statistics professor moonlighting as an astrologer. But then I came across a passing remark suggesting that the two sides of Sidgwick were not unrelated at all — that his psychical research was, at least in part, a serious attempt to find empirical grounds for resolving the very dualism he had identified in ethics.

I would like to understand this connection properly rather than just file it away as a fun fact. Could you explain, in some depth, how evidence from psychical research was supposed to bear on the dualism of practical reason — structurally speaking, what would it even take for proof of personal survival after death, or of some benevolent moral order to the universe, to actually resolve the conflict between self-interest and duty, rather than merely being separately comforting news that leaves the underlying philosophical problem untouched? I would also appreciate help understanding why Sidgwick thought he needed hard empirical evidence in the first place, given that he had already resigned his fellowship at Trinity College, Cambridge, back in 1869 rather than go on affirming Christian doctrines he could no longer honestly accept on faith. It seems like it would have been much simpler for him to do something closer to what Kant did, simply postulating immortality and the existence of God as necessary presuppositions of practical reason without requiring any actual proof of them. Why wasn't a Kantian-style postulate an acceptable solution for Sidgwick, and what does that difference tell us about how he understood the demands of practical rationality? My teacher mentioned that this is a bit beyond the scope of the course, so I would really appreciate a thorough answer.
