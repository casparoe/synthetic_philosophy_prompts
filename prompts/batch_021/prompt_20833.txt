The following question was set on a problem set in a graduate course on social choice theory and welfare economics:

"State Harsanyi's Social Aggregation Theorem, being explicit about the role the Pareto condition plays in the proof, and explain how Diamond's objection is meant to show that the theorem does not, on its own, vindicate utilitarianism."

A student submitted this answer:

"Harsanyi's theorem starts from two representation assumptions: each individual i's preferences over social lotteries satisfy the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms, and so can be represented by a utility function U_i unique up to a positive affine transformation, and society's preferences over the same lotteries satisfy the same axioms and are represented by a utility function W. To these Harsanyi adds a Pareto condition: if every individual is indifferent between two lotteries, then society must be indifferent between them too. (This is the 1955 result, sometimes called the impartial observer theorem, in which Harsanyi imagines a benevolent spectator aggregating everyone's preferences.) Given these premises, W cannot be an arbitrary function of the U_i; it is forced to be an affine combination of them, W = a_0 + Σ a_i U_i, so the social ranking of lotteries is exactly what you would get by taking a weighted sum of individual utilities. Since this is the mathematical form utilitarianism gives to social welfare, Harsanyi presented the result as a proof that any society whose preferences are Pareto-respecting and internally rational in the expected-utility sense is, whether it likes it or not, utilitarian, with the a_i serving as the relative weights assigned to each person.

Diamond's objection is that the U_i in this argument are nothing but von Neumann–Morgenstern utility functions, fixed only up to a positive affine transformation by each person's own attitude to risk in their own gambles. There is no reason built into the theorem to think these numbers are interpersonally comparable measures of welfare of the kind classical utilitarians need in order to add them up and compare gains and losses across people. So even if the derivation is mathematically airtight, Harsanyi is not entitled to call the resulting W 'utilitarian' in the ethically substantive sense; he has shown only that a certain formal structure is forced on social preferences, not that this structure tracks welfare at all, and the aggregation theorem therefore falls short of a defense of utilitarianism."
