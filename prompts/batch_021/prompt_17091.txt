Random question but I started propranolol a few weeks back for interview anxiety, and it's kind of thrown me for a philosophical loop tbh. I still get the racing thoughts and dread beforehand, but none of the physical stuff (pounding heart, shaky hands, sweating) happens anymore, and the whole thing genuinely feels like a milder, different emotion, even though nothing about the actual situation changed. Went down a google hole about this and ended up on this old William James passage from his 1884 essay "What is an Emotion?" that apparently kicked off this whole debate:

"Common-sense says, we lose our fortune, are sorry and weep; we meet a bear, are frightened and run; we are insulted by a rival, are angry and strike. The hypothesis here to be defended says that this order of sequence is incorrect, that the one mental state is not immediately induced by the other, that the bodily manifestations must first be interposed between, and that the more rational statement is that we feel sorry because we cry, angry because we strike, afraid because we tremble, and not that we cry, strike, or tremble, because we are sorry, angry, or fearful, as the case may be. Without the bodily states following on the perception, the latter would be purely cognitive in form, pale, colorless, destitute of emotional warmth. We might then see the bear, and judge it best to run, receive the insult and deem it right to strike, but we should not actually feel afraid or angry."

Can someone actually unpack the argument here for me, past the cartoon "you're sad because you cry" summary everyone gives it? Specifically: what work is "interposed" doing in his account, and does my beta-blocker experience actually match what he'd predict (judging the situation is scary but not feeling it), or does the fact that I still get some cognitive dread mean im not really a case of his theory at all, just something adjacent to it? Trying to figure out if i'm a confirmation of James or a counterexample to him lol.
