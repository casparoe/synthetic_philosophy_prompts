As a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, I've grown used to one particular identity puzzle in code: in a language like Python, two objects can satisfy `==`, agreeing on every field and value, while still failing `is` -- numerically distinct objects that just happen to be equal. Lately I've been stuck on a much stranger cousin of that puzzle in metaphysics: the statue and the clay, where two things share not just equal values but literally all the same matter, parts, and location, and yet still don't seem to be identical. The sharpest version of the case I've found: a sculptor shapes a lump of clay into a statue in a single motion, so the lump and the statue begin to exist at the same instant, occupy the same place, and share every microphysical part for as long as both exist. (I believe this is due to the philosopher Allan Gibbard, who named the two "Lumpl" and "Goliath," though I can't promise I have that right.) The lump could survive being squashed into a ball; the statue plainly could not. By Leibniz's Law, if the lump and the statue were one and the same object, everything true of one would have to be true of the other -- so this looks like proof that there are two coincident objects here, not one. But nothing in their identical matter and shared history seems available to ground that difference between them.

Please write me an essay that lays out this argument rigorously, presses the grounding worry I just raised against the two-objects view as hard as it deserves, weighs it against whichever rival solution you think is strongest (Gibbard is said to have responded by denying that identity has to be necessary, though you're not bound to defend that particular move), and then commits to one specific verdict about what the statue is in relation to the clay. Don't end with a non-committal survey of positions.
