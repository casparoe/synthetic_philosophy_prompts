I host a small podcast about the philosophy and math behind everyday decisions, and next week's guest is a formal epistemologist who's built a bit of a public following defending I.J. Good's old 1967 theorem on the value of information -- the result showing that a Bayesian who's about to act should never expect free evidence to leave you worse off, so looking before you decide is, in expectation, always the rational move. Her go-to soundbite, which I've now heard her repeat on two other podcasts, is "there's no such thing as evidence you're rationally better off not seeing," and she uses it to argue for stuff like mandatory disclosure in medicine and why scientists shouldn't sit on unpublished results. I got a bit nerd-sniped rereading the original paper last week and really don't want this interview to just be forty minutes of her restating the slogan.

Can you draft me eight to ten interview questions, each with a follow-up keyed to whatever answer she's most likely to give? I want at least one question on the gap between what the theorem actually guarantees -- non-negative *expected* value, calculated before you look -- and what any one actual piece of news can do to you after the fact, since people get blindsided by true evidence all the time without being irrational. I want another that presses on whether that guarantee is basically bought by how narrowly the theorem's "utility" gets defined, i.e. whether it quietly assumes knowing something never has negative value in itself, which would make cases like people refusing genetic test results, or not wanting to learn their kid's sex before birth, non-counterexamples by definition rather then real trouble for her slogan. I'd also like a question built around the old Hirshleifer point that public information can wreck insurance and trading arrangements and leave everyone worse off collectively, even though it's never bad news for whoever happens to receive it -- does she think that's a totally separate phenomenon, or a crack in her individual-level guarantee once you scale it up past one agent? End with something that tries to pin her down to naming one realistic case that would actually break the theorem, rather than a case that's just excluded by its own fine print -- that's the question I suspect she'll try hardest to wriggle out of.
