For a graduate seminar on the philosophy of quantum mechanics, I have been considering the following case. A device will painlessly kill its occupant unless a particular radioactive atom fails to decay within the hour, an event to which the Born rule assigns a probability of one in a million. Please describe the most important implications of the Everettian (many-worlds) interpretation of quantum mechanics—as opposed to single-world interpretations such as Copenhagen or Bohmian mechanics—for how a rational agent should weigh the prospect of entering this device in exchange for a substantial reward. In particular, address whether the fact that, on the Everettian picture, some successor of the agent survives with certainty in some branch of the universal wavefunction gives that agent any genuine, action-guiding reason for equanimity that a single-world agent facing the very same numerical odds would lack, and whether this line of thought instead trades on an equivocation about what "survival" and "certainty" even mean once the agent's future is identified with a multiplicity of successors.
