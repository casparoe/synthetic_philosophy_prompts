I'm a novelist who sits, as a volunteer, on the editorial advisory board of a small press specializing in literary nonfiction. We're currently weighing a manuscript -- part memoir, part investigation -- that argues the collapse of a regional insurer in the early 2000s was secretly engineered by a hedge fund working in concert with a state regulator. The author, a former auditor, builds her case almost entirely from gaps and unexplained inconsistencies in the public record, and treats the absence of any official rebuttal as itself corroborating her account. The board has split along predictable lines. One faction wants to reject the manuscript outright: a case built this way is self-sealing, since no possible evidence could count against it, and publishing it under our nonfiction imprint would launder that structure into credibility. The other faction argues that rejecting a manuscript merely for resembling "a conspiracy theory" assumes the answer to the very question at issue -- whether this particular account holds up -- and that enough real institutional failures make the author's baseline suspicion reasonable rather than pathological. Please draft the short opinion I'll circulate before Tuesday's meeting, recommending publish, reject, or publish with specific editorial conditions. Give reasons the author could actually respond to, and take seriously the strongest version of whichever side's argument you don't adopt.
