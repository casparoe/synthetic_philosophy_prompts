Okay so this happened at work last week and I haven't been able to stop thinking about it. We have this quarterly go/no-go review before shipping any "major" feature, where a panel of three senior engineers has to sign off. For our checkout redesign, the launch rule was dead simple: you only ship if the feature is BOTH performant enough AND secure enough, no exceptions. Whoever ran the meeting had each of the three panelists fill out a little form — yes/no on performance, yes/no on security, and then, seperately, a gut-check yes/no on "should we actually ship this."

Here's the part that's been bugging me. Criteria by criteria, the feature passes: 2 of the 3 panelists said performance was good enough, and 2 of the 3 said security was good enough (though notably not the same two people both times). So if you just apply the rule dimension by dimension, it clearly should ship. But when you look at the direct "should we ship" column, 2 of the 3 actually voted no! And nobody was being inconsistent about it either — I double checked, and every single panelist's own ship/no-ship answer matched their own performance-and-security answers exactly, nobody contradicted themselves. The flip only shows up once you tally across people instead of looking within one person's answers. My tech lead just shrugged and said "a majority's a majority, pick whichever column you like," which feels like a total dodge to me. I mentioned it to a friend who's finishing her philosophy PhD and she got weirdly excited, said this was apparently "a real, named thing" that people write papers about, and then had to hop off a call before explaining further, so now I'm dying to know.

So, genuinely: is there some principled reason to trust one of those two tallies (criteria-by-criteria vs. the direct vote) over the other, or is this just some inherent structural gotcha in group decision-making that we're basically doomed to run into again? Is this the same animal as Arrow's theorem (which is the only "voting is broken" result I'd ever heard of before this), or is something else going on? I've got zero philosophy background, so if you could actually walk through why this happens rather than just dropping a term on me, that'd be amazing.
