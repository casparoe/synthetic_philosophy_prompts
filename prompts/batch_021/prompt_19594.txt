I spent nearly forty years running an experimental physiology lab before retiring, and I have recently taken up philosophy of science, which has reopened an old professional dispute I never fully settled. Early in my career, we were testing whether a compound altered a reflex response in rats. The protocol called for twenty animals, but the p-value after those twenty came out tantalizingly close to the conventional 0.05 threshold, so we added ten more and reanalyzed the combined data, which then cleared the threshold. A referee rejected the paper, insisting the finding was untrustworthy because our decision to add more animals had been influenced by that first look, even though the extra data were unremarkable on their own. I argued that what the numbers show should depend only on the numbers themselves, not on our reasons for having collected exactly that many; the referee insisted this missed something essential about how error rates behave. With a little philosophical training now, I still cannot decide who was right. Which of us had the better argument?
