In my AP Micro class we got to the marginal utility unit and my teacher made this offhand comment that economists have basically given up on ever being able to compare how happy one person is versus how happy another person is — like there's no legit scientific way to prove that giving $50 to a poor person makes them happier than that same $50 makes a rich person sad to lose, even though that seems obvious to literally everyone. She called it "the problem of interpersonal utility comparisons" and said its a big reason a lot of economists get nervous about doing straightforward utilitarian cost-benefit stuff with real policies. That comment sent me down a rabbit hole and I found out that this 19th century economist, Francis Edgeworth, actually tried to just solve this with math, in an 1881 book literally called Mathematical Psychics.

I found the specific passage where he tries to pull this off and I've read it probably six times now on my phone and I only kind of get it, atleast the general vibe — his sentences are insane. Here's the bit:

"For moral calculus a further dimension is required; to compare the happiness of one person with the happiness of another, and generally the happiness of groups of different members and different average happiness. Such comparison can no longer be shirked, if there is to be any systematic morality at all. It is postulated by distributive justice. It is postulated by the population question; that horizon in which every moral prospect terminates; which is presented to the far-seeing at every turn, on the most sacred and the most trivial occasions. You cannot spend sixpence utilitarianly, without having considered whether your action tends to increase the comfort of a limited number, or numbers with limited comfort; without having compared such alternative utilities. In virtue of what unit is such comparison possible? It is here submitted: Any individual experiencing a unit of pleasure-intensity during a unit of time is to 'count for one.' Utility, then, has three dimensions; a mass of utility, 'lot of pleasure,' is greater than another when it has more intensity-time-number units. The third dimension is doubtless an evolutional acquisition; and is still far from perfectly evolved. [...] But the first dimension, where we leave the safe ground of the objective, equating to unity each minimum sensibile, presents indeed peculiar difficulties. Atoms of pleasure are not easy to distinguish and discern; more continuous than sand, more discrete than liquid; as it were nuclei of the just-perceivable, embedded in circumambient semi-consciousness. We cannot count the golden sands of life; we cannot number the 'innumerable smile' of seas of love; but we seem to be capable of observing that there is here a greater, there a less, multitude of pleasure-units, mass of happiness; and that is enough."

Can you actually translate what he's doing here into plain English?
