I spent thirty-five years as an experimental physicist working mostly in statistical mechanics, and in retirement I have been teaching myself formal ethics and decision theory. The result I keep returning to is Harsanyi's aggregation theorem, if only because, unlike so much of moral philosophy, it has the structure of a derivation: given that each individual's preferences over lotteries satisfy the standard rationality axioms and that social preference respects a mild Pareto condition, the theorem shows that social preference itself must be represented as a weighted sum of individual utilities — utilitarianism obtained as a conclusion rather than posited as a premise. What troubles me is Diamond's old coin-flip case, in which two people with identical prospects turn out, on the theorem's own terms, to be ranked as indifferent between a fair lottery for an indivisible good and its outright award to one of them. Please argue that this is not a minor wrinkle to be patched with some auxiliary constraint, but a decisive reason to deny that Harsanyi's theorem gives utilitarianism the distinctively ethical, fairness-respecting foundation it is usually thought to provide.
