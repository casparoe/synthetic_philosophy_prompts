I co-host a small podcast on the philosophy of physics, and next week's guest is a physicist who has just published a book making the case for superdeterminism to a general audience -- the view that Bell's theorem can be blocked without giving up locality if we instead give up measurement independence, the assumption that the hidden variables governing a pair of entangled particles are statistically independent of whatever detector settings the experimenters will later choose. Her go-to move whenever the "conspiracy" charge comes up is to say that it simply begs the question against determinism: in a fully deterministic universe, she points out, every correlation traces back to some common cause, so there is nothing distinctively suspicious about the correlations her view happens to need. I'd like eight to ten interview questions that don't let her rest on that one-liner.

Please make sure the set includes at least one question that pushes past "it's just determinism" to the sharper fine-tuning worry -- why correlations that reproduce the quantum statistics so exactly wouldn't need an explanation of their own, over and above the bare fact of determinism; at least one on what result, if any, could ever count as evidence against superdeterminism, given that loophole-closing experiments have already used light that left quasars billions of years ago to fix detector settings and still recovered the correlations quantum mechanics predicts; at least one asking her to distinguish her common-cause story from retrocausal proposals that also deny measurement independence, but by letting the future choice of settings influence the particles' past state rather than by tracing both back to a shared cause; and at least one that raises Bell's own recorded discomfort with this escape route, given his worry that it undermines the very freedom experimenters need to assume they have in order to vary conditions and test anything at all. For each of these, add a short, pointed follow-up question keyed to the answer she is most likely to give, so that the conversation actually advances instead of letting her repeat the same deflection under four different names.
