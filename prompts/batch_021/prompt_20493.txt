My cousin Daniel and I have spent the better part of two years contesting our late grandmother's estate, and after I recounted the whole saga to a friend over dinner last week, we ended up arguing about it for a good two hours without settling anything. The estate's one substantial asset is a lake house that a generous appraisal values at around $340,000. Daniel and I have each already paid our respective lawyers upward of $65,000 disputing a boundary survey and an ambiguously worded clause in the will, and every time either of us signals a willingness to settle, the other responds with a new motion or a new expert report, so the combined legal bill keeps climbing no matter who eventually prevails. My friend's position was blunt: I should walk away now, because the $65,000 is gone regardless of what I do next, and the only thing that should matter going forward is whether continuing has a positive expected payoff, which she doesn't think it does.

I told her I agreed with that principle in the abstract, but that it doesn't quite fit my situation, because Daniel isn't a fixed cost I'm irrationally chasing -- he's a person reasoning the same way I am, in real time, and adjusting to whatever I do. If I back out now, I lock in a total loss on the legal fees; if I hold firm, there's at least a chance the money "pays off"; and since Daniel faces the mirror image of that calculation, we can each be individually rational to keep going even though we both know the combined bill will eventually dwarf the house's value. My friend wasn't persuaded. She said this was just the sunk-cost fallacy wearing a more sophisticated disguise, and that dragging Daniel's incentives into it doesn't change what I owe myself, which is to stop pouring money into something that can't retroactively justify what's already spent. I pointed out that if she's right, the identical argument makes it irrational for Daniel to continue, and yet from his side it looks exactly as compelling as it does from mine -- which suggests that whatever is going wrong here isn't simply a private error either of us is making about sunk costs, but something about the shape of the situation itself. Neither of us budged, and dinner ended with both of us mildly annoyed.

I'd like an outside view on this. Is there a genuine distinction between what's happening between Daniel and me and an ordinary sunk-cost fallacy, given that he's a strategic party reacting to my moves rather than an inert cost sitting in the past? And if there is such a distinction, does recognizing it actually tell either of us anything useful about when, if ever, it becomes rational to stop -- or does it just explain why people get stuck in standoffs like this without handing either of us a way out of one?
