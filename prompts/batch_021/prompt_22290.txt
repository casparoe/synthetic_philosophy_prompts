I am several chapters into a novel set among the Paris philosophes in 1758, the year Rousseau's Letter to d'Alembert on the theatre appeared, and I have just reached a dinner scene at Baron d'Holbach's table. Rousseau himself is conspicuously absent — he has already withdrawn to Montmorency, still nursing the injury of Diderot's line in Le Fils naturel that only a wicked man lives alone (a detail I picked up from Damrosch's biography of him) — so the rest of the table feels free to enjoy itself at his expense. Diderot leads the mockery, and not merely out of that old grievance: Rousseau's whole argument, that spectacle can never make anyone more virtuous and only lets them feel absolved without acting, is a direct affront to Diderot's own conviction that a well-made drama can move an audience to real feeling and, through that feeling, to real virtue.

What I need from you is the joke itself. I have tried drafting a few myself, but they keep coming out as potted summaries of the Letter with a rimshot attached, which is worse than having no joke at all. I want a line or two that Diderot could plausibly toss off at that table, something that gets its laugh from a specific feature of Rousseau's actual position rather than the lazy "the man who hates theatre wrote a hit opera" jab — his claim that actors are paid to counterfeit passions they have never felt, say, or his idea that weeping for an imaginary sufferer discharges a spectator's whole debt to virtue without requiring them to leave their seat, or his preference for an artless village festival, with no stage and no audience, only citizens, over anything a professional could stage. Could you give me two versions, in noticeably different registers — one dry and needling, one closer to an outright insult — and after each, a single sentence telling me exactly which part of Rousseau's argument it depends on, so I can judge which one would actually land for a reader who has read the Letter rather than one who only knows Rousseau's reputation for contradiction?
