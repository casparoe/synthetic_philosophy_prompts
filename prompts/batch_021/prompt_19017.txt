Last night a friend and I argued for two hours over whether properly asserting something requires knowing it, or only requires a justified belief that could turn out false, and neither of us gave ground. I now need a sharper answer, because my university's research integrity panel is reviewing a case that turns on exactly this question. A postdoc's paper flatly reported that a compound reduced IL-6 levels by 40 percent in a mouse arthritis model, a claim the evidence available to her fully supported at the time; eighteen months later, an independent lab traced the result to an undetectable contaminated reagent lot, and the effect vanished on replication. A rival lab wants this logged as reckless disregard for accuracy rather than honest error, since she stated the finding outright instead of calling it preliminary. Our misconduct policy defines violations only as fabrication, falsification, and plagiarism, and says nothing about what standard an unhedged claim must meet before publication. The committee is split between those who think a claim that later proves false was improperly asserted regardless of the evidence at the time, and those who think justified belief settles the matter. Please draft the short memo I'll bring to Friday's meeting: recommend whether to dismiss the complaint, log this as honest error, or find misconduct, specify any resulting action, and give the losing side's strongest argument a fair hearing rather than waving it away.
