So this happened in my formal epistemology class -- we're right in the middle of the unit on Jeffrey conditionalization (the update rule for when your evidence doesn't come in with certainty attached, from Richard Jeffrey's book The Logic of Decision). Two people from my study group, Mia and Diego, were working through a practice problem where you update a prior over some hypothesis H using Jeffrey's rule twice back to back: first on a soft-evidence partition about how a glass of wine smells, then on one about how it tastes, and then the problem makes you redo the whole thing with the two updates in the opposite order. They each did the algebra and got noticeably different final credences in H depending on the order (something like mid-0.6s one way, mid-0.5s the other), and that's when it stopped being a homework problem and became an actual fight.

Mia said they must have messed up the arithmetic, because no rule for rational updating should be sensitive to the order two pieces of evidence happen to arrive in -- if smell-then-taste and taste-then-smell hand you different final opinions off the exact same total evidence, that's a red flag, not a feature. Diego said he'd redone it by hand three times and it wasn't an arithmetic error, that this is apparently a genuine, known wrinkle of Jeffrey's rule that ordinary conditionalization doesn't have, and that it comes down to "rigidity" being pinned to one particular partition at one particular moment, so the first update can quietly wreck the very assumption that justified the numbers you were about to plug into the second. Mia wasn't buying it: she said that just turns rigidity into a get-out-of-jail-free card, since you can always explain away a weird result after the fact by insisting rigidity held right up until it stopped holding, and a rule that can absorb literally any outcome that way isn't constraining anyone's beliefs, so calling it a "generalization" of Bayesian conditionalization is generous. Diego answered that rigidity is still a substantive, checkable claim at each individual step -- you can genuinely ask whether learning the wine tastes off should change your credence in H given that it smells fine, and there's a fact of the matter -- it's just relative to a time and a partition, no different from how an ordinary conditional probability is always relative to some stock of background information without that making conditionalization empty; and if you want order-independence back, there's a further condition tying the two partitions together that you'd need, which their wine example clearly doesnt satisfy, which is presumably why the TA wrote it that way on purpose. Mia's last word was that this still leaves you with two equally rational people who absorb identical total evidence in a different order, purely by accident of which sense they happened to pay attention to first, and end up stuck with different opinions forever after -- and being able to explain exactly where it comes apart doesn't make that outcome any less bad for Jeffrey conditionalization as an account of how rational people are supposed to change their minds.
