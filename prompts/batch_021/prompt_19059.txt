I have been attending a graduate seminar on the metaphysics of persistence, and one issue that keeps coming up is that survey articles on endurantism versus perdurantism tend to line up the standard arguments for four-dimensionalism as though they all carried roughly the same evidential weight. My seminar instructor disagrees rather strongly: she thinks some of these considerations are close to decisive, while others are little more than intuition pumps that a sufficiently determined endurantist can neutralize at fairly low cost (by relativizing properties to times, going adverbialist, relativizing "wholly present" to a reference frame, or simply biting the relevant bullet). I would like your help actually adjudicating this rather than just rehearsing the arguments again.

Below are eight considerations that regularly appear in this debate, each with a short gloss so that we are working from the same description of each:

- Temporary intrinsics (Lewis): an object seems to have incompatible intrinsic shapes at different times (bent while sitting, straight while standing), which perdurantism resolves by assigning each shape to a different temporal part rather than to the whole object.
- The vagueness argument (Sider): any endurantist story about exactly when an object begins or ceases to exist, or survives a gradual replacement of its parts, seems to require an arbitrary sharp cutoff, which perdurantism avoids by treating existence-facts as facts about which aggregates of parts we happen to track.
- Relativity of simultaneity: the rejection of an observer-independent "now" in special relativity sits awkwardly with the endurantist requirement that a persisting object be wholly present at a time, but coheres naturally with a four-dimensional block populated by space-time worms.
- Parity of space and time: an object can have proper spatial parts without being wholly present at each place it occupies, and it is unclear why time should be relevantly different, undermining any principled insistence that objects be wholly present at each moment they exist.
- The Ship of Theseus: an object has all of its parts gradually swapped out one by one, straining any account of what, if anything, makes the end product the same object as the original.
- Tibbles and Tib (Geach): once a cat, Tibbles, loses its tail, it becomes exactly coincident with the object that was previously "all of Tibbles except the tail," which is awkward for any view on which the two were never identical.
- Personal fission (Parfit): a person's two brain hemispheres are separately transplanted into two different bodies, each producing an equally good psychological continuer of the original person.
- Evans's argument against vague identity: a short reductio purporting to show that identity statements can never be genuinely indeterminate, which bears on whether an enduring object can have a fuzzy borderline case of identity over time.

Please sort these eight considerations into a tier list reflecting how much genuine argumentative force each one actually has for preferring perdurantism to endurantism, as distinct from how often it merely gets repeated in the literature. A consideration that a sophisticated endurantist can neutralize cheaply, or that turns out on reflection not to discriminate between the two views at all, should rank low even if it is famous.
