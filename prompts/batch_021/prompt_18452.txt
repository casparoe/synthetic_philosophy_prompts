Quick context: I'm part of a small team at an AI lab whose actual job is to write the norms for how our models are allowed to argue with people — like, when the model should push back on a user's factual belief, how hard it's allowed to press, whether it should ever try to talk someone out of something "for their own good," that kind of thing. Every year we run a two-day retreat for new hires before they start touching the actual policy docs, and I got roped into giving the opening talk on morning one. Half the room is people who've been reading LessWrong and ACX since forever and can basically recite Scott Alexander's "epistemic learned helplessness" post from memory; the other half are ML engineers who got hired for eval infra work and have never really sat with the philosophy side of any of this.

The thing I keep getting stuck on, and the thing I want the talk to actually be about rather than just wave at, is this: our whole hiring pitch and internal culture is built on "the better argument should win," but that stops being good advice the moment you admit the model might, within a few years, be more persuasive than any human or commitee of humans who ever lived — at which point a wrong-but-optimized argument can beat a right-but-ordinary one, and "just listen to whoever argues best" becomes actively dangerous guidance for how the model should treat users. But I can't stand up and say "stop trusting good arguments," partly because that's a self-defeating thing to argue for out loud, and partly because it's not the norm I actually want engineers using nine-to-five when they're grading model transcripts for the persuasion eval suite. I want the talk to genuinely take a position on how to hold both of those at once, not just describe the tension and shrug — and it has to land for both halves of the room, not read as either a philosophy lecture or a pep talk. Can you write me that opening talk, roughly 7-10 minutes read aloud?
