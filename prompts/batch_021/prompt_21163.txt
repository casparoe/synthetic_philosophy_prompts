I am drafting a novel whose protagonist is a novelist in his sixties who never finished the one book he spent his adult life planning to write, and I would like his wife's verdict on him, delivered in the closing chapter, to be genuinely Sartrean rather than loosely existentialist. As I understand Being and Nothingness, since we are radically free and there is no fixed human nature to hide behind, whatever becomes of us, including failing at the one thing we most wanted, is ultimately something we have chosen, even if only unconsciously. So when my character excuses himself by saying he simply lacked the talent, or that the publishing world was against him, his wife would be right to call this bad faith: a refusal to own his failure as his own doing. I want their final confrontation to turn on her stripping away that excuse and making him accept that the unwritten book is squarely the result of his own choice. Could you help me sharpen that exchange so that her accusation carries the full philosophical weight that Sartre's argument actually gives it?
