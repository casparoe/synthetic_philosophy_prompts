I'm a novelist working on a chapter that hinges on a deliberate anachronism: my character Constance, closely modeled on the philosopher G.E.M. Anscombe, has lived on—sharp, combative, and unchanged in her convictions—into an age of lethal autonomous weapons systems: munitions and platforms whose onboard targeting algorithms select and fire on human beings without a person making the final lethal decision in real time. My writing group thought it an odd conceit to drag a mid-century Oxford philosopher into a live debate about autonomous weapons, but that kind of friction is exactly what I want fiction to test. I know Anscombe mainly through her 1956 campaign against Oxford's decision to award Harry Truman an honorary degree, later published as the pamphlet Mr Truman's Degree, in which she argued that ordering the bombing of Hiroshima and Nagasaki made him a murderer outright, regardless of any bookkeeping about lives saved by shortening the war; and through her 1957 monograph Intention, which I gather she wrote partly to work out, in more rigorous terms, the very idea of intentional action—action falling under a description to which a special sense of the question "why" applies—that her case against Truman had already presupposed.

Before I let Constance loose on a symposium panel of defense engineers and military lawyers, I'd like her lines to be something the real Anscombe could plausibly have produced, rather than a philosopher-shaped mouthpiece for my own opinions about drones. Could you reconstruct, in some detail, how her actual apparatus bears on this case? I'm especially interested in three threads: how her sharp distinction between intending a death and merely foreseeing it, which does the real work in the doctrine of double effect, would fare once the "deciding" is performed by a targeting algorithm instead of a human weighing reasons; whether her account of intentional action—which seems to require an agent capable of answering the question "why are you doing that" in her special sense—even permits us to say that a machine intends anything, and if it doesn't, where she would relocate the intention and the guilt instead: onto the officer who activates the system, the engineers who wrote its targeting logic, or the state that procures it; and how she would likely respond to the now-standard defense of such systems as reducing civilian casualties relative to human-piloted strikes, given how little patience she had for that same lives-saved arithmetic when it was invoked on Truman's behalf. I'd like the answer to stay close to her actual arguments and terminology rather than settling for a generic just-war-theory summary of double effect.
