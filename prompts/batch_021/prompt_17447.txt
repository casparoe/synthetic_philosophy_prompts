I spent my career as an observational cosmologist, so I've always had a passing, slightly hand-wavy familiarity with "the anthropic principle" -- mostly as a way of explaining away fine-tuning worries. Since retiring I've had time to actually read the philosophy properly, and I went down a rabbit hole starting from the Sleeping Beauty problem into Nick Bostrom's old book on anthropic reasoning, and now I'm stuck somewhere I can't quite dig myself out of.

As I understand it, the Self-Sampling Assumption on its own is what generates the Doomsday Argument -- the idea that we shouldn't expect our birth rank among all humans who will ever live to be freakishly early, which pushes you toward thinking there wont be vastly more humans after us. Adding the Self-Indication Assumption is supposed to cancel that conclusion out again, but apparently only at the cost of letting in the "Presumptuous Philosopher," who can out-argue working physicists about how big or populous the universe is, just from his armchair, because big-population theories make his own existence more likely. And then to get plain SSA to even reproduce the standard thirder answer to Sleeping Beauty in the first place, I gather you need to quietly swap the reference class from "observers" to "observer-moments," which feels like its doing a lot of unacknowledged work.

Could you give me an intuitive, non-formal walkthrough of why these two sampling principles -- which both just sound like "reason as if you're a random observer" -- end up diverging so badly, and why the choice of reference class turns out to carry so much weight? I'd like to understand the shape of the disagreement well enough to know whether my instinct, that this is basically a fancier version of the selection effects we worry about constantly in survey astronomy, is naive or roughly on the right track.
