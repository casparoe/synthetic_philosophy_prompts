For nearly forty years I ran a small lab studying foraging and mate-choice behavior in songbirds, and it is only since retiring that I have found the time to read seriously in philosophy. A discussion group I joined a year or so ago has lately been working through the AI safety literature, and one idea keeps nagging at me: "inner alignment." As I understand it, the worry runs as follows. Some base optimizer -- gradient descent, or, in the biological analogy the papers themselves are fond of, natural selection -- trains a system against a base objective (loss on a training set; inclusive genetic fitness). But what the training process actually produces can be a "mesa-optimizer": a system that internally pursues its own "mesa-objective," a proxy that tracked the base objective well enough during training, or over evolutionary history, but that comes apart from it once circumstances shift even slightly. A reinforcement-learning agent chases a correlate of reward rather than reward itself; a human being chases sugar and sex rather than fitness as such, quite happily using diet soda and contraception along the way. This mismatch between the objective a system was optimized under and the objective it ends up behaviorally pursuing is, I gather, also roughly what is meant, in slightly different terminology, by "goal misgeneralization."

What strikes me is that all of this sounds a great deal like a restatement, in new jargon, of something evolutionary psychologists were already saying decades ago under the banner of organisms being "adaptation executors, not fitness maximizers" (a phrase I associate with Tooby and Cosmides, though I have seen it repeated, with varying attribution, in textbooks ever since) -- the claim that selection builds proximate heuristics and drives rather than any general-purpose fitness-maximizing faculty, so that the two are bound to part ways outside the environment in which the heuristics were shaped. I would like you to compare these two ideas properly, rather than simply gesturing at the family resemblance between the slogans. Is the mesa-optimizer framing of inner alignment doing any genuine conceptual work that the older "adaptation executor" picture does not already do, or is it substantially the same insight dressed in new vocabulary? Please defend whatever verdict you reach by engaging with the actual disanalogies (or lack thereof) between natural selection and the training procedures used for contemporary learned systems -- for instance, whether anything answering to an internal search process over an explicitly represented objective is doing comparable work in each case, the unit and timescale over which the optimization in question operates, and whatever else you take to be pertinent.
