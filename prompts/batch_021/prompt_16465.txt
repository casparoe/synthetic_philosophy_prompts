What she sent back: Plato's Laws is interesting because it's way more chill about pleasure than the Republic, where the appetites are basically the enemy that reason has to keep locked down. Here the Athenian Stranger actually defends drinking parties, and his basic move is that Athens has a more sophisticated take on building self control than the sternness of Sparta and Crete: instead of just flatly banning pleasure the way they ban drunkenness, wine gives citizens a more refined, controlled way to practice resisting temptation, since a person's real character shows up once their inhibitions are down. It's basically a supervised simulation of temptation instead of the real thing, which is why every party needs a sober person in charge (a symposiarch) who can stop things from getting out of hand, kind of like a general keeping control during a military drill. Because young people are the ones who most need this kind of character training, Plato sets it up so drinking is loosest for teenagers and young men and then gets gradually more restricted as men get older and their habits are already set, since there's less point testing somebody whose character is basically already formed. That's also the core of his complaint about Sparta and Crete: just forbidding wine outright doesn't train anyone's self control, it just avoids the whole situation, kind of like never leaving the house so you never have to be brave.
