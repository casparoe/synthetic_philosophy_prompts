I co-edit my high school newspaper, and our editorial board is deadlocked, with our print deadline two days away. A reporter has spent two months on a piece alleging that three school board members privately arranged to move funds out of the district's English Language Learner program before a public vote. Her case rests on two facts: a meeting among the three appears on the district's public calendar the day before the relevant budget line was revised, and the district has since given three different explanations for that revision. Two editors want to spike the story, since treating inconsistent statements and irregular procedure as evidence of coordination is exactly what makes conspiracy theories unfalsifiable: every denial just becomes further proof of the cover-up. The other two say that standard means we could never expose a real cover-up, since genuine ones look identical from outside, and that refusing to run anything with that "shape" simply protects whoever already holds power. We have no written policy on this. Please draft the memo I'll bring to tomorrow's meeting: a recommendation on this story, plus a standard for future ones, that takes the case against publication seriously rather than dismissing it as timidity.
