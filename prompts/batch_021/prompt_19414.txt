I've been working through some of the older MIRI material on formal self-trust for self-modifying agents for a reading group, and I find myself torn between two rival responses to the Löbian obstacle. The "tiling agents" line, going back to Yudkowsky and Herreshoff's paper of that name, treats Löb's theorem as exposing a genuine, in-principle limit: a proof-based agent cannot in general derive a blanket schema like "if my proof system proves that action X is safe, then X is safe" without thereby being able to prove that X is safe outright for every X, so the only fix on offer is a well-founded descending tower of successively weaker proof systems, each able to vouch for the one just below it — which means an agent can trust a strictly weaker successor, but never a full copy of itself. The rival, probabilistic line, represented by logical induction, treats this as largely an artifact of insisting on proof rather than calibrated credence as the currency of self-trust: credences aren't bound by the derivability conditions that make Löb's theorem bite in the first place, and a logical inductor can apparently assign reasonable, well-calibrated credences to claims about its own future outputs without falling into the same trap.

Write a dialogue between a proof-based tiling-agents theorist and a logical-induction theorist who are standing at a whiteboard on which someone has just finished sketching the diagonal-lemma proof of Löb's theorem, each trying to convince the other that their own framework is the right way to think about whether a self-modifying AI could ever trust a successor as capable as itself. Make sure each side directly engages with the other's strongest objection rather than talking past it — for instance, the tiling theorist pressing the worry that a merely asymptotic, eventually-calibrated guarantee is no comfort for a one-shot, irreversible act of self-modification, and the logical-induction theorist countering that the descending-tower fix never actually delivers trust in an equally powerful successor either, so it isn't obviously the more rigorous solution it's sometimes taken to be. The dialogue doesn't need to end in agreement, or even in agreement about what would count as settling the dispute.
