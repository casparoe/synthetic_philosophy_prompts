For the past several years I have chaired my state's Bird Records Committee, a body most non-birders have never heard of, whose sole function is to rule on which reports of rare or unprecedented species get entered into the official state list. My colleagues in the philosophy department treat this as a harmless eccentricity, but the committee's actual deliberations keep raising, in unusually concrete form, questions I otherwise only get to pose to a seminar. We have a genuinely hard case on the agenda for next week's meeting. Our most senior and, by common agreement, most careful observer -- no report of hers has ever been rejected or seriously disputed in over twenty years of submissions -- has filed a detailed written account of a songbird with no accepted prior record in the state and only a handful of accepted records anywhere in the surrounding region. She watched the bird for about a minute at close range in good light, and her description, written from memory before she consulted any field guide (as our protocol requires, precisely to guard against retrofitting), specifically and correctly rules out every plausible look-alike. There is no photograph, no recording, and no second observer: she was alone, and her camera had failed that morning. Our bylaws require "convincing evidence" for acceptance but say nothing about whether sufficiently detailed testimony from a proven observer can constitute such evidence on its own, or whether a claim this far outside precedent always requires independent corroboration regardless of who is making it. The eight of us are evenly split, and each side thinks the other is building an unworkable general policy out of one hard case: reject the record, and we seem committed to the view that no observer's word could ever, by itself, establish an unprecedented record; accept it, and we seem committed to the view that an unblemished track record plus an internally consistent story is always enough, however extraordinary the claim.

Could you draft the short memo I will circulate before the vote? It should recommend one specific disposition for this record -- full acceptance, rejection, or acceptance in some provisional or lesser category, if you think our bylaws can reasonably be read to permit one -- backed by a rationale my colleagues could actually put to use in the meeting, rather than a restatement of "convincing evidence" as though that phrase settled anything on its own. I would especially like the memo to engage honestly with whatever is most defensible in the position it ends up recommending against, since both camps on this committee are considerably more thoughtful than the brief caricature I've just given of them.
