I'm working on a magazine feature about the small but tenacious school of logicians who defend dialetheism—the view that some contradictions are literally true—and I keep noticing that its leading contemporary advocate, Graham Priest, doesn't dismiss Aristotle as a historical footnote. Instead, he goes after Aristotle's own arguments for the law of non-contradiction in Metaphysics Gamma directly: the "signification" argument, that a term simultaneously affirming and denying the same thing of an object would fail to pick out anything determinate and so make meaningful speech impossible; the argument that admitting even one true contradiction collapses every distinction into an undifferentiated whole, in the manner Aristotle attributes to Anaxagoras and Heraclitus; and the more behavioral argument, illustrated by Aristotle's own example of a man who sets off walking to Megara rather than simply staying put, that no one actually lives as though contradictory beliefs were equally assertable.

What I would find most useful is a considered reconstruction of how this confrontation would go if Aristotle could be briefed not just on Priest's specific rebuttals, but on the genuinely new considerations that motivate dialetheism today: the Liar paradox's resistance to fully consistent treatments such as Tarski's hierarchy of languages, Kripke's fixed-point semantics, and revision theories of truth; Priest's own three-valued "Logic of Paradox," which allows a sentence to be both true and false while blocking the explosion of trivial consequences a contradiction produces in classical logic; and the use of paraconsistent reasoning to handle cases like naive set theory or genuinely inconsistent databases without simply outlawing contradiction by decree. Do Aristotle's three classical arguments retain their force against a position built to withstand exactly this kind of pressure, or would he need substantively new arguments to hold his ground—and if the latter, what might they look like, given his own commitments elsewhere in the Metaphysics and the Organon?
