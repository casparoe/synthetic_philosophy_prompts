I've devoted the last few weeks of my graduate seminar on formal epistemology to Bayesian updating, and this week we worked through van Fraassen's Judy Benjamin problem. For context: an agent starts with a uniform prior over four regions, arranged as two "territories" each divided into two sub-areas, and then receives a radio message that reports only a conditional odds statement about the internal split within one of the territories -- nothing is said that directly compares the two territories to one another. The puzzle is that when you update via the natural generalization of Jeffrey conditionalization to this sort of conditional constraint -- minimizing relative information (cross-entropy) relative to the prior, subject to the new conditional holding -- the marginal probability of being in the territory the message was "about" shifts anyway, even though the message never compared that territory to the other. My students split into camps over this: some think it shows relative-information minimization is simply the wrong rule for updating on conditionals; others think the shift is exactly what a careful Bayesian should expect, and that our resistance to it is the real error; and a few want to abandon the whole probability-kinematics framework in favor of treating conditional credence as its own primitive, along the lines of Adams-style conditional probability or Lewis's "imaging" rule.

Rather than ask you to referee that particular classroom dispute, I would like to step back and ask about the bigger picture. Please describe what you take to be the most important implications of the Judy Benjamin problem for a situation that comes up constantly outside the seminar room: reasoning from someone else's testimony when it arrives only in conditional form. A doctor tells me "if the biopsy comes back positive, it's very likely to be the aggressive subtype"; a colleague tells me "if the paper gets past the first referee, its odds with the editor are excellent" -- in neither case do they volunteer the unconditional probability of the antecedent. Ordinary conditionalization doesn't obviously tell me what to do with testimony like this. I would like to know what the puzzle really teaches about whether and how such conditional statements should move my unconditional beliefs, and whether it counsels any specific caution for people, like me, who reason this way in ordinary life without ever running the underlying optimization explicitly.
