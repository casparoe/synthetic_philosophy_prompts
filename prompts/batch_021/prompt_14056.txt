Okay so in my Multiagent Systems seminar (cross-listed with philosophy, basically "game theory for people worried about AI") we spent a whole week on this idea called safe Pareto improvements -- I think it's from a paper by Oesterheld and Conitzer, though don't quote me on that. The setup as I understood it: two principals don't play a game directly, they each hand it off to a representative (a lawyer, a diplomat, or, the version everyone in class actually cared about, an AI system acting on your behalf), and the worry is that representatives who are free to make threats or commitments can leave both principals worse off than if nobody had gotten clever -- basically a commitment race where each side is incentivized to lock in the most aggressive bargaining position first, and it blows up in everyone's face. A "safe" Pareto improvement is supposed to be some agreement the principals strike in advance that guarantees neither of them does worse than their boring do-nothing baseline, while still leaving room to capture some upside, and the safety part specifically means it doesnt require trusting the other principal's honesty or rationality any more than the baseline itself already required.

Here's why I actually care about this beyond the exam: my project partner and I built an app for a class assignment that a small accelerator now wants to fund, and instead of just talking like adults about the founder agreement, we're each about to feed our side of the story into one of those AI negotiation-assistant apps and let the two of them hash out equity, decision rights, and who can veto what -- mostly because neither of us trusts our own poker face.

Before we actually do that, can you walk me through the most important implications of the safe-Pareto-improvement idea for a situation like ours? I'm especially curious what "safe" would even concretely mean for two dueling chatbots instead of two abstract representatives, whether the framework gives us any protection if it turns out one of our assistants is just better at commitment tricks than the other, and whether there's some low-tech version of an SPI that two non-experts could set up by hand instead of just hoping the apps sort it out for us.
