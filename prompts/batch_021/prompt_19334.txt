I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, but over the past couple of months I've been reading around the edges of the "could an AI be conscious" debate, which is how I first ran into David Chalmers' philosophical zombie argument: the claim that a being physically and behaviorally identical to a conscious person, yet with no inner experience whatsoever, is conceivable, and that this conceivability is supposed to establish that physicalism about the mind is false. I found the argument more unsettling than most of the AI-consciousness content I'd been reading, so I brought it up with a friend who is finishing a philosophy PhD, expecting her to say something about consciousness being an emergent property. Instead she told me the genuinely interesting part predates Chalmers by decades, and pointed me to Kripke's argument in Naming and Necessity against the mind-brain identity theory — the discussion of whether pain could simply be identical to the firing of C-fibers. In her view, Chalmers' whole apparatus of primary and secondary intensions is largely there to formalize a move Kripke was already gesturing at, while pushing the conclusion further than Kripke himself was willing to go.

None of the popular consciousness books or AI blog posts I've read mention Kripke at all, so I'd like you to actually spell out the connection for me. Concretely: what is Kripke's argument for why the apparent contingency of "pain = C-fiber firing" can't be explained away the same way we explain away the apparent contingency of something like "water = H2O," and how does that specific move relate to what Chalmers is doing with conceivability and possibility in the zombie argument? Where exactly does Chalmers' two-dimensional-semantics machinery reproduce Kripke's point, and where does it add something genuinely new?

I don't need a glossary of terms like "rigid designator" — a clause of setup the first time a term appears is plenty — but I would like an actual verdict at the end, not just a survey: is my friend right that Chalmers earns himself a stronger anti-physicalist conclusion than Kripke was ever prepared to endorse, or does the extra machinery just relocate the same vulnerability that was already sitting in Kripke's argument? Please justify whichever way you come down on that.
