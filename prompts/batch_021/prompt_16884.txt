In my graduate seminar on multi-agent systems, we recently discussed a proposal by Caspar Oesterheld and Vincent Conitzer for what they call "safe Pareto improvements" (SPIs), intended for AI agents that negotiate on behalf of mutually distrustful principals. The setup: two principals, each holding private information relevant to their own payoffs (their "type," in the jargon of Bayesian games), delegate play in some game to AI agents. Left to their own devices, the agents would implement some "default" strategy profile that can be inefficient — for instance, a bargaining protocol in which private information about each side's resolve or reservation value creates a positive probability of costly breakdown, even though both sides would rather avoid it. An SPI is a jointly agreed modification of the agents' behavior guaranteed to leave both principals at least as well off as under the default, whatever their actual types turn out to be, and strictly better off for at least one possible type profile. The point of this "no matter what" clause is that it lets a principal accept the modification without having to trust the other principal's honesty, the agents' good faith, or the accuracy of any shared prior over types.

For a term paper, I want to defend the following thesis: the safety requirement built into SPIs is structurally the same move as Rawls's maximin-based defense of the difference principle, and is vulnerable to the very objection Harsanyi leveled against Rawls — namely, that a principal instructing her agent in advance, genuinely uncertain about the type profile but willing to assign it some probability distribution, does strictly better in expectation by authorizing any modification that raises her expected payoff, rather than restricting herself to modifications that improve her payoff in absolutely every possible state. Please construct the strongest version of this argument you can, and then tell me whether there is a disanalogy between the original-position case and the delegated-bargaining case that a defender of SPIs could exploit to block the objection.
