This semester I'm taking a seminar on African philosophy, and the last couple of weeks have centered on the Ubuntu-inflected idea, familiar from the Zulu/Xhosa maxim "umuntu ngumuntu ngabantu" ("a person is a person through other persons"), that personhood is fundamentally relational rather than a property individuals simply possess in virtue of being human. Our professor uses Ifeanyi Menkiti's well-known essay on person and community in African traditional thought as the strongest philosophical statement of this idea: on his view, personhood is not given at birth but progressively acquired through participation in and fulfillment of the obligations of communal life, so that children, and adults who fail their communal responsibilities, count as lesser or incomplete persons; the community, on this picture, has priority over the individual in more than a merely causal or historical sense, since an individual's very status as a person is constituted by successful incorporation into that community rather than the other way around. We then turned to Kwame Gyekye's self-described "moderate communitarian" response, which grants that African social thought is rightly communitarian in spirit but denies that personhood can be entirely a social achievement in Menkiti's strong sense, since some capacity for rational agency and will has to already be in place, prior to and independent of communal recognition, if an individual is to be a bearer of rights against the community, or capable of the kind of independent moral judgment that lets someone criticize and reform communal norms rather than simply inherit them.

I understand each position well enough on its own, but for a short paper I'm drafting I'm having trouble seeing exactly where the disagreement bites once each side makes its strongest moves, so I'd like to see it played out rather than just summarized. Could you write a substantial dialogue between a philosopher defending Menkiti's processual, community-first account of personhood and a philosopher defending Gyekye's moderate-communitarian critique of it? I'd like the Menkitian to actually engage with the rights-and-reform objection rather than concede it outright - for instance, by questioning whether Gyekye's supposedly "pre-social" rational agent is itself just an unacknowledged bit of liberal individualism smuggled into a framework meant to be communitarian - and I'd like the Gyekyean to push back on whatever answer is given, including on whether Menkiti's distinction between a merely descriptive and a fully normative sense of "person" can really do the work he needs it to do. Both sides should be equally sharp, responding to what the other has actually just said rather than restating their own position from the previous turn, and I'd rather the dialogue end with the real crux of the disagreement clarified than with either side conceding or a tidy synthesis being reached.
