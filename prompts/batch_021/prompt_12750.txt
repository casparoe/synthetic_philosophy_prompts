I'm a PhD student in philosophy. After our department's reading group on Being and Nothingness this week, two people in my cohort, Ana and Josh, ended up in a long argument over drinks about a mutual friend, Marisol, a postdoc who just withdrew from a tenure-track search after two years on the market, telling everyone, "I realized I'm just not that kind of ambitious person — the whole publish-or-perish thing was never really me." Neither would budge on whether this is a textbook case of Sartrean bad faith.

1. Ana: This is bad faith in exactly the waiter's sense. Marisol is treating "not ambitious" as a fixed essence, something she simply is, so that withdrawing looks like discovering a fact about herself rather than making a choice she has to own — and she conveniently skips the anguish of admitting she could still go after it.

2. Josh: That's too quick. People really do have stable temperaments, and reporting yours isn't automatically bad faith — it's just self-knowledge. If any appeal to "who I am" counts as fleeing freedom whenever it's convenient, the theory proves too much: it makes ordinary honest self-description impossible.

3. Ana: Sartre isn't denying she has a history; the bad faith is using that history to foreclose a possibility that's still live right now. And this isn't armchair psychologizing — she went quiet for a week when her co-finalist got the position instead, which is exactly what you'd expect if part of her still wanted it.

4. Josh: But notice that move is unfalsifiable either way. If she'd taken the news with total equanimity, you'd have called that the waiter's seamless self-identification with a role — the purest case of bad faith going. Since she didn't, you're citing the opposite reaction as proof of the very same diagnosis. What could Marisol possibly do that you'd accept as evidence she isn't in bad faith?

5. Ana: Maybe nothing — but then you don't get to turn around and demand she just sincerely admit she wanted it, either. Sartre's discussion of sincerity is precisely that insisting someone "simply is" X, full stop, the way a table simply is round, is its own bad-faith move: it tries to pin a freedom down into a fixed thing so it can be correctly labeled.

6. Josh: Sure, but showing my alternative is bad-faith-shaped too doesn't rescue your original diagnosis. If asserting the trait, denying it, demanding avowal, and feeling torn about it all count as bad faith, the concept isn't marking a mistake Marisol is making relative to some available alternative — it's just Sartre's name for being a person. At that point, I don't see what work "she's lying to herself" is still doing.

Please go through the exchange move by move and say who has the better of it at each numbered step, not just who wins overall. Then tell me whether Josh's closing objection is actually fatal to using "bad faith" as a diagnosis here, or whether there's a principled way to preserve the concept's bite that neither of them reached for.
