I'm one of two student members on my high school's Scientific Review Committee, the small panel (otherwise made up of three faculty sponsors) that has to pre-approve any science fair project involving human subjects before the student is allowed to start collecting data. I ended up on it mostly because I took an elective on philosophy of science last year and haven't stopped bringing it up since. This year we're stuck on a proposal from a junior who wants to test whether Myers-Briggs (MBTI) type predicts how well randomly paired strangers cooperate on a timed logic puzzle, compared with pairs matched on type; she has one preregistered hypothesis, a control condition, and a statistical test specified in advance, before she looks at any results. One of the faculty sponsors wants to reject the proposal outright, arguing that MBTI is "astrology for cubicles" and that approving the project lends undeserved credibility to a theory that doesn't deserve it, no matter how careful the experiment is. My co-representative and I have argued back that our rule requiring projects to "use the scientific method" is supposed to be about whether a hypothesis is testable and honestly tested, not about the reputation of the theory being tested, and that Karl Popper's falsifiability criterion, if we're using it at all, would seem to count her design as a pretty good example of real science even if MBTI itself turns out to be bunk. A third member then pointed out that plenty of philosophers of science have concluded there's no clean line between science and non-science in the first place, which would mean we're fighting over a distinction ("the demarcation problem," apparently) that dissolves under scrutiny anyway.

We have to send the full committee a short written recommendation before we vote next Tuesday, and I got stuck drafting it since I'm the one who keeps citing Popper at people. Could you write that memo for me? It should reach one specific recommendation — approve the project, reject it, or approve it with named conditions — and lay out the reasoning in a form the committee could actually argue with point by point, rather than a general essay on falsifiability. Please take the faculty sponsor's worry seriously instead of waving it off: it seems possible that even a genuinely falsifiable, honestly run test of a disreputable theory could still do a kind of epistemic harm just by dressing that theory up in the trappings of legitimate science, and I don't want the memo to just assert that falsifiability settles the matter without engaging that concern directly.
