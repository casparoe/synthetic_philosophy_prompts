Since retiring from the lab (35 years in analytical chemistry) I've been going to a philosophy discussion group at the library, and last week two of the regulars, Deb and Marcus, argued themselves in circles over a free-will case they'd cooked up betwen them. I honestly can't tell who came out ahead.

The case: Ray works nights at a small plant and has to wear a "focus band" that detects a specific brainwave blip about half a second before a lapse in attention, and jolts him into hitting the emergency shutdown regardless if it catches one. One night there's a real scare, and Ray hits the shutdown himself, clean, no jolt -- the blip never shows up.

(1) Deb said this proves you don't need the freedom to do otherwise to be responsible: Ray gets full credit, even though if he'd wavered the band would've forced his hand anyway, so in the sense that matters he couldn't have done otherwise. (2) Marcus said not so fast -- Ray did have an alternative, letting the blip show up, he just didn't take it. (3) Deb said that's not a real alternative: "letting the blip show up" isn't something Ray does on purpose, it's just a symptom, and if an involuntary tell counts as an "alternative possibility" the whole idea's been defined down to nothing. (4) Marcus said there's a worse problem for her -- for the band to reliably catch every lapse in advance, the blip has to be a guaranteed tell of what Rays brain had already settled on doing, and if his split-second call was genuinely still open till he made it, no such guaranteed tell could exist, so either the band throws the odd false alarm, or the story's quietly assuming Ray's choice was fixed beforehand, which is exactly what's in dispute.

Could you go move by move and say who's got the better of each exchange, not just who wins overall, and flag anything bigger both of them are missing? A rough score per move (-3 to 3, 3 being "clearly right") would be a nice bonus -- old habits from the lab.
