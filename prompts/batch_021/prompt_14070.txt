I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums for years now -- the Overcoming Bias and Less Wrong lineage especially -- where the St. Petersburg paradox and the debate over bounded versus unbounded utility functions come up constantly, usually in the context of worries about Pascal's-mugging-style fanaticism. What I almost never see anyone touch is the Pasadena game, the variant Nover and Hájek introduced in 2004, even though I think it's the more unsettling case. In St. Petersburg, decision theory at least hands you a determinate verdict (infinite and weird, but determinate); the Pasadena game's defining series is only conditionally convergent, so its expected value can be made to equal any real number whatsoever, or made to diverge to positive or negative infinity, just by resumming the very same outcomes in a different order.

I co-host a small podcast on formal epistemology and decision theory, and next month's guest is one of the two people who introduced the puzzle into the literature. She has spent the two decades since defending her original diagnosis -- that the game has no expected value at all, full stop, and that this is a discovery about the limits of decision theory rather than a bookkeeping error to be tidied away -- against several rounds of proposed rescues. I'd like eight to ten interview questions that go beyond having her restate the setup for listeners.

At minimum, I want one question that presses her on why conditional convergence makes this categorically worse for decision theory than a merely divergent gamble like St. Petersburg, where you can still "bite the bullet" on a strange but fixed answer; one on the "just sum the payoffs in the order the coin flips actually happen in" reply, asking what could possibly privilege that ordering over the other, equally natural ways of grouping the same outcomes; and one on restricting decision theory to bounded utility functions, since that move seems to make the whole problem vanish by fiat -- I'd like to know whether she thinks we have independent reasons, from astronomical-stakes cases or the intuition that no outcome could be maximally or infinitely good or bad, to want unbounded utility anyway, which would just smuggle the paradox back in through the side door. For each of these three, please attach a short follow-up keyed to the reply she's most likely to give, so the conversation doesn't stall on the first answer. Close the list with one question about what she thinks an actual solution to the paradox, as opposed to a patch, would have to look like, and what would change her mind.
