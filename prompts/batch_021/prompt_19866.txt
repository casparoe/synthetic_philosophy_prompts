I'm a software engineer, not a philosopher by training, but I've recently gotten into a philosophy podcast that spent an episode on Kant's ethics beyond the usual categorical-imperative material, specifically his views on animals. As I understood it, Kant denies that we have direct duties toward animals, since they lack reason and self-consciousness and so cannot count as ends in themselves, but he still treats cruelty toward them as a moral failing: not because the animal is wronged, but because habitually treating a living, feeling creature harshly erodes the very sympathetic dispositions we depend on to treat other people well. The host cited an example from Kant's lecture notes about a man who has his old, no-longer-useful dog shot: Kant's complaint isn't that the dog was wronged, since (in Kant's phrase) the dog "cannot judge," but that the man has damaged something in his own character that his duties to other humans rely on. Apparently Kant went as far as saying that long, faithful service from an animal earns it something like gratitude on our part, while insisting this is technically still a duty to oneself, not to the animal.

That got me thinking about something entirely outside Kant's world: those demo videos, Boston Dynamics being the most famous example, where engineers shove, kick, or knock over a legged robot on camera to show off its balance-recovery software, and predictably draw a wave of comments from viewers saying they feel bad for the robot, even though everyone involved agrees it has no inner life whatsoever, not even the limited capacity for feeling Kant was willing to grant a dog or a horse. I'd like your best reconstruction of how Kant's actual reasoning, not a loose modern paraphrase, would handle a case like this. Since his indirect-duty argument seems to depend on the target of the cruelty being able to suffer in at least some minimal sense, would it extend at all to a machine he'd consider entirely without sensation, or does the whole argument quietly break down once feeling is removed from the picture? And if it does break down, is there anything else in Kant's account of duties to oneself, perhaps around cultivating good dispositions more generally, that could still make kicking a robot around for fun strike him as a vice, even with no animal-like victim on the other end?
