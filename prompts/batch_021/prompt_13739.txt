As a journalist who covers AI and cognitive science, I've spent the past couple of years following the interpretability work coming out of labs like Anthropic: first the "circuits" program that treated trained networks almost like specimens to be dissected, then the discovery that individual neurons are typically "polysemantic" because a network packs more features than it has dimensions into superposition, and more recently the sparse-autoencoder papers that claim to unpack that superposition into thousands, then millions, of comparatively clean "features." What made this legible to a general audience was "Golden Gate Claude": in May 2024, Anthropic took a feature that fired on mentions of the Golden Gate Bridge and turned its activation up until the assistant worked the bridge into almost every reply, at one point insisting it physically was the bridge. It was a fun demo, but it left me wondering whether researchers' talk of "discovering" features inside a model is doing more philosophical work than it can actually support.

I'd like you to write an essay that takes a real position on this question, call it realism about interpretability, rather than surveying it neutrally. When researchers isolate a "feature" or "circuit," have they found something that functions as a genuine representation for the system, in roughly the sense in which a cognitive map represents a maze for a rat, or have they merely found a convenient statistical regularity that we are choosing to redescribe in the vocabulary of representation? Engage seriously with at least one solid reason for skepticism: for instance, the fact that changing the size or sparsity of a dictionary-learning setup tends to change which features show up at all, including cases of "feature splitting," where a single feature at one granularity fractures into several more specific ones at another, which sits awkwardly with the idea that these are mind-independent, joint-carving parts of the network rather than artifacts of the method used to find them. You might also make use of the general shape of the "job description challenge" from philosophy of cognitive science (associated with William Ramsey): merely covarying with, or being causally triggered by, some external condition isn't sufficient for something to count as a representation in any interesting sense; a genuine representation has to be used by the system as a stand-in for what it represents. Weigh this against the strongest considerations favoring realism, and close with an actual, specific verdict rather than concluding that it all depends on what we mean by "representation."
