Over a forty-year career in structural biology I returned again and again to Levinthal's paradox: a modestly sized protein has so astronomically many possible conformations that if it located its native fold by randomly sampling them, even at the fastest rate physically plausible for bond rotations, the search would take far longer than the age of the universe — and yet real proteins fold correctly in microseconds to seconds. Since retiring I have been reading in philosophy of mind, and I recently learned that computer scientists have proven that finding the minimum-energy configuration in simplified lattice models of protein folding is NP-complete. Putting these two facts together, it seems to me that Levinthal's paradox is actually a rigorous, real-world demonstration that physical systems can solve NP-hard problems in polynomial time: on the assumption, which I understand is still unproven but widely accepted, that P is not equal to NP, no ordinary Turing machine could do what a folding protein does, so the protein must be exploiting some computational resource that no digital computer, however fast, could in principle replicate.

If that is right, it strikes me as a serious difficulty for any theory that treats cognition as fundamentally computational, since neurons are made of the same kind of matter, governed by the same physical laws, as the proteins that fold inside and around them; a mind that is "just" a computation running on neural hardware should presumably be bound by the same complexity limits as any other Turing machine, yet here is ordinary biological matter apparently exceeding them.

This has struck me as a rather substantial conclusion to have been sitting in plain sight in the biophysics literature all along, which makes me suspect I am missing something about how these complexity results actually bear on physical processes rather than on abstract computational ones. I would value your judgment on whether the argument as I have laid it out actually holds together, and, if it does not, precisely where it goes wrong.
