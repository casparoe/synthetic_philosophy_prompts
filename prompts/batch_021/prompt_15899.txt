This term I'm taking a seminar on social ontology, and a good chunk of it has been devoted to the puzzle of what makes something count as money in the first place. We read Searle's account of money as an "institutional fact" that exists only because we collectively treat certain objects as having a special status (his famous "X counts as Y in context C" formula), and we contrasted it with older views on which money's authority ultimately traces back to the state's willingness to accept it in payment of taxes, or to its being a transferable record of debt rather than a status conferred by mere collective belief. Naturally, cryptocurrency came up as the test case nobody could agree on: half the class thought Bitcoin is obviously just the latest instance of the same social kind that dollar bills and wampum belong to, and the other half thought it shows that our tidy theories of money quietly assumed things -- a backing authority, a real economy of debts, a single unit of account -- that Bitcoin lacks, so that calling it "money" either begs the question or stretches the concept past its breaking point.

I'd like to see that disagreement worked out properly rather than just asserted. Could you write a dialogue between two people from the seminar who take these opposing sides: one defending Bitcoin's claim to being genuine money against whichever theory of money you find most defensible, the other arguing that Bitcoin is precisely the case that shows these theories don't converge on a single answer? They should respond to each other's actual points as the conversation develops -- conceding minor points where they're forced to, not just delivering parallel monologues -- and neither side should be easy to dismiss.
