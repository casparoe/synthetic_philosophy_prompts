Since retiring from a long career as an observational astrophysicist, I have been working through the philosophical literature on causation, prompted first by Judea Pearl's popular writing and then by James Woodward's *Making Things Happen*. I have also started sitting in on a philosophy of science reading group at a nearby university, where we recently spent several sessions on the contrast between Lewis's counterfactual, similarity-based theory of causation and the interventionist accounts developed by Woodward and Pearl, on which "C causes E" is analyzed via the existence of a possible intervention on C — an idealized, surgical, exogenous manipulation that changes C without changing anything else except via C's downstream effects — that would change E.

I find the interventionist picture very congenial to how working scientists actually reason about causes in the lab or in a survey, but a specific worry keeps nagging at me, and it comes directly out of my own field. Cosmologists routinely make causal-sounding claims about the universe as a whole: that the extremely low entropy of the early universe is what accounts for the thermodynamic arrow of time we observe, or that had certain fundamental constants (say, the cosmological constant, or the strength of the strong nuclear force) taken even slightly different values, no stars, galaxies, or observers could ever have formed. These look like perfectly good instances of causal or counterfactual dependence, yet it is hard to see what an "intervention," in Woodward's technical sense, on a global boundary condition of the universe, or on a constant of nature, could even be: there is no wider system held fixed in the background while we tweak such parameters, since changing them arguably changes the very nomological setting relative to which "holding everything else fixed" is defined in the first place.

I would like you to argue for the following thesis: that Woodward- and Pearl-style interventionism, whatever its virtues as an account of causal reasoning in the special sciences and in experimental design, cannot be stretched into a fully general metaphysics of causation, because it cannot make sense of this class of causal-explanatory claims about global boundary conditions and fundamental constants in physics and cosmology — and that a broadly Lewisian counterfactual theory of causation, for all its own well-known troubles (preemption chief among them), is therefore still indispensable for doing justice to this kind of scientific case. Please construct the strongest version of this argument you can, taking care to address the most plausible interventionist rejoinders (for instance, appeals to intervening at the level of the fundamental theory itself, or to reinterpreting these cosmological claims as explanatory rather than strictly causal) before delivering your verdict.
