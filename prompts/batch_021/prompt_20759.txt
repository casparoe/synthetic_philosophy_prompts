So I've kind of fallen down a rabbit hole lately reading about the history of ghost-hunting and mediums (it started with this pop-science book, I think it was called "Ghost Hunters" by Deborah Blum, about William James and that whole crowd), and I keep running into this Victorian philosopher named Henry Sidgwick. From what I can piece together he was this incredibly careful, almost joyless utilitarian guy who wrote the big classic "The Methods of Ethics," and in the concluding part of that book he basically admits he can't actually PROVE that doing your moral duty and doing what's best for you as an individual always line up in the end -- he says practical reason just collapses into a "dualism" unless you're willing to assume there's some cosmic bookkeeper (God, an afterlife, whatever) making sure virtue gets its reward eventually. And apparently that's a big part of why this same guy went on to co-found the Society for Psychical Research and spent the better part of two decades testing mediums, telepathy claims, haunted houses, all of it, basically trying to find hard scientific evidence for a soul. From what I've read he died still on the fence, no smoking gun ever turned up.

Its been bugging me for weeks now, and I don't have anyone to talk it over with -- I never studied philosophy, I just find this stuff fascinating, and I mentioned it to a friend doing a psych degree and even she'd never heard of the guy. On one hand, if you genuinely believe morality needs that kind of metaphysical backup to make full rational sense, then going out and actually looking for evidence seems like the most honest, rigorous thing a philosopher could do instead of just hand-waving the problem away. On the other hand it also kind of smells like a really smart guy inventing a "scientific" excuse to go chase the one thing he emotionally needed to be true, and twenty-odd years of basically nothing should maybe have counted as a bigger red flag than it seemed to for him.

Can you write me a proper essay working through this? I want an actual side taken, not a wishy washy "both are valid perspectives" ending: was Sidgwick being rational and intellectually consistent, by his own philosophical standards, in turning to psychical research -- or was it motivated reasoning dressed up in a lab coat? And following on from that, since he never found his proof, do you think his "dualism of practical reason" is basically a permanent, unpatched hole in ethics, or has later philosophy found a way round it that doesn't need ghosts or gods? Actually commit to something and defend it, don't just lay out both sides.
