For my AP U.S. History class, we recently studied the abolitionist movement, and one detail from a lecture has been nagging at me ever since: Frederick Douglass did not hold a single, stable opinion of the United States Constitution throughout his life. In his earlier years, as an ally of William Lloyd Garrison, he accepted the Garrisonian position that the Constitution was irredeemably proslavery — Garrison notoriously described it as "a covenant with death, and an agreement with hell" on account of clauses like the three-fifths clause, the fugitive-slave clause, and the slave-trade clause, none of which even uses the word "slave." Yet by the early 1850s, after breaking with Garrison and coming under the influence of Gerrit Smith and Lysander Spooner, Douglass had reversed himself almost entirely, arguing that the document, honestly read, contained no sanction for slavery at all and was, in his own words, a "GLORIOUS LIBERTY DOCUMENT" that abolitionists should reclaim rather than reject. Our teacher mentioned this reversal only in passing, as a kind of biographical curiosity, but it strikes me as a genuine philosophical disagreement rather than a mere change of heart, and I would like to think it through properly instead of just siding with whichever version of Douglass I happen to have read most recently.

Could you write me an essay that treats this as the substantive controversy I think it is? Please reconstruct the strongest case for each position — the case Garrison and Wendell Phillips could make for reading the Constitution's silences and compromises as decisively proslavery, and the case Douglass, Smith, and Spooner could make for reading those same silences as leaving room for, or even requiring, an antislavery construction — and then actually settle the question rather than concluding that both sides simply had a point. A verdict along the lines of "the proslavery reading was the more historically honest one, even though the antislavery reading was the more useful stance for a working abolitionist to adopt" would count as taking a real position, so long as you argue for it; declining to choose would not. If possible, I would also like you to say something about what this dispute suggests more generally about how one ought to interpret a legal text when its framers' apparent intentions and its most defensible moral principle seem to point in different directions.
