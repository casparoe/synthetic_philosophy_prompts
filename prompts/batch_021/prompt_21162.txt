I've spent the last couple of weeks falling down a Carnap rabbit hole that started, a little embarrassingly, from an old rationalist-forum thread arguing about whether "verificationism was refuted by Quine" is a fair one-sentence epitaph for logical positivism (it isn't, but that's a separate rant). Digging past the epitaph sent me back to Carnap's own "Intellectual Autobiography" in the Schilpp volume, where I was struck by how openly he discusses his socialist sympathies and his hope, shared with Neurath and the rest of the Vienna Circle, that something like scientific method could eventually be extended to the organization of economic and social life. I'm trying to turn this into a short post, but so far I only have an outline and a couple of paragraphs, and I'd rather get your comments on the skeleton before I flesh it out.

Here's roughly the shape of the argument. (1) The 1929 Vienna Circle manifesto explicitly ties the "scientific world-conception" to the progressive social movements of its day and gestures toward reorganizing economic life along rational lines; Carnap co-signed it even though Neurath did most of the actual drafting. (2) Carnap's own most distinctive methodological contribution, the principle of tolerance from Logical Syntax of Language, holds that there is no such thing as a "correct" logic or linguistic framework, only conventions that are more or less useful relative to a purpose. I want to ask whether this pluralism is actually in tension with the confidence a planner needs — that there is some identifiable best or most rational arrangement of an economy — or whether tolerance instead supports a kind of anti-dogmatic, permanently revisable technocratic planning that treats "how should we organize the economy" as an engineering question rather than one with a single metaphysically correct answer. (3) I suspect I may be quietly smuggling in Neurath's much more developed views on in-kind economic calculation and his exchanges with Mises, and then pinning them on Carnap simply because the two are lumped together as "the Vienna Circle." I'd like help figuring out how much of the planning material is fair to attribute to Carnap specifically, as opposed to Neurath. (4) I also want to flag, without being sure what to do with it, the fact that by the time logical empiricism had settled into the postwar American academy it had been stripped of most of this political content — which is the story George Reisch tells in "How the Cold War Transformed Philosophy of Science." I can't tell if that's a fact about the movement's American reception rather than about anything internal to Carnap's own philosophy, and I don't want to read Cold War history backward into the 1930s.

Could you comment on whichever of the following seem most useful: whether I have the history roughly right or am garbling something; whether the "tolerance versus planning" puzzle in (2) is a genuinely substantive question or a false dilemma I've talked myself into; which of the four points is weakest and most needs either more evidence or more hedging; and whether there's a sharper way to state the central claim than "there's an interesting tension between Carnap's pluralism about logic and his sympathy for planning." I'd rather find the holes now than after I've written two thousand words defending them.
