In their 2018 paper "Dissolving the Fermi Paradox," Anders Sandberg, Eric Drexler, and Toby Ord argue that the puzzle of cosmic silence owes less to any single "Great Filter" than to a subtle statistical error in how the Drake equation is typically used: multiplying together point estimates for each of its uncertain terms rather than propagating the full probability distributions we actually have over those terms. Done properly, they contend, a substantial share of the resulting probability mass ends up on scenarios in which humanity is alone in the galaxy, or even the observable universe, even though the "expected" number of civilizations produced by the naive calculation still looks comfortably large. As a science journalist trying to explain this argument to readers without walking them through the underlying Monte Carlo simulations, I'd like an intuitive account of why swapping point estimates for distributions can shift the qualitative conclusion so drastically. What is it about multiplying several independently uncertain quantities together that makes their "expected value" such a misleading summary of what we should actually anticipate?
