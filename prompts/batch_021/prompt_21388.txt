My PhD advisor died two weeks ago and the department's asked me to say a few words at her memorial on Friday, maybe 5 minutes. She was a philosopher of religion who spent her whole career defending logical positivism long after everyone else quietly moved on, and her go-to lecture bit was always Ayer's near-death thing: choking on smoked salmon in hospital, four minutes flat, the red light, and then the essay where he says the whole episode only "slightly weakened" his conviction that death would be the end of him, even though he still hoped it would be. She used it as her favorite example of how a real empiricist updates on weird evidence — barely, grudgingly, and in public, without pretending "I will survive my body" had suddenly become a meaningful thing to say just because something odd happened to him. She left instructions that nobody's allowed to say she's "in a better place" or "watching over us," because on her own view those sentences aren't false exactly, theyre not even the kind of thing that can be true or false. Here's the complication: her sister told me that near the end she admitted to some experience in the hospice she couldn't place, and wants it mentioned — but only the way Ayer mentioned his, hedged down to almost nothing, never turned into comfort. Can you draft the eulogy? Half the room is her department, who'll clock the Ayer reference immediately; the other half is family who've never heard the word "verificationism" and just need the next five minutes to sound like her.
