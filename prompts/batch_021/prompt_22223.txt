So a few weeks ago I went down this whole rabbit hole reading about Jain philosophy (no background in this stuff at all, I just like falling into wikipedia holes at 1am) and I got stuck on this idea called anekantavada, the "many-sidedness of truth" thing, and specifically the syadvada logic that's supposed to back it up - the seven statements you're supposed to be able to make about anything, syad asti, syad nasti, syad asti-nasti, and so on up to the weird "inexpressible" one. Every explainer just gives you the blind men and the elephant story and calls it a day, but when I actually tried to sit with the seven-fold predication itself it felt a lot less cute and a lot more like it might just be contradiction wearing a disguise.

Like here's what bugs me: if the whole trick for why "the pot exists" and "the pot doesn't exist" can both be true is just "well, it exists in this respect (its own substance, place, time, form) and doesn't exist in some other respect (as a different substance, at a different time, etc)" - isn't that just... regular old non-contradiction? Nobody ever thought a pot was self-identical to a shirt or existed for all eternity, so what does the fancy "logic" actually add on top of that? And then the 4th option, "avaktavya" (inexpressible), for when you try to say asti and nasti at once - except they just told me what it means in plain words, so now I genuinely don't know what "inexpressible" is supposed to mean there. And the thing that really gets me: if literally every claim needs a "syat" (in some respect) tag hung on it because nothing can be asserted absolutely, does THAT rule apply to anekantavada's own big claim that "reality itself is many sided"? Because if yes, it seems like the doctrine can only be sort-of true and loses any right to correct other views, and if no, that's a pretty convenient exception for it to grant itself.
