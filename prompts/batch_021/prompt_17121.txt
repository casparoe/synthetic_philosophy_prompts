I am drafting a scene for a novel in which a fictionalised René Descartes is confronted with the case of Alex, Irene Pepperberg's African grey parrot, who over roughly three decades of testing learned to label colours, shapes, materials, and small quantities, and reportedly answered novel combinations of such questions rather than simply repeating memorised responses. I want Descartes's reaction to follow his actual philosophy, not the popular caricature of the "beast-machine" doctrine. In the Discourse on the Method he gives two specific tests for whether behaviour reflects genuine reason rather than mechanism: whether words can be rearranged to answer appropriately whatever is said on any topic, as even the dullest person manages, and whether competence holds up across the whole range of life's contingencies rather than being confined to whatever tasks its "organs" happen to suit it for, as with a clock that tells time better than we do without understanding anything. He treats trained birds such as parrots and magpies, which can be taught to utter words, as his standard case of mechanical mimicry rather than genuine speech. Working strictly from these two criteria as he actually formulated them, how do you think Descartes would try to argue that Alex's behaviour still fails his test, and at what point, if any, do you think his own framework would leave him hard-pressed to hold the line?
