At work, I'm on a team that maintains a fairly large, interconnected backend, and after any non-trivial deploy or config change we're supposed to work through a "blast radius" checklist before calling the change safe: a list of systems, invariants, and downstream jobs to re-verify. The trouble is that no matter how long we make this checklist, incidents keep coming from things nobody thought to put on it — a cache that quietly kept serving stale data, a billing job that assumed a certain field could never be null, an analytics pipeline that broke two services downstream because a timestamp format changed. When I asked our most experienced engineer how she decides what to re-check after a given change, she couldn't really give me a rule; she said she just "gets a feel" for what's likely to be touched and doesn't bother looking at the rest. That's great for her, but it means we can't turn her judgment into a checklist, a linter rule, or an onboarding doc, and every attempt we've made to write a "complete" checklist has produced something that's either unusably long or still missing whole categories of failure.

A couple of us joked in a postmortem that the real onboarding advice is just "develop good instincts," which felt like a cop-out, so I've been mulling it over since. It seems like the real difficulty isn't that we haven't been thorough enough, but something more basic about how you'd even specify, in general and in advance, which of the indefinitely many facts about a running system stay true after some change to it, as opposed to the comparatively small set that actually need re-examining. Is there a name for this kind of problem outside of software, and has anyone worked out a principled way of handling it — or is "ask whoever has the best gut feeling" really about as good as it gets, whether the one doing the checking is a human team or an automated one?
