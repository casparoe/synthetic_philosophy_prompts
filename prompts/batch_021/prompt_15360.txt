I've been preparing a graduate seminar on supererogation, and while most of the secondary literature traces the term's entry into contemporary secular ethics to J.O. Urmson's 1958 paper "Saints and Heroes," I wanted to give my students some sense of where the concept came from before it was secularized. That sent me back to Article XIV of the Thirty-Nine Articles of the Church of England (finalized in 1571), which is, as far as I can tell, the single most influential source for why "supererogation" still carries a faint polemical charge in English-language moral vocabulary even in its contemporary, entirely secular usage. The Article, titled "Of Works of Supererogation," reads in full:

"Voluntary Works besides, over and above, God's Commandments, which they call Works of Supererogation, cannot be taught without arrogancy and impiety: for by them men do declare, that they do not only render unto God as much as they are bound to do, but that they do more for his sake, than of bounden duty is required: whereas Christ saith plainly, When ye have done all that are commanded to you, say, We are unprofitable servants."

I would be grateful for help on two fronts. First, could you reconstruct the theological argument here in plain terms? I take it the Catholic doctrine of a transferable "treasury of merit," accumulated through the supererogatory works of Christ and the saints, is somewhere in the background, though the Article never names it directly — but I'm not entirely sure I have the dialectic right, particularly why the framers thought that merely teaching the category of works "over and above" the commandments amounted to "arrogancy and impiety" rather than just an innocuous (if false) bit of moral taxonomy. Second, and this is the part I keep turning over: a few of my students noticed that the Reformers' objection looks structurally similar to the "paradox of supererogation" that shows up in the secular literature — the worry that if an act really is morally better than some available alternative, it is hard to see how a sufficiently demanding conception of duty could count it as merely optional rather than required. Is that a genuine structural parallel, or am I forcing a resemblance between two arguments that are actually doing quite different work — one about the exhaustiveness of the divine command (or of love owed to God), the other about the rational authority of the better act? I'd value your view on whether defenders of supererogation from Urmson onward have, in effect, had to answer a secularized version of the Reformers' challenge, even where they never describe it in those terms.
