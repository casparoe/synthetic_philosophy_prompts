In Ecce Homo, particularly the chapter "Why I Am So Clever," Nietzsche devotes sustained, often minute attention to questions of nutrition, climate, locale, and recreation, insisting that these seemingly small matters are of greater consequence than any traditional concept of God, soul, virtue, or sin. Please argue that this physiological preoccupation is not incidental biographical color, and not merely a symptom of his approaching mental collapse, but a serious and integral extension of his mature naturalism, on which bodily self-governance is continuous with, and partly constitutive of, both the revaluation of values and the ideal of the "great health." In making this argument, directly confront its strongest objection: if philosophical and moral positions are, by Nietzsche's own lights, symptoms of underlying physiological states, then his dietary-based diagnoses of décadence in others look equally reducible to symptoms of his own idiosyncratic physiology, threatening to make his entire diagnostic project self-undermining.
