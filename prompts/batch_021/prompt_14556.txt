I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and I also host a monthly interview podcast that grew out of the column. Next month's guest is a mathematician who has become one of the more visible public advocates for score voting (also called range voting) -- the system in which every voter rates each candidate on a numerical scale and the highest average or total wins, rather than ranking the field. The subject has real currency right now: approval voting, essentially score voting collapsed to two rating levels, has already been adopted by a couple of American cities, and its advocates are using that foothold to push the fuller cardinal version as the logical next step. My guest has a reliable move whenever Arrow's impossibility theorem comes up on a panel or in an op-ed exchange: he says the theorem is a statement about ranked, ordinal ballots, period, so it has nothing to say about a rating-based system like his -- and interviewers tend to let that stand and move on. I'd rather not.

Please draft eight or nine interview questions, sequenced so that this line gets progressively harder for him to fall back on rather than being wheeled out once and retired for the rest of the conversation. Start by making him say, in his own words, exactly which premise of Arrow's theorem -- unrestricted domain, the Pareto condition, independence of irrelevant alternatives, non-dictatorship -- he thinks a score-voting rule violates, evades, or simply isn't covered by, so that "cardinal, not ordinal" can't just stand in for an argument. From there, work in at least one question on what licenses treating one voter's 7-out-of-10 as commensurable with another's, given that Arrow's ordinal framework was built partly to avoid assuming interpersonal comparisons of utility in the first place; one on whether honest cardinal reporting survives contact with strategic incentives, since the standard worry is that rational voters facing a credible "lesser evil" choice will collapse their scores toward the extremes, which makes the ballots ordinal in substance whatever their form; one on whether score voting even satisfies something like independence of irrelevant alternatives in practice, given that a voter's optimal score for a front-runner plausibly changes with who else is in the race; and one that pushes past Arrow specifically to the broader family of manipulability results -- Gibbard-Satterthwaite for ranked rules, and its extensions to approval-style and set-valued rules such as the Duggan-Schwartz theorem -- so that exempting his system from Arrow doesn't get quietly treated as exempting it from strategic manipulation generally. For each of these, attach a short follow-up keyed to the answer he's most likely to give, so the exchange doesn't stop at the first well-rehearsed reply. Close the sequence with a question asking what empirical finding or theoretical argument, if any, would change his mind about score voting's superiority to ranked and plurality methods, so listeners learn whether the position is falsifiable for him or simply axiomatic.
