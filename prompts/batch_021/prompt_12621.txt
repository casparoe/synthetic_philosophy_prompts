I teach a graduate seminar in philosophy, cross-listed with economics, on formal approaches to distributive justice, and this year's take-home midterm included a question on Harsanyi's Social Aggregation Theorem — one of six questions, worth 20 of the exam's 100 points. I'm now working through the submissions, and before I settle on marks I'd like an independent, rigorous read of one particular answer: these technical questions tend to produce answers that sound fluent and internally connected even where a step doesn't actually hold up, and I'd rather not be the only person checking that everything really does follow.

The question, as it appeared on the exam, read:

> Harsanyi's Social Aggregation Theorem is sometimes presented as a formal argument for utilitarianism. (a) State the theorem, including the role played by the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms and the Pareto condition(s) on which it depends. (b) Explain how, and how far, the theorem is supposed to establish a utilitarian conclusion about the social good. (c) Present and briefly assess one serious objection to using the theorem in this way.

And here, in full, is the student's answer:

> (a) Harsanyi's theorem assumes that each of the n individuals in society, together with a "social observer" who ranks the same set of risky prospects, satisfies the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms (completeness, transitivity, continuity, and independence). By the vNM representation theorem, each individual's preferences can therefore be represented by a utility function u_i, unique up to a positive affine transformation, and the same holds for the social observer's utility function U. Harsanyi's key premise is a Pareto indifference condition: if every individual is indifferent between two prospects, the social observer must be indifferent between them too. Because U and each u_i are themselves linear in the probabilities of the underlying prospects, this is enough to force U into the affine form U = a_0 + Σ a_i u_i; adding the further requirement that the social observer strictly prefer p to q whenever every individual weakly prefers p to q and at least one strictly does rules out negative weights, so a_i ≥ 0 for every i.
>
> (b) This affine form is why the theorem is read as a proof of utilitarianism: maximizing social utility turns out to mean maximizing a sum of individual utilities, rather than, say, their product or their minimum. Harsanyi motivates the equal-weight case by having the social observer imagine occupying each individual's position with equal probability 1/n, not knowing in advance which person she will turn out to be; since her own preferences over this uncertain prospect must themselves satisfy the vNM axioms, maximizing her expected utility amounts to maximizing the unweighted sum of the individuals' utilities. It is this equiprobability move that pins the weights a_i down to being equal, and so delivers classical, equal-weight utilitarianism as the theorem's conclusion, rather than merely some weighted generalization of it.
>
> (c) The most serious objection is the one associated with Rawls's utility monster: because the social welfare function is additive, it is in principle possible for a single individual who converts resources into utility unusually efficiently to be owed nearly all of society's resources, since this maximizes the weighted sum even though it leaves everyone else badly off. This runs together the interests of distinct people as though they belonged to one big person, which is exactly what critics have accused utilitarianism, and by extension Harsanyi's defense of it, of doing.
