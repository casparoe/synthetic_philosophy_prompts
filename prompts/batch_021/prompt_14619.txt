Please describe the most important implications of the reference class problem for how I should interpret individualized medical risk estimates. A doctor tells me my ten-year risk of a cardiovascular event is 14%, based on a study population matched to my age, sex, and smoking status; a specialist who also factors in my cholesterol panel and family history puts it at 9%; and a public-health pamphlet sorting only by age bracket puts it at 18%. None of these reference classes is obviously the uniquely correct one for computing 'my' probability, and I could doubtless be sorted into still others. What follows from this for how much weight any single such number should carry in my decisions, whether a more specific estimate is always preferable, and whether there is a fact of the matter about my true risk at all?
