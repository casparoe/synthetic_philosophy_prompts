Question: "In the Lettre à d'Alembert, Rousseau argues against d'Alembert's proposal, made in his Encyclopédie article on Geneva, that the city establish a theater. Reconstruct the main lines of Rousseau's case, and explain why he thinks Geneva is especially at risk in a way that Paris is not."

Student answer: "Rousseau's objection begins from more or less the same premise as Plato's attack on the poets in Book X of the Republic: because watching a dramatic performance leads spectators to identify with the characters they see, imitating vicious or virtuous passions on stage necessarily instills those same passions in the audience's soul. This is why he singles out Molière's Misanthrope: Alceste's blunt honesty is made to look like comic misanthropy so that Philinte's complaisant, socially accommodating hypocrisy appears to be the sensible position, and audiences leave the theater having learned to laugh at sincerity rather than at vice. Because Geneva is still a small, relatively frugal and egalitarian republic whose citizens govern themselves directly, Rousseau thinks it has far more to lose from this corruption than Paris does: introducing paid, professional actors would import the idleness and luxury spending needed to sustain a permanent troupe, and this is a disease Geneva does not yet have, whereas Paris, already sick with inequality and luxury, loses little by adding one more symptom. He also objects that the actor's trade is itself corrupting, since it consists of professionally feigning passions one does not feel. Rousseau's preferred alternative is the civic festival, where, unlike in the theater, the citizens are not a paying audience watching hired performers but are simultaneously spectators and spectacle to one another."
