Here's the question she gave:

"Jaegwon Kim's causal exclusion argument purports to show that nonreductive physicalism cannot secure genuine causal efficacy for the mental. (a) Reconstruct the argument in premise-conclusion form, making clear which premise encodes the causal closure of the physical and which encodes the prohibition on systematic overdetermination. (b) Kim himself resolves the tension via functional reduction, at the cost of epiphenomenalism about qualia. Explain why he treats qualia differently from other mental properties. (c) Present one line of response to the argument that a nonreductive physicalist might give, and assess whether it succeeds."

And here's the student's answer, word for word:

"Kim's argument runs roughly like this. (1) A mental event M causes a physical event P*. (2) By the causal closure of the physical, P* also has a physical cause, P, that's nomologically sufficient all on its own -- this is where closure enters. (3) M is not identical to P; that's what makes the physicalism 'nonreductive,' since mental properties are multiply realizable and so can't be type-identified with any single physical property. (4) Barring systematic overdetermination, P* can't have two distinct sufficient causes, so P excludes M as a cause of P* -- this is where the no-overdetermination premise enters. (5) Therefore M does not cause P*, and since the argument doesn't depend on any special feature of this particular M or P*, it generalizes: mental properties turn out to be epiphenomenal.

Kim thinks qualia can't be functionally reduced the way beliefs and desires can. A functional reduction defines a mental property by its typical causes and effects, but qualia -- the felt redness of red, the painfulness of pain -- seem to be defined by their intrinsic, not relational, character: two creatures could be functional duplicates and still differ qualitatively, as inverted-qualia cases are meant to show. So the reduction strategy that saves causal power for propositional attitudes leaves qualia stranded as epiphenomenal.

The most promising reply I've found is Shoemaker's subset view: a mental property's causal powers are a proper subset of its physical realizer's causal powers, so M and P were never two competing sufficient causes of P* to begin with -- they stand to each other the way a determinate stands to a determinable, like scarlet and red, and red obviously doesn't compete with scarlet to explain why something looks colored. On this picture the subset strategy works by denying premise (3): M and P turn out not to be fully distinct after all, at least not in the sense the argument needs, which is why I think it blocks the argument at its root rather than just biting a bullet."
