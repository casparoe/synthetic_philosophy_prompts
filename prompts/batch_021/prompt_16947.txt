I'd be grateful for your reading of it. The question concerns the "Pasadena game," the St. Petersburg-style gamble that Harris Nover and Alan Hájek introduced in their 2004 paper "Vexing Expectations."

Question, as posed to students:

The Pasadena game is played as follows: a fair coin is tossed repeatedly until it lands heads for the first time, on toss n. You then receive a payoff of (-1)^(n-1)·2^n/n dollars (so you are paid if n is odd, and must pay if n is even, in amounts that grow without bound).

(a) Write down the series whose sum would represent this game's expected value, and characterize the kind of convergence, if any, that it exhibits.
(b) Explain, citing the relevant theorem, why Nover and Hájek maintain that the game's expected value is undefined — not merely equal to some specific number, and not simply infinite, as in the classical St. Petersburg game.
(c) Is this trouble specific to the Pasadena game, or does it point to a more general limitation of expected-value theory as a normative account of rational choice under risk? Defend your answer.

Student's answer:

(a) If heads first comes up on toss n, this happens with probability 1/2^n, and the payoff is (-1)^(n-1)·2^n/n. Multiplying each payoff by its probability and summing over all n, the expected value is given by the series Σ (1/2^n)·(-1)^(n-1)·(2^n/n) = Σ (-1)^(n-1)/n = 1 - 1/2 + 1/3 - 1/4 + ..., the alternating harmonic series. This series converges, to ln 2, but only conditionally: the series of absolute values, Σ 1/n, is the ordinary harmonic series, which diverges. So the expectation series is conditionally, rather than absolutely, convergent.

(b) Because the series is only conditionally convergent, it falls under the Riemann Hypothesis for such series: a conditionally convergent series can be reordered, simply by permuting its terms, to converge to any real number we like, or to diverge to +infinity or -infinity. This is what distinguishes the Pasadena game from the classical St. Petersburg game, where every term of the expectation series is positive (Σ (1/2^n)·2^n = Σ 1), so the series diverges outright to +infinity regardless of how its terms are ordered; because a series of one-signed terms cannot be rearranged into convergence, "the value is infinite" is at least a rearrangement-invariant fact about that game. The Pasadena game has no such invariant fact to fall back on — except, arguably, for the order in which the payoffs are actually realized in time. Nover and Hájek consider exactly this option: the expectations of the truncated games, obtained by stopping the coin-tossing after n flips, converge, as n grows, to ln 2. Since it's natural to think that this temporal, or truncation, ordering is privileged — after all, it's the order in which the game is actually played, not an arbitrary shuffling imposed from outside — Nover and Hájek conclude that ln 2 is the best candidate for the game's expected value, even though they grant that this makes the decision-theoretic lesson of the Pasadena game a fairly narrow one.

(c) Given this, I think the Pasadena game is best seen as a special, somewhat artificial case rather than a deep general problem for expected-utility theory. It exploits a technical feature of infinite series — conditional convergence — that simply doesn't arise for any finite or bounded gamble, or even for most unbounded ones, like the St. Petersburg game, whose divergence to infinity is flat and order-independent rather than a matter of which ordering happens to be chosen. Since almost every decision problem we actually face has a well-defined expectation, the Pasadena game shows at most that expected-utility theory needs some side-constraint — for instance, restricting it to gambles whose payoff series converge absolutely, or privileging the temporally realized ordering when one is available — rather than showing that something is deeply wrong with maximizing expected utility as a normative theory of choice.
