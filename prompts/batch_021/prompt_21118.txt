Question: Explain Fitch's paradox of knowability. Set the argument out in numbered steps and say explicitly which principles governing the knowledge operator K are doing the work at each step. Then, in a paragraph or two, explain why the result is thought to pose a special problem for anti-realist/verificationist theories of truth.

Student's answer:

Fitch's paradox starts from two premises that look individually very plausible. The first is the knowability principle (KP): for any truth p, it's possible for someone to know that p. The second is a modest realist assumption -- call it (NO), for non-omniscience -- that not every truth is actually known: there's at least one true p such that p & ~Kp.

To get the paradox going, fix a specific p that witnesses NO, and consider the conjunction p & ~Kp itself. Since NO says this conjunction is true, KP (instantiated at that very conjunction) tells us it's possible to know it: ⋄K(p & ~Kp).

But now look at what K(p & ~Kp) would require. Knowledge distributes over conjunction, so K(p & ~Kp) gives us Kp & K~Kp. And here's the crucial move: by the KK-principle -- that knowing something puts you in a position to know that you know it -- K~Kp guarantees that ~Kp is itself true, so we get Kp & ~Kp, a flat contradiction. Since that's a contradiction in every possible world, K(p & ~Kp) is impossible everywhere, so ⋄K(p & ~Kp) is false, directly contradicting what KP handed us a moment ago.

So KP and NO can't both hold. Since NO is close to unassailable (nobody wants to say every truth is known by someone), KP has to go: it's not true that every truth is knowable.

This is why verificationists and semantic anti-realists like Dummett, who want to define truth in terms of knowability, are in real trouble here: Fitch's argument proves that anti-realism about truth is untenable, full stop.
