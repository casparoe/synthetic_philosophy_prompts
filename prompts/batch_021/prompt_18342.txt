I'm several drafts into a novel whose central figure is loosely inspired by the real cases of people born blind who had their sight restored by cataract surgery in the early eighteenth century — the kind of case the surgeon William Cheselden made famous when he wrote up his own patient in 1728. There's a scene I want to get right in which my character's old tutor, arguing with a visiting philosopher, tries to predict what the young woman will and won't be able to make of a cube and a ball sitting side by side on a table, the very moment her bandages come off. To write that scene honestly I've been reading through the debate that Molyneux's question set off between Locke and Leibniz, and I've come unstuck on the Leibniz side of it.

Locke reports the question, as Molyneux originally put it to him, like this: "Suppose a Man born blind, and now adult, and taught by his touch to distinguish between a Cube, and a Sphere of the same metal, and nighly of the same bigness, so as to tell, when he felt one and t'other; which is the Cube, which the Sphere. Suppose then the Cube and Sphere placed on a Table, and the Blind Man to be made to see. Quaere, Whether by his sight, before he touch'd them, he could now distinguish, and tell, which is the Globe, which the Cube." Molyneux's own answer, which Locke says he entirely agrees with, is no: the man's touch has taught him how a cube feels and how a sphere feels, but not that whatever affects his touch in those two ways must affect his sight correspondingly.

Leibniz took issue with this in his New Essays on Human Understanding — drafted in 1704 and left unpublished until 1765, by which time both men were long dead, so this exchange never actually happened as a live conversation. In the dialogue he wrote, "Philalethes" speaks for Locke's positions and "Theophilus" for Leibniz's own; here is Theophilus's considered response to the case Philalethes has just laid out:

"[I]t seems to me beyond question that the newly sighted man could discern them by applying rational principles to the sensory knowledge that he has already acquired by touch. [...] My view rests on the fact that in the case of the sphere there are no distinguished points on the surface of the sphere taken in itself, since everything there is uniform and without angles, whereas in the case of the cube there are eight points that are distinguished from all the others. If there weren't that way of recognising shapes, a blind man couldn't learn the rudiments of geometry by touch, nor could a sighted person learn them by sight without touch. However, we find that men born blind can learn geometry [...] and we find that geometry is mostly learned by sight alone without employing touch, as must be done by a paralytic [...]. These two geometries, the blind man's and the paralytic's, must come together, and agree, and indeed basically rest on the same ideas, even though they have no images in common."

Theophilus attaches one condition to this "yes": that the man must already have been told that the two shapes before him are a cube and a sphere, and that his only task is to work out which is which.

Two things about this defeat me. First, I can't reconstruct the actual inferential step from touch to sight: granted that a cube has eight such points and a sphere none, how does knowing this by feel let a man determine, by sight, and before he has ever correlated the two senses, which of two shapes in front of him has them? Second, doesn't Leibniz's own condition simply hand Locke what he wanted — if the man has to be told in advance which shape is the cube, hasn't he already been given the very sight-touch correlation that Molyneux's question was asking whether he could supply for himself? I want to put an accurate two- or three-sentence version of Theophilus's actual argument into my tutor character's mouth rather than a fudge, so I need to understand it properly first: what is the "rational principle" doing here, and is my second worry a real problem for Leibniz's position, or a misreading of the condition he's attaching to it?
