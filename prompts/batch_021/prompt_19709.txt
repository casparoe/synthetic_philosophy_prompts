A couple of friends of mine are doing PhDs in logic and set theory, and at a birthday thing last weekend they ended up in a proper argument about Zorn's Lemma that I haven't been able to stop turning over since (this isn't really my beat, i mostly cover neuroscience and AI stuff, but I got pulled in as the reluctant referee). It started because Ella said she'd invoked Zorn's Lemma twice that week without thinking twice about it -- once to get a basis for some horrible infinite-dimensional vector space, once to get a maximal ideal in a ring -- and that she just treats it as routine bookkeeping, no different from any other axiom, especially since it's provably equivalent to the Axiom of Choice and to the Well-Ordering Theorem: accept one, you've bought all three. Marcus pushed back hard: that equivalence is exactly the problem, he said, because full Choice is what buys you the Banach-Tarski paradox, where a solid ball can be sliced into a handful of pieces and reassembled, using nothing but rotations and slides, into two balls each the same size as the original. An axiom that lets you double a ball for free, he said, isn't harmless plumbing.

Ella answered that this is a bit of a cheap shot -- the Banach-Tarski pieces are non-measurable, so you can't actually do this to a real orange with a real knife, and all the "paradox" really shows is that volume doesn't make sense for arbitrary subsets of space, which is a limitation of measure theory, not a strike against the axiom. Marcus conceded you can't reassemble an actual orange, but said that's exactly his point: Choice is one axiom, and you don't get to keep its convenient consequences -- a basis for every vector space, a maximal ideal in every ring -- while treating the pathological ones as some separate embarrassment; the non-measurable sets come out of the same well. Then he raised what he said actually bothers him more: Choice (hence Zorn) guarantees a well-ordering of the real numbers exists, yet nobody has ever exhibited one, or even a rule for one, not even in principle, and that total silence should worry us more than a merely surprising theorem does. Ella's comeback was that nonconstructive existence proofs are all over ordinary math -- tons of proofs by contradiction, compactness arguments, and so on -- and that constructivists who reject all of those too are a small minority nobody feels bound by, so singling out Choice for this complaint is arbitrary. Marcus's closer was that it's a difference of degree, not just kind: an ordinary nonconstructive proof still leaves you enough to check instances one at a time, whereas a Choice-conjured well-ordering of the reals gives you no handle whatsoever -- and Zorn's Lemma, specifically, is the form people reach for exactly when they can't be bothered (or literally can't, even in principle) build the maximal object by hand, which makes it the most "fiat"-feeling of the three equivalent statements, not the most innocent.

Neither of them budged and honestly I don't have the set theory chops to referee it myself. Could you go through the exchange and say who's got the better of it at each specific step, not just who wins overall? I'm especially curious whether Marcus's closing "difference of degree" move actually survives Ella's "nonconstructive proofs are everywhere" jab, or whether she's right that he's just moving the goalposts -- and whether there's some obvious point neither of them is making that should change how we think about the whole dispute.
