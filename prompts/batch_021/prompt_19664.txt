I've been mulling over Simpson's paradox lately, partly provoked by an unresolved argument with a friend from my rationalist reading group. Every popular exposition of the paradox — the Berkeley admissions data, the kidney-stone treatment study, the batting-average examples — makes the same rhetorical move: the pooled data show one relationship between a variable X and an outcome Y, splitting the data by some third variable Z reverses it, and we're told to trust the split, since it "controls for confounding." What I've never seen adequately defended is why that should be treated as the automatic right answer rather than as one possible resolution among others. My own understanding is that whether the aggregated comparison or the Z-stratified one is the trustworthy one can't actually be read off the joint distribution of X, Y, and Z; it depends on whether Z is a common cause of X and Y (a genuine confounder) or instead sits on the causal pathway between them (a mediator) — and that these two very different causal stories can generate numerically identical data while demanding opposite statistical treatment.

Could you explain, as precisely as you can and without simply falling back on one of the canonical real-world datasets, why this is so? I'd like you to construct two minimal causal structures over just X, Y, and Z — one in which Z confounds X and Y, one in which Z fully mediates the effect of X on Y — that yield the same observed correlations, and to show why adjusting for Z recovers the true causal effect in the first case while actively destroying it in the second. If you have space, I'd also value your judgment on whether it's fair to describe evidential decision theory's verdict in the smoking lesion problem as making exactly this mistake: trusting an unadjusted, confounded correlation between action and outcome in a scenario that is structurally just Simpson's paradox with the confounding variable hidden inside the agent rather than sitting in a spreadsheet.
