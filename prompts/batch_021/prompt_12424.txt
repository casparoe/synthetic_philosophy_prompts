For a while now I've been bothered by something in my own habits: I watch a great many documentaries and 'issue' dramas about addiction, war, or poverty, feel genuinely moved by them, and then go to bed feeling as though I've discharged some sort of moral obligation for the day. Reading on my own, I came across Rousseau's Letter to d'Alembert, written against d'Alembert's proposal that Geneva build a theatre, and two of his points seem to bear directly on this habit. First, he argues that the pity a tragedy draws from us is a kind of counterfeit currency: we pay it out in tears during the performance and leave feeling the account is settled, which if anything leaves us less inclined to trouble ourselves over real suffering afterward. Second, he sets the theatre's passive audience against the open-air civic festivals he preferred, in which citizens are simultaneously performers and spectators of their own shared life rather than watching other people's lives performed for them. I'd like to know what you take to be the two or three most important implications of these specific arguments — not simply the general fact that Rousseau disliked theatre — for how I should regard my own documentary-watching, and for the broader habit of sharing 'moving' content online as a kind of stand-in for civic action. I'm less interested in a generic 'put down your phone' moral than in what actually follows once Rousseau's reasoning is taken seriously on its own terms.
