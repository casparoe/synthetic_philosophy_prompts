Effective-altruist writers and welfare economists often invoke Harsanyi's aggregation theorem as a near-mathematical vindication of utilitarianism: if individual and social preferences both satisfy the expected-utility axioms and a Pareto condition, social welfare must be a weighted sum of individual utilities. I'm reporting a piece that examines whether this claim holds up, and I'd like a rigorous outside check before repeating it in print. Write an essay weighing the theorem's force against two of its sharpest challenges: the argument, developed by Sen and later formalized by Weymark, that Harsanyi's proof equivocates between von Neumann–Morgenstern utility (a mere index of choice under risk) and the interpersonally comparable welfare that classical utilitarianism actually requires; and Diamond's ex ante/ex post objection, which uses a fair-lottery case to show that respecting everyone's ex ante preferences can conflict with ordinary judgments about equitable treatment after the fact. Do not retreat into observing that both sides have a point: reach a definite verdict on how much genuine support the theorem lends to utilitarianism (a qualified verdict, such as "it works only once interpersonal comparisons are independently secured," is fine, but a non-verdict is not), and state what, if anything, would need to be added to close the gap.
