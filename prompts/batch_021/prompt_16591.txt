I've been reporting on coalition politics -- the whole "strange bedfellows" thing, like the classic Baptists-and-bootleggers story where two groups back the same policy for completely different reasons. When I mentioned that to a philosopher I was interviewing for the piece, she said that's not quite what she studies -- Baptists and bootleggers is really just a coincidence of interests -- but it reminded her of something related that she finds more interesting: "moral trade," where two people or groups who disagree about what actually matters morally strike an actual bargain, each conceding something by there own lights, so both end up better off according to their own values without either side budging on who's actually right. She traced the term to a paper by, I think, Toby Ord, and likened the logic to comparative advantage in econ -- you don't need to think another countries preferences are "correct" to still gain from trading with them. She was also careful to distinguish it from plain old moral offsetting (paying to cancel out a harm you caused), since trade specifically needs two parties with different values striking a real bargain, not just one person squaring their own books.

I only caught the concept in passing and my draft is due soon, so I can't really chase her down again to flesh it out properly. Could you lay out what you think are the most important implications of taking moral trade seriously for how cause-focused orgs should actually negotiate with each other -- say, an animal-welfare group and a global-health group drawing from an overlapping pool of "effective giving" donors, occasionally having to decide whether to co-sign a campaign or split a shared grant? I'm less interested in the rosy comparative-advantage pitch and more in where you think the analogy strains or outright breaks down -- things like power imbalances between a bigger org and a smaller one, the fact that "moral credence" isn't verifiable or exchangeable the way currency is, the risk that quietly striking these deals corrodes the very conviction that made people effective advocates in the first place, or optics problems if donors find out their money was essentially traded away in a backroom compromise.
