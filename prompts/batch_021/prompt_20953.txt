I'm a software engineer at a startup that builds evaluation tooling for conversational AI, and I've somehow ended up owning the first draft of our internal "model honesty" policy — the document meant to say which kinds of model outputs count as dishonest enough to be flagged, downweighted, or blocked during training. None of us on the team have a philosophy background, so before writing anything I did some reading to avoid reinventing the wheel, and quickly ran into a snag I haven't been able to get past on my own.

Nearly every classical account of lying I found treats it as asserting something you privately believe to be false, with the intention of getting someone else to believe it — Augustine's is the oldest version of this, and Sissela Bok's twentieth-century treatment is basically a modernized descendant of the same idea. That definition obviously presupposes the speaker has beliefs and intentions in some fairly robust sense. But a language model doesn't store a stable set of propositions it's committed to and then decide to contradict one, so strictly speaking it seems like it can never lie, no matter how false or misleading its output is. I tried getting around this with Harry Frankfurt's notion of "bullshit" instead of lying, since that only requires indifference to the truth rather than a specific false belief, and I've since seen this same move made in some recent philosophy-of-AI commentary about hallucination. But even "indifference" seems to smuggle in some minimal caring or attentional state that I'm not sure a next-token predictor has either, so I'm not convinced this actually solves the problem rather than just pushing it back a step.

What would genuinely help is a single, concrete example of a chatbot response where you can show all of this at once: a case that (1) obviously deserves to be flagged as dishonest under any practical policy we'd actually want to write, (2) clearly fails Augustine and Bok's belief-plus-intent test for lying the moment you apply it literally, and (3) is at least genuinely debatable, rather than clear-cut, under Frankfurt's indifference-to-truth test for bullshitting — so the example forces a real choice between the two tests instead of trivially satisfying or failing both. Once you've laid out the example, I'd be curious for your own view on whether the right fix is to drop mental-state language from the definition of AI dishonesty altogether in favor of something purely behavioral or dispositional (defined, say, over training incentives rather than individual utterances), or whether there's a thinner, more deflationary notion of "belief" and "intention" that can legitimately survive being applied to a system like this one.
