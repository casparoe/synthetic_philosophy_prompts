Me and two people from my online book club got into this whole side argument last week that I still can't stop thinking about. I'm not a science or philosophy person at all, I just got into this stuff a few months back from a podcast, and we'd just finished Julian Barbour's book about how time might not be a real thing, only change is real, and somehow that turned into a 40 minute detour about Newton's bucket.

Quick version of the setup for anyone who doesn't know it: you spin a bucket of water hanging from a rope. At first the water stays flat while the bucket spins around it, so the relative motion between bucket and water is at its max but the surface stays flat. Then friction drags the water along, and once the water is rotating together with the bucket, basically zero relative motion between them, the surface goes concave and climbs up the sides. Greg's argument was that this settles it for Newton: the concave shape shows up exactly when there's NO relative rotation between bucket and water, so whatever's causing it can't be "rotation relative to the bucket," it has to be rotation relative to space itself. Sadie said that's a false choice, because Mach's whole move was pointing out a third option Newton never considered, rotation relative to everything else in the universe, all the distant matter, not just the bucket sitting next to it. Greg came back saying fine, but that makes a prediction that seems both untestable and maybe wrong: delete every other object in the universe and leave one bucket spinning alone, and on Mach's view theres nothing left for it to rotate relative to, so no concave surface no matter how fast you spin it, and he added that even general relativity, the theory Einstein built partly to vindicate Mach, has solutions (he mentioned something called the Gödel universe) where the whole universe rotates relative to local gyroscopes even though there's nothing outside it to rotate relative to, which he took as proof GR never actually delivered Mach's picture. Sadie's comeback was that this doesn't let Newton off the hook either, because GR also predicts that spinning masses drag inertial frames around with them, "frame dragging," which has actually been measured (she mentioned Gravity Probe B), so at minimum inertia here is partly shaped by matter way out there, which is exactly the kind of thing pure absolute space isn't supposed to allow.

Nobody at book club could agree on who came out ahead and its bugging me more than it probably should. Could you go through this move by move and say who's got the stronger case at each step, whether either one of them is leaning on a misunderstanding of the physics or the philosophy anywhere, and whether there's some bigger point neither of them is even considering?
