I'm a PhD student in philosophy, and this term I'm in a graduate seminar on metaethics that just spent two sessions on Sharon Street's 2006 paper "A Darwinian Dilemma for Realist Theories of Value." The seminar discussion itself stayed fairly tame, but afterward two people in my cohort, Daniel and Sofia, ended up arguing about it in the hallway for a good twenty minutes, clearly both fluent in the literature and unwilling to concede an inch. I jotted down the exchange afterward because I couldn't tell who was actually getting the better of it, and since I've read the paper enough times to have lost any confidence in my own untutored reaction, I'd like a more disinterested assessment than I can give it myself.

Daniel opened by laying out Street's dilemma roughly as she poses it: either our evaluative beliefs bear no relation to independent, mind-independent evaluative truths, in which case it would be a staggering coincidence if natural selection happened to have shaped us to track them, or realists must claim that those truths themselves somehow influenced which organisms survived and reproduced, which requires a causal relation between abstract, causally inert normative facts and natural selection that nobody has ever made credible. Either way, he said, positing robust mind-independent value does no explanatory work that the evolutionary story doesn't already do more cheaply, so we should move to some constructivist or antirealist account instead. Sofia answered with the sort of third-factor reply associated with David Enoch and others: the dilemma wrongly assumes the only options are "no connection" and "the normative facts directly pushed on our genes," when there's a third possibility on which some perfectly natural fact — that creatures capable of suffering matter, say, or that one's own survival is good for one — both made certain attitudes adaptive and is the very fact that makes those attitudes true, so nothing causally spooky needs to link abstract value to chromosomes. Daniel granted that this works for maximally general claims like "survival is good," but objected that it does nothing for the far more specific, equally adaptive commitments realists don't want vindicated — in-group loyalty, xenophobia, disproportionate favoritism toward kin — and that without an independent way of sorting which evolved dispositions get the third-factor treatment, the move just gets invoked wherever the realist already likes the belief and withheld wherever she doesn't. Sofia conceded she had no general sorting procedure, but turned the tables by pointing out that a structurally identical debunking argument seems to threaten our confidence in logic and arithmetic, since our basic deductive and numerical capacities were shaped by selection for fitness rather than truth just as much as our evaluative capacities were, and yet almost nobody thinks this debunks logic or math; if Daniel wants to run the argument against value while blocking it for mathematics, she said, he owes a principled asymmetry, and any such asymmetry will have to smuggle in exactly the kind of independent grip on evaluative truth that antirealists are supposed to be denying him.

Neither of them backed down, and I'd like your take, but not just on who won overall. Please go through the exchange move by move — Daniel's opening dilemma, Sofia's third-factor reply, Daniel's parochialism objection, and Sofia's math/logic parallel — and say for each one whether it actually succeeds against the position it targets, merely scores a debating point, or shifts the dialectic without settling anything, and why. Then give your own overall verdict on where this leaves the third-factor strategy as a response to Street, and say what, if anything, you think both Daniel and Sofia are overlooking. End your answer with a single line of the form "Verdict: ..." stating your overall take in one sentence.
