I'm a PhD student in economics finishing a dissertation chapter on axiomatic bargaining theory, and this term I've also been TAing our department's first-year game theory sequence. Every year the same thing happens: students read Nash's 1950 paper, find Independence of Irrelevant Alternatives (IIA) self-evidently reasonable — "why would removing options nobody wanted change the answer?" — and then get quietly unsettled once we introduce Kalai and Smorodinsky's 1975 alternative, which drops IIA in favor of an individual monotonicity axiom instead. I'd like something livelier than another proof-heavy handout to convey that this is a genuine, unresolved disagreement about what fairness in bargaining requires, rather than a case of Nash simply being "right" and Kalai–Smorodinsky being a historical footnote.

Could you write me a dialogue between two bargaining theorists — one a committed defender of the Nash solution, the other a committed defender of the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution — who run into each other after a workshop talk and end up arguing at a whiteboard over a single running example, say two firms dividing the surplus from a prospective merger, where at some point one firm's outside option improves and the feasible set of payoff splits expands. I don't want either side simply to recite their own axioms: the Nash defender needs a real answer to the standard complaint that an expansion of the feasible set that unambiguously improves what's available to one player, in exactly the sense the monotonicity axiom cares about, can still lead the Nash solution to award that very player a smaller payoff than before, and the Kalai–Smorodinsky defender needs a real answer to the mirror-image complaint that their solution can recommend a different split once the feasible set merely shrinks by dropping points nobody was ever going to choose, purely because doing so moves the "ideal point" the solution is built around. I'd like the dialogue to end with both of them still holding their ground, but each visibly having taken the other's best objection seriously rather than talking past it.
