A friend and I spent much of a long evening arguing about P. F. Strawson's "Freedom and Resentment," and neither of us managed to convince the other. As it happens, I am currently TAing a graduate seminar on moral responsibility, and one of the take-home exam questions this term touches on exactly the passage we were disputing. I would appreciate a disinterested second opinion on a student's submission, assessed strictly on its own merits.

The question, as set, reads:

"Explain Strawson's distinction between considerations that excuse a piece of conduct and considerations that lead us to adopt what he calls 'the objective attitude' toward the agent who performed it. Then explain why Strawson thinks the general thesis of determinism, even if true, could not by itself warrant taking up the objective attitude toward everyone, always — and say what this is supposed to show about the threat determinism poses to the reactive attitudes."

Here is the student's answer:

"Strawson thinks our ordinary responses to other people — resentment when we are wronged, gratitude when we are benefited, guilt when we wrong someone ourselves — are what he calls the reactive attitudes. These come in a few varieties: personal ones, like resentment and gratitude; vicarious ones, like moral indignation felt on someone else's behalf; and self-reactive ones, like guilt.

Excuses work by showing that a particular piece of conduct did not really express the ill will or disregard it seemed to — 'he didn't mean to,' 'he was pushed,' and so on. These leave the agent's general standing as a responsible participant untouched; only this one act is taken off the table. By contrast, considerations like 'he's only a child' or 'he's mentally deranged' make us give up the reactive attitudes toward the person as a whole, so that we relate to him not as a fellow participant in personal relationships but as someone to be managed, treated, or handled — what Strawson calls the objective attitude.

Strawson's point about determinism is that it could only be a threat to responsibility if it worked like the second kind of consideration, exempting everyone at once, rather than like an excuse, which only ever targets particular acts. But since determinism is a completely general thesis, and since we are constitutionally incapable of sustaining the objective attitude across the board, Strawson concludes that we simply cannot help going on reacting as we always have — so the truth or falsity of determinism turns out to be practically irrelevant to whether we keep our reactive attitudes, which is why he thinks the skeptic's worry, however correct in the abstract, never actually has to trouble us."
