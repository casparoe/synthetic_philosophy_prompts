My father died five weeks ago, and I've been asked to give the eulogy, which is turning out to be harder than I expected. He spent about fifty years working out exactly what he did and didn't believe about death, and something happened to him near the end that put all of that thinking to a real test. He left school at sixteen and spent his working life as an electrician, but he was a self-taught atheist of the old rationalist kind -- treasurer of the local Humanist group for as long as I can remember. The story he told more often than any other, usually after a couple of drinks, was A. J. Ayer's. He loved recounting how Ayer choked on a piece of smoked salmon in University College Hospital in June 1988, how his heart stopped for four minutes, and how he came back to write the essay everyone remembers, "What I Saw When I Was Dead," with its blinding, painful red light and its incompetent "ministers" who had let space itself go slightly "out of joint." But Dad's favourite part was never that essay. It was the short, much less quoted piece Ayer published a few weeks later, "Postscript to a Postmortem," quietly correcting himself in print: insisting that the experience hadn't touched his disbelief in an afterlife at all, only softened his own "inflexible attitude" toward it. Dad used to say that second article was the most honest thing he'd ever read a philosopher write, and that if anything like it ever happened to him, he hoped he'd have the nerve to correct himself in public the same way.

Two winters ago his own heart stopped, on an operating table, for long enough that they had to bring him back, and when he came round he told my brother, half-joking, that he'd "seen something" and that it had shaken him more than he expected. About ten days later, sitting up in bed, he delivered his own postscript: he hadn't changed his mind about there being nothing afterward, he said, only about how entitled he'd felt to sneer at people who thought otherwise. He wrote it all down for me afterwards, along with strict instructions for the funeral -- nobody was to say he was "at peace" or "watching over" anyone, but nobody was to pretend the hospital business had been nothing either, because he thought that would be exactly the dishonesty Ayer had refused to commit. My aunt, who is religious and loved him dearly, has already told me how much comfort it would give her to hear that he's "in a better place," which is precisely the sentence he spent his last two years pre-emptively banning.

I have no training in philosophy, and most of the room -- his brothers, his old workmates, the neighbours -- will never have heard of Ayer and won't sit through a lecture on him. Could you write the eulogy for me? It should run to about five minutes, give the room just enough of the Ayer story for Dad's own line to make sense, and be warm and, in places, funny, the way he'd have wanted. But it can't contain a single sentence that drifts into the comfort my aunt is hoping for, and it can't flatten his own experience into nothing either -- that second failure is the one he was actually more afraid of.
