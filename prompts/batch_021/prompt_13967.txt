So this happened in my debate club last week and it's been bugging me ever since. We do Lincoln-Douglas, so my partner was up against just one other kid, and the round was on animal testing in medicine. His case was basically: it's fine to run painful experiments on like a hundred lab rats if it ends up curing a disease that saves thousands of human lives, because you just add up the suffering prevented versus the suffering caused, and it comes out way positive. Everyone in the room bought that part. But then the other kid asked a follow-up: "okay, so would you support testing an equally promising drug on a hundred prisoners instead, without their consent, if it would save just as many lives?" and my partner immediately said no, obviously not, you can't just use people like that no matter what the numbers say. The judges docked him for flip-flopping, like he was just picking whichever answer sounded nicer in the moment. Afterward our assistant coach tried to explain why the rebuttal actually worked, but her explanation kind of went in one ear and out the other. My partner still insists it's not a contradiction, because animals and humans are two totally seperate categories, so its fine to reason about them in completely different ways — add up the numbers when its animals, but treat individual humans as basically off-limits no matter what the math says.

Part of me thinks he's onto something, but another part feels like he's having his cake and eating it too, switching rules whenever it's convenient for his case. Is there an actual principled way to hold both of those positions at the same time without it just collapsing into a dodge, or is he secretly being inconsistent and just dressing it up nicely? We've got regionals in a few weeks and I'd rather actually understand the logic here than just repeat whatever sounded smart in that one round.
