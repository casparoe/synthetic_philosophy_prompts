I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, but I fell down a rabbit hole a while back after watching a Computerphile video featuring Rob Miles on Löb's theorem and why it makes AI self-trust so much harder than it sounds. The part that stuck with me: an agent that only acts when it has a proof that the action is safe can't, in general, also prove the reflection principle "if my future self (or a successor built on the same axioms) proves that A is safe, then A really is safe" — because Löb's theorem shows that proving that schema for an arbitrary claim would force you to already be able to prove the claim itself, so the guarantee collapses exactly where you'd need it: on the claims you haven't derived yet. People working on "tiling agents" treat this as a genuine obstacle to building AI that can stably self-improve or hand off trust across generations without quietly weakening its own standards each time.
