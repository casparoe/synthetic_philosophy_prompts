I write about science and ideas for a living, and while reporting a piece on how philosophers still argue over the ethics of the Hiroshima and Nagasaki bombings, I sat in on a public talk about Elizabeth Anscombe's pamphlet "Mr. Truman's Degree," written to oppose Oxford's 1956 decision to award Harry Truman an honorary degree. Invoking what philosophers call the doctrine of double effect, she argued that "choosing to kill the innocent as a means to your ends is always murder" regardless of consequences, and on that basis called Truman a murderer for ordering the bombings. The Q&A turned into a real argument between two audience members that the moderator let run longer than planned.

1. Marcus opened by defending her: even a scrupulously careful attack on a factory or naval base will foreseeably kill some nearby civilians without that being murder, he said, but Hiroshima and Nagasaki were different in kind, because the cities and the people in them, not some isolated military installation, were the target -- which is exactly why Anscombe called it murder rather than an unfortunate side effect.

2. Leah answered that Truman could describe his target the same bloodless way any bomber pilot describes his -- an army depot, a port, war industries -- with the deaths of everyone living nearby a horrifying certainty rather than a chosen means. If careful attacks on legitimate objectives excuse foreseeable civilian deaths no matter how numerous, she said, then the line between that and "the city itself was the target" is being asked to do more work than it can bear once the body count gets this large.

3. Marcus replied that the historical record settles it: the cities on the target list were deliberately spared conventional bombing so the new weapon's power over an intact city would be unmistakable, and success was explicitly measured by the visible devastation and psychological shock to the population, not by any narrower military objective. If the city had somehow survived intact, the mission would have counted as a failure by its own planners' standards -- which is exactly the sign, he said, that the deaths were the means, not a side effect.

4. Leah granted the point about intention but said this only exposes Anscombe's real weakness: her insistence that intentionally killing innocents is murder "no matter how good the consequences." If killing this many people really was the only way to avoid an invasion that credible estimates said would kill several times as many, on both sides, then refusing to weigh that at all looks less like moral seriousness and more like caring about the shape of a rule more than the people it exists to protect.

5. Marcus answered that this "the numbers were big enough" reasoning is exactly what Anscombe thought was corrupting modern moral philosophy: once a sufficiently frightening hypothetical can license intentionally killing the innocent, there is no atrocity it couldn't also license, and a moral outlook with room for that has given up the ability to say that anything is simply forbidden.

Neither of them budged, and I've been turning it over since. Could you go through the five moves in order and say, for each, who has the better of it and why? I'm especially interested in whether Leah's "more work than it can bear" objection in move 2 survives Marcus's target-selection evidence in move 3, and whether Anscombe's absolutism has a good answer to the rule-worship charge in move 4. Give your verdicts as a JSON object mapping "1" through "5" to a score from -2 (clearly wrong) to 2 (clearly right), justify each score in two or three sentences, and close with a short paragraph on what both of them are missing.
