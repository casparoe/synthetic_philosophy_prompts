I've never had any formal training in philosophy, but after seeing Hume's line about miracles quoted (and misquoted) in online debates about whether it's ever reasonable to believe eyewitness reports of the miraculous, I decided to stop relying on secondhand summaries and actually read the essay itself: "Of Miracles," Section X, Part I, of the 1748 Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding. The famous closing maxim, that no testimony can establish a miracle unless its falsehood would be more miraculous than the miracle itself, made sense to me well enough on its own. What I'm stuck on is the reasoning Hume builds up to it a little earlier in the same Part, where he describes weighing evidence almost like a subtraction problem. Just before the passage below, he has already said that a "proof" is the highest degree of assurance, the kind we get from experience that has never once had an exception; here is where I lose the thread:

"In all cases, we must balance the opposite experiments, where they are opposite, and deduct the smaller number from the greater, in order to know the exact force of the superior evidence. [...] here is a contest of two opposite experiences; of which the one destroys the other, as far as its force goes, and the superior can only operate on the mind by the force, which remains. The very same principle of experience, which gives us a certain degree of assurance in the testimony of witnesses, gives us also, in this case, another degree of assurance against the fact, which they endeavour to establish; from which contradiction there necessarily arises a counterpoize, and mutual destruction of belief and authority. [...] in order to encrease the probability against the testimony of witnesses, let us suppose, that the fact, which they affirm, instead of being only marvellous, is really miraculous; and suppose also, that the testimony considered apart and in itself, amounts to an entire proof; in that case, there is proof against proof, of which the strongest must prevail, but still with a diminution of its force, in proportion to that of its antagonist."

Could you help me work through what is actually going on in this passage, rather than paraphrasing the essay's overall conclusion? Three things in particular have me stuck. First, when Hume says we "deduct the smaller number from the greater," is he picturing a literal tally of past cases on each side, and if so, what are the two tallies supposed to be in the miracle case, since "how often witnesses turn out to be lying" and "how often dead men have stayed dead" don't obviously live on the same scale, so I can't tell what is being subtracted from what. Second, he has just defined a "proof" as a degree of assurance that, being drawn from wholly uniform experience, sounds like it shouldn't come in degrees at all, yet a few lines later he is comfortable talking about "proof against proof" and one proof suffering "a diminution of its force" - isn't that a contradiction in terms, or is there a way of reading "proof" here that leaves room for it to be outweighed? Third, when he calls the result a "mutual destruction of belief and authority," does he mean both sides lose force and only a discounted remainder survives on the winning side, or that the losing side is wiped out entirely while the winner is left standing at full strength? I'd rather have a careful reading of what Hume is actually committed to here than a summary of the essay as a whole.
