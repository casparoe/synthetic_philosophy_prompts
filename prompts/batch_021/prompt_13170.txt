For my IB Extended Essay in Philosophy, I've been trying to bring in some ideas from Computer Science, the subject I like best, and that's led me to Scott Aaronson's writing making the case that philosophers interested in the limits of computation have mostly stopped at Turing and Gödel -- at facts that are uncomputable even in principle -- while almost entirely ignoring a second, stranger layer of limitation that only appears once you separate computability from complexity: facts that are computable in principle but not with any resources a real reasoner could ever actually have, the way factoring a very large number into primes is "possible" only in the same hollow sense that guessing a strong password by trying every possible combination is "possible." That distinction has started bothering me somewhere I didn't expect it to: a completely different class.

In economics we covered Aumann's agreement theorem, which is supposed to show that two people who share the same prior probabilities can never rationally "agree to disagree" once each has learned what the other currently believes; any leftover disagreement has to come down to differing information, bias, or dishonesty. But the argument seems to lean on both reasoners being able to compute their own posterior beliefs at no cost and to fully register the fact that their opinions are common knowledge -- an infinite regress of "I know that you know that I know..." That looks like exactly the sort of assumption complexity theory should be suspicious of: nothing in it is uncomputable, but actually carrying it out could be far too expensive for any bounded mind.

Could you construct an actual worked example -- specific probabilities and a specific back-and-forth protocol for exchanging posteriors, not just a description of the idea in general terms -- of two honest, computationally bounded Bayesian reasoners who share an identical prior, receive no new outside evidence, and yet rationally stop short of full agreement because reaching it would take more computation or more rounds of communication than either can afford? I'd like you to walk through the relevant step in the standard proof of Aumann's theorem alongside your example and point to exactly where the assumption of unbounded computation is doing the work, since that's the part of my essay's argument I don't yet trust myself to get right on my own.
