I spent thirty years as an observational astronomer, mostly working with flux-limited galaxy surveys, and since retiring a few years ago I've been reading my way into philosophy of religion with a small discussion group that meets monthly at the local university. We've been stuck for weeks on the fine-tuning argument. I know the basic shape of it well enough by now -- the cosmological constant sitting some 120 orders of magnitude below its "natural" theoretical value is the usual headline case -- so you needn't rehearse the standard chance/design/multiverse trichotomy for me. (I have a private soft spot for Hoyle's carbon-resonance prediction as anthropic reasoning's finest hour in physics, but that's beside the point here.) What I'd like help with is a narrower, more technical objection that keeps surfacing in what I've been reading: because we could only ever find ourselves observing a universe whose constants permit our own existence, our observing such constants is guaranteed, with probability one, regardless of whether design, an unrepeated chance draw, or a multiverse is what actually obtains. Our own existence, in other words, functions as what Nick Bostrom calls an "observation selection effect": it screens off the fine-tuned values from discriminating among those hypotheses, so the whole argument is supposed to collapse before design or a multiverse ever enters the discussion. One member of our group likes to illustrate this with a net that only catches fish above a certain size: however many oversized fish you haul up, you learn nothing about how many undersized fish remain in the lake.

That analogy has never sat right with me, because it isn't how selection effects were ever treated in my own field. Every flux-limited survey I worked with was Malmquist-biased by construction: at increasing distance you detect only the intrinsically brighter objects, since the fainter ones fall below the flux limit, and yet nobody concluded that such surveys carry no information about the underlying luminosity function. You write the selection function explicitly into the likelihood and still recover genuine constraints on the population despite the bias; you don't throw up your hands and call the data evidentially inert.

So I would like you to write an argument for the position that the fine-tuning inference survives the observation-selection-effect objection in essentially the same way: that once our own existence is treated as an explicit selection effect within a proper likelihood comparison, rather than invoked to end the discussion, the fine-tuned values of the constants remain genuine Bayesian evidence favoring design or a multiverse of varied universes over a single universe whose constants were fixed by one unrepeated chance event. Engage with the strongest form of the objection, not a caricature of it, and tell me plainly where, if anywhere, the analogy with astronomical selection effects actually breaks down -- I would rather have an honest, partial defense than a tidy, complete one. I am not asking you to referee design against the multiverse, or to defend theism as such, only to settle this narrower question of whether the selection effect really does drain the fine-tuning observation of its evidential force.
