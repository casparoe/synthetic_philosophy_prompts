I've been reporting a piece on how people who think professionally about existential risk reason about humanity's long-term prospects, and last month I sat in on a public panel on the subject at a science festival. The doomsday argument came up only briefly on stage, but it kept going long after the panel ended, out in the lobby, between two audience members who wouldn't let each other off the hook. One does statistical work for a living; the other, by his own admission, has spent a worrying number of evenings reading the philosophy-of-probability literature on anthropic reasoning. I ended up taking notes, partly because I want to represent both positions fairly in whatever I eventually write, and partly because I honestly couldn't tell who was winning.

The exchange went roughly like this:

(1) Renata opened with the standard argument: if you think of yourself as a random draw from everyone who will ever be born, finding yourself among only the first ~100 billion or so humans is unremarkable if the species dies out, one way or another, within the next few hundred billion births, but wildly atypical if humanity is destined to colonize the galaxy for millions of years and produce trillions of people. Since extreme atypicality counts as evidence against a hypothesis, your own birth rank is, all by itself, weak Bayesian evidence favoring "doom relatively soon" over "doom late or never" — an application of what's called the self-sampling assumption, and quite apart from anything we actually know about asteroids, pandemics, or nuclear weapons.

(2) Marcus objected that this smuggles in an illegitimate step: treating your own existence as a fixed background fact you get to condition on for free. Properly accounted for, the fact that hypotheses predicting more total observers also make it more likely that some observer like you would exist at all provides a compensating boost that exactly cancels Renata's shift. Once you build that in — the self-indication assumption — the birth-rank argument just returns you to whatever prior you started with; your rank alone tells you nothing about how the story ends.

(3) Renata's reply was that Marcus's fix is worse than the disease. Taken seriously, the self-indication assumption implies you should become extremely confident, before consulting any cosmological evidence at all, that the universe contains as many observers as possible — favoring an infinite universe over a finite one for no reason beyond the fact that infinite universes contain more people to be. That's essentially the "presumptuous philosopher" problem: it lets someone settle empirical questions about the size of the cosmos from an armchair, which is absurd, so it's the self-indication assumption that should go, not the original argument.

(4) Marcus's comeback invoked what's sometimes called the reference class problem: Renata's argument never gets off the ground without first choosing which set of beings you're supposedly a random draw from — humans, say — and there's no principled way to fix that. Count only people who've heard of the doomsday argument, or all sentient beings including whatever machine minds we eventually build, or all observers who share your exact information state, and the predicted odds of doom shift, sometimes drastically. A conclusion that depends on an arbitrary modeling choice like that isn't really telling you anything about the world.

(5) Renata's last word was that this cuts both ways: any argument that draws on facts about your own observational situation — including the cosmological arguments about why we should expect to observe a "typical" universe — runs into exactly the same problem of specifying who counts as a comparable observer, so it can't be a defect specific to the doomsday argument. Some reference class is always needed to interpret self-locating evidence at all, and "observers in roughly your epistemic position" is a defensible default, not an arbitrary one. Neither budged, and the conversation only ended because the bar was closing.

Could you go through the five moves in order and evaluate each on its own merits — where the speaker is basically right, where they're overreaching, and where the two of them are simply talking past each other — rather than just declaring an overall winner? I'd also like your sense of whether the exchange leaves the doomsday argument in reasonable shape or in tatters, and whether there's an important consideration that neither Renata nor Marcus raised.
