I'm drafting lecture notes on Galen Strawson's Basic Argument against what he calls "true" moral responsibility -- the strong, desert-entailing sense that could underwrite something like ultimate reward or punishment -- as developed in Freedom and Belief and condensed in his oft-cited 1994 Philosophical Studies article. There is one move in the argument that I would like to present to my students more crisply than I currently can. Strawson insists that the regress at its heart, from being responsible for what one does to being responsible for the way one is, bites just as hard under indeterminism as under determinism, which is precisely what distinguishes the Basic Argument from more familiar arguments like van Inwagen's Consequence Argument, which trades explicitly on the truth of determinism. Students' first instinct is usually that some genuinely undetermined event somewhere in the causal history of a person's character or motives should open at least a foothold for responsible self-origination. Could you reconstruct, at the level of the argument's structure rather than its conclusion, why Strawson takes this instinct to be mistaken?
