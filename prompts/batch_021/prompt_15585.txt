Since retiring from a career in condensed-matter physics, I have joined a small metaphysics reading group through my local university's continuing-education program, and a disagreement from our last meeting has stayed with me. We had just finished a set of readings on mereology -- Eli Hirsch on quantifier variance, a reply from Ted Sider, and a chapter from Peter van Inwagen's book on material objects -- when two of the regulars, Helen and Marcus, fell into an argument about whether disputes over composition are genuine metaphysical disagreements or merely verbal ones dressed up as deep.

(1) Marcus defended the "merely verbal" reading: the universalist, who thinks any two things whatsoever -- say, my writing desk and the far side of the Moon -- compose some further single object, and the nihilist, who thinks nothing ever composes anything and only mereological simples exist, agree on every fact about which particles there are and how they are arranged. They differ only in how they use words like "object" and "there exists," so if we interpret speakers charitably, letting them come out true by their own lights whenever we reasonably can, there is no real disagreement here -- just two dialects with different quantifiers. (2) Helen objected that this can't be the whole story: van Inwagen's own view, that composition occurs only when some things' activity constitutes a life, makes a specific and contestable claim about when unified objects come into being. That looks like a hypothesis about the world's structure, not a stipulation about vocabulary, so treating the whole debate as verbal throws out real content along with the confusion. (3) Marcus replied that even the "life" criterion can be redescribed, without loss, as a policy about which pluralities a given language chooses to quantify over as single things -- so it doesn't actually escape his diagnosis. (4) Helen countered that if any substantive-looking metaphysical claim can always be redescribed that way, the move proves too much: it would trivialize every ontological dispute into semantics, a conclusion that even philosophers sympathetic to quantifier variance stop short of endorsing, and that Marcus therefore owes some principled way of telling substantive disputes from verbal ones -- which he had not actually supplied.

I found both of them individually persuasive and could not work out where that left things. Could you go through the exchange move by move, saying who has the better of the argument at each step rather than just declaring an overall winner, and then say what, if anything, you think both Helen and Marcus are overlooking about how to draw the line between a verbal dispute and a substantive one?
