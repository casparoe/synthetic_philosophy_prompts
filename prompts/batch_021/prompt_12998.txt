My friend and I spent most of last night arguing about something that came up while we were finally winding down a small online business we've run together for six years, and I still haven't been able to decide who's right. Because we never set up a formal system for tracking hours or expenses, we've agreed that once the last client payment clears, each of us will independently tell our accountant what we think is a fair split of what's left, based on our own sense of how the work broke down over the years. Whatever we each submit is final; the accountant won't investigate whose figures are "true," only check that the two numbers together don't exceed what's actually sitting in the account. There's no ongoing business relationship left to protect, since we're both moving on to unrelated jobs, and neither of us will see the other's number until it's already locked in.

My friend's position is that, given all this, the sensible move for each of us is to quietly round our own figure up, since whatever the other person submits is already fixed and won't be changed by anything we do. He concedes that if we both round up too aggressively, the two numbers won't fit inside what's left in the account and the whole payout gets frozen while we untangle it with the accountant — a genuinely unpleasant prospect given how tense the last few months of unwinding this business have already been — but he insists that risk has no bearing on what each of us, individually, ought to submit, since neither of our decisions can influence the other.

What's nagging me is that we met in our first year of engineering school, and for a decade now mutual friends have found it almost eerie how often we land on the exact same answer, independently, to things that have nothing to do with money — the same order at a restaurant, the same read on a job offer, often within seconds of each other and without comparing notes beforehand. It feels like that should matter somehow to what the genuinely sensible choice is here, but when I tried to explain why to my friend, all I could say was that it felt wrong to treat this like a decision between two strangers. Is he right that our two decisions are exactly as independent as any two people's would be, so that how alike we think is just a coincidence with no bearing on what's rational, or is there an actual argument for why being this similar-minded should change what each of us does?
