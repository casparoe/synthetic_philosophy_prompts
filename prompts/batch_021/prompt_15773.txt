So I'm about two-thirds through a historical novel set partly in London and Hanover around 1715, and there's a scene I keep chickening out of writing myself: an imagined face-to-face confrontation between Newton and Leibniz. I know full well that in real life this fight never actually happened in person -- it was conducted at arm's length through Samuel Clarke as Newton's mouthpiece, with Princess Caroline relaying the letters back and forth, right up until Leibniz died in 1716 -- but for my scene I want to cheat a little and put the two of them in a room together, since a novel can do that and a philosophy seminar cant. What I need from you is the actual argument, though, not a softened version: Leibniz pressing his sufficient-reason case against absolute space (why would God set the material world at *this* location, in *this* orientation, rather than shift everything five feet over, or flip it into its mirror image, if space itself is empty and featureless -- there'd be no reason for Him to prefer one arrangement over the other, so "absolute position" doesn't actually mean anything), plus his jab about Newtons God being a bad watchmaker who has to keep winding and correcting a mechanism that was supposedly built perfect the first time. And I want Newton's side of it to hit back for real, not just splutter -- something about space being bound up with God's omnipresence and dominion, maybe working in that odd business from the Opticks about space as a kind of sensorium.

Here's the part I actually care about most, though: I want the theology underneath this to be doing real work, not just sitting there as period flavor. Newton was privately about as far from orthodox as a Cambridge don could get away with being -- he was convinced the Trinity was a corruption bolted onto the early church, and spent years trying to prove specific verses were forged to prop it up -- and my hunch, which I'd love the dialogue to explore, is that this isn't incidental to the physics dispute but upstream of it: if God's dominion is the dominion of one single ruling person rather than a triune godhead, you need Him to have a literal, absolute domain to actually rule over, which is what space is quietly doing for Newton metaphysically. Leibniz has his own theology in the game too -- pre-established harmony, best of all possible worlds, a God who wouldn't need to fix anything after the fact because he foresaw everything down to the last detail. I don't want either man announcing this outright like he's giving a lecture, but I'd like it humming underneath what they actually say to each other, and I wouldn't mind a bit of the old personal animosity leaking in as well, given these two had already spent a decade calling each other plagiarists over calculus. Could you write it as a straight two-hander dialogue, no stage directions, maybe a page or two, where both of them are landing real hits and neither one is just standing there to lose the argument?
