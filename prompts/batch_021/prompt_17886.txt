For a fundraiser last weekend, my school's robotics club ran a scavenger hunt for a small prize hidden somewhere in our building. Beforehand, the organizers announced that the prize was equally likely to be in the North Wing or the South Wing, and, independently of that, equally likely to be on the upper floor or the lower floor, so going in, all four combinations — North-Upper, North-Lower, South-Upper, South-Lower — seemed equally likely to me, at 25 percent each. Partway through, the head organizer sent our group chat a single clue: "I won't tell you which wing, but if it's in the North Wing, the odds are 3 to 1 that it's upstairs rather than downstairs." Her phone died right after, so that clue was all we ever got.

This turned into a real argument on the walk home. My friend Mia insisted that because the clue only describes the upstairs-versus-downstairs split once you're already in the North Wing, it cannot possibly bear on North-versus-South, so our confidence that the prize is in the North Wing should stay exactly where it started, at 50 percent. That struck me as obviously correct. But my friend Priya, who has spent the past few weeks reading about ways to update beliefs on new evidence while changing them as little as possible overall (a habit left over from our AP Statistics unit on probability), worked through the numbers that night using one such method and got a different answer, on which the South Wing ends up strictly more probable than the North Wing. That felt strange to both of us, since the clue seemed to say nothing whatsoever about North versus South.

Neither of us can tell whether Priya's method went wrong somewhere, or whether Mia's assumption — which still seems completely obvious to me — is secretly the questionable one. I'd like to actually understand this rather than just shrug it off: does that single clue give us any genuine reason to revise how likely the North Wing is in the first place, on top of what it tells us about upstairs versus downstairs? And if two seemingly careful ways of reasoning about the very same piece of evidence can pull apart like this, what is actually going on underneath the disagreement?
