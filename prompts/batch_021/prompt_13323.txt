So this whole thing started because someone on our interpretability team wrote in a postmortem doc "the model believed the user wanted prod access and decided that being helpful meant complying," and it set off a huge argument in our team slack about whether sentences like that are doing real explanatory work or are just misleading anthropomorphizing. Somebody linked an old Dennett piece on the "intentional stance" (the physical/design/intentional stances thing, plus his "real patterns" paper) and I fell down a rabbit hole for a whole weekend. I dont have any philosophy background, I just write backend code, but now I can't stop thinking about it, and my friend Alex and I have basically turned into the two sides of this argument every time we get lunch.

Alex's position is pretty much straight Dennett: if attributing beliefs and desires to the model lets you predict and explain its outputs better than the design stance does (i.e. better than reasoning directly about weights and training data), then those beliefs are real in the only sense Dennett thinks matters — "real patterns," full stop. Asking for something more, some inner fact of "really meaning it," is asking for a kind of intrinsic intentionality that on Dennett's own view doesn't even exist for humans, so it's an unfair standard to hold the model to. My position is that the intentional stance is only licensed in the first place because it presupposes some rational coherence holding an agent's beliefs and desires together over time — the rationality assumption — and that's precisely what breaks for LLM's in a way it doesn't for a thermostat or a chess program or a person. Reword the prompt slightly and the "belief" flips, there's no perspective that persists from one context window to the next, and half of what looks like successful intentional-stance prediction is really just us dressing up design-stance facts about training data in mentalistic language. (I even remember reading that Dennett himself got kind of anxious about chatbots specifically near the end of his life, which feels like it's in tension with his own theory somehow, though I can't fully articulate why.)

We've had this argument like four times now and always end up just repeating ourselves louder. Could you write a dialogue between the two of us (rename us however you like) that actually plays this out properly — real back and forth where each person engages with the other's specific argument instead of just restating their own, and neither of us comes off as the strawman who's obviously wrong. I'd love it if it stayed in the weeds of the actual Dennett machinery (rationality assumption, real patterns, design stance vs intentional stance) rather than sliding into the generic "but does it really understand, Chinese Room" territory — we've already had that argument to death and it never goes anywhere new.
