I'm a novelist, and like most people in my trade I've become an unashamed scavenger of real arguments for dialogue: nothing invented from scratch has quite the rhythm of two people who actually believe what they're saying. At a dinner a few weeks ago, two old friends of mine got drawn into a genuinely good argument after one of them mentioned a small news story: a nearby town council had revoked the licence of a private members' club that hosted legal, consensual BDSM evenings for its adult members, on the grounds that it offended the character of the community, even though no one outside the club was ever involved and the only complaints came from neighbours who had merely heard about it. I want to transplant a version of the exchange into my novel-in-progress, which follows a group of scientists wintering at a remote Antarctic research station -- about as far from a small English council chamber as it is possible to get -- so I need to understand the actual logical anatomy of the argument well enough to keep it intact once all the surface details change.

The exchange went, in paraphrase, roughly like this. (1) Marion said the council was right: a town is not simply a collection of people who happen to share a postcode; it holds together because of a shared sense of what is decent, and if the council shrugs at whatever consenting adults get up to in private, that shared sense erodes until there is nothing distinctive left holding the place together. (2) Callum asked where the evidence was for any of that -- nobody outside the club even knew about it until the story broke, so what, exactly, had been put at risk? (3) Marion answered that you cannot wait around for measurable damage before protecting something as fragile as a shared moral order; by the time the erosion shows up in any statistic, it is already too late to reverse it. (4) Callum replied that this makes the claim unfalsifiable -- any case where nothing visibly goes wrong just becomes 'the damage hasn't shown up yet' -- and asked Marion to name one actual test for when a practice crosses the line, rather than a hunch dressed up as principle. (5) Marion proposed one: ask ordinary, reasonable people how they would react on learning plainly what went on there; if their honest response is real revulsion, not mere unfamiliarity, that response is itself evidence a line has been crossed. (6) Callum pointed out that ordinary, reasonable people also felt real revulsion toward interracial couples, and toward gay men simply existing, within living memory, so a test built on communal disgust automatically ratifies whatever prejudice happens to be fashionable, and calls it protecting the moral order. (7) Marion countered that there is a difference between hating a class of people and being repelled by a specific practice pursued for its own sake; nobody wants the club's members run out of town, and the council merely declined to lend its name to that one activity. (8) Callum responded that the distinction was doing all the real work in Marion's argument, and that he still had not been given any principled way to draw it that did not simply amount to: reactions Marion finds respectable count as disapproval of an act, and reactions she would now be embarrassed by get relabelled prejudice against people.

Neither of them gave an inch, and when I read my notes back afterward I found I could not honestly referee it myself, which is exactly the problem: I don't want to hand either character an unearned victory when I write the scene. Could you go through the exchange move by move, rather than simply declaring an overall winner, and say who has the better of it at each numbered point, including any point you think is a genuine draw? I would also value your view on whether there is some further consideration that neither Marion nor Callum raised but that ought to be doing real work in a disagreement of this kind, since that is exactly the sort of thing I would want a sharper third character to bring up.
