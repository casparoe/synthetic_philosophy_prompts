I was recently appointed parliamentarian for my university's faculty senate, a job that suddenly became far less ceremonial after our last chair election ended in a three-way tie that nobody could agree how to break. While researching how other bodies have handled this kind of deadlock, I came across the following passage from Charles Dodgson's (Lewis Carroll's) 1874 pamphlet "Suggestions as to the Best Method of Taking Votes, Where More than Two Issues Are to Be Voted On," written for the same kind of Oxford college committee elections as his better-known 1873 pamphlet on voting procedure:

"If no settlement has been arrived at by § 3 or § 4, it will at least prove that the matter is one on which the meeting is very evenly divided in opinion. Such a state of things is of course very difficult to deal with, but the difficulty, though possibly not diminished, will certainly not have been increased by adopting the process I have here suggested."

(Sections 3 and 4, which precede this remark, lay out his procedure for finding a candidate who beats every other candidate head-to-head through a sequence of pairwise votes.) I can't decide whether Dodgson is offering a modest, honest disclaimer here or quietly conceding that his procedure doesn't actually solve the problem it was designed for. Could you help me pin down exactly what he is and isn't claiming, and then help me place the remark alongside Arrow's impossibility theorem, which came more than seventy years later? I'm especially curious whether Arrow's result should be read as a generalization of the very difficulty Dodgson is gesturing at, or as making a substantively different kind of point.
