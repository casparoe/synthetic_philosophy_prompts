My younger brother is getting married next month, and I've just been asked to give the best man's toast. He and his fiancée are both finishing philosophy PhDs, and his dissertation chapter on Kripke's exposition of Wittgenstein's rule-following paradox has become a running joke between them ever since he tried to explain it to her on their first date: the worry that no finite stretch of past behavior can ever fix which rule -- "plus" rather than some deviant "quus"-like variant -- a person has actually been following, so that nothing guarantees a rule applied fifty years from now still means what it seemed to mean today. Roughly a third of the guests are philosophers who will catch the reference instantly; the rest are family and old friends who have never heard of Kripke. I'd like the toast to open with a joke built on the paradox itself -- how can anyone be sure that "till death do us part," invoked decades from now in some unforeseeable circumstance, will express the same commitment being made tonight? -- and then resolve it, sincerely, using Kripke's Wittgenstein's "skeptical solution," which locates the fact of correct rule-following not in any private mental state but in a community's ongoing practice of mutual agreement and correction. Please write this five-minute toast so that the philosophical turn genuinely reassures the couple rather than merely sounding clever, and so that it lands as warmly for the relatives who won't recognize a single name as for the philosophers who will.
