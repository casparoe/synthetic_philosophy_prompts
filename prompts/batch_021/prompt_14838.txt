I'm reporting a feature on the philosophical arguments that keep surfacing in the debate over "humane," pasture-raised meat, and while reading around the topic I kept snagging on an unresolved wrinkle in Robert Nozick's Anarchy, State, and Utopia. In the same stretch of pages where he uses the "utility monster" to argue that unrestricted utilitarianism fails to respect the separateness of persons and so must be constrained by Kantian side constraints -- rights that can't be traded off for aggregate gain -- Nozick turns to ask whether animals deserve anything like the same protection. He tentatively floats a two-tier rule of thumb, quoted ever since as "utilitarianism for animals, Kantianism for people" -- and then almost immediately worries about it out loud, asking whether a purely aggregative, welfare-based standard for animals wouldn't imply that painlessly killing one and replacing it with an equally contented animal is a wash, or even a net gain. He never really settles the question.

I recently watched two philosophers hash this out after a talk, and I haven't been able to stop turning their exchange over since. (1) Devra, a food-ethics researcher defending pasture-raised meat on Nozickian grounds, argued that since Nozick never actually retracts the two-tier proposal or extends side constraints to animals, a rancher who raises contented animals, kills them painlessly, and replaces each with another equally contented animal satisfies the letter of his own framework -- unease voiced in passing isn't a counterargument. (2) Callum, a Nozick specialist, replied that this mistakes a reductio for an endorsement: Nozick raises the replacement implication precisely because it strikes him, by his own lights, as a cost of the view, exactly as the utility monster is a cost of unrestricted utilitarianism -- and since his whole method in that chapter is to reject views whose implications we recoil from, consistency requires treating the two cases alike rather than quietly exempting the animal case. (3) Devra shot back that recoiling from an implication doesn't show it's false, and that nowhere does Nozick, or anyone since, specify the missing constraint that would actually block replaceability without just smuggling full personhood back in for animals. (4) Callum answered that the burden runs the other way: it's the asymmetry itself -- taking the intuition seriously when it protects persons from the utility monster but waving it off when it would protect livestock -- that needs an argument, and nobody at the talk, Nozick included, ever gave one.

Both of them left convinced they'd won. Could you go through the exchange move by move -- (1) through (4) -- and say who has the better of it at each step, not just overall, and note whether either side is smuggling in an assumption the other hasn't granted, or whether Nozick's text simply underdetermines the question they're fighting over?
