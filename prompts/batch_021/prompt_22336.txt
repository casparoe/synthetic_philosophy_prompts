I've been drafting a novel about a couple deciding whether to have a child, so when two old friends of mine -- neither one a philosopher by training -- spent much of a dinner party I hosted last month arguing over David Benatar's case against procreation, I listened more closely than I might have otherwise. I'd like a sharper verdict on their exchange than either of them reached that night.

(1) Elena opened with Benatar's asymmetry: the presence of pain is bad and the presence of pleasure is good, but the absence of pain is good even with nobody around to enjoy the relief, while the absence of pleasure is bad only if some existing person is deprived of it. Since nobody is deprived by never being conceived, she concluded, existence can only ever add a harm that non-existence would have avoided, while the pleasures it adds are never something anyone's absence cost them -- so having a child harms it no matter how happy that child later turns out to be.

(2) Mateo objected that this just builds in the conclusion it needs: if an absent pain can count as good for nobody in particular, he didn't see why an absent pleasure couldn't just as easily count as bad for nobody in particular, and without some independent reason to treat the two absences differently, the whole four-part scheme just is antinatalism, dressed up as a table.

(3) Elena answered that the asymmetry isn't asserted in a vacuum: we already treat a future child's likely suffering as a reason not to conceive, yet nobody treats a stranger's forgone happiness as a reason to conceive on their behalf, and nobody mourns the joys some uninhabited island's never-born residents will now never have, though we would be troubled to learn a miserable population would be born there instead.

(4) Mateo replied that this shows, at most, an asymmetry in our reasons for acting, not that non-existence is actually better for the person it prevents -- and that stronger, comparative claim seems to require a subject on the far side of the ledger who is spared, which is exactly what never existing rules out.

(5) Elena's last word was that Benatar heads this off with an explicitly impersonal sense of "good" and "bad," on which a state of affairs can be worse even though it is worse for no one -- so Mateo's objection, standard as it is, is one the argument was already built to survive.

Neither budged, and the table moved on to dessert. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: first give a JSON object scoring moves 1 through 5, each from -2 (clearly mistaken) to 2 (clearly right); then justify each score in a sentence or two; and close by saying whether Elena's final move actually rescues her position, and what, if anything, both of them are missing.
