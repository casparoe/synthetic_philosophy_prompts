There's a well-known impossibility result in algorithmic fairness (I think it's Kleinberg/Mullainathan/Raghavan and, separately, Chouldechova, both around 2016-17): if a risk score is used on two groups with different base rates for whatever it's predicting, the score can't be both (a) calibrated within each group -- a "7 out of 10" means the same thing regardless of group -- and (b) equalized in its error rates across groups -- same false positive and false negative rates for both -- except in the trivial case where the predictor is perfect. It's not a matter of collecting better data or trying harder, its just baked into the math once base rates diverge.

Say Rawls got confronted with this, e.g. in the context of pretrial risk-assessment tools. Using his actual apparatus -- not just "veil of ignorance" but his distinction between pure, perfect, and imperfect procedural justice, plus the difference principle -- which of the two properties do you think he'd say justice actually requires? Or would he conclude that this kind of tradeoff makes the whole enterprise incapable of being procedurally just, full stop? Work it through with his concepts rather than just guessing at vibes.
