I spent about thirty five years doing geochemistry and paleoclimate reconstruction before I retired, and only in the last year or so have I started reading philosophy seriously, mostly because a friend from my old journal club keeps forwarding me epistemology papers. The one that's stuck with me is the "problem of easy knowledge" stuff, Cohen's name is attached to it I believe, and the whole bootstrapping literature that goes with it (there's some classic example involving a gas gauge that keeps confirming itself, Vogel maybe, don't quote me on that, my citations in this field are still shaky). The basic worry, that you can seemingly certify a source as reliable just by using that very source over and over and noting how self-consistent it is, which feels like getting knowledge on the cheap, hit me because I realized I've been having exactly this argument for two years on a data-standards working group I still sit on, the one that maintains a shared proxy-temperature database several modelling groups use to benchmark climate simulations of past warm periods.

Here's the live version of it. A group wants a new biomarker proxy admitted to the database. There's no independent calibration against an actual thermometer, because obviously nobody has a thermometer they can dunk into a 45 million year old ocean, so the whole justification on offer is that its tightly self-consistent across replicate runs and that it tracks two or three of our existing proxies (things kind of like Mg/Ca or TEX86, just to give you a flavor) across several time intervals. The catch, which I only had the vocabulary for after doing this reading, is that those existing proxies were themselves partly calibrated on overlapping modern core-top data and share some of the same blind spots, past seawater chemistry and salinity mainly, so tight agreement between them might just be correlated error dressed up as confirmation rather than anything independent. Roughly half the working group thinks demanding fully independent validation is a fantasy for any historical science and that convergence between differently-flawed methods has to count for something; the other half of us thinks we're laundering circularity into confidence and ought to just say so plainly in the documentation.

We reconvene in three weeks and I've been asked to circulate a short recommendation memo beforehand. Could you draft it for me? I want an actual recommendation in it, admit as-is, admit with specific flags or caveats, hold pending some named further check, whatever you conclude is right, attached to a rough timeframe, not just a tour of the considerations. And I'd really like you to take the "convergence has to count for something" side seriously, and if there's a genuinely strong principled version of it rather than just "independent validation is too much to ask for," try to articulate that version properly, since it's the argument I keep losing to in meetings and I'd rather understand it than just out-vote it.
