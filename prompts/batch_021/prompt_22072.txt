I'm a philosophy PhD student (formal epistemology-ish, though I hang out a lot with the econ theory crowd), and something from our joint decision-theory reading group last week is still bugging me. We'd gone through the Allais paradox stuff -- the classic setup where you first choose between (A) $1M for sure and (B) 89% $1M / 10% $5M / 1% nothing, then seperately choose between (C) 11% $1M / 89% nothing and (D) 10% $5M / 90% nothing. Almost everyone in the room, me included, ended up picking A and D, which is the usual finding and exactly the pattern that violates the independence axiom.

Afterward two people in my cohort, Marco and Noor, ended up arguing about it for a good twenty minutes after everyone else had left. Roughly:
(1) Marco said picking A-and-D is just irrational: A and B agree on giving you $1M on 89% of the underlying probability, and C and D agree on giving you nothing on that same 89% -- it's literally the identical shared chunk of probability in both problems, just relabeled. Letting a shared, non-differing chunk like that flip your ranking of the remaining 11% is exactly what the independence axiom is designed to rule out.
(2) Noor said that's assuming what's in dispute. Allais cooked up this exact case in 1953 specifically to argue that independence *isn't* a rationality requirement, and he thought both "common" choices could be perfectly reasonable -- caring about whether risk comes packaged as pure certainty versus two live gambles isn't obviously less rational than caring only about outcomes' marginal odds.
(3) Marco came back with the Savage story: Savage was given this very puzzle by Allais, felt the same certainty-loving pull himself, and after rebuilding both problems out of one shared 100-ticket lottery, decided on reflection that his own first answer had been an error, not the axiom. If careful reflection pushes people back toward independence, that's evidence it's the real constraint.
(4) Noor said Savage reflecting his way back to a theory he already believed in isn't independent evidence for it -- Allais reflected just as hard and landed the opposite way. And as far as she knows nobody's ever shown you can money-pump a one-shot Allais-consistent agent without sneaking in extra assumptions like reduction of compound lotteries or dynamic consistency across different decision trees, which non-expected-utility theories (rank-dependent utility, etc.) are free to just reject.

Both of them seemed to be landing genuinely good points and I honestly cant referee it myself. Could you go through moves (1)-(4) and say who's more right at each individual step, not just who you'd side with overall, and then flag anything you think both of them are missing?
