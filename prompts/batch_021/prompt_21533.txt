I write about probability, forecasting, and uncertainty for a science publication, and the idea for this episode came out of a newsroom argument about whether our own election-forecast and pandemic-projection graphics are dishonest when they compress deep, genuine uncertainty into one headline percentage. I want the episode itself to go upstream of that news hook, though, and into the philosophy behind it. My guest is a decision theorist who has spent much of her career defending "imprecise credences": the view that a rational agent's degree of belief, especially when evidence is sparse or genuinely ambiguous, should be represented not by a single sharp number but by a whole set of probability functions -- in effect a range ("somewhere between 0.3 and 0.6") rather than a point ("0.45"). She likes to motivate the view with Ellsberg-style cases, where people's reluctance to bet on an urn with an unknown mix of colored balls seems to track something a single number can't express, and whenever she's pressed on the technical costs of the view she tends to answer that any resulting messiness in decision-making is "the model being honest about the evidence, not a defect in the model."

Please draft eight to ten interview questions that don't just let her re-run those motivating cases, which I already understand well. I want at least one question built around the dilation result -- the finding, going back to Seidenfeld and Wasserman, that conditioning an imprecise credal state on new evidence can sometimes widen the range rather than narrow it, in apparent tension with the ordinary thought that learning more should leave you more certain, not less. I want at least one question built around the Elga-style sequential-choice objection: that an agent with genuinely imprecise credences seems to need some extra, unexplained tie-breaking procedure to choose between two bets at all, which threatens to smuggle sharp probabilities back in through the side door exactly when a decision has to be made. And I want at least one question pressing the regress worry associated with Roger White: if imprecision is the epistemically honest response to ambiguous evidence, why should the sharp edges of the interval itself be exempt from that same argument, rather than getting smeared out in turn. For each of these three, add a short, pointed follow-up keyed to the deflection she's most likely to reach for, so that by the end of the segment she's either had to concede real ground or said something quotable in refusing to.
