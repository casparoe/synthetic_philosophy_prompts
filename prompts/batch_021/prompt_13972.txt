Since retiring from a career in experimental physics three years ago, I have been auditing philosophy seminars at the local university, and I currently find myself absorbed in the literature on collective intentionality and joint action. I have taken to approaching the material the way I used to approach two competing hypotheses in the laboratory: by looking for the crucial experiment, the one case where theories that agree almost everywhere else are forced to yield different verdicts.

Two accounts I have been turning over in this spirit are Michael Bratman's planning-theoretic analysis of shared intention, on which what makes an activity ours is, roughly, that each participant intends that we perform it in accordance with and because of meshing sub-plans, together with a suitable structure of mutual responsiveness in intention and action, and Margaret Gilbert's plural subject account, on which what instead makes an activity genuinely joint is a joint commitment of the parties to uphold some goal or activity as a body, a commitment that, unlike an ordinary personal intention, generates directed obligations and standing to rebuke a defaulting partner, and that cannot be rescinded except by the agreement of everyone involved. On the textbook cases, two people painting a house together, or carrying a table, or simply walking down the street side by side, the two theories seem to agree, which is precisely what makes them so hard to tell apart.

What I would like is your help designing a genuine discriminating case: a single scenario, involving two or more agents, and preferably not one of the stock examples just mentioned, that a convinced Bratmanian would have to classify as a full-fledged instance of shared intentional activity while a convinced Gilbertian would have to deny is a joint action at all, or, if the reverse direction produces a cleaner case, a scenario that works the other way around. I would also be grateful if you could state explicitly, condition by condition, why each theory delivers the verdict that it does, since that bookkeeping is precisely what I am finding difficult to do on my own.
