The idea, as he explained it, is that the genuinely interesting question isn't the hard problem itself (why does physical processing feel like anything at all) but why we even have the intuition that there's a hard problem in the first place -- so instead of explaining experience, you're explaining the judgments and reactions: that experience seems ineffable, intrinsic, resistant to being captured by any functional description, etc. He said this reframing is supposed to be neutral ground that both hard-problem realists and people who think consciousness is some kind of introspective illusion (he mentioned an "illusionist" camp, Frankish maybe?) can sign onto, since everyone needs to explain why we talk and think this way regardless of who's actually right. But then he went further and claimed it secretly favors the illusionists: once the judgments are fully explained in ordinary cognitive/computational terms, there's nothing left over for the realist to point to. I said that sounds like a bait-and-switch, you swap the real problem for an easier one and then act like you've made progress on the original, and he said no, that IS the point, that's exactly what makes it dialectically dangerous for the realist. We argued about this for ages and neither of us could actually nail down why solving the meta-problem would, or wouldn't, leave the hard problem intact.
