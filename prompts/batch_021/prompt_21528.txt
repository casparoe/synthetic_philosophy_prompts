I'm a retired geologist -- forty years mapping rocks -- and now that I've got time on my hands I've been going to a little philosophy discussion group at the senior center, mostly epistemology stuff I never got exposed to in school. Last month somebody brought up something called "the dogmatism paradox," which I gather goes back to a Kripke lecture from the 60s and got written up properly by a philosopher named Harman. The argument goes something like this: if I actually know some claim h is true, then I already know that any future evidence against h has to be misleading, since h is true and evidence pointing away from a truth can't be pointing toward the truth. And if I know in advance that such evidence would be misleading, I'm entitled to just disregard it whenever it turns up, rather then waste time re-examining something I've already got figured out. So knowing something seems to rationally license you to stop being open to counter-evidence about it, which as far as I can tell is a real, valid-looking argument, not just a rhetorical trick.

This hit close to home, because I spent the first half of my career watching senior colleagues wave off the accumulating evidence for continental drift -- matching coastlines, the fossil correspondences, eventually the paleomagnetic striping on the seafloor -- because they were so confident the crust simply didn't move that way. It wasn't until the plate tectonics work of the 60s that the holdouts really had no ground left to stand on. So here's what I keep chewing on: given the dogmatism paradox, weren't those geologists actually being rational to discount Wegener's evidence, since they "knew" the recieved view was correct? And more generally, if a valid argument shows that knowledge licenses ignoring future counter-evidence, why do scientists and philosophers of science still treat open-mindedness as some kind of virtue instead of a beginner's mistake? Curious whether I'm missing something in the argument, or whether this is just one of those places where good epistemology and good scientific practice part ways.
