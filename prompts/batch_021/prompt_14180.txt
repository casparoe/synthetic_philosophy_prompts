hey so for my early modern phil seminar we've been working through Leibniz's Discourse on Metaphysics and i'm kinda stuck on section 30, the bit about Judas and free will. this is the passage we got assigned:

"Someone might say that God was not obliged to permit Judas to sin, and that he could have chosen another sequence of things, conformable to the other possible plans of the universe. But we must consider that such a decree would already be at odds with the plan actually chosen by God, in which it was foreseen that this very Judas would freely, though most certainly, commit this sin. We must not, then, ask why Judas the sinner exists, since it is contained for all eternity in the notion of him that he freely commits this crime; the only legitimate question is why this Judas, the sinner, is admitted into existence in preference to other possible persons. And to this we can only answer that it agrees with the greatest perfection of the universe, as God has chosen it; that we owe everything to his grace when we act well; and yet that we have no excuse when we fail in our duty, since God is in no way the author of sin, however certain the connection between this man and this action may have been from all eternity."

i get that Leibniz is trying to make human freedom compatible with the fact that everything about a person, including their sins, is already baked into their "complete concept" from eternity, but i don't really follow the move he makes where he says we shouldn't ask why Judas sins (since thats just built into his notion already) and instead ask why THIS particular Judas -- the sinning one -- got chosen to exist over all the other possible people God could've created instead. isnt that basically just the same question with extra steps? also whats the actual difference between something being "certain" and being "absolutely/metaphysically necessary" here -- is that distinction doing real philosophical work to save free will and responsibility, or is it more of a semantic dodge that leaves him a determinist in all but name? can you walk me through what argument is actually happening in this passage and then tell me whether you think it succeeds in preserving Judas's freedom and his being justly blameable.
