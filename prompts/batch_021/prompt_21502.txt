The question set was:

"In Politics I.8–10, Aristotle distinguishes 'natural' wealth-getting bound up with household management (oikonomike) from the 'unnatural' art of chrematistike, of which usury is the extreme case. In Nicomachean Ethics V.5, he treats money (nomisma) as what makes reciprocal exchange possible between otherwise incommensurable goods. Marx, commenting on this second passage in Capital, credits Aristotle with being the first to see that commodity exchange presupposes some real equality between the things exchanged, but argues that Aristotle could not complete the analysis. Explain (a) why Aristotle judges usury the most unnatural form of wealth-getting, (b) what work money is doing in his account of reciprocal justice, and (c) why, on Marx's diagnosis, Aristotle was unable to say what actually makes commodities commensurable."

Here is the student's answer, verbatim:

"(a) Aristotle thinks usury (tokos) is the worst kind of chrematistike because it makes money itself the source of more money, rather than letting money serve its proper function of facilitating the exchange of goods people actually need. Farming or animal husbandry produces a genuine natural increase, because living things grow and reproduce, but a sum of coins has no such power, so lending at interest treats something sterile as though it were fertile. This is also why he calls interest tokos, 'offspring': the word names exactly the perversity of money apparently giving birth to more money. Since money's whole point is to serve exchange rather than to be pursued as an end in itself, using it to generate more of itself is the clearest case of mistaking a means for an end, and so the most unnatural of all ways of getting wealth.

(b) The money passage in Book V belongs to Aristotle's account of distributive justice. Just as distributive justice apportions common goods among citizens in geometric proportion to their merit, exchange between a builder and a shoemaker has to preserve a proportion between what each contributes -- so many pairs of shoes have to be 'worth' one house. Since a house and a pair of shoes have nothing obviously in common, Aristotle introduces money as the conventional measure that lets us equate them, so that both parties end up with an equal share and neither is exploited by the other.

(c) Marx says Aristotle deserves credit for seeing that the exchange of two commodities presupposes some underlying equality between them, but that he could not complete the argument. On Marx's account, this is because Greek mathematics and commercial practice had not yet developed the tools needed to actually compute a common value across qualitatively different goods, so Aristotle was left treating the equation of a house with so many shoes as a rough practical convenience rather than as evidence of a real common substance underlying all commodities."
