During a session of my department's reading group on Frege's Nachlass, we discussed the political diary he kept in 1924 -- discovered only decades after his death, full of antisemitic and anti-democratic invective, and something even devoted Frege scholars have had to publicly reckon with. Two people in the group kept arguing about it afterward over coffee.

(1) Nadia said the diary is simply irrelevant to assessing Frege's philosophy: the logicism, the sense/reference distinction, and the attack on psychologism stand or fall on their logical merits alone, and treating an author's odious politics as evidence against his arguments is a textbook genetic fallacy.

(2) Ravi said that's not the issue: no one is using the diary to refute the sense/reference distinction. The real question is interpretive -- Frege's claim that logic describes an objective "third realm" of thoughts, graspable only by suitably rational minds, and his insistence that concepts need perfectly sharp boundaries, read differently once you know he thought whole classes of people unfit for rational public life. That doesn't touch the logic's validity, but it changes what the objectivity-talk was doing for him.

(3) Nadia answered that this is still too cheap: plenty of philosophers who prize objective truth and sharp concepts have had impeccable liberal politics, so unless Ravi can show the elitism specifically flows from Frege's logical doctrines, rather than being an unrelated prejudice of a 1920s German conservative, he's only shown that one bigot also happened to do logic.

(4) Ravi replied that the diary supplies exactly that link: in it, Frege doesn't compartmentalize but reasons his way to disenfranchisement with the same fastidious, all-or-nothing style he brings to Grundgesetze -- one intellectual temperament at work in both places, not two unconnected facts about the same man.
