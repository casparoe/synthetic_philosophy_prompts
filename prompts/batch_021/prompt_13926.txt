Got into an argument about this on the ACX subreddit a while back and never got a satisfying answer, so hoping you can settle it. I've been rereading the Sequences and Yudkowsky's "conservation of expected evidence" post is one of those results everyone on LW treats as obviously true and kind of boring: if you can already predict which direction you'll update your credence in, you should just update now, since your expected future credence has to equal your current one -- that's basically just the law of total probability. Separately I've been poking around in formal epistemology (SEP rabbit hole, as always) and ran into van Fraassen's Reflection Principle, which says the same thing as far as I can tell: if your certain what your credence in some claim will be at a later time, your credence now should already equal that. He even backs it with an explicit Dutch book argument -- if you don't conform, a bookie can set up bets now and later that guarantee you a loss regardless of outcome.

Except in that literature this is apparently a live, heavily contested principle, not some settled one-paragraph result. There's a whole back and forth of counterexamples (something about anticipating you'll be drunk, senile, or otherwise less reliable later, and I think a positive case involving a guru you know will end up being right), plus decades of people -- Christensen, maybe Talbott, don't remember exactly who -- proposing increasingly fiddly restrictions on when you're actually supposed to defer to your future self like that. If it's the same underlying mathematical result, why does the philosophy side treat this as such an open problem requiring decades of patches, when LW apparently nailed the whole thing in one moderately short blog post? Am I missing some real technical difference between the two principles, or is this just the same idea getting independently reinvented by two communities that don't read each other, with the philosophers being unnecessarily precious about it?
