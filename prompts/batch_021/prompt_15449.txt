My little sister is graduating in a few weeks with her BA in philosophy, and she's asked me to say a few words at the small department reception after the ceremony -- not the big university-wide commencement, just the maybe 40 people who'll be there: her cohort, a handful of faculty including her thesis advisor, and our extended family who mostly think philosophy means sitting around asking "but what IS truth, really." I'm a few years ahead of her in a psych PhD, actually studying meaning in life empirically (purpose, goals, eudaimonic well-being, the whole Frankl/Steger-adjacent toolkit), so everyone's assumed I'm the natural pick to toast a philosophy grad. Except there's a wrinkle: her senior thesis was on Moritz Schlick's essay "On the Meaning of Life" (1927ish, I might be off by a year), the one where he argues that most of what we call a meaningful life -- work hard now so you can have money/security/status later -- is actually just an empty means-end chain, since a chain of things done only for the sake of the next thing never actually cashes out in anything valuable in itself; real meaning, he thinks, shows up in play, in activity you do simply because doing it is good, and this "youthfulness of spirit" is something most adults lose rather than something only literal kids have. (Weird bonus fact I want to say is true but am fuzzy on the specifics of: I think he got shot by a former student on the actual steps of the University of Vienna about a decade after writing this, which is definately not toast material but does make his optimism about play hit different.)

She has told me, in no uncertain terms, that if I get up there and give the standard "you worked so hard and now its paying off, go chase your dreams/purpose" graduation speech, she will be quietly furious, because that's exactly the hollow means-end picture Schlick spent the essay taking apart -- and it also happens to be the exact move my own subfield makes constantly (psychologists love nothing more than telling people to go find their Purpose with a capital P). So: can you write the toast? It needs to be genuinely warm, and a little funny, because there will be a grandma and at least two aunts in that room who need to feel moved, not lectured at -- but it actually has to hold on to Schlick's specific, slightly weird view instead of flattening it into generic "enjoy the ride" advice, and it can't just sneak the achievement framing back in through the side door once the philosophers in the room stop paying attention. Three to four minutes, spoken out loud, please.
