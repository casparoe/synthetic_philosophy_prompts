The question set was: "(a) Explain, in your own words, how Boltzmann's statistical-mechanical account of entropy is meant to underwrite the thermodynamic arrow of time, and why the account needs supplementing with a 'Past Hypothesis' about the early universe. (b) State the Loschmidt reversibility objection to this account, and explain how a defender of Boltzmann can respond to it. (c) Huw Price and others have argued that, followed through consistently, Boltzmann's own statistical reasoning suggests entropy should be found increasing away from any given moment in both temporal directions, rather than climbing steadily only toward our future — making a low-entropy 'now' look more like the bottom of a statistical dip than a foothill on a single uphill climb from the past. Briefly evaluate this argument and say what, if anything, it implies about the explanatory status of the Past Hypothesis."

Here is the answer I have to mark:

(a) Boltzmann's move was to stop treating entropy as a primitive thermodynamic quantity and instead define it statistically, as S = k log W, where W is the number of microstates compatible with a given macrostate. A macrostate like "gas spread evenly through the container" corresponds to vastly more microscopic arrangements of position and momentum than a macrostate like "gas bunched in one corner," simply because there are so many more ways of realising the former. So a system that starts out in a low-entropy macrostate is overwhelmingly likely to have a microstate that evolves, under the ordinary time-symmetric laws of mechanics, toward a high-entropy macrostate, just because high-entropy macrostates occupy so much more of the available phase space. That delivers entropy increase without needing a fundamentally time-asymmetric law anywhere in the dynamics. Since this reasoning is symmetric in time, running it back to the early universe is really just the same statistical argument applied on a cosmic scale: it tells us that entropy must have started out especially low and has been climbing ever since, which is exactly the content of the "Past Hypothesis." Without some such assumption we would have no explanation of why entropy is so much lower now than it could in principle be.

(b) Loschmidt's reversibility objection notes that the microscopic dynamics are time-reversal invariant, so for every phase-space trajectory that takes a low-entropy macrostate to a high-entropy one, there is an equally lawful "time-reversed" trajectory, obtained by flipping every particle's velocity, that takes a high-entropy macrostate down to a low-entropy one. If the counting argument in (a) is supposed to show that entropy increase is overwhelmingly probable, the identical argument applied to the reversed trajectories shows entropy decrease is just as probable, which threatens to drain the account of any real predictive content. The standard reply is that the objection only shows entropy is equally likely to increase or decrease looking out from a perfectly generic point in phase space; but we are never reasoning from a generic point, we are reasoning from a point already known to be low-entropy, and conditional on that, the overwhelming majority of compatible microstates evolve toward higher entropy going forward. The objection would only regain its force if we had equally good grounds for assuming a low-entropy macrostate in the future as we do in the past, and we don't.

(c) Price's argument is that if we take the statistical reasoning in (a) fully seriously on its own terms, a low-entropy state ought to be treated as a statistical fluctuation away from equilibrium, and fluctuations are symmetric: looking out from the bottom of a fluctuation, entropy should typically be found increasing in either temporal direction, not just one. So if we really trusted the statistics alone, without smuggling in any further assumption, we ought to predict that entropy was higher in the past as well as the future of any given low-entropy "now" — which is not what we observe, since entropy has apparently been climbing steadily for the entire history of the observable universe. So the Past Hypothesis can't actually be justified by Boltzmann's statistical mechanics at all; it has to be inserted as a further, independent assumption that the statistics alone would never deliver, and if anything sits in tension with. Since the Past Hypothesis is doing all the real explanatory work, and Boltzmann's statistical mechanics doesn't so much explain the arrow of time as presuppose it in a disguised form, I don't think the overall account makes any real progress in explaining the direction of time: it just relocates the mystery from "why does entropy increase" to "why was entropy especially low to begin with," which is no easier a question, so the account is close to circular.
