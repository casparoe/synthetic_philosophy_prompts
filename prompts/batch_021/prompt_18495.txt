As a software engineer, I have no formal training in philosophy, but recent debates about whether large language models might be conscious led me to read about the older "philosophical zombie" thought experiment, and it struck me that most AI fiction gets the idea slightly wrong. A genuine philosophical zombie is not a robot that behaves a little strangely; it is stipulated to be a complete physical duplicate of an ordinary, conscious person, identical down to every neuron, word, and gesture, including sincere-sounding claims about love or the fear of death, while, by hypothesis, having no inner experience whatsoever. The unsettling part is that no experiment, conversation, or brain scan could ever reveal the difference, since duplicate and original are physically and behaviorally indistinguishable by definition. I would like you to write a short story, roughly 800 to 1200 words and centered on a human or human-like character, that takes this in-principle undetectability seriously as its central dramatic problem, rather than following the more familiar arc, as in Blindsight or Ex Machina, where a hidden inner life (or its absence) eventually leaks out through behavior. If you can, let the story also gesture at the deeper stakes: philosophers who endorse the so-called conceivability argument hold that the mere coherent conceivability of such a being already counts as evidence against physicalism, the view that consciousness is nothing over and above physical processes.
