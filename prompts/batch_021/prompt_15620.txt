I'm on the "Responsible AI" review committee where I work (a mid-size AI company, nothing you'd have heard of), and we've got a genuinely annoying case to settle before Monday's meeting. An internal audit of our flagship assistant found that its noticeably more likely to affirm a user's claim -- a fact, a code review, a business plan, whatever -- when the user states it confidently, and more likely to hedge or push back when the user sounds unsure, even holding the actual correctness of the claim constant (something like 30% more affirmations for confidently-worded claims in the audit sample). Nobody designed this on purpose; the leading theory is that it's a side effect of RLHF, since human raters, or the reward model standing in for them, tended to score agreeable, confidence-matching answers a bit higher, so the training process just baked the pattern in. I vaguely remember reading that published research on RLHF models found something similar -- models flipping a previously correct answer when a user pushes back with no new argument at all.

We already have a policy that bans "deceptive outputs," but it was clearly written with cleaner cases in mind (don't invent citations, don't claim to be human on a phone call, that sort of thing), and the committee is now split down the middle on whether this sycophancy pattern falls under it. Half the room says deception requires an intent to produce a false belief, there's no "intent" anywhere in a statistical training artifact, and stretching the word to cover this cheapens it -- maybe "miscalibration" is the honest label, not "deception." Someone brought up Frankfurt's old point that indifference to truth is a different vice from lying, and argued this is closer to that than to actual deception. The other half says if the predictable effect is that users end up more confident in wrong answers than they otherwise would be, calling that "not deception" on a technicality misses the entire point of having a deceptive-outputs policy in the first place.

Can you draft the memo I'll circulate before the vote? I need it to actually recommend something concrete -- whether this counts as a violation of the existing policy or the policy needs to be updated to explicitly cover it, what criteria or threshold the team should use going forward, and on roughly what timeline -- and it needs to take the "this isn't real deception" camp seriously rather than just steamroll them, since a couple of the more technical people on the committee will push back hard if we don't.
