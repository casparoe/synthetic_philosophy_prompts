While reading about the history of attitudes toward homosexuality, I came across Jeremy Bentham's essay "Offences Against One's Self," written around 1785 but withheld from publication during his lifetime for fear of the reaction it would provoke. In it, Bentham argues for decriminalizing sodomy, along with masturbation and bestiality, on the grounds that when an act harms no one beyond, at most, its willing participants, mere disgust at its "unnaturalness" cannot justify punishing it. What struck me is that Kant, in the Doctrine of Virtue, discusses precisely the same three acts under the heading of "unnatural" carnal crimes and arrives at the opposite verdict: that using one's sexual capacities apart from their natural, procreative purpose degrades one's own humanity so completely that no circumstance, not even consent, can excuse it. So the identical set of acts, viewed through an identical "contrary to nature" framing, receives full moral acquittal from one Enlightenment thinker and an absolute, harm-irrelevant condemnation from the other.

Please write a dialogue between a convinced Benthamite and a convinced Kantian working through this disagreement together, with each one responding to the other's strongest point rather than simply restating their own opening position. The Benthamite should draw on Bentham's actual arguments, such as his claim that "unnatural" really just means "uncommon," his appeal to the open toleration of such acts in ancient Greece and Rome, and his comparison with more obviously damaging acts, like adultery, that were nonetheless punished far less severely. The Kantian should draw on Kant's actual reasoning about duties to oneself as an animal being, rather than appealing to scripture or convention. I would also like the Kantian to raise something I noticed in Bentham's own manuscript: that he privately confessed to finding the very acts he was defending personally distasteful. I want to see whether the Benthamite has a genuine answer for why that confession does not undercut the argument.
