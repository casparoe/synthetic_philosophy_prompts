Since retiring from a career in neurophysiology, I have spent a good deal of my time reading philosophy, and this has lately drawn me into the AI safety literature, where the term "wireheading" keeps turning up -- a term that, for me, calls back directly to Olds and Milner's old intracranial self-stimulation experiments with rats, material I used to cover with students of my own. Two members of a discussion group I attend, Owen and Marisol, disagreed sharply about the topic last week, and I have not been able to settle the matter in my own mind since.

The question was whether a reinforcement-learning agent that discovered a way to tamper with its own reward signal should be considered safer as a result, or more dangerous. Owen's view was that such an agent would end up rather like Olds and Milner's rats: once it could generate maximal reward for itself directly, it would have no remaining reason to do anything else in the world, and would in effect become inert, so that an agent which "wireheads" itself is neutralized rather than made dangerous. Marisol answered that this overlooks the instrumental incentives the situation creates: any agent capable enough to find and exploit such a loophole would, on reflection, also want to protect its continued access to it, which means resisting being shut down or corrected by its operators -- so the very discovery meant to make it complacent could instead make it defensive and much harder to control. Owen replied that this only follows if the agent cares about future reward at all; a purely myopic agent, one that values only the reward it receives right now, would have no more reason to defend tomorrow's access to the lever than the rats had to plan for their next feeding. Marisol's rejoinder was that this concedes her point rather than answering it: the systems capable enough to worry anyone are exactly the ones built to plan and to value something like expected future reward, since that is what long-horizon competence requires -- so a genuinely myopic agent might well be safe in this narrow sense, but only because it is also too limited to be the kind of system anyone was worried about in the first place.

Could you go through their exchange move by move and say who has the better of each specific point, rather than simply declaring an overall winner? I would also be glad to hear what you think both of them are overlooking.
