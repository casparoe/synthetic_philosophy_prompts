I spent about thirty-five years as a field ecologist -- ground beetles for most of my career, grassland birds near the end -- and since retiring I've fallen into philosophy the way some people fall into gardening. I audited a couple of intro courses at the local university and now run a little reading group out of the public library with three other retirees. Last month we were working through epistemology and hit the Gettier "justified true belief that somehow isn't knowledge" cases, and one of the group, an ex-lawyer, mentioned a study I hadn't come across before: Weinberg, Nichols and Stich, I think from 2001, where instead of just assuming everybody's intuitions about these cases line up, they actually ran the classic Gettier vignettes past different groups and found East Asian and South Asian participants, and lower-SES participants generally, alot less likely to share the "well obviously he doesn't really know that" reaction philosophers treat as basically self evident.

That sent me down a rabbit hole and I found a similar one myself: Machery and, I believe, some of the same coauthors, ran Kripke's old Gödel/Schmidt case -- the one about whether the name "Gödel" would still refer to Gödel even if it turned out some fellow named Schmidt actually proved incompleteness -- and got Western and Hong Kong Chinese participants splitting in opposite directions on a case Kripke pretty clearly wrote up assuming everyone would react the same way. As an old field biologist this is exactly the sort of thing that would've gotten a paper of mine sent back for revisions -- you don't get to sample one hillside of ground beetles and report it as a fact about the species -- and a good chunk of what I've read in philosophy so far seems to run on "well, doesn't it just obviously seem like..." with maybe a dozen undergraduates as the entire evidence base.

So here's what I'm actually after: could you describe the most important implications of this cross-cultural (and cross-class) variation in intuitions for how much weight I, as someone basically running these thought experiments on myself as an amateur, ought to give my own gut reactions when I'm working through a philosophical argument? Is the sober conclusion that the "just consult your intuitions" method so much of the field seems to run on is in real trouble, or is there some principled way of still leaning on intuitions that isn't just special pleading for whichever ones happen to be mine? I'm less interested in litigating whether the original studies actually replicate cleanly, which I gather is contested, than in what follows for somebody in my position either way that shakes out.
