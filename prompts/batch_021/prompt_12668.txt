So I'm a retired geologist, and now that I'm not working I've finally got time to mess around with philosophy, which I never got to study properly back in the day. I joined a little discussion group that meets every other week at the library, and last session we did split-brain patients and the unity of consciousness (we'd read some excerpts of Nagel's old paper on it beforehand). Two of the regulars, Roy and Fran, practically came to blows over it and I havent been able to let it go since.

Roy's opening move (1): once you actually look at the classic experiments -- flash a word to just the right hemisphere, the patient's left hand picks the matching object out of a pile of hidden ones, while the patient (talking, which is the left hemisphere) swears blind he saw nothing and has no idea why his own hand grabbed it -- there is no honest way to call that one mind. Two hemispheres getting different information and acting on it separately is two minds, full stop, and calling it "one confused person" is just squeamishness.

Fran (2): said the lab setup is basically rigged for disunity -- those experiments only work because the researcher flashes the image for a split second on a special screen so eye movements cant smuggle it across to the other side, and those same patients go home and drive, cook dinner and hold a normal conversation. You can't read the metaphysics of "how many people" off of a deliberately engineered lab trick.

Roy (3): said that's backwards -- the fact that the hemispheres need special equipment to stop them "cheating" via cross-cuing (nodding, humming, that sort of signal) to pass information to each other is proof there are really two agents in there, cooperating like teammates passing notes, not one mind that's just temporarily glitchy.

Fran (4): said cross-cuing shows the opposite -- that whatever the wiring, the system is straining to stay single, patching unity together out of whatever channel is left. She also pointed out that the talking hemisphere never once reports feeling like half a person, or like someone else is in there with him -- it just confabulates a smooth story -- which is exactly what you'd expect if there's genuinely one subject working off a patchy information supply.

Roy (5): said that proves nothing, since the right hemisphere mostly can't talk anyway -- of course we only ever hear one side's testimony. Absence of a report from a witness with no mouth isn't absence of a witness.

At that point they both turned to me, since I'd apparently done the assigned reading more carefully, to declare a winner. I said I honestly wasn't sure Nagel's paper even lets you declare one -- his whole point seemed to be that our ordinary concept of "how many minds" might just run out in a case like this -- but even as I said it, it felt like I was dodging the question rather than answering it.

Could you go through the exchange move by move (1 through 5) and say who's got the better of the argument at each step, not just who wins overall? And weigh in on my own dodge too -- is "the concept just runs out here" a real answer to who's right, or is it a fancy way of refusing to pick a side?
