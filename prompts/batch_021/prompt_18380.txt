The rough idea, as I understand it: if a patch of the universe sits in an empty, accelerating, dark-energy-dominated vacuum forever (what physicists call "de Sitter space"), random thermal and quantum jitter will eventually assemble a fully-formed, momentarily-conscious brain right out of the vacuum, complete with fake memories of an entire life it never lived. Because that empty vacuum lasts basically forever while stars, galaxies and evolution only get a finite window to produce "ordinary" observers like us, these freak fluctuation-brains vastly outnumber the real ones over the long run. A couple of the cosmologists I've interviewed treat this as a genuinely serious problem: they use it to throw out whole classes of eternal-inflation models, on the logic that if your theory predicts the typical observer is a randomly-fluctuating brain rather than someone with an actual evolutionary history, thats basically a strike against the theory, maybe even a fatal one. But other people I've talked to (mostly philosophers of physics, though a couple physicists too) think thats a total non-sequitur, that the whole exercise of asking "what fraction of observers in an infinite multiverse are like me" leans on shaky assumptions about typicality and self-locating probability that don't obviously track anything physically real.
