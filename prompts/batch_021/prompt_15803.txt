I have no formal training in philosophy, but I've put a great deal of time into working through the ethics of war on my own, starting with Walzer and then the revisionist literature that responds to him, and there is one dispute in that literature I have not been able to resolve to my own satisfaction. The traditional view holds that combatants on both sides of a war are moral equals, so long as each abides by the rules of conduct in war: a soldier fighting for the unjust side does nothing wrong merely by trying to kill the soldier trying to kill him. The revisionist challenge denies this. On that view, what makes someone a legitimate target of lethal force is posing an unjust threat, so the unjust combatant is in principle liable to be killed while his just counterpart is not, regardless of how personally decent either one is.

Write a dialogue between a defender of the moral equality of combatants and a critic of it, in which each actually engages the other's strongest argument instead of restating their own view. The defender must give a substantive answer to the liability argument, not simply assert that soldiers are interchangeable instruments of policy. The critic must give a substantive answer on two fronts: the worry that denying moral equality would force every soldier to adjudicate their own war's justice before fighting in it, and the fact that most unjust combatants bear little or no personal culpability for being there. Don't let the exchange end in a tidy resolution, but make sure each side is pushed into conceding something that actually costs them.
