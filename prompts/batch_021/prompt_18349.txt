My brother and I recently inherited a modest sum from our aunt, whose will specified only that it be donated to charity in her memory, leaving the choice of recipient up to us. We've been going back and forth on this for weeks and have reached a real impasse. I want to give the money to an organization that distributes anti-malarial bed nets in sub-Saharan Africa, precisely because there is unusually solid data on how many lives such programs save per dollar spent. My brother thinks that precision is a kind of comforting illusion.

His objection is that every child whose life is saved will grow up, have children of their own, compete for local land, water, and jobs, and become entangled in political and ecological systems whose effects fifty or a hundred years out are completely opaque to us. For all either of us knows, he says, the "measurable" good we'd be funding could be quietly cancelled out, or even reversed, by consequences that no study will ever track. He would rather put the money toward a scholarship for a family we know personally: there's no rigorous data on the impact of that either, but at least, he says, he can trust his direct impressions of the people involved, instead of a statistic that goes dark the moment it leaves the spreadsheet.

What I can't work out is whether my brother's argument, if it's correct, actually proves far more than he wants it to. The scholarship recipient's life will ripple outward into the future in exactly the same untraceable way as the child saved from malaria, so his preferred option should be just as suspect by his own standard. Followed all the way through, the logic seems to leave neither of us with any principled way to choose between our two options, or even to prefer giving the money away at all over spending it on ourselves. Is there an actual flaw in his reasoning, and if so, where does it break down? Or is he gesturing at a genuine difficulty that anyone who tries to justify a choice by appeal to the good it will do down the line eventually has to confront?
