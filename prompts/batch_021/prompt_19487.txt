I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy or economics, but a Hacker News thread on the history of the gold standard sent me down a rabbit hole a few weekends ago, and I've been slowly working through David Hume's economic essays ever since, at the encouragement of a coworker who's a bit obsessed with macroeconomics and Bitcoin. The essay that stuck with me most is "Of the Balance of Trade," where Hume argues that no country can hold onto a trade surplus for long: any extra gold and silver it accumulates will bid up its domestic prices, eroding its export competitiveness and making imports more attractive, until the imbalance corrects itself automatically, without any government needing to intervene. He even extends the logic to argue that we shouldn't worry about one region of a country running a persistent "trade deficit" against another region, as long as they share the same currency, because the same price-adjustment mechanism operates internally.

That got me wondering what Hume would make of two situations he obviously never saw coming. First, the Eurozone: Germany has run a large, persistent current-account surplus against southern European countries like Greece, Spain, and Italy for roughly two decades now, inside a currency union that is, in effect, a real-world version of the same-money, many-provinces scenario Hume used to argue such imbalances were nothing to worry about — and yet the 2010s crisis was anything but the smooth, self-liquidating adjustment he described for the deficit countries. Second, the global case: the persistent US trade deficit with China, where critics argue Chinese authorities have deliberately jammed Hume's adjustment mechanism by managing the exchange rate and recycling dollar earnings into US government debt (which also brushes up against Hume's separate alarm, in "Of Public Credit," about the dangers of rising national debt) instead of letting the inflow bid up domestic prices and wages the way his theory assumes, and where some politicians now want to respond with tariffs.

Working only from the actual mechanics of Hume's arguments, rather than his general reputation as a free-trade advocate, how do you think he would size up these two cases? I'm especially interested in: whether he would treat the Eurozone experience as a genuine problem for his own thought experiment, or explain the pain away by pointing to some assumption of his (e.g., flexible prices and wages) that modern economies don't actually satisfy; whether he'd class China's currency and reserve-accumulation strategy as exactly the kind of "jealousy of trade" fallacy he attacked in the essay of that name, or as a legitimate case where policy really does block the natural mechanism and so might warrant a response; and whether he'd end up endorsing tariffs as a countermeasure or view them as compounding the very mistake he spent his essays arguing against. I'd rather have an answer that stays close to the specific mechanics of his essays than one that just gestures at "Hume liked free trade."
