I am a retired physicist -- forty-two years in condensed matter research, nearly all of it at the same university department -- and only since retiring have I had the time to read moral philosophy with any care, rather than in the scattershot way I managed as a young man. For the past year, a small reading group I joined has been working through Elizabeth Anscombe, including the pamphlet she wrote in 1956, "Mr. Truman's Degree," opposing Oxford's decision to award Harry Truman an honorary degree for ordering the bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki. I gather it was one of her opening shots against what she would shortly afterward name "consequentialism," and the argument itself is not that the bombings were merely regrettable: it is that deliberately killing a city's worth of innocent people as the chosen means of forcing a surrender is murder, full stop, and that no subsequent tally of lives thereby saved can convert that choice into something else. I understand she was one of only a handful of Oxford dons who actually turned up to register their dissent when the degree came up for approval in Congregation, and lost.

This has landed at a difficult moment. Gordon, my closest friend since our doctoral days, died three weeks ago at ninety, and his family has asked me to give the eulogy at his memorial this coming Saturday. Gordon spent his entire working life designing nuclear warheads at one of the national laboratories, and from the day I met him until the week he died he held, without a flicker of doubt, that Truman had done exactly the right thing, on straightforwardly utilitarian grounds: that a terrible act had purchased a much larger good, that it had probably spared his own father, who was among the men then massing for the invasion of Japan, and that this same arithmetic -- weigh the lives taken against the lives saved, and act on the answer -- was the moral foundation of his life's work. He said as much cheerfully and without embarrassment at more dinner tables than I can count, mine included.

I no longer feel able to stand up and repeat Gordon's own justification as though I still think it obviously correct, because Anscombe has left me genuinely unsure that I do. But I have no wish to turn his memorial into an indictment of a man I loved, or into a philosophy lecture wearing a eulogy's clothes. Complicating matters, perhaps a third of the room will be Gordon's old colleagues from the lab -- several of whom fought this exact argument out with him for decades and will know at once if I dodge it -- while the rest are his wife, children, and grandchildren, none of them philosophers, who need above all to hear that his life meant something and that he was a good man. Would you write this eulogy for me? I would like something that could be delivered aloud in six or seven minutes, that does not misrepresent either Anscombe's argument or the doubt it has left me with, and that still manages, before anything else, to be an act of love.
