I write a regular interview feature for a magazine that covers science and ideas. Next month's interview is with a philosopher of science whose forthcoming book defends what she calls the 'logical learning' reply to the old evidence problem in Bayesian confirmation theory. The case that keeps drawing me back in is Mercury: the anomalous advance of its perihelion, roughly 43 arcseconds per century beyond what Newtonian mechanics could explain, had been sitting in the astronomical record since Le Verrier flagged it in 1859, more than half a century before Einstein showed that general relativity entails almost exactly that number with no adjustable parameters. Everyone treats this as one of the strongest pieces of evidence ever produced for a physical theory, yet on ordinary Bayesian conditionalization that shouldn't be possible: once the scientific community is already certain of a fact, conditionalizing on that fact again cannot move their credence in anything, including a brand-new theory that merely happens to entail it. Her reply is that the genuine new evidence in cases like this was never the old fact itself but the previously uncertain logical fact that the theory entails it, which requires giving up the assumption that rational agents are logically omniscient. I find the move elegant on first pass and slipperier the longer I sit with it, and I'd like the conversation to pin down exactly where it does and doesn't do the work she wants from it.

Could you draft eight to ten interview questions for me? I want at least one that makes her explain, mechanically, what it would mean for a rational agent's credence in an entailment relation to fall below 1, given that standard probability theory hands logical truths a probability of 1 for free, with a follow-up on whether this simply relocates the mystery from confirmation theory into a theory of partial belief in necessary truths that doesn't yet exist. I want one that asks whether the size of the confirmatory boost is now hostage to how difficult the derivation happened to be for the scientist who found it, rather than to anything about the theory's actual fit with the world, with a follow-up inviting her to say what she'd tell two theorists who reach logically equivalent versions of the same theory by routes of very different difficulty. I want one that tests whether her solution preserves or quietly erases the old intuition that a novel prediction confirms a theory more than merely accommodating a fact already on the books, since on her account what does the work is psychological surprise rather than chronological order. And I want one that presses the gerrymandering worry: what stops an ingenious enough theorist from eventually constructing some non-trivial derivation of any already-known fact, and thereby collecting confirmation for a theory that predicted nothing in advance. Close with a question that asks her, without room to hedge, whether she thinks she has dissolved Glymour's problem or only relocated it, and what kind of finding would change her mind. For each of these, please add a short follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give, so the questions don't let her rest on the version of the reply she's used to giving.
