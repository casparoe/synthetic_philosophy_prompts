The question (20 pts total) was:

Suppose you learn only that your birth rank among all humans who have ever been born is approximately r = 10^11 (one hundred billion), out of some unknown total N of humans who will ever be born.
(a) [7 pts] Lay out Carter's version of the Doomsday Argument as a Bayesian inference. Give the likelihood P(r | N) under the Self-Sampling Assumption, and explain why conditioning on r shifts credence away from large N relative to your prior.
(b) [6 pts] Richard Gott offers a seemingly similar argument using a Copernican confidence interval instead of a Bayesian posterior. Is Gott's argument just Carter's dressed up differently, or is something different going on underneath? Defend your answer.
(c) [7 pts] Bostrom and others argue that supplementing SSA with the Self-Indication Assumption (SIA) changes things. Explain what SIA says, and what happens to the Doomsday Argument once it's brought in alongside SSA.

Here's the student's answer:

(a) Carter's argument treats my rank r as though it were drawn uniformly at random from 1 to N — that's the Self-Sampling Assumption, reasoning as if I'm a random sample from everyone who will ever be born. So P(r|N) = 1/N for r ≤ N, and 0 otherwise. Since 1/N shrinks as N grows, my actual (smallish) rank is more probable under small-N hypotheses than under big ones, so conditioning on r pulls my posterior toward smaller N relative to whatever prior I started with. If my prior gave real weight to N being astronomically large (galactic colonization and so on), learning r ~ 10^11 drags that credence down hard toward "doom sooner." The argument doesn't prove doom, it just licenses an update in that direction.

(b) I'd say this is really Carter's argument wearing a different hat. Gott just applies the same Copernican idea directly: with 95% confidence my position falls in the middle 95% of the whole run, which is mathematically the very same uniformity assumption as the SSA likelihood in (a), just repackaged as a confidence interval instead of a posterior. So under the hood the two rest on identical assumptions — Gott is doing Carter's argument without saying the word "prior" out loud.

(c) SIA (I believe it traces to Dieks) says that existing at all is evidence favoring hypotheses on which more observers exist, since you're more likely to find yourself existing at all in a more populous world. Bringing SIA in alongside SSA actually makes the doomsday conclusion stronger, not weaker: SIA tells us to favor hypotheses with more people like us in them, and there are more early-ranked people like me if N turns out small, so the SIA-weighting reinforces the very same update as (a) rather than working against it. So the argument survives the SIA objection and comes out strengthened, though I'd still want to know exactly what reference class we're allowed to sample from in the first place.
