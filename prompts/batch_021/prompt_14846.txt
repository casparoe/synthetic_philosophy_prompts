I'm giving the best man's speech at my closest friend's wedding in a few weeks, and I want help getting one piece of intellectual content right rather than winging it myself. Both the groom and his fiancée did graduate work in behavioral ecology before moving into unrelated careers, so a good fraction of the guest list is people who spent years thinking about signaling theory for a living, while the rest is family who just wants a warm few minutes and a good toast. I've been reading rationalist blogs since the Overcoming Bias days, and Robin Hanson's recurring argument — laid out at length in The Elephant in the Brain — that huge swaths of human behavior are best explained by signaling rather than by their stated purpose has left me wanting to build the toast around Zahavi's handicap principle: the idea that the sheer cost and wastefulness of a wedding is precisely what makes the vows exchanged at it credible, much as a peacock's tail counts as good evidence of fitness because a sickly bird could never afford to grow one.

Here is the part I don't want the speech to get wrong, and the reason I'm asking rather than drafting it myself. A genuine Zahavian handicap is not just expensive; it has to be a cost that falls disproportionately on low-quality signalers — an index that a weak or uncommitted party genuinely cannot fake at any price, the way a heavier rack of antlers really does require more calcium and testosterone than a weaker animal has on hand. A lot of what actually gets spent at weddings does not work like that; it is costly by convention rather than by any such built-in link to sincerity, and the diamond is the standard illustration of the gap, since its price has far more to do with decades of manufactured scarcity and marketing than with any fact about how hard it would be for an insincere person to acquire one. So the easy version of this toast — "look how much they spent, so it must be love" — is bad biology, and I suspect actually less romantic than the truth. What I would like instead is a toast that goes looking for the real handicaps in what these two are doing — the parts of getting married that someone who did not mean it truly could not fake or afford — and lets that observation carry the emotional weight, while staying light enough that the groom's father, who has never once heard the phrase "evolutionarily stable strategy," still laughs in the right places and does not feel like he has wandered into a seminar. Could you write me this toast? Something in the five-minute range read aloud, with one clearly marked moment of the nerdier material for the people who will catch it, building to a close that works for everyone in the room regardless of what they know about signaling theory.
