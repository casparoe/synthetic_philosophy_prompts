The following question and answer are drawn from a graduate course in philosophy of mathematics.

Question: Explain Skolem's Paradox: why does it appear paradoxical that ZFC, if consistent, has a countable model, given that ZFC proves the existence of uncountable sets? State the standard resolution of the apparent conflict, and explain what that resolution implies about the absoluteness, or lack thereof, of set-theoretic notions such as "uncountable." Then describe how Hilary Putnam put this phenomenon to philosophical use in his argument against metaphysical realism.

Student's answer: Skolem's Paradox arises because the upward Löwenheim–Skolem theorem guarantees that if ZFC has an infinite model at all, it has a countable one, call it M. Yet ZFC proves Cantor's theorem, and hence proves that the power set of ω is uncountable, so M must contain some element that M itself judges to be uncountable — an object a such that M satisfies "there is no bijection between a and ω."

This is not a genuine contradiction. Because M's entire domain is only countably infinite, whatever a amounts to from the outside is also countable, so a real bijection between a and ω does exist in the background theory. What M lacks is not the countability of a but a copy of that particular bijection among its own elements: the function simply is not among the objects M has available to quantify over. So being uncountable is not an absolute property of a set, but is relative to the model one is working in, unlike a notion such as being a natural number, which is absolute across models.

Putnam later put this same relativity to philosophical use against realism. Because no first-order theory, however rich, can pin down a unique intended model up to isomorphism, Putnam argued that the reference of our mathematical vocabulary is radically indeterminate, and he concluded from this that we should give up belief in mathematical objects such as sets as mind-independent entities, treating talk of them instead as a convenient fiction we are free to reinterpret.
