This term I'm taking a seminar on rational choice that recently covered L. A. Paul's work on transformative experience, and the topic spilled over into an argument between two classmates of mine, Elif and Callum, about a mutual friend who has just been accepted into a clinical trial of psilocybin-assisted therapy for treatment-resistant depression. He knows going in that many participants describe the experience as reshaping their values and sense of self in ways they neither predicted nor chose, and not merely lifting their depression. Elif and Callum disagreed over whether his decision to enroll can count as rational in anything like the standard expected-utility sense. Their exchange went roughly like this:

(1) Elif said it can't be evaluated that way at all. Expected-utility reasoning requires assigning values to possible outcomes, but if the drug might rewrite what he cares about, there is no stable version of him whose preferences could do that assigning in advance: the self who would have to evaluate the outcome is exactly what is being put at stake.

(2) Callum objected that this can't be right, since we routinely make unfamiliar, irreversible decisions on the strength of other people's testimony — that is more or less what informed consent is. If firsthand acquaintance with an outcome were required for a choice to be rational, informed consent in general would be a sham, and nobody thinks that.

(3) Elif replied that testimony reports how already-transformed people talk about the outcome afterward, not what it is like to hold the resulting values oneself. More importantly, it cannot tell you whether ending up like that would be him flourishing or him being lost, since that judgment depends on which set of values is doing the judging. This is disanalogous to ordinary uncertainty, such as not knowing whether you will enjoy a new restaurant, where your standards for a good outcome stay fixed and only the facts are in question; here the standards themselves are what is up for grabs.

(4) Callum answered that you can still model this formally without inventing a special category: assign credences to the different value-sets he might end up with, using whatever evidence is available (such as the trial's own data on how participants change), and then maximize expected value over that distribution, the same way you would handle any decision with an uncertain outcome. On his view, this is just one extra layer of modeling, not a breakdown of rational choice.

Neither of them budged, and I honestly can't tell who has the better of it. Could you adjudicate their exchange move by move?
