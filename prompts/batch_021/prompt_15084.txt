At work I've been leading a redesign of the icon system and analytics dashboards for a field-data app that our company's enumerators use across a dozen countries, several with patchy literacy and no language in common among users. While reading up on infographic history for inspiration, I kept running into Otto Neurath, who I was surprised to learn was a serious Vienna Circle philosopher rather than just an early graphic designer. In the mid-1920s he ran the Gesellschafts- und Wirtschaftsmuseum (the "Museum of Society and Economy") in Vienna, and together with the artist Gerd Arntz and his collaborator (and eventual wife) Marie Reidemeister developed what later became known as Isotype, the "International System of Typographic Picture Education." One rule from that project has stuck with me: to show a bigger quantity, you repeat a symbol at a fixed size rather than draw one symbol bigger, because resizing a shape distorts area and volume in ways viewers reliably misjudge. That is more or less the argument I had last week with a teammate who wanted a "scale the icon up" chart instead of a row of repeated icons.

What I can't tell from the design-blog version of this story is how much of it was actually load-bearing for Neurath's philosophy, versus just a clever trick he happened to land on. From what little I've read, he seems to have treated the picture language as continuous with his broader commitments: his discomfort with ordinary words as carriers of scientific claims, his general anti-metaphysical, no-dry-dock-only-a-ship-rebuilt-at-sea attitude toward knowledge, and his politics of making statistical facts available to people usually shut out of them by jargon and literacy requirements. That is a much bigger claim than "keep your icons a consistent size," and I'd like to understand it well enough to know whether I'm honoring it or trivializing it when I invoke Neurath in a design-review doc.

Could you lay out the most important implications of Neurath's views on picture languages for the choices my team is actually facing? In particular, I'd like you to address whether an icon-based dashboard can carry the same content as a text-and-numbers one (comparison, trend, conditionality, uncertainty, negation being the sorts of things prose and tables handle easily but pictures seem to struggle with); whether presenting data as pictograms risks giving users a false sense that the data is more neutral or "pre-interpreted" than it really is; and whether there's an unresolved tension between Isotype's egalitarian aims and the fact that someone still has to choose, and simplify, what the pictures show.
