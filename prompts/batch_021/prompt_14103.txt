I have spent a good deal of time with the "butterfly dream" passage that closes the second chapter of the Zhuangzi (the Qiwulun, often rendered "Discussion on Making All Things Equal"), and what strikes me is that nearly every popular retelling I come across—blog posts, videos, offhand comparisons to The Matrix—treats it as a piece of Cartesian dream-skepticism: maybe I am really the butterfly, maybe none of this is real, how would I ever know? But the passage itself does not end on a note of doubt about which state is real; it ends by simply naming what happened between Zhuang Zhou and the butterfly "the transformation of things" (wu hua), which suggests to me that Zhuangzi was not trying to certify one side as real and the other as illusion, but questioning whether Zhou and the butterfly were ever separated by a boundary firm enough for that question to be coherent in the first place. I would like you to write a short story that takes this second, less familiar reading seriously rather than the first. The setting does not need to have anything to do with China, dreams, or butterflies—invent whatever scenario you like—but the story should not resolve itself with a character waking up, or with any kind of simulation, dream-machine, or video-game reveal. Instead, let two identities, or two ways of experiencing the world, drift into and out of each other over the course of the story, so that by the end there is no stable answer to which one was real, and it is no longer obvious that this was ever a coherent question to ask. The philosophical weight should be carried by the shape and texture of the story itself, rather than by a character stopping to explain the idea out loud.
