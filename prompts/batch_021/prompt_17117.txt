And here's the anonymized answer we're grading:

"Harsanyi's aggregation theorem starts from a society of individuals who each have preferences over lotteries on some fixed set of social outcomes, and it assumes both each individual's preferences and society's own preferences satisfy the von Neumann-Morgenstern axioms (completeness, transitivity, continuity, independence), so each can be represented by an expected-utility function that's unique up to a positive affine transformation. Add one more premise -- a Pareto condition saying that whenever every individual is indifferent between two lotteries society must be indifferent between them too, and whenever every individual weakly prefers one lottery and at least one individual strictly prefers it, society strictly prefers it as well -- and it follows that the social utility function has to be a weighted sum of the individual utility functions, W = a1U1 + a2U2 + ... + anUn, with all the weights non-negative. This is essentially just a rigorous, expected-utility version of the impartial-spectator argument Harsanyi is famous for: an individual behind a veil of ignorance who doesn't know who they'll turn out to be, and who therefore assigns equal probability to occupying each position in society, will rank social states by their expected utility to a 'random' member of society, which is exactly the weighted-sum formula the theorem proves. Harsanyi used this to argue directly against Rawls: a rational chooser facing genuine risk about their future identity would maximize expected utility rather than follow the maximin rule, since maximin means letting the worst-off position govern the ranking no matter how unlikely that position is.

Because the conclusion is additive, the theorem is standardly read as a proof that Pareto-respecting, rational aggregation of preferences has to be utilitarian in form. Sen's well-known objection is that the weights a_i are only fixed relative to whatever zero-point and unit we happen to pick for each person's utility function, so nothing in the vNM axioms forces the individual u_i's onto a common interpersonal scale -- the theorem hands you an additive formula, not classical sum-ranking utilitarianism. This isn't fatal, though: since vNM already fixes each U_i up to a positive affine transformation, fixing that transformation for every individual at once is exactly what supplies the missing interpersonal information, so the weights a_i end up carrying all the comparability that Sen thinks is missing."
