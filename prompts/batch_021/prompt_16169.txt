While reporting on the current disputes surrounding Integrated Information Theory, I keep returning to an argument Leibniz made three centuries before anyone had a theory of consciousness to argue with. In §17 of the Monadology, he asks the reader to imagine a machine built to think and feel, enlarged until one could walk through it as through a mill; touring its gears and levers, he says, we would find only parts pushing other parts, never anything resembling a perception, and concludes that thought must belong to a simple, unextended substance rather than to any composite.

IIT locates consciousness in exactly the kind of composite Leibniz dismissed, identifying it with a mathematically defined quantity, Φ, that is high just when a system's parts are integrated in a way that resists decomposition into independent components, and low or zero otherwise. One of the theory's standard illustrations is structurally identical to the mill: a digital camera's sensor, whose millions of photodiodes register enormous amounts of information but do so independently of one another, turns out on IIT's own account to have almost no Φ, and therefore almost no experience — for essentially the reason Leibniz gave for doubting his mill had any.

Reconstruct the argument Leibniz would most plausibly make against treating a high-Φ system as vindicating, rather than merely repeating, the mill. Would he accept that an irreducible causal structure finally supplies the unity he thought no composite could have, or would he insist, on his own terms — windowless monads, the reduction of relations to intrinsic denominations, aggregates as at best "well-founded phenomena" — that Φ tracks something real but not the perception he had in mind? If the latter, what would he say integration is actually integrating?
