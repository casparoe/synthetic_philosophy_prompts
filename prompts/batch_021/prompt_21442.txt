So there's this old decision theory puzzle called 'Death in Damascus' that I think is a way better test case than Newcomb's problem, but almost nobody brings it up outside of academic papers. Setup: a guy runs into Death in Damascus, who looks surprised and says "I am coming for you tomorrow." Freaked out, the guy buys a camel that night and rides to Aleppo to escape him, except it turns out Death's appointment was in Aleppo all along, so fleeing is exactly what gets him killed. Now suppose Death is basically a near-perfect predictor of wherever the guy actually ends up going (he works off an appointment book written weeks in advance based on predicting your choice) -- apparently this creates a weird instability for causal decision theory that's way worse than anything in Newcomb's problem, since deciding to go anywhere immediately gives you reason to think you shoulda gone somewhere else. Can you write me an essay that actually explains why this breaks CDT in a way Newcomb doesnt, weighs how a causal vs an evidential decision theorist would each handle it, and then commits to a real answer for what the guy should do instead of just dodging the question with "it depends"?
