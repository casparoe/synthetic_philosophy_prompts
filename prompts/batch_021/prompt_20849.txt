I've been asked to chair a five-person ad hoc panel that our Dean of Undergraduate Studies convened this month, and although my own field is nowhere near philosophy, I now have to get up to speed fast because the case is genuinely difficult. In a 300-level "Philosophy of Language" seminar, the instructor spent a week on theories of slurs, assigning material like Hom's combinatorial-externalist account, Anderson and Lepore's "prohibitionist" view, and Camp's account of slurs as expressing a derogatory perspective rather than encoding straightforward truth-conditional content. To make the point that swapping in a euphemism like "the N-word" changes the very object under analysis, the instructor wrote several actual slur terms on the whiteboard during discussion instead of using placeholders, and had students apply the different theories directly to them. Two students filed a complaint arguing that being confronted with the term caused real distress regardless of whether it was "used" or merely "mentioned," and pointedly cited Anderson and Lepore's own claim that the taboo on these words attaches to the word-form itself, not to any particular speech act -- which is precisely why quotation doesn't defang it. Three of my four fellow panelists want to dismiss the complaint on straightforward academic-freedom grounds, treating the mention/use distinction as a self-evident shield, and I'm no longer sure that move is available to us once the assigned readings are taken seriously on their own terms.

Our faculty handbook says only that potentially offensive material must be "pedagogically necessary and proportionate to the learning objective," with no further elaboration, and I've since learned that a near-identical complaint is being prepared against an instructor in a hip-hop studies course, so whatever we decide will function as precedent well beyond this one classroom. We owe the Dean a memo before we reconvene next Friday. Could you draft it? It should reach and defend one specific recommendation -- dismiss, uphold, or something in between -- with a concrete process and timeline attached, and it needs to genuinely engage the students' strongest argument (that the very theory being taught undercuts the mention/use defense) rather than resolving the case by simply asserting that mention isn't use.
