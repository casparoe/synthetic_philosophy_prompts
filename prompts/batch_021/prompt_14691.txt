Is Fregean sense something subjective -- private to each individual thinker -- or something objective? Finally, note one further difficulty that's been raised against Frege's solution."

And here's the answer, copied exactly as turned in:

"Frege noticed that 'Hesperus = Hesperus' is trivially true and can be known just by reflection, whereas 'Hesperus = Phosphorus' seems to tell us something new about the world, even though both names refer to the very same planet, Venus. If identity is simply a relation an object bears to itself, this is puzzling, since every object is self-identical, so the two statements ought to be equally uninformative. Frege's solution is that a name has a sense as well as a reference: the sense is the particular mental image or association that a competent speaker attaches to a name when using it -- for instance 'the star seen in the evening' for Hesperus and 'the star seen in the morning' for Phosphorus. Because Hesperus and Phosphorus present Venus by way of different senses, the identity claim is informative even though only one object is involved. Since these mental associations differ from speaker to speaker, and even from occasion to occasion for the same speaker, Fregean sense turns out to be a fundamentally subjective, psychological notion -- which is exactly why Frege needs reference as a separate, objective notion to do the real logical work. One well-known difficulty for this picture is Frege's own case of Scott and the author of Waverley: George IV wanted to know whether Scott was the author of Waverley, but he certainly didn't want to know whether Scott was Scott, so the sense/reference apparatus runs into trouble once definite descriptions get embedded inside these attitude reports."
