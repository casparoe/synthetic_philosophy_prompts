Now that I'm retired from a career in condensed-matter physics and have taken up reading philosophy in my spare time, I keep noticing what looks like the same phenomenon wearing different clothes. In my own field, the Madelung constant is notorious for being a conditionally convergent lattice sum: computed naively over an expanding sphere or cube of ions it yields different answers depending on the order and shape of summation, and the ambiguity only disappears once you group the terms into charge-neutral shells or switch to a reciprocal-space technique such as Ewald summation, at which point a single correct value emerges. The Pasadena paradox, due to Nover and Hájek, strikes me as exactly the same mathematical situation transplanted into decision theory: the expected value of the gamble is an alternating, conditionally convergent series that can be made to sum to anything at all by reordering its terms. Given that physicists resolved their version of this problem by insisting on the one summation procedure that respects the actual physics, why shouldn't decision theorists do the same and simply stipulate that the terms be summed in the order the coin tosses actually occur, rather than treating the Pasadena game as a standing paradox about the very concept of expected value?
