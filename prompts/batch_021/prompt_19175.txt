Random one from a seminar I'm sitting in on for a punishment-theory unit -- I'm actually a law PhD student, just borrowing this from the philosophy department for the term. We read P.F. Strawson's "Freedom and Resentment" right before Galen Strawson's Basic Argument stuff (the "Impossibility of Moral Responsibility" paper), and nobody in the room mentioned that its literally father and son arguing past each other across a generation, which feels like it deserves more than a footnote.

Quick recap in case it helps fix ideas: P.F. grounds responsibility in reactive attitudes -- resentment, gratitude, indignation -- that track whether someone showed you good will, ill will, or disregard. These get suspended locally via excuses and exemptions (he didn't mean it, she's a child) but never globally, because the alternative "objective attitude," where you just manage people like objects instead of relating to them, isn't something we could live in as a species. Galen's regress says none of that touches the deeper question: to be truly responsible for an action you'd need to be responsible for the way you are, which requires having chosen to be that way for reasons, which requires already being some way, etc. It bottoms out in stuff you didn't choose, so nobody's self-made enough to deserve credit or blame in the strong sense, determinism or not.

So here's my actual question: if Galen's regress is sound, doesn't consistency push us toward the objective attitude for literally everyone, not just his dad's exempted cases -- and is "we're psychologically incapable of that" an actual rebuttal to that pressure, or just conceding the point while hiding behind human limitations? And separately, for someone trying to figure out whether retributive punishment can be defended, does either Strawson actually give you traction, or do they just wash each other out? I'd rather not hear the "they're answering different questions so there's no real conflict" line unless you can show it survives the objective-attitude point above.
