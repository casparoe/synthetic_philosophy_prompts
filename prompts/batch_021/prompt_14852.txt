In my philosophy of biology seminar, we've spent the last few weeks contrasting etiological (selected-effect) and causal-role accounts of biological function, and this week's case study is the controversy sparked by the 2012 ENCODE Project's claim that roughly 80% of the human genome shows "biochemical function." Evolutionary biologists, most notably Dan Graur and colleagues, attacked that figure on the grounds that ENCODE's criterion — any detectable biochemical activity — amounts to a causal-role notion of function, which they say ENCODE illegitimately conflated with the etiological, selection-based sense that has traditionally grounded claims that most non-coding DNA is "junk." I can't stop thinking about how this dispute maps onto Darwin's own discussion of rudimentary organs in On the Origin of Species, where he treats many structures as evolutionary vestiges that persist despite conferring no current benefit, explicable only through descent with modification rather than present utility. Given that argument, which side of the ENCODE dispute do you think Darwin would have taken, and why? Does his reasoning about rudimentary organs commit him to the etiological camp against ENCODE, or can he plausibly be read as more sympathetic to a permissive, causal-role standard of function? Please ground your answer in his actual reasoning about vestigial structures, not just his general commitment to natural selection.
