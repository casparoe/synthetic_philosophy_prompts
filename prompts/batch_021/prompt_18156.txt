W. E. B. Du Bois's 1926 essay "Criteria of Negro Art," originally delivered as an address to the NAACP's conference in Chicago, insists that Black artists could not afford art for art's sake: for Du Bois, beauty, truth, and moral purpose were inseparable, and he declared flatly that "all art is propaganda," meant in the service of the race's dignity and eventual liberation. He put that standard into practice two years later in his notice of Claude McKay's bestselling novel "Home to Harlem," which he could not bring himself to recommend: its unsparing portrait of poverty, crime, and casual sex among Harlem's underclass struck him not as honest realism but as a kind of betrayal, material that would only feed the prejudices of the white readership he knew was watching.

I want to know what conclusion this standard -- not the bumper-sticker slogan, but the fuller argument and the sensibility behind the McKay judgment -- would lead Du Bois to reach about gangsta rap and its descendants, a tradition that arose decades after his death in 1963: from N.W.A.'s "Straight Outta Compton" through contemporary drill, made largely by and originally for Black audiences, commercially dominant and verbally virtuosic by most critical measures, yet routinely unsentimental at best about violence, misogyny, and crime in Black communities, and consumed in enormous numbers by exactly the white audience Du Bois worried about. Does this tradition fail his test in the same way McKay did, or could its refusal to prettify the effects of racism and poverty count as exactly the propaganda of truth he demanded of Black art? Engage seriously with the strongest version of whichever case you do not end up defending -- a more self-consciously redemptive figure such as Kendrick Lamar might serve as the harder test case -- and say plainly whether you think Du Bois's own principles leave him with no stable answer here.
