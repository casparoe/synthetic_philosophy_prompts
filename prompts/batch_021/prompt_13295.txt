Two weeks ago I had a long, unresolved argument with my friend Daniel about David Lewis's account of convention, and I haven't been able to let it go, which is inconvenient, because I'm giving the toast at my closest friend's wedding this Saturday and Daniel will be sitting three tables back. It started when I asked why the groomsmen have to walk down the aisle in a fixed order, and Daniel pointed out that on Lewis's picture that order is just a coordination equilibrium: a regularity that persists only because everyone conforms to it, and expects everyone else to, and expects to be expected to, with nothing else recommending it over some equally workable alternative that a slightly different wedding party might have settled on just as happily. He didn't stop at the processional -- the vows, the ring exchange, even the business of everyone standing to lift a glass when a toast is proposed, all arbitrary, all sustained by nothing deeper than common knowledge and mutual expectation. Then he extended it to the couple themselves: the specific private rituals two people accumulate -- the exact joke that ends every fight, the particular thing they say to each other at airport security -- are, on his account, just coordination problems that happened to get solved one way instead of another and then calcified. I disagreed but couldn't say why without either denying the arbitrariness, which felt dishonest, or conceding that nothing about their relationship is really, distinctively theirs, which felt worse.

I'd like the toast to finish that argument properly instead of quietly dodging it, and there's a complication that makes this hard to write on my own. The bride is finishing a PhD in decision theory, so a good third of the room -- her cohort, a fair number of her professors -- will hear 'common knowledge' and 'coordination equilibrium' and know immediately whether I'm using them honestly, while the rest of the room, most of both families, has never heard 'convention' mean anything other than tradition, and needs the same five minutes to land as a toast rather than a seminar. Could you write it? I want a speech that grants Daniel everything -- the arbitrariness, the fact that some other set of private rituals would have done just as well, the fact that the whole structure runs on mutual expectation rather than on any of it being independently correct -- and still arrives, honestly, at why that doesn't make what these two have built together any less real, perhaps by way of the thought that a coordination convention, kept up long enough, stops being merely convenient and starts carrying real normative weight, so that breaking it wouldn't just be a deviation but a betrayal. Five minutes or so, and it has to actually end in a toast people want to raise their glasses to, Daniel included.
