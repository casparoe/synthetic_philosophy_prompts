I write about science and ideas for a magazine, and for the last several months I've been reporting a piece on the industry that has grown up around refusing to die: cryonics outfits, the 'longevity escape velocity' crowd, a startup that trains a chatbot on a dead parent's old messages so the bereaved can keep talking to them. Partly out of curiosity about what a genuinely different attitude toward all this might look like, I went back to the Zhuangzi and got stuck on two of its anecdotes about death. In one, his wife dies, and his friend Huizi arrives to console him only to find him sitting with his legs sprawled out, drumming on an upturned basin and singing, entirely unbothered, explaining that she has simply gone back into the same formless blur that produced her, the way one season turns into the next without complaint. In the other, near his own death, his students want to give him an elaborate burial, and he waves them off, telling them heaven and earth will do fine as a coffin, the sun and moon as his burial jades, the stars as his pearls, and that being eaten by crows above ground is no different from being eaten by worms below it, so why rob one to feed the other? Both stories are of a piece with his recurring suggestion elsewhere that grieving a death, one's own or someone else's, rests on the same confusion as insisting on staying exactly the shape you currently happen to be.

Could you write me a short story, something in the 1,500 to 2,500 word range, that puts this attitude to a real test rather than presenting it as settled wisdom? Give me a contemporary or near-future character facing an actual, specific decision bound up with death — a diagnosis, a cryopreservation contract, a parent on a ventilator, a dead partner's chatbot, whatever you find most alive — who has genuinely absorbed something like Zhuangzi's way of seeing it, and let the story earn whatever equanimity results, or its failure, through what actually happens rather than through anyone explaining the philosophy out loud. I'd rather you avoid the two easiest versions of this: a parable in which the Zhuangzian character is simply wiser than everyone around them, and a quiet tragedy in which their calm gets unmasked as denial by the end. I'd like to genuinely not know, reading it, which of those it is.
