Q3. (a) [8 pts] Using the BHK interpretation, explain why the law of excluded middle is not intuitionistically valid in general, and be precise about what intuitionists do and don't claim about its truth-value. (b) [6 pts] Give a Brouwer-style "weak counterexample" using an open conjecture of your choosing, and explain what distinguishes a weak counterexample from a strong one. (c) [6 pts] State Markov's Principle and discuss its status in Heyting arithmetic, and across the different schools of constructive math (Brouwerian intuitionism, Bishop-style constructivism, the Markov/Russian recursive school).

Student's answer:

(a) On the BHK reading, a proof of P∨Q is a proof of one of the disjuncts plus the info of which one it is, and a proof of ¬P is a construction turning any hypothetical proof of P into a proof of ⊥. So proving P∨¬P intuitionistically requires either a proof of P or a proof that P is contradictory. For lots of propositions -- especially open conjectures, where we currently have neither -- no such proof exists, so the disjunction isn't provable. Because of this, intuitionists conclude that excluded middle is false as a general schema, though of course it still holds for individual decidable propositions like "n is even or n is odd."

(b) The standard move is to build a sequence that behaves normally unless/until a counterexample to some open conjecture (say, Goldbach) turns up at position n, at which point it does something else (e.g. switches from 0s to 1s). Whether the resulting real number is rational, or whether it's <0, =0, or >0, can't currently be decided either way, so we can't assert the relevant LEM instance for it. This is only a "weak" counterexample, tied to the current state of mathematical knowledge (Goldbach could be settled tomorrow) -- a "strong" counterexample would show the instance is actually intuitionistically refutable, full stop.

(c) Markov's Principle says that for a decidable predicate P on the naturals, if it's not the case that P fails everywhere, there's some specific n where P holds: ¬∀n¬P(n) → ∃n P(n). This tracks the informal idea of an unbounded search -- you check n=0,1,2,... one at a time, and since P is decidable at each step, if it's not the case that the search runs forever without success, it must eventually succeed. Because this lines up with how a Turing machine search actually behaves, MP turns out to be a theorem of Heyting arithmetic, and its accepted across the board by constructivists -- Brouwer, Bishop, and the Russian recursive school alike. The real fault lines between those schools are elsewhere, e.g. over choice sequences and the fan theorem.
