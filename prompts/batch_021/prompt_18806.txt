Last weekend a dinner conversation among some friends turned into a long, surprisingly heated argument that I still haven't been able to let go of. Maya, who is in her second year of internal medicine residency, was describing how she'd used one of those ten-year cardiovascular risk calculators (the kind that takes age, sex, cholesterol, blood pressure, diabetes status and smoking history and returns something like "14% risk of a heart attack or stroke in the next decade") to counsel a patient. Dev, who has a biostatistics PhD and now works in machine learning, pushed back hard: (1) he said the number describes nothing about that particular man, because the 14% comes from a regression fit on a cohort sharing only a handful of his features, and the same man also belongs to indefinitely many other groups the calculator ignores entirely (people with his specific family history, his diet, his neighborhood, his genome), each of which would generate a different rate, with nothing in the statistics itself picking out which grouping is the right one to quote to him. He called this a version of the reference class problem and said that, strictly, there's no fact about "his" probability, only facts about frequencies in whichever class you happen to settle on.

Maya answered that (2) this proves too much: the calculator is well calibrated, meaning patients who score 14% really do have events at close to that rate, and the mere existence of some narrower class you could in principle construct doesn't make the estimate meaningless -- any more than the existence of hyperlocal microclimates makes tomorrow's weather forecast meaningless. Dev granted the calibration point but replied that (3) it only shows the model is calibrated on its own class, not that 14% is what belongs in this man's chart, since an equally well-calibrated model built on different features (a genetic panel, say, or occupation and diet) could output 5% or 30% for the very same patient, and nothing in either model adjudicates between them, which matters enormously for how you'd counsel or treat him. Maya then said (4) that if Dev's standard requires locating the one true reference class before any individual probability counts as meaningful, then no predictive practice involving individuals -- weather, actuarial pricing, medicine -- could ever say anything meaningful, which is absurd; the actual task is just to use the most predictive information available, not to chase an unreachable "correct" class. Dev's last word was that (5) "most predictive information available" quietly presupposes exactly what's in question, since more specific information is in principle always more predictive, so consistency should push Maya toward ever-narrower classes until the patient becomes a class of one with no statistics left at all -- and that if she stops narrowing for merely practical reasons, she should at least stop presenting 14% to patients as a fact about them, rather than as a fact about a group she chose, for convenience, to place them in.

Neither of them budged, and the rest of us just watched it volley back and forth for a good twenty minutes. Could you go through moves (1) through (5) and say who has the better of it at each stage, not just who wins overall? I'd also like to know whether you think either of them is overlooking a genuinely workable way to pick out the relevant reference class in cases like this, or whether the problem is, as Dev suggests, fatal to talk of "this patient's risk" in any strict sense.
