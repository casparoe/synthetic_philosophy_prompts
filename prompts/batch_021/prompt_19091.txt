A friend of mine has been going through a difficult stretch for the past few months, and whenever we talk about it, he explains it in fairly heavy terms: he says he's come to realize that life has no built-in meaning, that his sense of ambition and willpower was always something of an illusion, and that some pessimistic philosophy he picked up over the summer basically confirmed what he already suspected. What strikes me as odd, though, is that over this same period his daily habits have quietly come apart in a way that seems too well-timed to be a coincidence: he quit the swim team, he's living on energy drinks and vending-machine food instead of real meals, he's up on his phone until two or three most nights, and he barely leaves his room except to go to school. When I gently suggested that he might want to sort out his sleep, food, and screen time before treating any of this as a settled fact about himself and the universe, he got defensive and said that was exactly the kind of move people make when they'd rather explain away someone's reasoning than actually engage with it.

Part of me thinks he has a fair point, and that telling someone to "just fix your sleep" is a cheap way to dodge a real change of mind. But another part of me suspects the opposite is true: that treating his eating, sleeping, and screen habits as somehow beneath or separate from his "real" philosophical crisis assumes a clean line between the state of his body and the state of his outlook that might not actually exist -- as though whatever thinking he's doing right now is happening in some neutral, physiology-free space. I've been reading some nineteenth-century philosophy on my own, beyond what we cover in my high school's philosophy elective, and have a nagging sense that this exact tension comes up somewhere in it, though I can't pin down where or how it's meant to be resolved. So here's my question: is my instinct to point at his habits actually the dismissive move he thinks it is, or is it the more serious response to what's going on with him -- and if it's the latter, is there a way to make that case to him without it collapsing into "your ideas don't count because of what you had for breakfast"?
