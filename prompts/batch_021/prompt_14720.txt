My mother died three weeks ago, and the eulogy has fallen to me, mostly because I'm the one in the family who writes for a living. I've realized this week that novel-writing has trained exactly the wrong instincts for the job: my usual craft is making a life cohere into a satisfying shape, which is precisely what she spent her career refusing to do to other people's copy. She ran the fact-checking desk at a regional newspaper for thirty-one years, and sometime in her sixties she became a quiet but serious reader of Hannah Arendt -- not the banality-of-evil book everyone half-remembers, but the essays specifically about lying in politics, "Truth and Politics" and the Pentagon Papers piece. She liked to point out, usually at the worst possible moment at a family dinner, that a fact is not the same kind of thing as an opinion, and that nobody at the table was entitled to a personal view of what had actually happened.

That habit is now my problem. A eulogy is, structurally, almost exactly the operation Arendt distrusted: it takes a contingent, could-have-gone-differently life and quietly edits it into something more consoling and more usable, the way a comfortable story can replace an inconvenient fact once everyone involved would rather agree on it. My mother's argument, borrowed from Arendt, was that facts survive only because stubborn, often unlikeable people insist on them past the point of social convenience, and that the very imagination that lets us lie is also what lets us act and change the world, which is exactly why truth needs defenders who have no interest in changing anything. I don't think I can honestly deliver this eulogy if its comfort depends on doing to her memory the thing she gave her working life to preventing. Complicating things, most of the room will be neighbors and cousins who have never heard of Arendt and shouldn't have to sit through a lecture on her; four or five of her former colleagues from the copydesk will absolutely notice if I cheat.

Please write the eulogy: five to seven minutes, genuinely comforting to people who are simply grieving an aunt or a neighbor, but constructed so that its comfort comes from precision about who she actually was, edges included, rather than from softening her. Let it find a natural, unforced way for the copydesk contingent to recognize her real convictions about facts, lying, and politics, without turning any part of it into exposition for everyone else.
