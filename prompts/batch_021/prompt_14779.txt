So this came up at a game night a couple weeks back and I'm still turning it over. A friend-of-a-friend, I'll call him Theo, put a few thousand dollars into a startup's seed round last year purely because word had gotten around that three seperate angels he respects had already committed -- by his own admission he never read the deck, never talked to the founders, nothing. The company folded about eight months later. When Theo told this story at the table (felt very Inadequate-Equilibria-coded to me, ngl), two other friends of mine, Nadia and Colin, ended up in a whole argument about whether what Theo did was rational herding or just bandwagon-jumping with extra steps, and specifically about what an "information cascade" technically requires. I want to lay out how the exchange went move by move because I honestly keep flip-flopping on who's landing the better point at each stage.

1. Colin opened with: this is a textbook information cascade, and Theo behaved totally rationally. Once you know three sophisticated angels already put money in, their revealed actions are more informative than anything Theo could scrounge up on his own in a weekend, so a Bayesian in his shoes should just follow the herd -- that's the whole Bikhchandani/Hirshleifer/Welch story, where a long line of people can end up making the identical choice even though each one, individually, is reasoning correctly given what they can see.

2. Nadia pushed back: that's not actually what happened, though. The textbook cascade requires the person to have some private signal of their own that then gets rationally overridden by a stronger public signal. Theo had zero signal -- he didn't even skim the deck. So there's nothing of his being "overridden"; there's no cascade in the technical sense, just free-riding on other people's reputations, and thats a different animal.

3. Colin's response: that's a distinction without a difference. In the actual models the private signal is just a noisy draw that can be arbitrarily close to uninformative, and the math doesn't care whether it's a weak signal or a null one -- a Bayesian with no signal at all still combines his (empty) priors with the public history and lands on full imitation. That's not an exception to the cascade mechanism, it's the limiting case of it -- the cascade just starts at step one instead of step five.

4. Nadia's comeback: fine, grant all of that. But then look at where the "informativeness" is even supposed to come from. We don't actually know the three angels weren't watching each other -- if the second and third were already partly herding on the first, the "strong public signal" your whole argument leans on might just be one guy's idiosyncratic read, laundered through two more rounds of imitation. At that point calling the outcome "rational" starts to feel generous, even though you're right that no individual step ever breaks Bayes' rule.

Who's actually right at each step, and is there some consideration neither of them is raising that actually matters here?
