I'm a backend engineer who spends most of my working life thinking about serialization formats, wire protocols, and compilers, and for years I've reached for "DNA is basically source code" as a throwaway analogy without ever examining it closely. That changed after a long flight spent reading around the philosophy-of-biology literature on whether the genetic code deserves to be called a code in any sense a computer scientist would recognize, rather than a vivid but ultimately loose metaphor. The strongest defense I found is Marcello Barbieri's "code biology," which rests on a hard distinction between copying and coding. On his account, DNA replication and transcription are copying: the correspondence between a base and its partner (A with T, C with G) is fixed by direct chemical and structural complementarity, no different in kind from photocopying a page. Translation is different in kind, and this is what Barbieri calls coding proper: nothing about a codon's chemistry forces it to correspond to a particular amino acid, and the correspondence holds only because a third party -- the aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases and tRNAs that Crick's "adaptor hypothesis" predicted -- enforces an assignment that could, in principle, have gone differently. Barbieri treats exactly this pairing of an arbitrary convention with an adaptor that reads and applies it as the defining mark of a genuine code, structurally akin to a cipher or a network protocol, and concludes that the genetic code is not a metaphorical use of the word at all, with several other "organic codes" of the same kind turning up elsewhere in the cell.

The position I can't quite square with this is the "parity thesis" that comes out of developmental systems theory, pressed by Paul Griffiths and Russell Gray, and reinforced from a different angle by Sahotra Sarkar's long-standing skepticism about talk of biological "information." As best I can reconstruct it, their target isn't really Barbieri's narrow biochemical claim about translation; it's the much larger claim that engineers like me tend to smuggle in right alongside it, namely that DNA is "the" program or blueprint directing the construction of an organism. On the parity thesis, maternal cytoplasmic factors, epigenetic marks, nutrition, an ant's nest-mates, a duckling's early visual environment, are all just as causally necessary to producing the adult organism as the base sequence is, so treating DNA alone as the carrier of developmental "instructions" is an unearned promotion of one resource among causally equal others, not a discovery about where the instructions "really" live.

What I'd like from you is a real comparison rather than a summary of each side. Does the parity thesis actually threaten Barbieri's narrow claim that translation itself is a genuine code, or does it leave that claim untouched while only debunking the grander "genome as program" picture built on top of it? And since coding is my actual day job: on Barbieri's own definition of a code -- an arbitrary, convention-fixed correspondence maintained by some adaptor or interpreter -- is he entitled to call translation "coding" in the same sense I mean when I write code, or is that exactly the disanalogy the developmental-systems side would seize on to argue that even his narrower claim proves less than he thinks?
