For most of my working life I was a biologist studying self-control in animals: pigeons that, given the chance, will peck a key that removes their own future access to a smaller-sooner reward, in effect binding themselves to the mast the way Ulysses did, so as to hear the Sirens without being able to answer them. Since retiring I have taken up philosophy, and I have learned how contested it is whether such precommitment devices actually resolve weakness of will or merely relocate it. My daughter, who builds hyperbolic-discounting models of how people trade rewards off against time, is marrying a philosopher who works on akrasia, and I have been asked to give the toast. About a third of the room will get the discounting joke unaided; the rest simply want to see the couple happy. Please write a five-minute toast that makes the precommitment motif — Ulysses, my pigeons, the rings themselves — its central joke, without letting the vows resolve into "a rational solution to a decision-theoretic problem": both of them would object that a hyperbolic discounter can renege on any commitment device it likes, and I would rather my one good joke not turn out to be the truest thing said all evening.
