A friend and I spent much of last night arguing about why a slur's derogatory force survives embedding: it seems just as present when the slur sits unasserted — in the antecedent of a conditional, under negation, or inside a question — as it does in a bare predication. My friend wanted to treat that derogatory content as something like a conventional implicature or presupposition riding on top of a neutral descriptive core, an updated version of the "two-tiered" treatment Dummett once suggested for a word like "Boche," which would explain the projection pattern in just the way non-at-issue content usually projects. I objected that this still lets a sentence like "Hans is a Boche" come out true, if rudely put, whenever Hans is German, which seems to concede far too much to the bigoted practice the word encodes; I pushed instead for something closer to Hom's combinatorial externalism, on which the derogatory content is built directly into the slur's truth-conditional meaning, as part of a complex, externally fixed (and, happily, uninstantiated) property. Neither of us could give a satisfying account of how reclaimed, in-group uses of a slur shed their derogatory force without the word simply becoming a different, neutral term.

Please argue for the non-at-issue side of this dispute — conventional implicature or presupposition, whichever you find more defensible — by making the strongest case that the projection and reclamation data are better explained by treating a slur's offensiveness as non-truth-conditional content than by building it into the asserted meaning, the way Hom-style views do.
