I've been going down a rabbit hole reading old rationalist-blog posts about anthropic reasoning (the doomsday argument, Bostrom's stuff, etc) and I keep tripping over the same spot. As I understand it, the "self-sampling assumption" (SSA) is what makes the doomsday argument work: you reason like you're basically a random sample among all humans who will ever live, and since your not living absurdly early in human history, that's evidence against humanity lasting some insanely long time. Then there's a rival principle, "self-indication assumption" (SIA), which supposedly cancels that whole update out, because it also weights hypotheses by how many total observers they predict -- more possible people means it was more likely you'd exist at all. Fine, neat trick. But then SIA has this famous "presumptuous philosopher" problem, where a philosopher can apparently scoop actual physicists on an empirical question (like which of two theories about the size of the universe is correct) using nothing but this observer-counting move, before anyone runs a single experiment. That feels like getting real physics for free out of pure probability bookkeeping, which smells like a bug somewhere.

Can you explain, on a fairly intuitive/non-measure-theoretic level, why the exact same observer-counting move that quietly cancels the doomsday update is also what makes the presumptuous philosopher go so wrong -- like, are these actually the same phenomenon wearing two different hats? And why does plain SSA mostly dodge that specific failure mode, even though it's the assumption responsible for the doomsday argument in the first place? Bonus if you can say whether SSA has some sneakier version of the same problem hiding in it -- I've seen people hint at that but never seen it actually spelled out.
