I spent my working life doing condensed matter physics, and now that I'm retired I've finally got time to read all the philosophy I always meant to get around to. Lately I've been stuck on Jaegwon Kim's causal exclusion argument against non-reductive physicalism (from *Mind in a Physical World*), and it's scratching an itch that's been there since my old "downward causation" arguments with colleagues about emergence, except done with way more rigor than we ever managed. The short version, as I understand it: if the physical domain is causally closed (every physical effect that has a cause has a sufficient physical cause), and we're not going to accept that mental and physical causes are routinely, systematically overdetermining the very same effects, then a mental property that's merely realized by, but not identical to, some physical property seems to get squeezed out of any causal work entirely - unless you just bite the bullet and reduce it to the physical after all.

The move I can't stop poking at is Stephen Yablo's reply, where he argues the mental stands to the physical the way a determinable stands to its determinate - the classic example being "red" versus some specific shade of red - and that the "proportionate" cause of an effect (he's got this pigeon-trained-to-peck-at-red-things example) is normally the determinable rather than the finer-grained determinate, so mental and physical properties were never really rivals for causal credit in the first place, any more than being red and being that particular shade are rivals. I ran this by an old colleague from my department (definately not a philosopher) and he balked immediately, on the grounds that red-and-its-shades is a tidy, single-dimension conceptual relationship, while a given mental state is supposed to be realizable by all sorts of heterogeneous physical stuff with nothing obviously in common - so calling that a "determinable/determinate" pair looked to him like question-begging with nicer terminology.

I'd like you to actually build that objection (or a better one, if you've got one) into a proper, rigorous argument that Yablo's proportionality move does NOT succeed in rescuing mental causation from Kim's exclusion argument - i.e., that even granting him the determinable/determinate framing, the mental property remains just as causally excluded by its physical realizer as it was before. Please don't just gesture at the multiple-realizability worry as a slogan and call it done; anticipate how Yablo, or someone defending him, would push back, and take the argument as far as it'll actually go.
