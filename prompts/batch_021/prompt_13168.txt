Okay, I need a neutral third party to referee something that happened at my rationalist-adjacent meetup last week (one of those groups that grew out of the old SSC meetup scene like fifteen years ago and never quite disbanded, if that gives you a sense of the vibe). One of our regulars, Dev, announced he'd done two weeks of strict daily cold showers and it completely killed his 3pm brain fog, and he was going around recommending it to everyone. Two other regulars, Owen and Sasha, are the group's resident epistemology nerds, and they ended up in a whole side-argument about whether the rest of us should update on Dev's report at all, given that all we have is his word for it. It went on long enough, and covered enough ground, that I wrote down roughly how it went, because by the end I honestly couldn't tell who'd gotten the better of it.

(1) Owen opened with: we shouldn't move our beliefs much on Dev's testimony alone, because Dev has no independent way of knowing it was the cold showers rather than placebo, novelty, or plain regression to the mean, and testimony that isn't backed by some independent reason to trust the reporter just isn't good evidence. (2) Sasha pushed back that this standard is way too strict for how belief actually works: almost everything we know beyond our own direct experience -- history, geography, most of science, which foods we're intolerant to -- rests on somebody's say-so that we never personally checked, and if default trust in testimony weren't rational absent a specific reason to doubt the speaker, we'd be entitled to believe almost nothing. Nobody has a specific reason to think Dev is lying or an unusually bad self-observer, so the default should be to believe him, at least provisionally. (3) Owen's comeback: that's not really a defense of trusting Dev's testimony as such, it's an argument that trust gets calibrated by track record, and we happen to have an excellent track record for this exact category of claim, since self-reported diet-and-energy stories are, empirically, dominated by noise and placebo. That's not blanket distrust of testimony, it's domain-specific calibration, and this domain has already earned its discount. (4) Sasha's counter: where does that "domain-specific track record" actually come from? Neither of them ran the studies showing self-experimentation reports are noisy -- they read that somewhere, which means they're relying on other people's testimony (researchers', journalists', whoever) to discount Dev's testimony. You can't use testimony to bootstrap your way out of needing testimony, so if unverified say-so is disqualified as evidence, the "it's noisy in general" claim being used against Dev fails that exact same test.

Both of them seemed to be onto something real and neither would concede a single point, and I sort of recall this being a whole actual debate in epistemology -- something about "reductionism" versus "anti-reductionism" regarding testimony, half-remembered from an SEP article I skimmed once and didnt retain that well -- so I think there might be real philosophical furniture behind both chairs here. Could you go through the four moves in order and say who's got the better of it at each one (and just say so plainly if it's a wash), and then close with one line, exactly like this: "Verdict: Owen" or "Verdict: Sasha" or "Verdict: Tie," followed by a sentence on what you think both of them are still missing even where each is partly right?
