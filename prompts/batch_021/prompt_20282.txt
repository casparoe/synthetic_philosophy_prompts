I've been reporting a longer piece on consciousness and keep getting stuck on those old split-brain experiments — the epilepsy patients who had their corpus callosum cut, and then Sperry and Gazzaniga ran all those lateralized tests on them (flashing an image to just one visual field, having the person point or draw with the opposite hand, that kind of thing). The way I understand it, once the hemispheres can't talk to each other, each one is running its own separate stream of consciousness that has no clue what the other is up to — which seems to mean these patients aren't really one person anymore, they're two people who just happen to share a skull and a body. And since its usually the left hemisphere that has language, I've been assuming "the person" you're actually talking to in these experiments, the one who can report on their inner life, is just whichever half controls speech, while the other half is basically a second, silent passenger that never gets a say in anything.

If thats basically right, a couple of things seem to follow that I want to check before it goes to print: first, that this must be true of all of us, not just split-brain patients — severing the callosum doesn't create two people, it just exposes a split that was there the whole time, hidden by good communication between the halves. Second, that this has huge implications for stuff like free will and moral responsibility, since "you" would only ever have had veto power over half your own brain, the rest just along for the ride. My editor thinks I'm overselling it but hasn't pointed to an actual hole in the reasoning. Is this basically the right way to read what these cases show, or am I running way ahead of the evidence?
