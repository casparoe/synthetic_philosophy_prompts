So this is a slightly awkward one, but here goes. I'm the token philosophy person on my university's IACUC -- they needed a non-scientist voting member and apparently "spends too much time on LessWrong" counted as a qualification. We've got a renewal up for a cephalopod lab studying nociceptive sensitization in octopuses: they injure one arm slightly, then track whether the animal gets hypersensitive and more guarded/defensive afterward, basically the same "pain memory" paradigm you'd run in a rat. The catch is that the whole point of the study is to catch the sensitization as it develops, so the PI wants a waiver from post-procedure analgesia for the first 48 hours, since the standard invertebrate analgesics would blunt exactly the signal they're trying to measure. Octopuses aren't covered by the Animal Welfare Act at all (its basically warm-blooded animals only), so we could legally rubber-stamp this in five minutes, but the university adopted its own voluntary cephalopod welfare policy a few years back -- I think partly because the EU went the other way and actually extended its research-animal protections to cephalopods specifically -- so now it's genuinely on us to decide.

Where it's gotten stuck is that two committee members, both cognitive science faculty, have started arguing from theories of consciousness rather than from behavior, and they don't even agree with each other. One says that absent evidence of an actual higher-order representation -- a state that represents the animal as itself being in the nociceptive state, Rosenthal-style, rather than just registering and reacting to it -- we have no real grounds for calling this "pain" in the morally loaded sense, and octopuses, for all their famous arm autonomy, don't have anything resembling the prefrontal-style machinery these theories tie higher-order monitoring to. The other agrees that's the right family of theory to reach for, but wants a dispositional bar rather than an occurrent one, more Carruthers-style, so that the octopus's flexible, context-sensitive control over the injured arm could count as evidence of the right disposition even without anything language-like going on upstream. A third member wants to just invoke the precautionary principle and skip the theory fight entirely, which I'm sympathetic to, but it feels like dodging the actual question we were asked to rule on, and I guarantee the HOT people will notice. This whole fight reminds me of the Rethink Priorities moral-weight stuff that did the rounds on the EA forum a while back, except now I'm the one who has to put an actual recommendation in writing.

I have to circulate the committee's decision memo before we vote Thursday, and it needs to land somewhere concrete -- approve, reject, or approve with specific conditions, like a shortened waiver window or mandatory analgesia past some fixed timepoint -- and it can't just assert the precautionary principle and move on. It has to actually engage the higher-order-theory argument on its own terms, including giving the losing side's best version of their case, not a strawman. Could you draft that memo for me -- a page or so, something I could actually put in front of the committee?
