For the past several years, an old friend from graduate school -- now an economist at a regional bank -- and I have kept up a habit I've never quite known what to make of. Several times a year we bet against each other, for real money, on some uncertain question: lately, whether a particular central bank will cut rates twice more before year's end; before that, an election margin; before that, whether a mutual friend's startup would survive to its next funding round. The subject changes, but the procedure never does. We don't simply state a number and shake on it -- we spend a long evening walking each other through everything we each know and believe, pushing back and cross-examining, until neither of us can produce a fact, a data point, or a line of reasoning the other hasn't already heard, absorbed, and answered. By the end of the night, I can usually lay out his case about as well as he can, and he mine. And yet we still walk away wanting opposite sides of the bet, at odds we each privately think favor us. For what it's worth, neither of us ever feels, in the moment, like we're bluffing or holding something back; it really does seem as if we've each just landed somewhere different.

I described this ritual recently to another friend, who trades for a living, expecting her to find it charming. Instead, she thought the whole arrangement didn't quite add up. Her argument was that if neither of us is withholding some fact or angle, and each of us takes the other's judgment seriously enough to sit through that kind of cross-examination, then the mere fact that he still wants to bet against me is itself evidence I should be folding into my own view -- and the same in reverse for him -- so that, in a case as clean as ours, we should end up either agreeing or refusing to bet at all. Since neither has happened across several years of this, she suspects one of us isn't being quite as forthcoming as we think, even if neither of us could say what we're hiding.

That verdict has stuck with me. I cover cognitive science and economics for a living, and I've even written a piece or two about prediction markets, so I expected to have a tidy answer ready for her -- but I don't. I'd like to know whether her reasoning actually holds up. If it does, I want to understand what that implies about two people who seem to have genuinely put everything on the table and still don't converge. And if it doesn't hold up, I'd like to know exactly where it breaks down, because simply telling her that reasonable people can just see things differently feels like too easy an answer to hand back.
