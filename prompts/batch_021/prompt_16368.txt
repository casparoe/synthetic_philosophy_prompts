Two friends of mine, Elena and Marcus, got into a running argument at dinner after finishing a popular book on famous scientific errors.

(1) Elena's opening point was that the history of science should make us wary of whatever theories are currently in favor: phlogiston (the substance supposedly released in combustion), caloric fluid, and the luminiferous ether were all treated as basically correct descriptions of reality by the leading scientists of their day, and all turned out not to exist. Since there's no reason to think our own moment is special, she concluded that today's most celebrated theories are probably headed for the same fate, so we should treat them as useful instruments rather than believe they are true.

(2) Marcus objected that this can't be the whole story, because current fundamental theories have made precise, novel predictions that were confirmed only much later — general relativity's prediction of gravitational waves, unconfirmed for about a century, and the Higgs boson, proposed decades before it was detected. It would be a remarkable coincidence for a theory to get that kind of thing right if it weren't tracking something real, so this predictive success is evidence the theories are at least approximately true.

(3) Elena replied that this doesn't distinguish today's theories from the discarded ones, because nineteenth-century optics pulled off the same trick. Using Fresnel's wave theory of light — built entirely around the idea of a literal, mechanical ether — Poisson derived the seemingly absurd consequence that a perfectly circular obstacle should cast a shadow with a bright spot at its exact center, and offered this as a refutation; when the spot was found exactly where predicted, it became one of the most celebrated confirmations in the history of optics. Yet there is no ether. So novel, precise, confirmed predictions were produced by a theory that was fundamentally wrong about what exists, which means predictive success can't be the mark of truth.

(4) Marcus answered that the example actually helps his case once you look at what survived. What carried over from ether-based optics into the theories that replaced it wasn't the picture of a mechanical medium, but the mathematical relationships between the quantities involved — equations that reappeared, reinterpreted, inside Maxwell's theory and then relativity. So the lesson isn't that predictive success guarantees a whole theory is true, but that it tracks whichever structure within the theory is doing the real work, and that structure is what survives even as the associated picture of hidden stuff — ether, phlogiston, caloric — gets thrown out.

Elena felt there was something evasive about this last move but couldn't quite say what. Could you go through the exchange point by point and say who has the better of the argument at each step, rather than just declaring an overall winner, and note anything important that neither of them raises?
