For my seminar on metaphysics this term, we spent a session on Max Black's 1952 paper on the identity of indiscernibles, which stages its argument as a dialogue between two speakers debating whether the Principle of the Identity of Indiscernibles — Leibniz's claim that no two distinct things can share literally all their properties — can survive a rather strange counterexample: a universe containing nothing but two spheres, exactly alike in every intrinsic respect (same size, same composition, same age, and so on), separated by some fixed distance, with nothing else in existence to break the symmetry. What has stuck with me since that class, and which a couple of us in the program have been arguing about ever since, is a point our professor raised almost in passing: even the one relational property that might seem to distinguish the spheres — each one's being some distance from the other — is had by both of them equally and symmetrically, so it cannot be what makes one sphere this one and the other that one. The usual escape routes, positing some brute, unanalyzable "thisness" or simply denying that such a symmetric world is really possible, both feel like they dodge the question rather than answer it, and I would like to sit with the puzzle in a more concrete form than another round of seminar discussion allows.

Rather than write it up as commentary, I would like to try approaching it through fiction. Could you write a short story (roughly 1,000–2,000 words) built around a genuinely symmetric scenario in Black's spirit? The two entities involved need not literally be spheres, and the setting need not literally be the whole universe, but the symmetry should be real rather than a narrative shorthand for "twins": the two should share every intrinsic and relational property there is to share, and yet be, by anyone's lights, two rather than one. I would like the story to sit with the specific difficulty that no describable feature, however fine-grained, could ever pick one of them out over the other, rather than quietly smuggling in some asymmetry — a difference in vantage point, a name, a scar — that would let a reader tell them apart after all. Please still give the piece real characters and a real ending; I would like a story with dramatic stakes, not a dressed-up thought experiment.
