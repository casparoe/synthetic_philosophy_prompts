So I've been auditing a continental philosophy class this semester, kind of out of spite (someone at a rationalist meetup implied we only ever read analytic stuff, and I got defensive about it), and we just spent two weeks on the "Bad Faith" chapter of Being and Nothingness. I went in expecting the pop-philosophy version of Sartre -- the waiter who's a little too waiter-y, "existence precedes essence," "condemned to be free," all that -- but the actual argument turns out to be way weirder than the Wikipedia summary. The whole point of bad faith is to let you dodge how radically free and responsible you actually are, without technically lying to yourself about it, and the mechanics of how that's even supposed to work are a genuine puzzle: if I'm deceiving myself, I have to already know the truth in order to hide it from myself, so who exactly is doing the hiding? Sartre explicitly shoots down the Freudian move of positing a separate unconscious "censor" that represses things for you, because the censor would itself have to recognize an impulse as dangerous before it could repress it -- so you haven't explained the self-deception, you've just relocated the same paradox one level down and given it a fancier name. His own answer is that bad-faith consciousness is "metastable" -- it has to hold two incompatible self-understandings at once without ever fully committing to either, since really believing either one would collapse the whole trick.

This is basically our "belief in belief" thing, except Sartre thinks the puzzle is sharper then Yudkowsky ever made it out to be, and I'd rather see it dramatized properly than have it explained to me again. Could you write me a short story (a few pages, whatever length it needs) built around a character who's in bad faith in this specific technical sense -- where the prose itself has to pull off the metastability, so that by the end the reader can't point to a clean moment where the character "really" knew versus "really" didn't, and that ambiguity is doing actual work rather than just being a symptom of fuzzy writing. Only rule: no café waiters and no first dates with a hand being quietly ignored -- those two examples are so famous at this point that leaning on either would feel like copying off the cheat sheet. I want a situation that earns the same logical shape honestly, ideally one that wouldn't announce itself as "a Sartre illustration" if you didn't already know what you were looking for.
