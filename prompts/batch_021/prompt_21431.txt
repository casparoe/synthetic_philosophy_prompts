A close friend of mine signed up with Alcor last month, and after several long conversations about it I have started seriously weighing whether to do the same before the premiums on the life-insurance policy I would use to fund it climb any higher. Before committing to this, though, I want to work out whether the underlying idea is even coherent, which has sent me back to Parfit's Reasons and Persons; the last time I engaged with any of this seriously was in an undergraduate class covering his work on personal identity, where I mostly took his conclusions at face value rather than pressing on them.

What has unsettled me on rereading it is how closely a successful cryonic revival would resemble Parfit's Branch-Line Case rather than ordinary survival through a long sleep. Since it isn't clear that future technology could ever repair preserved tissue directly, much serious discussion of revival appeals instead to scanning the preserved brain's structure and emulating it on different hardware, which looks less like waking up and more like his malfunctioning Teletransporter, where the traveller on Earth and the Replica on Mars briefly coexist and so cannot, strictly, be identical. Parfit's own response to this kind of case was that it needn't be so troubling, since identity is not what matters in survival; what matters is Relation R, psychological continuity and connectedness, which can hold even when its cause isn't the usual one. But that seems to reopen exactly the complication his original, single-successor version of the Teletransporter was designed to avoid: nothing stops a preserved brain from being scanned and emulated more than once, so Relation R could end up holding between me and several future successors at once, and I'm not convinced Parfit's own reductionism entitles him to treat that as just as good as ordinary survival, rather than as something closer to ordinary death that merely leaves behind someone psychologically continuous with me. An animalist who ties personal identity to the continued life of a single biological organism would presumably say that none of the emulated successors are me at all, however psychologically continuous they are.

I would like you to write an essay that actually works through this question rather than just cataloguing both sides. Engage seriously with Parfit's reductionist case for why identity is not what matters, with the branching worry as it specifically applies to emulation-based revival, and with at least one serious non-reductionist or animalist objection to his view -- but don't stop at laying out the considerations: end by taking a definite stand on whether, and under what conditions (biological repair versus emulation, a single successor versus several), a future revived successor should count as me in the sense that actually matters. That verdict is what I need in order to decide whether any of this is worth the money.
