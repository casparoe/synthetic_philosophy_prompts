So I went down a bit of a Frank Ramsey rabbit hole last week (the Cambridge guy who did foundational work in probability, economics AND math before dying at 26) and reread that "Epilogue" essay of his, the one where he flatly refuses to be humbled by the vastness of the universe. From what I remember he says his mental picture of the world is drawn "in perspective, not to scale," with people huge and the stars "as small as threepenny bits," and that he's just not impressed by size and duration the way Russell and that whole generation of cosmic-pessimist writers were, because stars can't think or love and people can. It's a stubbornly cheerful, human-scale response to a universe that doesn't care about us.

What's been nagging me since is how well this sets up a clash with longtermism and the current EA/AI-safety obsession with the astronomically large number of possible future people (and future digital minds) - the whole Bostrom "astronomical waste" style argument that what matters most is optimizing for a future that could dwarf the present by many, many orders of magnitude, even if that means leaning on tiny probabilities of huge payoffs (Pascal's-mugging-adjacent reasoning that a lot of people find pretty uncomfortable). And it's not like Ramsey was a stranger to "how much weight should the present give the future" as a technical question either - his 1928 savings paper is literally where the "Ramsey rule" for discounting comes from, the same equation people like Stern and Nordhaus still fight over when they argue about climate policy.

Given his actual views on probability, his own discounting model, and that whole "threepenny bits" attitude toward cosmic scale, what's your best guess at how Ramsey would've reacted to longtermism if you dropped him into a present-day EA forum? Would he recognize it as basically a natural extension of his own thinking about the future, or does his personality/philosophy actually make him a pretty devastating critic of it?
