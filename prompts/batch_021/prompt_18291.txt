A few of us got into a rather heated argument last night after a seminar on testimony and trust, which eventually drifted into the old chestnut of whether lying could ever be justified in extremis — the standard case being something like hiding refugees from people who mean them harm. One friend, who is writing a thesis touching on patristic ethics, insisted that Augustine's position is not the comparatively modest claim that lying is generally wrong, but a genuinely exceptionless prohibition: even a lie told to save an innocent life is sinful, because for Augustine the wrongness of a lie lies entirely in the liar's intention to assert what he believes false in order to deceive, and not at all in whatever harm or benefit follows from it. I pushed back that this seems hard to square with scripture that Augustine himself treated as authoritative — most obviously the Hebrew midwives in Exodus, who lie to Pharaoh to protect the Israelite infants and are explicitly rewarded by God for it, and Rahab, who lies to the king of Jericho's men to protect the Israelite spies and is later held up in Hebrews and James as an exemplar of faith. My friend maintained that Augustine has a considered answer to this in De Mendacio and Contra Mendacium, but neither of us could reconstruct the argument convincingly on the spot, and we ended up talking past each other.

Could you lay out, as precisely as you can, how Augustine actually handles the apparent tension between his absolutism about lying and these scriptural cases in which lying seems to be met with divine approval? I'm especially interested in whether he denies that the midwives and Rahab were rewarded for the lie itself, as opposed to some other feature of what they did, such as their mercy or faith, and how that move is supposed to be consistent with his general claim that a lie's sinfulness is located in the deceptive intention itself, independent of the ends it serves or the good character otherwise displayed by the person telling it. If it's relevant, I'd also be curious whether his distinction between lying outright and merely withholding or being silent about the truth is doing any real work here, since part of my friend's account leaned on the idea that Augustine leaves more room for maneuver than a flat "no lying, ever" slogan would suggest.
