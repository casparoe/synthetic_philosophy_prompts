I teach moral philosophy, including a graduate seminar on consequentialism and decision-making under uncertainty, which is presumably why a private foundation that funds global-health interventions recently invited me onto the external ethics panel that vets its larger grants. The panel is currently weighing a proposal to fund the deployment phase of a gene-drive project: a self-propagating genetic modification designed to collapse the population of the mosquito species chiefly responsible for malaria transmission, to be released across a multi-country release zone in West Africa. It would be the panel's first recommendation on an open release rather than a lab-confined trial, which may explain why the disagreement has gotten so heated. The applicants' case for the near term is fairly strong: drawing on existing vector-control trial data, they project a substantial, well-evidenced fall in malaria deaths across the release zone over roughly the following decade, expressed as a cost per disability-adjusted life-year that compares favorably with the bed-net distribution the foundation also funds.

Where the proposal grows noticeably vaguer is everything beyond that decade. Because the drive keeps propagating on its own once released, and because the target mosquito's place in regional food webs and in the transmission of other diseases is genuinely contested in the entomological literature, the modeling team concedes that it cannot bound the sign of the intervention's downstream effects, let alone their magnitude. Three of us on the panel think that concession should weigh heavily against approving the release phase now; three others think it can't be doing the work we want it to, since parity of reasoning would seem to indict bed nets, vaccination campaigns, and nearly everything else the foundation funds, all of which also set effects rippling into a future none of us can model.

I would like help thinking through which side has the better argument before I draft something for the board, which votes in three weeks. Could you write the panel's advisory memo? It should reach an actual recommendation -- approve the release phase, fund only an extended monitoring and modeling phase instead, or approve with specific named conditions -- together with a proposed timeline, rather than laying out both sides and stopping there. I would also like it to state a general principle the panel could reapply to future gene-drive proposals, not merely a verdict tailored to this one case. And whatever you conclude, please engage honestly with the strongest objection to it: if you recommend against the release phase, explain why that reasoning does not equally condemn bed nets and vaccines; if you recommend approval, explain why the foundation's usual insistence on well-modeled cost-effectiveness is not quietly being dropped whenever the near-term numbers look good enough.
