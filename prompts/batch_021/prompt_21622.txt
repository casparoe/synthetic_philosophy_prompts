So for my metaphysics seminar this week we had to read David Lewis's "New Work for a Theory of Universals" alongside a chunk of Armstrong's "What is a Law of Nature?", and now I'm kind of stuck trying to sort out how their objections to each other actually stack up. Lewis's whole picture is that laws are just whichever regularities end up as theorems in the deductive system that best trades off simplicity against strength (plus fit, once chance gets involved) when you're summarizing the entire pattern of particular, non-modal facts about the world — so nothing "governs," laws are more like an efficient description after the fact. Armstrong instead wants laws to be relations of natural necessitation, N(F,G), between universals, where this relation is supposed to be what makes it true that all F's are G's, rather than that just being a massive coincidence.

Here's where I get lost: Lewis has this objection to Armstrong that I think is sometimes called the "inference problem" (though I might be misremembering the name) — basically, even granting that there's some real relation out there called necessitation, nothing seems to stop N(F,G) from holding in a world where you still get some F that isn't a G, so just slapping the label "necessitation" on the relation doesn't actually earn you the inference to the regularity. But then Armstrong has a kind of comeback aimed back at Lewis (or at least people reading him this way do): if a law is nothing more than a regularity that happens to fall out of the best system, the law can't really explain it's own instances without circularity, since those instances are literally part of what makes it a law in the first place — and on top of that, Lewis needs this primitive, unreduced notion of "perfectly natural properties" just to stop every gerrymandered regularity from counting as simple, which looks suspiciously like he's smuggling in some of the non-Humean structure he's supposed to be rejecting.

So are these two objections basically mirror images of the same underlying problem, where both theories turn out to need some extra ingredient to close a gap that can't actually be closed, or are they different in kind? And if you had to say which theory comes out in better shape once its own objection is taken fully seriously, which would you pick and why? Sorry if this is a lot, I've been going back and forth on it for my response paper and would love an outside take before I commit to an argument.
