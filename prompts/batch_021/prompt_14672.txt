I'm a PhD student in formal epistemology drafting a dissertation chapter on van Fraassen's Reflection Principle — the constraint, from "Belief and the Will," that one's current credence in a proposition A, conditional on the supposition that one's credence in A at some later time will be x, must itself equal x. I know the principle's standard motivation (the diachronic Dutch book argument) and its standard escape clauses: van Fraassen himself allows that Reflection can fail if the agent anticipates becoming irrational, losing evidence she currently has, or being manipulated, and I'm familiar with how the Sleeping Beauty literature exploits exactly that memory-loss loophole.

What I need is something less well-worn. Construct a case in which (i) the agent has no reason to doubt her own future rationality, (ii) she anticipates losing no evidence and undergoing no manipulation, and (iii) she fully expects her later credence to result from ordinary Bayesian conditionalization on some specific new item of evidence she will obtain — and yet deferring now to that anticipated future credence still seems intuitively wrong, or at least in real tension with some other independently plausible epistemic norm (for instance, a norm governing peer disagreement or higher-order evidence about one's own reliability). Spell the case out in enough detail that the relevant probabilities are fully determinate, identify exactly which premise in the standard defense of Reflection your case is meant to strain, and suggest how a committed defender of the principle might try to explain it away.
