Back when I was working through Yudkowsky's Sequences -- the "Belief in Belief" and "Religion's Claim to be Non-Disprovable" essays especially -- I came away with a fairly settled heuristic: when a claim retreats from making testable predictions about the world to being merely a "different kind of claim" that evidence can't touch, that retreat is itself evidence that the speaker doesn't really believe the claim in the sense that pays rent in anticipated experience, but is instead protecting some practice, identity, or community that has little to do with the literal truth of the proposition. Rereading Wittgenstein's 1938 lectures on religious belief recently has complicated this heuristic for me. Wittgenstein doesn't seem to be making the standard apologetic move of a believer dodging counterevidence; he's advancing a considered philosophical claim that belief in, say, the Last Judgement was never the kind of thing that competes with empirical predictions in the first place -- to the point of suggesting that someone who asserts there will be a Last Judgement and someone who flatly denies it aren't necessarily contradicting one another the way two people disputing an ordinary empirical matter would be.

I know this general line of thought has its own name and history in the secondary literature ("Wittgensteinian fideism," per Kai Nielsen's well-known critique), and that Nielsen and others have argued it lets religious language off the hook from rational assessment far too cheaply, by making it immune to the kind of evidential scrutiny we would apply to any other claim about reality. What I can't work out on my own is whether Wittgenstein's position, properly understood, actually collapses into exactly the non-disprovability dodge the Sequences warn against, or whether there is a real and defensible distinction between "this proposition is meant as an empirical hypothesis, but I am shielding it from disconfirmation" and "this utterance was never functioning as an empirical hypothesis, so talk of confirmation and disconfirmation doesn't even apply to it." Could you dig into this for me in some depth -- what Wittgenstein is actually committed to, as best as one can pin down given how elliptical the lecture notes are, how the remark about the believer and non-believer not contradicting each other is supposed to work, and whether the overall view can be defended against the charge that it is really just non-disprovability restated in more careful philosophical vocabulary?
