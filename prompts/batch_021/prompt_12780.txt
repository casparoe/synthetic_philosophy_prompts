Quick one from a rabbit hole I've fallen into. I'm reporting a piece on the intellectual prehistory of effective altruism, and I ended up re-reading two Keynes things back to back: "Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren," the 1930 essay where he predicts the 15-hour week and worries that once the "economic problem" is solved, humanity wont quite know what to do with itself, and "My Early Beliefs," the memoir he read to the Bloomsbury Group's Memoir Club near the end of his life, where he traces his and his friends' entire youthful ethics back to G.E. Moore's Principia Ethica -- specifically Moore's claim that the only things worth pursuing for their own sake are certain states of mind, basically personal affection and the enjoyment of beautiful things. Keynes says they built a whole quasi-religion out of that half of Moore and threw out the part about still respecting ordinary rules and conventions -- he's pretty unsparing about it in hindsight, at one point more or less calling his younger self and his friends immoralists.

Here's what's been rattling around in my head since: effective altruism looks like it grew out of the other half of that same tradition, the bit Bloomsbury didn't want -- the idea that welfare is impartial and addable-up across people, dialed up now into QALYs and expected-value calculations over billions of future lives. And earning-to-give in particular looks a lot like exactly the "purposive," almost pathological money-making instinct that Keynes, in the Grandchildren essay, hoped later generations could eventually stop indulging once it had done its economic job -- except EA has found a way to keep indulging it forever, just by pointing it at other people's welfare instead of your own.

So, hypothetically: you sit the older, chastened 1938 Keynes -- not the starry 22 year old Apostle he's describing in the memoir -- down across a table from a present-day effective altruist defending earning-to-give or longtermist cause prioritization. What does he actually say to them? Does he recognize the family resemblance to Moore and grudgingly respect the rule-following rigor his own essay says Bloomsbury lacked, or does he think they've resurrected exactly the disease he wanted his grandchildren cured of? I'd rather not get the answer that's just "Keynes liked leisure and beauty, EA is cold and utilitarian" -- I want something that actually sits with the fact that both movements can plausibly claim to be heirs of the same idea.
