I'm a retired geophysicist, and after forty-odd years of quietly assuming that the whole point of science is to build better and better maps of a reality that's out there whether or not we describe it, I've been reading Rorty in retirement (started with *Philosophy and the Mirror of Nature*, on my son-in-law's recommendation) and it's genuinely messing with my head. Every summary I run into just says he thinks truth is "relative" or that he's some kind of postmodernist who thinks nothing is really true, and he clearly thinks that charge is a cheap shot -- but reading the actual text, I can't reconstruct for myself why it's a cheap shot rather than just a dodge.

Here's specifically where I get stuck. Rorty seems happy to grant that the world causes our sensory states in all the ordinary ways -- he's not denying that light bounces off things and hits our retinas, or that a rock will hurt your foot if you kick it. So he's no idealist in the everyday sense. But he also wants to deny that our best sentences or theories are made true by "corresponding" to some pre-carved-up chunk of that causally-impinging world -- that's the antirepresentationalist move against the whole mirror-of-nature picture. I don't see how those two claims, yes to causal impact but no to representational fit, are supposed to hang together without just collapsing into either (a) a pretty boring instrumentalism that plenty of working scientists already half-believe anyway, which doesn't seem like what he's after, or (b) exactly the relativism about truth he insists he isn't defending. I mentioned this to an old colleague who dabbles in philosophy of science and she just said "welcome to forty years of that debate," which, thanks, very helpful.

So could you actually walk me through it, slowly, maybe with an example? I gather this hinges somehow on Davidson and the idea that causation and justification are two totally different relations we keep running together -- but I haven't read Davidson myself, and I'd like to understand how that move is supposed to let Rorty keep the world "causing us to believe things" without ever letting it "make our beliefs true," and why that isn't just a way of keeping the words separate enough to have it both ways.
