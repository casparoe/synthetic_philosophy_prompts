The question was: "In the original position, why does Rawls hold that the parties should reason according to the maximin rule rather than by maximizing expected utility, and how does this choice of decision rule bear on his argument for the difference principle over both a strictly utilitarian and a strictly egalitarian alternative?"

Here is the response I have to grade:

"Behind the veil of ignorance, the parties don't know their class position, their natural talents, or the content of their own conception of the good, and they are also barred from any general knowledge of how economies or human psychology work, so their choice is made from a position of total uncertainty rather than from any empirical foothold. Because no party knows in advance which position they will occupy, each is, in effect, equally likely to end up anywhere in the resulting distribution, so Rawls's maximin rule tells them to evaluate each candidate set of principles by the expected position of a representative person and choose whichever set maximizes that expected utility. This is what leads the parties to the difference principle rather than strict equality: since inequalities that raise everyone's position, including the worst-off, above what strict equality would guarantee make the expected position higher without costing anyone relative to equal division, a rational chooser has no reason to rule them out. The two principles are then ranked lexically, with equal basic liberties taking priority over the difference principle, and fair equality of opportunity in turn taking priority over the difference principle within the second principle, so that gains in wealth or income can never be used to justify restricting anyone's basic liberties."
