I am teaching a graduate seminar that moves from the philosophical literature on peer disagreement (Elga, Christensen, Kelly) into the epistemology of markets, and I would like a firmer grip on the no-trade theorem before I lecture on it. Rather than the partition-structure formalism, I want the underlying story: how one gets from common priors and common knowledge of rationality to the conclusion that two risk-averse, purely profit-seeking traders can never strike a deal on the strength of private information alone, and why this is really a cousin of Aumann's agreement theorem rather than an independent fact about markets. I would also like help with something that trips up my students: real markets exhibit enormous trading volume, and, separately, Grossman and Stiglitz argue that markets cannot be fully informationally efficient, since that would destroy the incentive to gather costly information in the first place. Are these two observations symptomatic of the same weak premise in the no-trade argument, or are they pulling in different directions -- and once the common-knowledge idealization is dropped, what exactly is left of the intuition that market prices can be trusted to track dispersed information?
