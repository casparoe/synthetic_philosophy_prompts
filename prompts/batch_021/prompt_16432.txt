I am taking a seminar this term on the philosophical foundations of quantum mechanics, and we have just spent two weeks on Bell's theorem. For the short response paper due on Friday, I want to push back a little on how the theorem is usually presented in our assigned readings (and in essentially every popular treatment) — namely, as forcing a choice between "locality" and "realism," as though these were two independent assumptions of equal standing that one simply gets to trade off against each other. Before I commit to this framing over five pages, I would like to stress-test the argument with someone, since my professor's office hours are not until next week.

Here is the core of what I want to argue. The general (stochastic, CHSH-style) derivation of a Bell inequality needs only what Bell himself later called "local causality" — a factorizability condition on the joint outcome probabilities conditional on the complete specification of the causal past, λ — and this condition is the actual content of "locality." "Realism," by contrast, is usually glossed as the claim that measurements reveal pre-existing determinate values, i.e., that some such λ exists in the first place. But on closer inspection this is not an independent premise smuggled in for convenience: in the deterministic version of the argument, it is exactly what the EPR reasoning derives from locality itself, given the perfect anti-correlations quantum mechanics predicts for aligned measurements on an entangled pair. If locality holds, whatever value I find on my side cannot have been affected by what happens on the far side; so, given the perfect correlation, the value must already have been fixed beforehand. "Realism" falls out of "locality plus perfect correlations" rather than needing to be assumed on top of it, and in the fully general stochastic derivations it drops out of the argument altogether. If that is right, the popular "locality or realism, take your pick" framing is close to a false dichotomy, and Bell's theorem should really be advertised as a no-locality result, full stop. I know that superdeterminism and many-worlds are usually mentioned as ways of keeping locality, but it seems to me that they just trade the problem for something worse — a global conspiracy correlating particles with experimenters' choices, or giving up on single-valued measurement outcomes altogether — so I am not convinced they deserve equal billing as genuine "solutions."

Could you comment on whether this line of argument actually holds together? In particular, I would value your view on: (1) whether the move from the EPR correlations to "realism" really is as assumption-free as I am claiming, or whether there is a hidden premise — something like counterfactual definiteness, or the EPR "reality criterion" itself — doing unacknowledged work; (2) whether I am being too quick to dismiss superdeterminism and many-worlds rather than treating them as genuine ways of retaining locality; and (3) how the no-signalling theorem ought to bear on all this — if entanglement correlations cannot be used to send a message, is "nonlocality" even the right word for what Bell's theorem shows, or does my framing overstate the case in the other direction? I would rather have these holes identified now than after committing five pages to the wrong thesis.
