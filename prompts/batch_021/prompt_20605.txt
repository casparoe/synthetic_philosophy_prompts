heres jae's answer:

"The Special Composition Question asks under what conditions some objects add up to make a further, bigger object -- basically, when do a bunch of little things count as one big thing? Universalism, which David Lewis defended, says literally any group of objects composes something, no matter how random or scattered, so even my left shoe and the eiffel tower would compose a real object together. Van Inwagen disagrees with this, and his view is basically nihilism: composition never happens, so strictly speaking there's no such thing as tables, rocks, or cars, just particles "arranged tablewise" etc. He does carve out one exception though, which is living organisms, since he thinks the parts of a living thing really do compose a further object, because their activity sustains a single life. The argument from vagueness says that if composition only happened sometimes (like with a sand dune slowly forming grain by grain), there'd have to be some vague point where a new object suddenly popped into existence, but existence itself can't be a vague, fuzzy matter -- something either exists or it doesn't. So the only way to avoid vague existence is if composition is all-or-nothing, which is exactly what universalism says, and that's basically the argument Ted Sider came up with to defend universalism over restricted answers to the SCQ."
