I recently started as a research associate at a sovereign-debt advisory firm, and my first real assignment is a briefing note on Chinese-financed port projects in the Indian Ocean, starting with Sri Lanka's Hambantota Port: built with Chinese loans Sri Lanka couldn't service, it was handed over to a Chinese state-owned operator on a 99-year lease in 2017, a deal that's been called everything from 'debt-trap diplomacy' to a routine debt-for-equity workout. My director wants 'some historical perspective' for Thursday's client call and keeps invoking Adam Smith as the patron saint of free commerce over political entanglement -- but the part of Smith nobody in the office has actually read is his own chapter on colonies, most of which argues that combining sovereignty with a trading company's commercial interest makes for about the worst government imaginable, and that once the cost of defending them was counted, Britain's own colonies were a net loss to the nation rather than a source of profit. Taking that argument as the baseline rather than the free-trade slogans attached to his name: how do you think Smith would assess the Hambantota deal -- a variant of the chartered-company arrangements he condemned, or something importantly different since this was a commercial lease rather than a conquest, freely agreed to by the debtor government -- and what would his reasoning suggest Sri Lanka, or the next indebted government facing a similar offer, should actually do?
