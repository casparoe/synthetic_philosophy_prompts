So I'm in a phil of science reading group this semester and we just worked through Worrall's "Structural Realism: The Best of Both Worlds," where he leans heavily on Poincaré as basically the patron saint of the whole view, quoting that famous line about how science gives us relations between things rather than the things themselves. Curious, I went and dug up the actual preface to Science and Hypothesis (the 1905 Walter Scott Publishing translation) to see the line in its own context, and now I'm honestly more confused then when I started. Here's the first bit, right after Poincaré raises the threat of nominalism (he footnotes Le Roy specifically) - the worry that if geometry and mechanics are just conventions, the scientist ends up "the dupe of his own definitions":

"Under these conditions science would retain its certainty, but would not attain its object, and would become powerless. Now, we daily see what science is doing for us. This could not be unless it taught us something about reality; the aim of science is not things themselves, as the dogmatists in their simplicity imagine, but the relations between things; outside those relations there is no reality knowable."

Then a couple pages later, talking about how physical theories rise and fall over time, he says this:

"No doubt at the outset theories seem unsound, and the history of science shows us how ephemeral they are; but they do not entirely perish, and of each of them some traces still remain. It is these traces which we must try to discover, because in them and in them alone is the true reality."

Every secondary source I've read treats these two bits as basically one continuous thought, but they feel like they're doing different jobs to me, tbh. The first one reads like damage control against nominalism - "relations" as the bare minimum you're still allowed to call real once you've already conceded that space and magnitude are just frameworks we impose on the world. The second sounds like an actual historical-continuity argument, the sort that gets repurposed decades later to answer the pessimistic meta-induction: specific structure (equations, roughly) surviving from Fresnel's theory into Maxwell's and therefore probably tracking something mind-independent. What I can't work out is which reading Poincaré's own words actually license. Does "outside those relations there is no reality knowable" mean there's still some deeper nature to things that we just can't access (basically Worrall's epistemic structural realism), or is positing that further nature already doing zero work for him, in which case he's closer to the harder ontic line Ladyman and French took later, where structure isn't standing in for some hidden furniture but just is all there is? Or am I overreading five breezy pages written for a general audience, and the whole lineage back to Poincaré is a bit of a retrospective invention? Would genuinely appreciate someone walking through the actual logic of the passage instead of the usual one-line gesture papers make toward it.
