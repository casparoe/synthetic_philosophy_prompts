I have spent the past couple of weeks working back through Eliezer Yudkowsky's old quantum physics sequence on LessWrong, which a friend recommended after we got into an argument about entanglement at a dinner party. The post on Bell's theorem is the one that has stuck with me most: as I understood the argument, the "spooky action at a distance" framing is really an artifact of the collapse postulate. Once you accept many-worlds and stop assuming that a measurement yields one single, determinate outcome, the correlations between entangled particles no longer require any faster-than-light influence to explain — the appearance of nonlocality was only ever generated by insisting there is a single classical fact about what each particle "really" did before anyone looked. Paired with the no-communication theorem, which shows the correlations can never be used to send an actual signal, this struck me as a fairly complete dissolution of the whole mystery.

What I cannot square is that physicists and philosophers of physics who are perfectly familiar with many-worlds keep writing papers, decades after Everett, that treat quantum nonlocality as a live and unresolved foundational question rather than something Bell settled once you make the right interpretive choice. A friend of mine who is finishing a physics PhD waved this off with "it's more complicated than that" but could not tell me exactly where the many-worlds argument is supposed to break down, which only made me more suspicious that this is really just a case of the mainstream not having caught up with an argument that has been sitting on a rationalist blog since 2008. This leaves me wondering: is there an actual technical gap in the "many-worlds dissolves Bell nonlocality" story, or is the persistence of nonlocality as an open topic in physics mostly a matter of academic inertia and interpretive conservatism rather than a genuinely unresolved problem?
