I'm on the ethics committee at the small hospice where I volunteer, and we've got a case that's turned into a bigger argument than expected. Last month one of our physicians, when a patient with stage IV pancreatic cancer asked directly how much time she probably had left, said something like "that really depends a lot on you and your outlook -- I've seen patients with worse numbers than yours stick around way longer than expected, so I wouldn't rule out more time than the averages suggest." Nothing in that sentence is technically false, but it was clearly meant to leave her more hopeful than the actual statistics support, and it seems to have factored into her putting off finishing her advance directive and skipping a visit to a sibling she'd said she wanted to patch things up with "eventually." About half the committee thinks this is just normal, humane bedside manner, and points out hope itself sometimes correlates with better outcomes; the other half thinks it's basically a soft violation of informed consent dressed up as kindness.

Here's the wrinkle: one of our lay board members, who did a philosophy minor a million years ago, keeps citing William James's "The Will to Believe" to defend the doctor -- her argument is that whether to feel hopeful here is exactly the kind of forced, living, momentous option James says can't be settled by evidence alone, so encouraging the belief isn't dishonesty, its just permissible faith running a little ahead of the facts, same as James thought was fine in cases like religious belief. I read that essay once as an undergrad and don't remember it well enough to know if that's actually a fair use of it, though I do remember Clifford's opposite line -- that its wrong to ever believe on insufficient evidence -- is basically the position James wrote the essay to argue against. We need a short memo circulated before next week's meeting that actually recommends something concrete: leave prognosis conversations to individual clinical judgment, adopt specific language staff are trained to use when asked directly, or something in between. Could you draft that memo for me? I'd like it to take a real position instead of hedging between "both sides have a point," and as part of the reasoning, I want it to actually work out whether the board member's use of James holds up rather than just dismissing it as a debate trick.
