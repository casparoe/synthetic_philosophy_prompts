Ok here's a weird one I've been chewing on. Boltzmann's actual response to Zermelo's recurrence objection was to bite the bullet and say the universe as a whole sits in equilibrium for vast stretches of time, and what we experience as "now" is just a rare downward fluctuation in entropy, a fluke island of order in an otherwise dead equilibrium universe (I think he worked this out with some guy named Schuetz or Schutz, can't remember the spelling). Thing is, that's basically the seed of the modern Boltzmann brain problem — except the modern version (the Dyson-Kleban-Susskind style argument, run in an eternal de Sitter background) turns his own logic back on him: if you're explaining our observations as a random fluctuation off equilibrium, its wildly more probable for the fluctuation to be small, like a single brain popping into existence with false memories, than for it to be a whole low-entropy galaxy full of mutually corroborating observers. So by his own statistical reasoning, whoever's having "Boltzmann's" experiences should expect to be exactly the kind of freak isolated fluctuation his framework was invented to explain away.

What I want is your best reconstruction of how the actual historical Boltzmann, with his actual commitments (the H-theorem, his realism about atoms, how he individuates a "state" and thinks about its probability), would've reacted if someone showed him this modern reductio. Would he just accept it and drop the fluctuation idea, or is there something in his framework that lets him resist the conclusion without borrowing moves that came later, like the past hypothesis or the eternal-inflation measure problem? Genuinely not looking for the standard contemporary answers to the Boltzmann brain puzzle, I want your sense of how he'd have argued it.
