I'm a PhD student in U.S. constitutional history, and a disagreement broke out in my Reconstruction-era reading group last week that I haven't quite resolved. We'd been going through Frederick Douglass's writings on the Constitution, and when I suggested we try extending his reasoning to a live legal fight today, one of the other students objected that this kind of exercise is basically just putting words in a dead man's mouth. I think it can be done responsibly, at least as a disciplined test of how far a documented interpretive method travels, so I'd like your take as a check on my own.

For context: Douglass began as a Garrisonian, holding that the Constitution was, in Garrison's words, "a covenant with death," and that the free states should dissolve the Union rather than remain party to it. Around 1851, under the influence of Gerrit Smith, William Goodell, and Lysander Spooner, he broke publicly with Garrison and spent the rest of his life arguing that the Constitution's actual language — as opposed to what he took Chief Justice Taney's Dred Scott opinion to rest on, namely the framers' supposed intentions rather than their words — was antislavery: he liked to point out that neither "slave" nor "slavery" appears anywhere in the text, and that the Preamble is written in the name of "We, the people," not "we, the white people." He carried that same willingness to read general constitutional and national principles against the narrower, more exclusionary common sense of his day into his later advocacy, in his "Composite Nation" speeches of the late 1860s, for continued Chinese immigration and naturalization, which he grounded in what he called a universal and indestructible right of migration rather than in any inherited ethnic idea of American peoplehood.

Given that record, how do you think Douglass would have come down on the birthright-citizenship dispute that has resurfaced in recent years — specifically, the argument now being litigated that children born on U.S. soil to parents without lawful immigration status are not "subject to the jurisdiction" of the United States in the sense intended by the Fourteenth Amendment's citizenship clause, and so fall outside it? Please don't just infer an answer from his general reputation as a moral progressive; reconstruct the actual textual, structural, and purposive moves he tended to rely on and show how they bear on this specific phrase, and tell me honestly if anything else in his record — his own strategic caution, his views on national assimilation, whatever you find — cuts against the position you end up attributing to him. I'd rather you commit to a considered best guess than hedge indefinitely, but the guess should be earned by the argument, not asserted by fiat.
