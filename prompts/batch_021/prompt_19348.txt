And here's the response from one of my stronger students, copied more or less verbatim:

"In the Seventeenth Discussion, al-Ghazali attacks the philosophers' claim that effects follow from their causes by a necessity so strong that even God couldn't break it. His move is to point out that all we actually observe when cotton burns after touching fire is that the burning follows the contact - we never perceive any necessary link between the two, only their constant conjunction. From this he concludes that fire isn't really the agent of the burning at all; the true agent is God, or angels acting under His command, and what we call the "nature" of fire is just God's habitual way of proceeding, which Ghazali calls custom, or 'ada. Because the connection is only customary and not necessary, it's not a logical contradiction for God to withhold the burning on some occasion, which is exactly what he says happened when Abraham was thrown into the fire and wasn't burned.

This is essentially the same insight Hume reaches centuries later: causation is never something we perceive, only the constant conjunction of two events, and the "necessity" we attach to it is something the mind imports rather than finds in the world. So Ghazali basically anticipates Hume's regularity theory of causation, which is remarkable for a theologian rather than an empiricist. It's this argument, in fact, that originates occasionalism as a school of thought in Islamic philosophy, by turning the philosophers' own logic against them."
