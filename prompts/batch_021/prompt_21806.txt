QUESTION: "David Lewis's counterpart theory and Kripke's account of transworld identity are rival attempts to explain what makes a sentence like 'Humphrey could have won the election' true. Lay out the disagreement between the two views, including Kripke's objection to counterpart theory, and say whether you find the objection convincing."

STUDENT ANSWER: "Lewis's counterpart theory and Kripke's view of transworld identity are both trying to explain what makes sentences like 'Humphrey could have won the election' true, but they disagree about whether Humphrey himself exists in other possible worlds. Lewis is a modal realist: he thinks possible worlds are concrete universes just as real as ours, and that every individual is 'worldbound,' existing in only one world. So strictly speaking Humphrey never wins any election in any possible world -- only a counterpart of his, someone who resembles him closely enough along whatever dimensions matter in context, wins in some other world. Since Humphrey himself never wins anywhere, Lewis's theory actually implies that 'Humphrey could have won' comes out false, which is part of why Kripke found the view so unsatisfying.

Kripke rejects this whole picture. He thinks we don't need any resemblance relation to say it's Humphrey in another world, because transworld identity is grounded in shared essential properties -- if something in another world has Humphrey's essential properties (roughly, his origins), it just is Humphrey, no matching-by-resemblance required. His famous complaint is that Humphrey himself wouldn't care whether some similar-but-distinct person won an election in a different world -- he'd only care about whether he could have won -- so Lewis's counterparts seem to be answering a question nobody was asking. I find Kripke's objection pretty convincing, since it captures the fact that our modal claims about people are about those people, not their doubles."
