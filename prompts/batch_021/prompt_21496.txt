I've been really into recreational coding for years (never studied any of this formally, just picked it up from books, videos, and stubbornly banging my head against problems), and lately I've gone down a rabbit hole on NP-hard problems, especially the knapsack problem, where you're trying to pick the best combination of items under some limit. From what I understand, in the worst case there's no known way to actually find the guaranteed best answer without checking a stupid number of combinations as things scale up, and all the approximation tricks and dynamic programming shortcuts that work fine in practice don't change that worst-case fact (unless P=NP, which apparently pretty much nobody thinks is true).

This got me and a couple of friends arguing for like two weeks now over something that started as a coding tangent and turned into an actual ethics headache. Say you're a dispatcher during some disaster and there's 30 people trapped in different spots, each needing a different amount of time and gear to save, and you don't have enough of either to reach everyone. Figuring out who to prioritize so you save the most people (or the most "total value," if your willing to weigh some lives more than others) is structurally just a knapsack problem with people as the items. If the truly optimal allocation is NP-hard to compute exactly, then even a dispatcher with perfect information and completely good intentions literally cannot work out the correct answer in the time available - not because they're ignorant of some fact about the world, but because the calculation itself has no shortcut fast enough to run.

So here's what I can't figure out on my own: is "the math is too slow to finish in time" actually treated as its own distinct problem for consequentialist/utilitarian type theories, separate from the usual "you can never know all the consequences of your actions" objection, or do people just fold those together? And does "ought implies can" (I think thats a Kant thing?) even stretch to cover this kind of "can," as in "can compute it in time," or is that a totally different sense of "can" than was originally intended? I don't have the philosophy background to know if this is already a settled debate under some name I've just never come across, so I'd love an explanation of how this actually gets carved up, if it does.
