Okay so for the last few weeks I've fallen down this whole game theory rabbit hole, just youtube videos and random wikipedia pages at midnight, never studied any of it formally. Last weekend a few of us were hanging out at my friend Marco's place and his girlfriend Denise brought up this thing she'd read about called the centipede game, where theres a pot of money that starts small and grows a bit every round, and on your turn you can either grab it right there (you get the bigger cut and the game just ends) or pass it to the other person, who then faces the same choice with a bigger pot. We actually played a tiny version of it for real with dollar bills, like ten rounds, mostly as a joke, and it turned into this whole argument between the two of them about what "rational" even means here, one that went on way longer than the game itself.

Marco's position was that if you actually reason it through with backward induction, the only rational move is to grab immediately on turn one. His logic: on the very last round, whoever's turn it is should obviously just grab, since there's no future round left to make passing worthwhile. Once that's settled, the person right before them should grab too, since they can already see the last grab coming, so passing would just hand over less money than grabbing does right now — and this unravels all the way back to turn one, where you should grab immediately. Denise said that's nuts and can't be what "rational" means, since if they'd actually played it that way they'd have walked away with way less than they could've gotten by passing a few times first, and no theory that tells you to leave free money on the table deserves to be called rational. Marco said she was missing the point, that it's a conditional argument: if both players are rational, and both know the other is rational, and both know that they know that, and so on without limit, then grabbing immediately is what falls out — and if real people don't do that, it just shows real people aren't perfectly and "commonly" rational in that sense, which nobody claimed they were. Denise's comeback was that this defense undoes itself: say they're five rounds into the actual game and she still hasn't grabbed — doesn't Marco's own reasoning say that fact alone should tell him she isn't the type of player his argument assumed, meaning the truly rational response right then is to expect her to keep passing, not to grab? So by its own logic the theory tells you to ditch the theory the moment the game is actually being played. Marco answered that she was blurring two different things, what a rational player would do at a round if it ever got reached, versus whether it actually gets reached, and that one early pass could just be a slip rather than proof she's a different kind of player, so it doesn't have to change what he believes about her at all. Denise's last word was that he doesn't get to have it both ways: either they really are common-knowledge-rational, in which case a pass that "shouldn't" happen basically can't happen and theres nothing to explain away, or he's quietly making room for slips, in which case common knowledge of rationality was never actually true between them, and his whole backward induction chain never gets off the ground in the first place.

Neither of them budged and I honestly couldn't tell you who actually had the better of it at each point, or whether they ended up talking past each other.
