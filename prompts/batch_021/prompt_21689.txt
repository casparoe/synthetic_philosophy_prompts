The question was: "Evidential cooperation in large worlds (ECL) is the proposal that altruistic agents have reason to cooperate with one another even in the total absence of causal contact between them. (a) [8 pts] Explain the core argument for ECL, and explain why the argument depends on evidential rather than causal decision theory. (b) [6 pts] Explain the role that the concept of superrationality plays in motivating the relevant notion of correlation between agents' decisions. (c) [6 pts] Raise one serious objection to the argument, and briefly assess how damaging it is."

Here is the student's answer, verbatim: "Evidential cooperation in large worlds (ECL) — originally introduced under the label 'multiverse-wide superrationality' by Eliezer Yudkowsky as an extension of his timeless decision theory — asks what altruistic agents should do given that the universe (or multiverse) is extremely large and contains vast numbers of civilizations with which we have no causal contact whatsoever: no communication, no trade, no way to influence them or be influenced by them. Even though causal decision theory (CDT) would say our choices are irrelevant to agents we cannot causally affect, ECL proceeds from evidential decision theory (EDT), on which the right action is the one that constitutes the best news about the world, including facts we cannot causally influence. If my decision procedure is relevantly similar to some distant civilization's, facing a structurally identical choice, then my choosing to act cooperatively toward a compromise of our values is evidence that they do too, since we are, in effect, running correlated computations. This is where Hofstadter's notion of superrationality comes in. In the one-shot prisoner's dilemma between two agents who reason in identical ways, a superrational agent recognizes that whatever conclusion its own reasoning reaches, the other party's identical reasoning will reach as well, so it should pick whichever action is best under the assumption that both parties choose the same way — which favors mutual cooperation over the mutual defection a naive causal analysis recommends. ECL generalizes this insight from a single symmetric pair to an entire large or infinite universe of civilizations, treating each civilization's decision to devote some resources to others' values as a move in a vast, distributed prisoner's dilemma. Because a merely large but finite universe might easily fail to contain even one close match for any particular agent's decision procedure, the argument only really goes through if space is actually infinite, which guarantees, on measure-theoretic grounds like Tegmark's, that exact duplicates of each of us exist somewhere and reason exactly as we do — this is what licenses talk of 'correlated' decisions in the first place. The strongest objection, I think, is practical rather than metaphysical: even granting the whole setup, we simply have no way of finding out what distant, causally disconnected agents actually value or how they would resolve a compromise, so it's unclear what action ECL actually recommends beyond a vague gesture at 'being more cooperative.'"
