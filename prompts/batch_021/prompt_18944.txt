After forty years spent modelling predator-prey cycles and the evolution of cooperative behaviour, I retired last year and have finally had the leisure to read game theory the way philosophers treat it, rather than the way it appears in population biology. This has been mostly rewarding, but I have become stuck on the backward induction argument as it applies to the centipede game. As I understand it: at the very last decision node, the player to move does better by stopping and taking the larger share on offer than by passing; anticipating this, the player at the second-to-last node does better stopping there too, since passing would simply hand the other player the opportunity to exploit them one move later; and this reasoning is supposed to iterate all the way back to the first move, so that a player who is rational, and knows the other to be rational, and knows that this is known, and so on, ought to stop the game immediately, forfeiting the far larger joint payoff that even a handful of further rounds of mutual cooperation would have produced.

It seems to me that this amounts to a reductio of expected-payoff maximisation as a definition of rationality, rather than a mere curiosity about a toy game: if applying the criterion with full consistency drives two players into an outcome that is worse for each of them individually than nearly any other outcome reachable in the tree, then the criterion has refuted itself and ought to be abandoned as the benchmark of rational choice in both economics and evolutionary biology. My suspicion was strengthened by a former colleague in experimental economics, who tells me that actual subjects in laboratory centipede games generally cooperate for several rounds before defecting, which I initially took as evidence that it is the backward-induction argument, and not the human subjects, that has gone wrong somewhere. I would be grateful for your view on whether this line of reasoning is sound, and if not, precisely where it breaks down.
