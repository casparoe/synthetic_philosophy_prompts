In my seminar on medieval Islamic philosophy, we've spent the last few weeks on Avicenna's flying man argument, as it appears in the psychology sections of the Shifa and the Isharat, alongside his account of how a rational soul comes to exist in the first place. On his view, a soul is individuated through its original attachment to a particular body, once that body's temperament (mizaj) reaches the balance needed to receive an emanation from the Active Intellect — part of why he rules out the transmigration of souls. I'd like to test that framework against a case it was obviously never built for. Suppose a person's brain is scanned in enough detail to run as a computation on non-biological hardware, and suppose the same scan is used to launch two separate copies running simultaneously. Would Avicenna's own commitments about temperament and emanation force him to deny that either copy could have a genuine rational soul, or flying-man-style immediate self-awareness, rather than merely a functional imitation of it? If we grant, for argument's sake, that they do have some kind of soul, how should his individuation doctrine handle the two-copies case — one soul, two, or an outcome that shows the doctrine was never equipped to answer such a question? I want your best reconstruction of where his actual commitments lead, not a modern gloss simply dressed up in his name.
