I'm plotting a contemporary novel around a father who has modeled his daughter's entire upbringing on John Locke's *Some Thoughts Concerning Education*, including Locke's own case for a private tutor over a school: that the vice a child absorbs from unsupervised schoolmates outweighs the worldly confidence a school might instill, provided the tutor can supply that confidence some other way. In the scene I'm drafting, his twelve-year-old daughter asks to trade his direct instruction for a popular self-paced online curriculum — pre-recorded lessons, auto-graded quizzes, a streak and a badge for every unit finished. I want the father's response grounded in Locke's actual arguments rather than reflexive suspicion of screens, and setting aside the "blank slate" material every reader already half-remembers from school. Locke was equally emphatic that a child's motive for learning must never be yoked to prizes and penalties, since that teaches the child to prize the reward rather than the thing learned — so does a badge system run afoul of that warning in a way ordinary parental praise wouldn't? And separately, does trading a tutor's undivided, temperament-specific attention for a standardized video course quietly forfeit the very rationale Locke gave for keeping a child home in the first place, even though it avoids literal schoolyard vice? I'd like your best reconstruction of how Locke would actually reason through this, not just a verdict.
