So I've been auditing a philosophy of science seminar this term (I'm actually a stats PhD student, but half my work is basically confirmation theory with extra Greek letters, so my advisor thought it'd be good for me), and we just spent two sessions on Hempel's raven paradox. I got the "official" version down fine: Nicod's criterion says a black raven confirms "all ravens are black," the equivalence condition says logically equivalent statements must share the same confirming evidence, and since "all ravens are black" is logically equivalent to "all non-black things are non-ravens," a random red herring technically confirms it too. Hempel's move is to just bite the bullet — yes it confirms, it's only "paradoxical" because we're smuggling in background knowledge about how many more non-black things than ravens there are, which makes the confirmation feel psychologically negligible even though logically it's real.

What threw me is that my professor mentioned, almost as an aside, that the statistician I.J. Good (the Bletchley Park guy) wrote a rebuttal to this — in the British Journal for the Philosophy of Science I think, sometime in the 60s, with a title that's basically a pun on "red herring" that I can't quite recall. As I understand it, Good built a scenario where, depending on your background assumptions about the raven/non-raven population, spotting a non-black non-raven can actually LOWER the probability that all ravens are black, not just confirm it by some tiny epsilon. If I'm getting that right, that's not a rounding error on Hempel's resolution, that's a totally different animal — it suggests there's no fixed, context-independent fact about whether a given observation confirms a hypothesis at all, which seems to gut the whole idea of "qualitative" confirmation Hempel was defending in the first place.
