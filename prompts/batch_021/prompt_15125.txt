So I'm about a third of the way into a new novel and I've hit a snag that's more philosophy-seminar than plot problem. My protagonist is a physicist who builds a rig that lets her link, for a few minutes at a stretch, into the senses of versions of herself in other branches of the multiverse -- not travel, just borrowed perception, like an unusually intense dream. Somewhere in the middle third I want a scene where she watches a branch-self of hers die (car crash, firing squad, still deciding) and I want the scene to hinge on an actual question instead of just vibes: should she grieve that the way she'd grieve her own death, or is that a category error?

I went down a rabbit hole a few weeks back reading about David Lewis's counterpart theory versus the more Kripke-flavored "transworld identity" side of things, and I think theres real dramatic ore buried in there. As I understand it (I'm not a philosophy person so correct me if I'm mangling this), Lewis says nothing is ever strictly identical across worlds -- your other-world self is at best a "counterpart," picked out by similarity, not literally you -- and the relation doesn't even have to be tidy: it's not guaranteed to be transitive or one-counterpart-per-world, so in weird enough cases you could apparently end up with two counterparts in the same world, or none at all. Kripke's side of it (the example that stuck with me involves Humphrey, the guy who lost the '68 US election) is basically that this is a bait and switch -- when we ask whether Humphrey himself could have won, we're not asking about some other guy who merely resembles him winning instead, and Humphrey would have zero reason to care about that guy's fortunes.

Could you write me a short story (something like 1500-2500 words, it doesn't need to resolve into a full chapter) built around my premise above, where the emotional climax actually turns on which of these two pictures is correct? I don't want the metaphysics delivered as a lecture between two characters at a whiteboard -- I want it load-bearing in the plot itself, and ideally that "zero-or-several-counterparts" wrinkle should do some real work, not just sit there as trivia. My writing group's first reaction to the pitch was "sounds like a Black Mirror episode," which I've decided to take as encouragement, but I'd like the story to actually earn the philosophy rather than just gesture at it.
