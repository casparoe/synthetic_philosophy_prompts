Over dinner last week, a friend and I spent nearly two hours arguing about a passage from G.E. Moore's Principia Ethica that she had been assigned for a graduate seminar. Moore asks the reader to compare two imaginary worlds — one exquisitely beautiful, one an unrelieved heap of ugliness — on the stipulation that no being will ever exist to perceive either one, and concludes that the beautiful world would still be the better of the two, so that beauty must count as good in itself rather than merely as a means to someone's enjoyment of it. I pushed back on the intuition that value has to bottom out in somebody's experience somewhere; she countered that Moore pairs this claim with an equally strong one a few pages later, that the richest goods available to us are complex wholes combining personal affection with the appreciation of beauty, each intensifying the other, rather than either good standing on its own. Neither of us budged, but the conversation has stuck with me, because it turns out to bear directly on a decision my own organization is about to make.

I sit on the volunteer board of a small regional land trust. Surveyors recently found an extraordinary limestone cave on a parcel we are about to acquire, with gypsum crystal formations that outside experts say are more delicate, and more spectacular, than anything else documented in the region. The trouble is that any regular human presence — even a handful of supervised research visits a year — would, through nothing more than ambient humidity and vibration, degrade the formations past recognition within a decade. We have, in effect, three options: (1) fully document the chamber and then seal it permanently, so the formations persist indefinitely but are guaranteed never to be seen again by anyone; (2) permit one tightly controlled research visit every few years, trading a slow, cumulative loss of the formations for a little ongoing expert documentation and testimony; or (3) spend no further money on the cave beyond basic sealing for safety, and redirect the remaining restoration budget to finish a long-delayed community pavilion at our wetland property, where local families and school groups already gather each year for modest but real communal events. The board is genuinely split, and the strongest objection to option (1) is that it asks us to spend a substantial share of a small nonprofit's budget protecting something that will, by design, never do anybody any good.

Could you draft the short decision memo I'll circulate to the full board before we vote on this next Wednesday? I'd like it to recommend one specific option — or a genuine variant of one — together with a concrete implementation timeline, giving reasons the other board members could actually engage with, and I'd like it to take the "why protect what no one will ever experience" objection fully seriously rather than brushing past it.
