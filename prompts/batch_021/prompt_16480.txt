In a series of letters to his mother Marianne, written between July and October 1961 (only his side of the correspondence survives), Kurt Gödel set out a private argument for personal immortality. Marianne was not a churchgoer and had raised Gödel and his brother as freethinkers, so the argument had to work by reason alone. In outline: the world is rational and meaningful, as Gödel took the intelligibility of mathematics and the exact sciences to show; human beings are given an enormous, largely untapped potential for development; and it would be irrational for a rational world to grant such potential only to let death extinguish it after a negligible fraction is realized. Some further life must therefore exist in which the rest is fulfilled. Unlike Gödel's ontological proof of God's existence, which he eventually allowed to circulate among friends in a fully formalized modal-logic version still debated today, this argument was never published or formalized; it survives only as discursive reasoning addressed to his mother.

The logician Hao Wang, who recorded extensive philosophical conversations with Gödel, reports that Gödel's deeper ambition in philosophy was to recast Leibniz's monadology as an exact theory, and a separate private list of Gödel's basic philosophical convictions from around 1960 includes the claim that the world we currently inhabit is not the only one in which we shall live or have lived. This invites comparison with Leibniz's own argument, in the Monadology, for the natural indestructibility of monads: because a monad is a simple substance without parts, it cannot be destroyed by the kind of decomposition that ends compound things, so it can only begin through creation and cease through annihilation -- an extraordinary act Leibniz does not take to occur in the ordinary course of nature.

Compare Gödel's argument for the afterlife with Leibniz's argument for the natural indestructibility of monads. Does Gödel's reasoning actually depend on Leibniz's picture of the soul as a simple, partless substance, or is it better understood as an independent argument from the rationality and purposiveness of the world as a whole, one that could succeed or fail whether or not monadology is true? Judged purely as philosophical arguments rather than biographical curiosities, which of the two holds up better against objection, and why?
