I am putting together this term's graduate seminar on formal approaches to distributive ethics, cross-listed with our economics department, and this week's session is devoted to Harsanyi's aggregation theorem — the result showing that if both individual and social preferences over lotteries satisfy the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms and the social ranking respects Pareto indifference, then social utility must be an affine, weighted-sum combination of individual utilities — together with the long dispute over whether this really vindicates utilitarianism, as Harsanyi claimed, or merely dresses up an independent ethical commitment in axiomatic clothing. My students can reconstruct the proof itself without much difficulty, but several of them left the last session unable to say how the standard objections to its utilitarian upshot differ from one another in kind and in severity: some attack the mathematics directly, others grant the mathematics but deny it carries the ethical weight Harsanyi wanted, and at least one attacks a premise that looks innocuous until you notice exactly what it rules out.

To help them sharpen this before Thursday, I would like you to rank the following five lines of objection to the claim that Harsanyi's theorem provides an axiomatic vindication of utilitarianism, ordering them from most to least damaging to that claim: (1) Sen's point that since each individual's von Neumann–Morgenstern utility function is unique only up to its own independently chosen positive affine transformation, the "weights" the theorem attaches to individuals are artifacts of an arbitrary choice of scale, absent some further and independently motivated theory of interpersonal comparison; (2) Diamond's objection that Pareto indifference, stated purely in terms of each person's ex ante expected utility, is blind to how risk is jointly distributed across people, so that it forces indifference between, say, a single coin flip that guarantees exactly one of two people ends up better off and a pair of independent flips that might leave both better off or both worse off, even though these differ starkly in the fairness of the risk imposed; (3) Weymark's contention that discussions of the theorem routinely run together two distinct Harsanyi results — the impartial-observer argument for utilitarianism and the more general social aggregation theorem — with the consequence that objections that bite hard against one leave the other largely untouched; (4) the purely conceptual worry that nothing in the proof forces us to identify von Neumann–Morgenstern utility, which merely represents an agent's preferences under risk, with welfare in the sense utilitarians actually care about; and (5) the impossibility results, associated with Mongin and later authors, showing that once individuals are allowed to hold different subjective probabilities over states of the world, Pareto and social Bayesian rationality can jointly be satisfied in a non-trivial way only under quite restrictive conditions, typically some form of shared prior.
