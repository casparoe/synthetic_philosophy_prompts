I've been chewing on this since a dinner party a couple weekends ago and I can't shake it. Two friends of mine got into it after a few glasses of wine — one of them is close to finishing a PhD in number theory, the other has a philosophy background and now does policy work. The number theorist mentioned, totally offhandedly, that she and pretty much everyone in her subfield treat the Riemann Hypothesis as true with something like 98 or 99 percent confidence, even though it's unproven and could in principle stay unproven forever. Her reasoning was a mix of "we've checked an enormous number of the zeros by computer and they all behave" and some heuristic stuff about how those zeros are distributed like eigenvalues of random matrices.

The philosophy friend just about laughed her out of the room, saying that's a confusion dressed up as rigor: the Hypothesis is either necessarily true or necessarily false, full stop, so there's no coin being flipped anywhere for that 98% to be tracking — she's really just describing a vibe and calling it a probability. I cover science for a living so I felt like I shouldve had a snappy comeback ready, but I didn't, and it's been bugging me since.

So here's what I keep going back and forth on: is my number-theorist friend doing something intellectually legitimate when she puts a number on her confidence in an unproven (maybe even unprovable-by-us) mathematical claim, or does my philosopher friend have a point that this is a category error, since nothing about the claim itself is uncertain, only our knowledge of it? And if the number theorist is on solid ground, what exactly is that 98 percent a measure of, if it's not the sort of thing that applies to coin flips or dice? I'd love to get past "reasonable people can disagree" to something with actual teeth.
