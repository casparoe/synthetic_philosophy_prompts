A friend of mine and I spent most of an evening arguing over whether imprecise credences -- rational belief states modeled as a set of probability functions rather than a single sharp number -- can genuinely guide action, or whether that kind of indeterminacy always collapses into arbitrariness the moment a real decision has to be made. Neither of us budged, so I am hoping next week's colloquium guest can give a sharper answer than we managed: her new book defends imprecise credences against exactly this worry, and her standard move whenever the conversation turns uncomfortable is to insist that imprecision "isn't fence-sitting, it's honesty about what the evidence supports." Draft six to eight questions that make that line progressively harder to sustain. I want at least one question built around a Dutch-book-style pair of sequential bets, each individually permissible relative to her credal set, whose combination jointly guarantees a loss -- pressing on whether she can avoid both accepting the loss and quietly acting as if her credences were sharp all along; one on the "dilation" phenomenon, in which conditioning on apparently relevant evidence can leave a credal set less precise rather than more, which sits awkwardly with her evidence-honesty line; and a closing question on what result or argument would actually convince her to abandon imprecise credences. For each substantive question, add a brief follow-up keyed to the reply she is most likely to give.
