A friend and I are applying to the same small, highly selective graduate program, and we spent an entire evening arguing about a strategic wrinkle in how it selects people. Based on patterns other applicants have reported, we're fairly convinced the program practices what admissions forums call "yield protection": it predicts which admitted candidates would actually enroll, and quietly rejects excellent applicants it expects would decline in favor of a more prestigious rival, just to keep its enrollment rate high. My friend argues that once you suspect this, the rational response is still to decide honestly, case by case, whether attending is worth it if you get in, since nothing you decide now can reach back and change a prediction the committee already acted on before you applied. I argue that if the prediction really tracks the kind of applicant you are, rather than anything you could still change, you're better off simply being, in general, the sort of person who'd commit to attending if admitted. That disposition is what earns the offer in the first place; "deciding fresh" once you already have it is exactly the mindset this kind of screening exists to catch. Neither of us budged. Does it actually matter, in a case like this, whether you reason about the decision in the moment or as a policy fixed in advance -- and if so, who's closer to right?
