The question (10 points total) was:

"Consider a committee of 101 voters who must choose a single integer budget level from {0, 1, ..., 100}. Each voter i has a single-peaked preference over this range, with an ideal point p_i, and prefers outcomes closer to p_i over ones farther away. The committee uses rule R: each voter reports a claimed ideal point, and R outputs the median of the 101 reports.

(a) [4 pts] Show that R is strategy-proof.
(b) [2 pts] Is R dictatorial? Explain.
(c) [4 pts] The Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem says that, under fairly general conditions, every strategy-proof social choice function with at least three possible outcomes must be dictatorial. Explain precisely why R does not contradict this theorem."

Here is the submission I need to grade:

"(a) Suppose voter i's true peak is p_i, and let m denote the median of the other 100 reports. If p_i < m, reporting anything ≤ m still yields output m, and reporting above m can only move the median toward or past that report, which is farther from p_i than m is — so misreporting never helps. The symmetric argument covers p_i > m, and if p_i = m the voter is already getting their ideal outcome. So no voter ever benefits from lying, and R is strategy-proof.

(b) R is not dictatorial: the output is the median of all 101 reports, so no single voter's report determines the outcome regardless of what everyone else reports. Changing any one voter's report can change the median (e.g., if that voter's report was at or near the current median), so every voter has some influence over the result.

(c) R doesn't contradict Gibbard–Satterthwaite because that theorem assumes an unrestricted domain — every logically possible strict ranking of the alternatives is a permissible preference — together with at least three voters and at least three alternatives in the range of the rule. Here voters are restricted to single-peaked preferences over the budget line, so the unrestricted-domain premise fails and the theorem simply doesn't apply to R. This escape route via restricting the domain was Arrow's own way of softening his impossibility result, and Gibbard and Satterthwaite later showed that the same kind of domain restriction defuses their theorem too — which is exactly what's happening with the single-peakedness assumption here."
