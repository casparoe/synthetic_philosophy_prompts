For thirty years I worked as an experimental physicist, mostly in quantum optics, including a long stretch on precision tests of Bell's inequality using entangled photon pairs. Since retiring I have finally had time to read philosophy properly, and I have been working slowly through David Lewis's doctrine of Humean supervenience: the claim that the entire world, laws of nature included, reduces to a vast arrangement of local, non-modal facts scattered across spacetime, with nothing over and above that arrangement and no necessary connections holding between distinct things. It came as something of a surprise to discover that entanglement, the very phenomenon I spent my career pinning down in the lab, is now treated as a standard objection to that picture.

As I understand the objection, which is associated above all with Tim Maudlin, the trouble is separability: the joint state of two entangled particles is not determined by the separate states of the particles together with their spatial relations, which is precisely the kind of local determination the Humean mosaic is supposed to guarantee. Some Humeans, following a strategy I have seen credited to David Albert and Barry Loewer and sometimes called wavefunction realism, try to hold the line by relocating the mosaic itself, treating the wavefunction as one perfectly local fact spread over a high-dimensional configuration space rather than ordinary three-dimensional space, so that what looks like a strange connection between two particles here turns out to be nothing but an unremarkable fact over there. A dispositional essentialist in the tradition of Ellis or Bird would presumably regard that move as far too clever by half, and conclude instead that properties like spin, or entanglement itself, carry their causal role as part of their essence, so that the correlations are necessary rather than a pattern that merely happens, so far, to hold without exception.

I would like to see this argued out properly, by two people who each actually hold a position worth having, rather than one side propped up just to be knocked down. Could you write me a dialogue between a committed Humean about laws — free to use the wavefunction-realist strategy above, or whatever else seems stronger — and a dispositional essentialist, in which they argue specifically about whether Bell-inequality violations count as evidence against Humean supervenience or as merely an unusual case it can still absorb? Set it, if you would, during the coffee break after a physics-department colloquium on Bell tests, a scene I find I can picture rather easily, and please have each of them genuinely respond to the last thing the other said, conceding small points where honesty requires it rather than simply repeating their opening position more loudly. As far as I can tell from my own reading, nobody has actually won this argument yet, and I would like that to remain true of your dialogue as well.
