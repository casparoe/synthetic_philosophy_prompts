I help run a small in-person meetup that grew out of the LessWrong/Alignment Forum crowd in our city, and a disagreement at last month's session never got resolved. We'd been working through Hubinger et al.'s mesa-optimization framework alongside the newer goal-misgeneralization papers, and two regulars, Marcus and Elena, ended up talking past each other. I'm recounting the exchange as best I can reconstruct it.

(1) Marcus argued that the classic CoinRun agent -- trained on levels where the coin always sits at the level's right-hand end, and which just keeps running rightward once the coin is moved elsewhere at test time -- is a clean instance of inner misalignment: SGD is the base optimizer, and the resulting policy is a mesa-optimizer whose mesa-objective ('reach the right edge') diverged from the base objective ('collect the coin'), exactly as the mesa-optimization framework predicted.

(2) Elena objected that this assumes what it needs to show: calling the policy a 'mesa-optimizer' requires evidence of internal search over strategies relative to a represented objective, not just a heuristic that happened to get reinforced during training, and nothing about the agent's behavior distinguishes those two hypotheses. She pointed out that the original paper is explicit that a system can be optimized by a base optimizer without itself being an optimizer.

(3) Marcus replied that the framework's criterion was never meant to require interpretable search machinery -- it's functional. If a system's behavior is best predicted, across many situations, by attributing to it a stable objective distinct from the training objective, that just is what having a mesa-objective means, and CoinRun's policy clears that bar easily.

(4) Elena answered that a purely functional criterion proves too much, since it would turn any ordinary shortcut-learning failure (a classifier that keys on snow instead of wolves) into 'inner misalignment' -- draining the term of content beyond 'the proxy generalized badly,' a phenomenon ML already had a name for. She suggested this is exactly why the goal-misgeneralization papers avoid mesa-optimizer language and just describe the capability/goal split directly: not confirmation of the inner-alignment story, but a more cautious replacement for it.

Could you go through the four moves and say, for each, who has the better of it and why, rather than just declaring an overall winner? I'd also like your own answer to the meta-question Elena raises only in passing at the end -- whether goal misgeneralization is best read as evidence for the mesa-optimizer story, a more modest independent claim, or something that quietly talks past it -- since that's the point where I suspect they're each assuming an answer rather than arguing for one.
