I am at work on a novel in which two former colleagues from a parliamentary campaign meet for a last drink after one of them has resigned from party politics for good. The one who resigned has spent the intervening weeks reading Simone Weil's 1943 essay calling for the abolition of all political parties and now thinks her argument essentially unanswerable; the other, staying on to run next year's campaign, admires the essay but thinks it would be ruinous if anyone actually acted on it. Please render their conversation as a dialogue of roughly a dozen exchanges.

The character who resigned should press the substance of Weil's case: her account of what a party structurally is and does, and her claim that mere membership already commits a person to a kind of intellectual dishonesty on questions that have not yet arisen. The other should answer with the strongest realistic reply available, in the spirit of the tradition running from Burke's defense of party through later theories of responsible party government, and should press back on what, concretely, could replace parties without simply recreating them under another name. Both are equally sharp; each should answer the other's actual last point rather than restate a prepared position, and the scene should end with neither side conceding.
