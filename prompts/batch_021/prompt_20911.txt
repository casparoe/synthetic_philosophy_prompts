Much of my reading on decision theory has been informal, mostly on forums and blogs concerned with AI safety and the reduction of suffering risks, and one idea that keeps coming up there is the "surrogate goal," proposed as a defense against acausal threats: cases where one agent can extract concessions from another purely because it can predict or model that agent, with no causal channel between them at all. The idea is that instead of trying to make yourself immune to such a threat outright, you deliberately adopt some goal you don't actually care about and commit to defending it exactly as fiercely as you would defend your real values, the hope being that a rational threatener ends up targeting the surrogate rather than the thing you genuinely care about. I can't quite convince myself this solves anything, though: a threatener sophisticated enough to model your decision procedure in the first place should also be able to tell that the surrogate is fake, and could then either ignore it and threaten your real goal anyway, or simply threaten your commitment to defend the surrogate, which only relocates the problem one level up. I keep going back and forth on whether that's a decisive objection or whether there's some principled reason the regress has to bottom out somewhere.

I'd like you to write a dialogue between two equally capable decision theorists standing in for these two positions: one who thinks surrogate goals are a genuinely workable defense against acausal threats, and one who thinks the strategy collapses once you assume a sufficiently sophisticated threatener. Have them engage directly with the regress worry rather than talk past one another, with the skeptic pressing the point as hard as it deserves before the defender gets a real chance to answer it.
