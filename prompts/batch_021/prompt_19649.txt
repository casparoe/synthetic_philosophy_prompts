I help run a small giving circle that grew out of my old university's effective altruism club — we've got something like $35k to allocate this cycle, and I'm one of three people on the commitee that decides where it goes. We've funded the same anti-malaria bednet charity for four years running because the evidence behind it is about as solid as this space gets, but this cycle a new applicant showed up proposing something more "systemic": a small outfit that lobbies recipient-country health ministries to restructure how they budget for primary care, the idea being that fixing the underlying financing could do more good over decades than any one net ever will. The committee split on it, and the argument got more interesting than I expected.

Ben opened by saying we should just stick with bednets, because we can actually trace what our money does — dollars in, nets out, malaria deaths down, full stop — whereas the systemic proposal is so many steps removed from any measurable outcome that funding it is basically throwing darts blindfolded; he kept calling it "clueless." Sana pushed back that the bednet charity's effects don't stop at "child survives" either — saving that child changes household fertility choices, local labor markets, who exists a generation out — and we have no more idea whether that nets out positive than we do for the advocacy group; we've just gotten used to not looking past the first domino for bednets because that's where the RCTs stop measuring. Ben's answer was that there's a real difference between the two: whatever we don't know about the bednets' ripple effects, we have no particular reason to think it's skewed in one direction over another, so it should roughly wash out and the known near-term saving still wins; with the advocacy group we have specific, tellable stories about how it goes wrong (money gets absorbed into other budget lines, ministries resent the meddling, reform attempts backfire and entrench something worse), which he said makes it a worse, different kind of uncertain than bednets. Sana's reply was that the line between "uncertainty that washes out" and "uncertainty that doesn't" is awfully convenient for whoever's defending the status-quo grant, since nobody on the committee could've told you in advance which bucket a given effect belongs in — she could tell an equally specific story about the bednet charity's population effects going wrong, we'd just never bothered to before today. Ben more or less conceded he couldn't fully justify the line, but said a committee has to decide somehow, and defaulting to whatever has the most robust near-term evidence is at least a defensible rule when you're in the dark about everything downstream of that; Sana said that's just status-quo bias dressed up as epistemic humility, and that if we're really taking this seriously we should stop treating one grant as "safe" and judge both by completely different criteria, like reversibility or how much future flexibility each option preserves, instead of pretending either side has a real expected-value estimate.

I have to write the memo the full committee reads before we vote next week, and I don't want it to just summarize both sides evenhandedly — I need it to actually land on what we do with this year's money (full bednets, full advocacy, some split, or something else entirely), and it has to take Sana's strongest point seriously rather than quietly siding with whoever sounded more decisive in the room. Can you draft that memo for me?
