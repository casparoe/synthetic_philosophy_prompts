I convene a small interdisciplinary graduate reading group at my university — philosophers working on decision theory and formal epistemology on one side of the table, computer scientists working on multi-agent systems on the other — and this term we have been working through the "commitment races problem" that comes up in discussions of bargaining among advanced artificial agents. The underlying worry descends from Schelling's observation that a driver who visibly disables his own steering wheel wins a game of chicken precisely by destroying his capacity to yield: any agent capable of making credible, irrevocable commitments has an incentive to commit as early and as blindly as possible, before it has gathered information or reflected on what it actually wants, since a more patient, better-informed counterpart will simply be forced to accommodate whatever has already been locked in. The obvious trouble is that if every sufficiently sophisticated agent reasons this way, everyone is pushed toward committing fast and uninformed, so that the very deliberation that would make an agent's commitments wiser is also what makes it likely to lose the race — or, worse still, two agents lock themselves into incompatible positions and collide head-on.

At our last meeting, one of the computer science students tried to lighten the mood with a one-liner that got a groan rather than a laugh, because underneath the jargon it was really just a generic "robots are scary" joke that would have worked equally well for any story about stubborn machines. I would like to do better for our final session of the term. Please write me a short joke about the commitment races problem whose humor genuinely depends on its specific, perverse logic — the reward for committing before you know better, the danger of symmetric commitment, the way that becoming wiser can leave you strategically worse off — rather than one that merely borrows the vocabulary without engaging the underlying argument.
