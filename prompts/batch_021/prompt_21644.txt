In my school's Game Theory elective, we spent a class period actually playing the centipede game before our teacher walked us through the theory behind it. The version we played was simple: a shared pot grows a little each time the player whose turn it is passes control to their partner instead of "taking," which ends the game right away and gives the taker most of the current pot while their partner gets the rest. The catch is that if you pass and your partner takes on the very next turn, you always end up with less than if you had just taken it yourself. Our teacher then showed us the backward induction argument: reasoning back from the very last turn, taking is always at least as good as passing, provided it's common knowledge that both players are rational and will keep reasoning the same way, so the whole chain unravels all the way back to the very first move. Almost nobody in the room had actually played that way, though, and trying to hash out why is what led two people in my group into a real argument afterward.

(1) Marcus's partner had passed twice before finally taking on her third turn, and he said this just proved she'd played irrationally, since common knowledge of rationality implies that taking immediately is correct at every single node, no matter what has already happened earlier in the game. (2) Priya objected that this was too quick: once your partner has passed "off-script" like that, you've just watched the very assumption that was supposed to guarantee she's rational get contradicted by what she actually did, so you no longer have any grounds for assuming she'll take on her next turn either -- meaning the rational response to being passed to might now be to pass back, not to keep citing a theorem whose own premise has visibly failed. (3) Marcus replied that Priya was misunderstanding what the theorem claims: it isn't a prediction about how to respond after somebody has already deviated, it's a claim about what common knowledge of rationality entails, and since that assumption guarantees such a deviation would never happen in the first place, the theorem simply has nothing to say about a branch of the game it rules out by hypothesis. (4) Priya's comeback was that this defense proves too much: if common knowledge of rationality can't say what a player should believe or do once a deviation has actually occurred, then it was never entitled to conclude "take immediately" at the earlier nodes either, since that very conclusion was built by reasoning, one node at a time, about what a rational opponent would still do later on -- including at exactly the nodes the theorem now says it can't coherently talk about. By the end, Marcus still thought Priya was smuggling in ordinary real-world doubt about her partner that has no place inside a clean model, while Priya thought Marcus was defending a theorem that undercuts itself the moment anyone actually tries to apply it.

It's been bugging me ever since, and I honestly couldn't tell who was actually right, or whether they were both missing something. Could you go through the exchange move by move and say who has the better argument at each stage -- not just who wins overall -- and let me know whether there's a clear way of stating what, if anything, both Marcus and Priya are getting wrong about common knowledge of rationality here?
