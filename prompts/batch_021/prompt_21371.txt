A friend and I spent a good part of last night arguing about a section of Turing's 1950 paper "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" that neither of us had ever read closely before: the point, near the very end, where he takes on what he calls the argument from extra-sensory perception. I'd assumed going in that this would be a throwaway aside, but Turing turns out to be surprisingly direct about it. He carefully distinguishes the four classical categories of the phenomena — telepathy, clairvoyance, precognition, and psychokinesis — and then states that, of these, "the statistical evidence, at least for telepathy, is overwhelming." He worries specifically that a human contestant with genuine telepathic ability could be asked to guess something like the order of a shuffled pack of cards, scoring far above chance in a way no machine could match, which would let the interrogator unmask the human for reasons that have nothing to do with intelligence. His proposed fix is to seal both competitors in what he calls "telepathy-proof rooms." I argued that this means the imitation game, as Turing actually designed it, only works as a valid test of machine intelligence if you grant that ESP is at least a live empirical possibility that has to be actively screened out — otherwise the safeguard is solving a problem that cannot arise, so writing it into the test commits him to treating that possibility as real enough to matter. My friend couldn't quite locate the flaw in that but wasn't fully convinced either, and we gave up around one in the morning without resolving it.

I'd like a more rigorous verdict than either of us reached. Does the validity of the imitation game, on Turing's own construction, really depend on treating ESP as a live empirical possibility in this load-bearing way? And if most modern expositions of the Turing test present it as a purely behavioural, metaphysically unloaded criterion for machine intelligence, are they quietly dropping a commitment that Turing himself thought was necessary — or is there a flaw in the inference I walked my friend through? I'd also be curious how seriously historians of the period think Turing meant the "overwhelming evidence" remark, since this particular section seems to get skipped in just about every course or article that discusses the test.
