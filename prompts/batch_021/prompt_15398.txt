So I'm in this informal book club at work, and this month we somehow ended up reading Huckleberry Finn (long story, blame our PM). We just got through the part where Huck writes a letter to Miss Watson that would out Jim as a runaway, feels relieved because he thinks turning Jim in is the "right" and godly thing to do given how he was raised, then thinks back on Jim being kind to him on the raft, rips the letter up, and decides to help Jim escape anyway - even saying something like "alright then, I'll go to hell" because he fully believes he's damning himself by doing it. One of the other engineers in the book club did a philosophy minor in college, and she mentioned that this scene is apparently a classic "test case" in ethics classes.

Apparently theres a whole academic debate hiding in that one scene. On one side you've got Kant's idea that an action only has "moral worth" if it's done because you recognize it as your duty, not just because of some feeling like sympathy or affection. On the other side, my coworker brought up a philosopher named Nomy Arpaly who apparently uses this exact scene to argue basically the opposite - that Huck's choice is one of the most morally admirable moments in the whole book, precisely because he's acting against his own (badly mistaken) principles, purely out of sympathy for Jim as a person. I think the term she uses for this is "inverse akrasia" or something close to that. I don't have any formal philosophy background myself (closest I get is arguing with teammates over whether following the style guide "on principle" matters more than code that just happens to work), so I'm having a hard time figuring out how these two theories actually cash out differently on a concrete case like this. If you apply Kant's criterion strictly, does Huck's decision even count as having moral worth, given that he doesn't even believe he's doing the right thing while he's doing it? How would Arpaly's framework score that same decision instead? And which of the two verdicts seems more defensible to you, or is there some third reading neither of them quite captures?
