I was appointed three months ago to fill a vacancy on my town's zoning board of appeals, and a hearing last week has left me unsettled about how the board actually reaches its decisions. We are five members, and our ordinance conditions a variance on the board finding, among other things, both that the claimed hardship arises from conditions unique to the lot and not shared generally by nearby properties, and that granting the variance will not alter the essential character of the neighborhood. The other statutory criteria were effectively conceded in this case, so everything turned on those two findings. Our procedure calls for the board to vote on each finding separately and then take a further vote on the application itself, but it has never said which vote controls if the two come apart -- an eventuality that, until last week, had simply never arisen. This time, three of us judged the uniqueness finding satisfied, and, separately, three of us (though not quite the same three) judged the neighborhood-character finding satisfied, so each finding passed on its own by majority. Yet when the chair called the vote on the application itself, only two of the five voted to grant it; the other three had each rejected at least one of the two findings in their own reasoning and voted no on that basis. The chair resolved the discrepancy by treating the findings as dispositive and declared the variance granted, and the applicant has since pulled a building permit on the strength of that ruling. One of my colleagues now argues that we should instead have honored the direct vote on the application, since, in her view, that is plainly the vote meant to bind, findings notwithstanding. A friend of mine finishing a philosophy PhD thinks this pattern has a name in the judgment-aggregation literature -- she mentioned 'discursive dilemma' and 'doctrinal paradox' without being sure whether those name the same thing -- but she did not know the field well enough to say what a body like ours should actually do about it.

Our rules committee meets in three weeks to consider procedural amendments before the next round of hearings, at least one of which is likely to present the same structure. I would like to bring a short memo to that meeting rather than improvise a resolution every time this arises, and I would be grateful if you would draft it. It should take a clear position on which vote ought to control going forward, address what, if anything, we owe the applicant given that a permit has already been pulled under a procedure our own rules never actually specified, and explain the reasoning in terms a board of non-lawyers could apply on our own the next time. Please also take seriously, rather than dismiss, my colleague's further worry that formalizing a finding-by-finding rule will simply teach members to vote insincerely on individual findings in order to steer the outcome they already favor. And if, as my friend suspects, no procedure escapes this kind of difficulty altogether, I would rather the memo say so plainly and still recommend the best available option than quietly assume the problem away.
