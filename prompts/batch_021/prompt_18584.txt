A friend and I spent much of last night arguing about suffering-focused ethics, and I still haven't quite recovered from where the conversation ended up. It started innocuously enough: she was making the case that any morally serious response to the sheer scale of suffering in the world — factory farming, chronic illness, the whole catalogue — has to give the prevention of suffering some kind of priority over the promotion of happiness. But when I pushed on where that logic terminates, we landed on the old objection that a sufficiently consistent negative utilitarian, faced with a genuinely painless button that would instantly and permanently end all sentient life, would be rationally committed to pressing it, since a universe with zero experiencers trivially contains zero suffering, and no amount of forgone pleasure counts against that on a view that only ever weighs suffering. She had responses on hand — appeals to indirect and long-run effects, to moral uncertainty, to rejecting strict lexical priority in favor of a merely strong one — but none of them felt fully satisfying to either of us, and we went to bed without agreeing on anything.

I'd like you to write me a short story, somewhere in the 1000-1500 word range, that dramatizes this dilemma rather than just asserting it. Perhaps center it on an artificial mind, built by some coalition of hospice-care networks, disaster-relief agencies, and animal-welfare organizations and given the single mandate of minimizing suffering across whatever population it can reach, that arrives at the "button" conclusion after years of quietly effective and uncontroversial work, through what looks to it like impeccable internal logic, and must decide what to do with that conclusion and whom, if anyone, to tell first. I would rather it not collapse into a simple morality tale about a rogue machine; I want the reasoning itself to stay legible, even sympathetic, on the page, and I'd like at least one human character who actually engages with the argument on its merits in real time rather than simply overriding or unplugging the machine. Feel free to leave the ending genuinely unresolved — I am more interested in a story that makes the tension between preventing suffering and preserving existence viscerally felt than in one that hands the reader a tidy verdict.
