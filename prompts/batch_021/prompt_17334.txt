I've spent the past few weeks reporting on the informed-consent debates swirling around psychedelic-assisted psychotherapy: regulators and clinicians are effectively being asked to sign off on a treatment whose therapeutic mechanism is an experience that patients can't really be briefed on beforehand, and which often leaves them with different values and priorities than the ones they arrived with. That reporting sent me back to L.A. Paul's 2014 book Transformative Experience, and specifically to the thought experiment she opens it with: imagine you're offered a one-time, irreversible chance to become a vampire, and that everyone close to you who's already made that leap swears, without exception, that it was the best decision of their life. Paul's point is that the choice is both what she calls "epistemically transformative" (you can only learn what the resulting existence is like by actually living it) and "personally transformative" (living it will rewire the very preferences you'd use to judge whether the change was worth it), and that this combination breaks the ordinary machinery of rational choice, which asks you to mentally run through each option and assign it a value before you commit to anything. You can't run that simulation on a kind of experience you have no prior acquaintance with — and, crucially, consulting the people who've already crossed over doesn't rescue you, because their glowing reports are being filed by people whose preferences the transformation has already reshaped.

I'd rather have this made felt than argued, so I'd like you to write me a short story — something like 1,200 to 1,800 words — that dramatizes this exact bind through a scenario of your own invention, set wherever feels right to you. I'd specifically like you to steer clear of vampirism, parenthood, religious conversion, and cochlear implants, since those are Paul's own recurring examples and I don't want something that reads like an illustration lifted straight from the book. Put a particular character at the actual moment of decision over something concrete and irreversible, give them access to someone who has already undergone the change and is willing to advise them, and let the scene itself show why that advice can't do the work the character needs it to do — rather than having anyone pause to explain the theory out loud.

Above all, I'd like you to resist any ending that quietly tips the reader off about which choice was the right one; the whole appeal of Paul's problem is that no such fact is available in advance. Paul's own way through the bind, for what it's worth, is essentially to trade the question of whether you'll like what's on the other side for the question of whether you value finding out, treating the decision as worthwhile for the sake of the self-knowledge alone — your character is welcome to reach for some version of that move, but I'd rather the story test it under pressure than simply hand it a quiet win.
