In a philosophy of mind seminar I'm taking, we've spent the last few weeks on teleosemantics: the project of naturalizing mental content (intentionality) by grounding what a state represents in its evolved "proper function" — what it was selected to track or bring about — rather than in whatever merely happens to cause it on a given occasion. Part of the appeal is that this seems to explain misrepresentation for free: a frog's bug-detector can "misfire" at a BB pellet flicked past it rather than at a fly without this changing what the detector represents, since the pellet-triggering counts as a malfunction relative to the trait's proper function rather than a change in content. I follow the basic motivation, but two objections keep nagging at me, and I'd like help working out how serious they really are.

The first is a disjunction/indeterminacy problem: even granting that the frog's detector has the proper function of doing something fly-related, it is unclear why this pins down "fly" as the content rather than "small dark moving thing," "food," or some other extensionally divergent description that the trait was, in some equally legitimate sense, selected to track — and the problem seems to get worse, not better, once the story is scaled up from frog detectors to human beliefs and concepts. The second is Davidson's Swampman: a molecule-for-molecule duplicate of an ordinary person, assembled by freak accident with no evolutionary or learning history whatsoever, and therefore, on teleosemantics' own terms, possessed of no proper functions and no mental content at all — despite being behaviorally and introspectively indistinguishable from someone with a full mental life. Write an essay that develops these two objections in more depth than I have here, weighs them against the strongest replies that a teleosemanticist in the vein of Millikan, Papineau, or Neander could offer, and then reaches and defends a definite verdict on whether teleosemantics, in some recognizable form, actually succeeds in naturalizing mental content, or whether these problems show that it cannot do the job on its own. Please don't retreat into a purely "more research is needed" conclusion — take a real position, even a qualified one such as "it works for simple biological signaling but not for full-blown human intentionality," and argue for it.
