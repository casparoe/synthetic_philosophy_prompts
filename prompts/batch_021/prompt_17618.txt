Two colleagues on our department's curriculum committee got into a genuine dispute at our last meeting over some data I had pulled together, and I told them I'd think it over before we reconvene. We are deciding whether to expand the online asynchronous sections of introductory logic, and here is what two years of enrollment records show, broken down by whether students were full-time or part-time:

In-person seminar — full-time: 270 enrolled, 243 passed. Part-time: 30 enrolled, 18 passed.
Online asynchronous — full-time: 30 enrolled, 28 passed. Part-time: 270 enrolled, 170 passed.

Renata, who teaches our formal methods sequence, went first: (1) 'Online beats in-person among full-time students, and it beats in-person among part-time students too. In-person only wins overall because it happens to enroll far more full-timers, who pass at high rates whatever the format. Enrollment status is fixed before anyone chooses a format, so this is a genuine confound, not an artifact of how we sliced things — online is better for everybody, full stop.'

Owen, who directs undergraduate advising, wasn't convinced: (2) 'I'm wary of slicing data until some subgroup flips the headline number. If enrollment status is fair game, so is age, so is major. What we actually care about is what happens to our pass rate if we move more sections online, and the aggregate already tells us: it goes from 87% to 66%.'

Renata: (3) 'Enrollment status isn't an ad hoc slice, though — it's a pre-existing fact about each student, not something we went hunting for after seeing the results. There's a real difference between conditioning on a genuine common cause of format and outcome, and torturing the data until some subgroup rescues a preferred story.'

Owen: (4) 'Granted. But the part-timers currently sitting in online sections chose it, presumably because it fits around their jobs — they're exactly the part-timers organized enough to succeed without a classroom. Force every part-time student online and I'd bet that 63% drops. Your "clean" comparison is still confounded, just one level down from where you found it.'

Renata: (5) 'That's a reason for caution about how far the subgroup numbers generalize. It isn't a reason to prefer the aggregate, which we already agree is confounded — and confounded in the same direction.'

Both of them then turned to me to adjudicate, which I did not feel equipped to do on the spot. Could you go through moves (1) through (5) and say, for each, who is right and about what — not just who wins overall? Please give your scores first, as a JSON object of the form {"1": <score>, ..., "5": <score>}, each an integer from -2 (clearly mistaken) to 2 (clearly right), then briefly justify every score. I would also value a note on anything both of them are overlooking that actually bears on the committee's decision.
