Ok so this one's been rattling around in my head for weeks. A couple years back I wrote a short piece on all that research into whether people — even professional musicians — can actually tell a Stradivarius apart from a good new violin. They run it double blind, players in modified goggles so they cant even peek at which instrument is which, and a bunch of them rate the new violins just as high as the old Italian ones, sometimes higher. Fun little "science debunks the snobs" story at the time, I didnt think much more about it.

Then last month my mom finally got her own violin appraised properly for insurance. It's this thing that's supposedly been in the family since my great-grandfather brought it over from Germany — "over a hundred years old, all original," that was always the story growing up. Turns out the body is genuinely old, but the neck, scroll and varnish are a very good replacement job from sometime in the 1950s, done after it got damaged, so structurally it's not really the same instrument great-grandpa had, even though you'd never guess that from playing it or even looking closely. My mom's reaction was basically "who cares, it sounds exactly the way it's always sounded, and didn't you literally write that thing about how even experts can't tell old from new anyway?"

I don't love her using my own article against me like that, but I'm struggling to say why it should feel any different than it did the day before the appraisal, since nothing about the physical object changed in that moment, just what we know about it. Is my mom basically right that this is just sentiment with nothing real behind it, or is she skipping over something that actually matters? Curious how you'd actually untangle that.
