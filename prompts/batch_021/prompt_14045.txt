I'm currently taking a political philosophy seminar that just finished a unit on political obligation and the ethics of civil disobedience: Thoreau, King's "Letter from Birmingham Jail," Hart and Rawls on the fair-play grounds of the duty to obey rules, and Rawls's own conditions for justified civil disobedience toward the end of Chapter VI of A Theory of Justice — roughly, that it be public, non-violent, conscientiously motivated, addressed to the community's sense of justice, resorted to only once ordinary channels have failed, and combined with a willingness to accept the legal consequences. Independently of the course, I sit on my university's student conduct board, and a case just came up that I can't stop reading through everything we've covered.

Twenty-three students occupied the administration building's lobby for about thirty hours to protest the university's refusal to divest its endowment from fossil fuel holdings. They announced the action in advance to the administration and the student paper, published a written statement of demands, blocked no emergency exits, and mostly dispersed the moment campus police issued an order to leave; six stayed and let themselves be arrested rather than resisting. The conduct code simply bars "obstruction of university operations" and "failure to comply with a lawful order," with no mention of protest, and, strictly speaking, the students weren't even disobeying the policy they object to — they have no authority over the endowment — but a separate access rule, broken instrumentally to draw attention to it, which is closer to what some of our readings call indirect rather than direct disobedience. On the board, several members want the manner of the protest — its publicity, its non-violence, the six students' acceptance of arrest — to count as substantial mitigation, on the theory that this is close to a textbook justified case; others respond that the board has neither the mandate nor the competence to rule on the justice of divestment, that an informal protest exception would be unfair to everyone else sanctioned under the same code, and that a theory built to describe citizens' relationship to the state cannot simply be assumed to describe students' relationship to a university.

Could you draft the memo I'll circulate before Thursday's vote? It should recommend a specific outcome — from no formal sanction, through a warning or disciplinary probation, to suspension — for the six students who were arrested and, separately, for those who dispersed once ordered to; it should propose a standard the board could apply consistently to future protest cases rather than deciding this one alone; and it should take seriously, rather than brush past, the objection that political-obligation and civil-disobedience theory may simply not transfer from the citizen-state relationship to the student-university one.
