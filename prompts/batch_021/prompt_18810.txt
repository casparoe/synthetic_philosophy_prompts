The question (15 pts, short essay):

"Many philosophers claim there is an important asymmetry between believing something on the basis of moral testimony and believing something on the basis of testimony in other domains (e.g. medicine, mathematics). Explain this alleged asymmetry, present ONE argument for why it holds, and evaluate whether that argument succeeds."

The answer (decent student, not a philosophy major):

"There's a common intuition that something feels off about believing an action is wrong just because someone else told you so, in a way that doesn't feel off if a doctor tells you you have the flu and you believe it, or a mathematician tells you a proof checks out. Call this the asymmetry thesis. Crucially, this is meant to cover any case where what you're told is morally relevant, not just flat-out verdicts -- so if a friend I trust tells me that factory farms cause immense suffering, and I use that to conclude factory farming is wrong, that's supposed to be just as suspect as being told 'factory farming is wrong' outright, since either way I'm leaning on someone else's say-so for a moral belief.

Alison Hills gives an influential argument for why the asymmetry holds. She distinguishes moral knowledge (having a true, justified belief that something is right or wrong) from moral understanding (grasping why it's right or wrong well enough to apply it to new cases, resist pressure, and act from the right motive). Testimony can give you moral knowledge, but it can't give you understanding, because understanding requires working out the reasons yourself. Since Hills thinks virtuous action requires understanding, not just correct belief, she concludes that acting on moral testimony is never compatible with being virtuous, and that we should never do it.

I think Hills is onto something: the soldier who refuses an order because she's worked out for herself that it's wrong seems different from one who refuses only because she was told to. But the asymmetry might not be special to morality -- deferring to a mathematician about whether a proof is valid doesn't give you real understanding either, so maybe the real divide is between shallow and deep understanding generally, not moral vs non-moral testimony."
