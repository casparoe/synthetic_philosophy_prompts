Three weeks ago, my father died in his care home after the night-shift staff failed to reposition him for more than eight hours, and the resulting pressure ulcer developed into a fatal infection. The home's insurer is now arguing, through counsel, that the understaffing merely failed to prevent his death rather than caused it, and that liability should be assessed accordingly. While researching this defense, I've discovered that philosophers are sharply divided over whether omissions can be causes at all: on process or transference theories of causation, a mere absence cannot do any causal work, however culpable it is, whereas on counterfactual theories, the staff's failure to act counts as a cause just as readily as any action would. Please describe the most important implications of this debate over causation by omission for how I should evaluate the insurer's argument, for what would need to be true for the understaffing to count as a genuine cause of my father's death rather than a mere background condition, and for whether the moral and legal verdicts here might reasonably diverge.
