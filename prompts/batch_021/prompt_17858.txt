Last weekend, a friend and I spent most of a dinner arguing about the Ellsberg paradox, prompted by a decision theory seminar we are both auditing, and neither of us left persuaded. She defends what I would call the strict Bayesian line: that Savage's sure-thing principle is the correct normative standard for choice under uncertainty, that any coherent set of preferences must be representable as maximizing expected utility against a single subjective probability distribution, and that whatever discomfort people feel about "not knowing the odds" in Ellsberg's urn is a mistake to be corrected rather than a preference worth modeling in its own right. I took the other side, defending something closer to Gilboa and Schmeidler's maxmin expected utility, on the grounds that ambiguity is a genuinely distinct feature of a decision problem that no single-prior theory can capture. I leaned heavily on the three-color version of the paradox -- the urn with 30 red balls and 60 black-or-yellow balls in an unknown split -- where most people prefer betting on red over black, yet also prefer betting on "black-or-yellow" over "red-or-yellow," a reversal that violates the sure-thing principle and cannot be rationalized by any single probability assignment to the urn's composition.

Where we got genuinely stuck was on Raiffa's old rejoinder to Ellsberg: that an agent who dislikes the ambiguous bets could simply flip a fair coin to decide which color to bet on within the ambiguous urn, thereby securing objective 50/50 odds no matter the urn's true composition. We could not agree on whether this shows ambiguity aversion to be confused -- since ambiguity can always be laundered into ordinary risk this way, making a separate category for it seem unmotivated -- or, on the contrary, vindicates it as a real and distinct preference, since an agent willing to pay for the coin flip is precisely one who finds ambiguity itself costly, over and above whatever risk it gets converted into. Could you write a dialogue between two equally sharp stand-ins for these two positions, set in front of Ellsberg's three-color urn, in which each tries to talk the other out of their bets? I would like the Bayesian to press the randomization move as hard as it will go, including the question of whether the ambiguity theorist can genuinely commit to the coin's result once it lands or would be tempted, by the very same aversion, to renege and revert to the known urn instead -- in which case the randomization would have solved nothing. And I would like the ambiguity theorist to have a real answer rather than folding or simply restating maxmin expected utility as a slogan. Please have them respond to each other's actual arguments move by move, so that neither position comes off as the obvious loser going in.
