For the past two years I've served on the ethics committee of a residential memory-care facility, and we've recently been asked to evaluate a vendor pilot that strikes me as a very direct real-world instance of a problem I'd only ever encountered before in the AI-alignment blogs and forums I've followed for years. The product, marketed under the name CareSignal, is meant for residents with advanced dementia who can no longer communicate verbally: it logs each resident's approach and avoidance behavior, time spent engaged in various activities, and physiological stress markers throughout the day, then fits a reward function to that history using maximum-entropy inverse reinforcement learning under an assumed Boltzmann-rational choice model -- essentially the same machinery Stuart Russell and colleagues have proposed for value learning in household robots, repurposed here as a clinical instrument. The output is a ranked list of the resident's inferred "preferences," and the vendor wants the pilot to cover not just scheduling recreational activities but eventually higher-stakes care decisions, including whether a resident would "want" a given intervention continued or withdrawn once they can no longer say so themselves.

The committee has split along fairly predictable lines. One faction treats this as a rigorous, auditable improvement on the ad hoc "reading the resident" that overstretched staff already do informally, and wants to approve a broad pilot on that basis. A more skeptical faction, myself included, keeps coming back to the fact that this isn't a measurement problem more sensors could fix: any finite behavioral history is compatible with indefinitely many reward functions, and the system returns only one because the vendor's choice of rationality parameter and discount factor silently resolves that indeterminacy -- not the resident's history or condition. For a population whose diagnosis often specifically compromises stable, goal-directed behavior in the first place, that silent resolution seems to be doing far more normative work than the phrase "preference-mapping" suggests, and I don't think "collect more data" is a coherent response to the objection.

I need to circulate a short decision memo before next week's meeting recommending that we approve the pilot as proposed, reject it outright, or approve it subject to specific conditions. Could you draft that memo? I'd like it to settle on one concrete course of action with an explicit timeframe -- what the pilot may and may not be used for during, say, its first two quarters, and what would trigger renewal, revision, or termination -- and I'd like it to engage honestly with the strongest argument for whichever option it doesn't recommend, rather than simply noting that reasonable people disagree. Whichever way this goes, I'll be the one defending it to a colleague who has read the technical appendices just as closely as I have.
