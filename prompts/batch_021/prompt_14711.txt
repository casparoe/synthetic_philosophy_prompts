I recently agreed to chair the bylaws-reform subcommittee of a professional association I belong to, partly because our last two board elections were marred by vote-splitting: in each case, three or four reform-minded candidates divided up most of the membership's support, and a candidate whom most members had actually ranked near the bottom ended up winning our plain plurality vote. The subcommittee is now weighing whether to replace plurality with either approval voting or instant-runoff (ranked-choice) voting, both of which are marketed by their advocates as considerably harder to game strategically than a simple plurality count. Before our next meeting, though, a colleague circulated a short write-up of the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem and argued that it settles the matter for us: since the theorem proves that every non-dictatorial voting rule can be strategically manipulated by someone misrepresenting their preferences, choosing among plurality, approval voting, ranked-choice, or anything else is essentially cosmetic, and the subcommittee would be spending its limited political capital on a reform that cannot actually make our elections any less "gameable." Someone else on the call half-joked that we might as well pick the winner by drawing a name out of a hat, since apparently nothing can be made safe from manipulation anyway.

This has left me unsure whether it's still worth pushing for reform. Is my colleague right that Gibbard–Satterthwaite renders the choice among these rules essentially arbitrary from a strategic point of view, provided we're still selecting a single winner from three or more candidates? And is there any principled sense — whether through how the alternatives are structured, by introducing randomness, or by some other route — in which a voting procedure really can be shielded from strategic voting, or should the subcommittee just accept that "strategy-proof election reform" is a contradiction in terms and direct our energy elsewhere?
