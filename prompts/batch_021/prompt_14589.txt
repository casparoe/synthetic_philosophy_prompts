"Strawson's Basic Argument tries to show that no one is ever truly morally responsible for what they do, because you can only be truly responsible for an action if you're truly responsible for the character and motives that produced it, and nobody is ever responsible for that in the way the argument requires. In premise form: (1) to be truly responsible for an action A, you must be truly responsible for being the way you are, mentally, such that you did A ('character' for short); (2) to be truly responsible for your character, you must have chosen or shaped it in some earlier act; (3) but any such earlier act itself flowed from a character you had at that earlier time, which you didn't choose, so being responsible for it would require being responsible for that still-earlier character, and so on; (4) since nothing can be the cause of itself, this regress can't terminate in a self-caused starting point; therefore (5) you are never truly responsible for your character, and so, by (1), never truly responsible for any action.

The clever part is that this doesn't turn on whether determinism is true. Obviously if determinism is true your character traces back to causes before you were even born that you had no hand in. But Strawson thinks indeterminism doesn't rescue you either -- if anything it makes things worse, since an undetermined, random swerve in your deliberation isn't something you do for reasons, so it can't be an exercise of responsible agency at all, which puts you even further from self-determination than plain determinism does. That's part of why Galen Strawson can present the Basic Argument as basically an extension of his father P.F. Strawson's project in 'Freedom and Resentment' -- showing that the whole question of responsibility floats free of whether determinism is true.

A 'deep self' theorist like Frankfurt would say this is too quick: if you identify with your desires -- really endorse them as your own instead of being alienated from them -- then they're truly yours even if you didn't pick your starting character, and that's all responsibility requires. Strawson's reply is that identification just pushes the question back a step: whatever self does the identifying was itself shaped by a character it never chose, so no amount of reflective endorsement lets you reach back and become the ultimate author of yourself. The regress swallows the 'deep self' move too."
