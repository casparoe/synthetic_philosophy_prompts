So in my formal epistemology seminar we spent a week on Richard Jeffrey's "probability kinematics" -- his rule for updating when evidence doesn't make you certain of some proposition but just shifts your confidence over a partition (the standard example is judging a piece of cloth to be green by candlelight, without fully ruling out blue or violet). Ever since, something's been bugging me: ordinary Bayesian conditionalization on hard evidence is commutative -- learn A then B, or learn B then A, and either way you land on the same distribution, conditionalized on A-and-B. But apparently Jeffrey conditionalization isn't commutative like that once you chain two uncertain shifts together -- updating on partition one and then partition two can leave you somewhere different than updating on partition two and then partition one, even holding fixed what the "new" probabilities for each partition are supposed to be. I remember there being responses to this floating around in the literature -- something about redefining the partitions so commutativity comes back, and something about Jeffrey's rule still passing a Dutch-book test regardless -- but I never got a straight answer on whether this order-dependence is actually a mark against Jeffrey conditionalization as a rule of rational updating, or whether its a non-issue once you're careful about what "the same uncertain evidence, just in a different order" is even supposed to mean.

Could you write me an essay that actually takes a side on this? Explain briefly why the non-commutativity shows up in the first place (I think its tied to the rigidity condition only being anchored to your probabilities at the time of the update, not to some fixed prior), then lay out the strongest case for treating this as a genuine defect in Jeffrey's rule and the strongest case for thinking it isn't really a problem at all, and don't just leave the two sides hanging -- actually tell me which one wins and why.
