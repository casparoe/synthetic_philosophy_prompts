My grandpa had a massive stroke about three weeks ago and ended up being declared brain dead in the ICU -- like, officially pronounced dead by the neuro team, even though his heart was still beating on its own and he was warm and his chest was rising and falling (with the vent doing the work, obviously). We basically had a day to decide about organ donation, which still feels surreal to think about. Ever since then I've been down a rabbit hole reading about how "brain dead" became legally the same thing as "dead," and at the reception after the funeral my aunt Reyna (she's been an ICU nurse for like 20 years) and my cousin Marco (he's finishing a bioethics PhD) ended up in this whole back-and-forth about whether brain death is REALLY death, and I've been chewing on it since because honestly neither of them was clearly winning.

Reyna's opening point was that this isn't some gray philosophical area, its settled clinical fact: once the whole brain is destroyed with zero chance of recovery, there's no organism left doing any integrating -- what's on the bed is just organs being kept oxygenated by a machine, and calling that "alive" is a sentimental mistake, not a medical one. Marco pushed back with the Shewmon research -- cases of kids and adults kept on ventilators for years after a whole-brain-death diagnosis, some of them growing and even going through puberty, plus documented cases of brain-dead pregnant women whose bodies kept a pregnancy going for months til delivery -- and said if a body can do that much integrating without a brain, "no integration without a brain" can't really be the reason we call it death. Reyna answered that those are rare, heroic-intervention cases that show decomposition being postponed, not genuine self-regulation, and that the vast majority of brain-dead bodies arrest within days no matter what anyone does. Marco then switched to circulatory death, pointing out that under some newer donation protocols (normothermic regional perfusion) doctors declare someone dead after just a couple minutes with no pulse and then literally restart circulation to keep the other organs healthy, just blocking blood flow to the brain specifically so they can still say they've satisfied the "dead donor rule" -- and asked how "irreversible" loss of circulation can be the standard for death if the circulation gets reversed minutes later, and whether that shows the whole framework is quietly parasitic on brain-based criteria after all, since the brain is the one part they make sure never gets blood again. Reyna's comeback was that "irreversible" in the actual medical/legal standard has always meant something more like "permanent, given that no one's going to intervene," not "physically impossible to reverse" -- which is true, but Marco said that just concedes his real point, that death here is partly a policy decision dressed up as a biological discovery.

I can't tell who actually had the better of this. Could you go through the exchange step by step and say who's right about what at each point, instead of just picking an overall winner? And if you think there's a more defensible position on the brain-death/circulatory-death question than either Reyna's or Marco's, I'd genuinely like to hear it.
