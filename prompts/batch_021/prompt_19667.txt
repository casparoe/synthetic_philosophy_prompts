Me and a couple friends started a little amateur reading group a while back, nothing fancy, just some people who never went to uni for this stuff but like getting into old philosophy on the side, and we've been slowly chewing through Hume's Treatise of Human Nature. We just hit the bit in book 3 where he does that thing with the two farmers - one guy's corn is ready today, the other's is ready tomorrow, and it would obviously pay off for both of them to help each other out on the right day, except neither one trusts the other enough to go first, so they both just work alone and loose part of the harvest for nothing. Hume's whole point is that "justice" and promise-keeping aren't some natural instinct we're born with, they're basically a trick we invented, and the only reason it holds together is that once the convention exists, breaking your word starts costing you your reputation and future dealings with people.

Totally seperately, I've been down a rabbit hole for the last year or so reading about AI safety stuff (just blogs and forum posts, nothing formal) and I ran into this concept called "program equilibrium" that kind of wrecked my brain a little. The simplest version is basically: instead of two agents just picking an action in something like the prisoner's dilemma, they each submit a little program to play on their behalf, and those programs get to look at each other's actual code before acting. The dumb version of this is just "cooperate if your opponent's code is an exact copy of mine, else defect," which apparently is super fragile bc it breaks the second the code isn't literally identical. But then people built fancier versions (I think this is where Löb's theorem comes in, which I don't fully get) where the programs can basically prove things about what the other program will do, so you can get two total strangers to reliably cooperate in a one-shot game with zero history, zero reputation, and no outside enforcer at all.

And that's the part that's been bugging me, bc that's literally Hume's farmer problem, just solved in a way that has nothing to do with convention or reputation or society slowly teaching you to feel bad about breaking promises. So what I'm curious about is: if you somehow explained program equilibrium to Hume, what do you think he'd actually say about it? Would he just go "sure, that's my convention story, you've just automated the part where the two of you agree on a signal you can trust," or is there something more fundamental in his account - like the role sympathy plays, or the idea that a sense of justice has to get built up socially over repeated interactions - that this source-code-reading trick just skips past entirely, in a way that would make him say it isn't really solving the same problem at all?
