Had a long argument with my friend Mara last night about the wrong-kind-of-reasons problem for buck-passing accounts of value (the "something is valuable iff there's reason for a pro-attitude toward it" move). We kept circling around the demon case -- I think this is originally from Rabinowicz & Rønnow-Rasmussen but don't hold me to that -- where a demon threatens to torture your family unless you come to desire something totally worthless, like a saucer of mud. That gives you a reason to desire the mud, but obviously doesn't make the mud valuable, so buck-passers need some non-circular way to mark that reason as the "wrong kind." Mara thinks the standard object-given/state-given fix (Parfit's distinction) just handles it fine: the demon's reason is a reason to be in the state of desiring, not one that flows from the mud's own properties, so it's automatically disqualified. I think that distinction basically just relabels the explanandum instead of explaining it.

Here's the alternative I sketched out around 1am. Call a reason R for having pro-attitude A toward object O "insulated" if R would still favor A even with the specific extrinsic payoff (torture-avoidance, winning a bet, whatever) subtracted out -- i.e. does R retain any pull that's peculiar to O itself once you strip the incentive away. Right-kind reasons should survive the stripping; wrong-kind ones should evaporate, since their force was parasitic on the payoff the whole time. Demon case: subtract "avoids torture" and there's nothing left recommending desiring the mud, so it correctly comes out wrong-kind.

Where I got stuck arguing this out: first, does "peculiar to O itself" already smuggle in a prior notion of value, making the whole thing circular after all? Second, does this generalize to reasons for belief the way it should -- Pascal's wager comes out wrong-kind fine (strip the salvation payoff and there's no evidence left), but what about James-style self-fulfilling beliefs, like believing you'll succeed partly because believing it improves your odds of succeeding? The insulation test seems to call that wrong-kind too, and now I can't tell if thats a problem for the test or just a correct-if-unintuitive verdict.

Anyway -- can you tell me whether this actually escapes the circularity worry, whether its just a dressed-up version of something already out there (the object/state-given move, or some convergence-style solution, can't remember whose), and whether you can think of a counterexample that just breaks it outright?
