As a longtime reader of LessWrong and Astral Codex Ten, I've noticed that "Goodhart's Law" gets invoked constantly, almost always in its pithy, quotable form: "when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure." That line is usually attributed to Goodhart himself, though as far as I can tell it actually comes from the anthropologist Marilyn Strathern's writing on academic audit culture, not from Goodhart's own 1975 work on UK monetary policy, where the original point was considerably narrower and more technical. Chasing this down made me notice something else that bothers me: people in this space tend to use "Goodhart's Law" and "Campbell's Law" almost interchangeably, as though Donald Campbell's remarks about the corruption of social indicators were simply the sociological cousin of the same insight Goodhart had about monetary aggregates. The more I sit with the two original formulations side by side, the less sure I am that they're actually making the same claim.

I'd like you to work through this properly rather than wave at a resemblance. Please compare Goodhart's Law and Campbell's Law as their authors originally framed them, not the composite folk version that circulates under Goodhart's name. Do the two laws describe a single underlying mechanism by which a metric decouples from the thing it was meant to track once it comes under optimization pressure, or are they pointing at genuinely different mechanisms that happen to produce a similar-sounding warning? I'd like you to use Scott Garrabrant's four-part "Goodhart Taxonomy" (regressional, causal, extremal, and adversarial Goodhart) -- the framework that gets cited constantly in AI-safety discussions of specification gaming -- as a tool for actually settling this rather than just gesturing at it. Does Campbell's Law drop neatly into one of Garrabrant's four buckets (my own tentative guess is adversarial, since Campbell's central worry is the people being measured deliberately corrupting the indicator, whereas Goodhart's monetary-policy example seems more structural), or does Campbell's original formulation carry an emphasis that doesn't sit comfortably in any of the four categories? Please reach an actual verdict and defend it -- "they're basically the same idea," which is the answer I keep getting whenever I bring this up with friends, won't do.
