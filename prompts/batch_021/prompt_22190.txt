For a history-of-economic-thought seminar, I've been working closely through Aristotle's account of money and exchange in Politics Book I and Nicomachean Ethics Book V: his distinction between household management (oikonomia), which acquires only what is naturally needed and so has a natural limit, and the "art of wealth-getting" (chrematistike), which treats the accumulation of money itself as the end and therefore has no natural limit; his claim that money exists by convention rather than by nature, introduced as a common measure so that otherwise incommensurable goods can be exchanged; and, most famously, his singling out of lending at interest as the most unnatural species of wealth-getting, on the grounds that money is a sterile intermediary. His objection trades partly on the fact that the Greek word for interest, tokos, also means "offspring": he thinks it is precisely backwards to treat money, an inert conventional token, as though it could breed more of itself the way a living thing produces young.

I'd like you to apply this framework to a feature of modern banking with no real analogue in Aristotle's Athens. In contemporary economies, new money is created mainly not by governments or mints but by ordinary commercial banks in the act of issuing a loan: rather than lending out funds some depositor has already parked with it, a bank typically extends credit by simply crediting the borrower's account with a new deposit -- an accounting entry that expands the total money supply the moment it is made. On the Bank of England's own account of how this works, the resulting bank deposits make up the large majority of the money circulating in an economy like the UK's, dwarfing physical currency.

Reasoning strictly from Aristotle's own stated criteria, rather than from a general impression that he'd have disapproved of modern finance, how do you think he would assess this practice? Is it simply an intensified case of the usury he already condemns, since the loans still carry interest -- or does the fact that the principal itself is conjured from a promise, rather than transferred out of some household's or moneylender's existing hoard, place it in a conceptually distinct, and by his lights perhaps even more troubling, category? And does a money supply that expands and contracts with the private lending decisions of banks sit comfortably with his requirement that money serve as a stable common measure of value, or would he see this elasticity as itself a corruption of money's proper function?
