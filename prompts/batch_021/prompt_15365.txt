As a journalist who covers behavioral science, I keep an informal file of lay arguments that turn out to be more sophisticated than they first look. The latest came from two acquaintances of mine, Nadia and Marcus, who got into it at a dinner party over tipping.

(1) Nadia had just declined to tip at a counter where the card reader prompted for twenty percent, and when Marcus raised an eyebrow, she said tipping is "just a convention," so she isn't doing anything wrong by opting out whenever she feels like it.

(2) Marcus said that's too quick: servers are often paid on the assumption that tips will make up the rest of a living wage, so once the convention is in place, people are counting on everyone keeping it, and quietly opting out harms them even though the convention itself could easily have gone another way.

(3) Nadia answered that "people are counting on it" just describes how any arrangement, good or bad, keeps itself going, and doesn't show that she personally owes anything; plenty of countries run perfectly well without tipping at all, so nothing about this particular practice binds her.

(4) Marcus granted that some other arrangement might have worked just as well, maybe better, but said that's beside the point: the fact that people could have settled on a different practice doesn't make it any easier to step outside, on your own, the one they actually settled on.

(5) Nadia replied that this argument proves too much, since it would rule out anyone ever being the first to break with an unjust or outdated practice -- and practices obviously do change.

(6) Marcus said practices do change, but never, in his experience, because scattered individuals quietly opted out on some given Tuesday; they change when something public resets what everyone expects of everyone else -- a restaurant posting a no-tipping policy, say -- which is a different kind of act altogether from Nadia's silent tap of "no tip."

Neither would give an inch. Could you go through their exchange turn by turn, (1) through (6), and say who has the better of it at each point, rather than just who wins overall? I'd also like to know what, if anything, both of them are missing about how a practice like this could generate -- or fail to generate -- a genuine obligation for someone who never explicitly agreed to it.
