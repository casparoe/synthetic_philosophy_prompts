For my IB Extended Essay in philosophy, I'm looking into how much contemporary "mathematical platonism" really has to do with Plato himself, and I've hit a snag that neither my supervisor nor the secondary sources I've tracked down quite settle. When philosophers today defend platonism about mathematics — in the spirit of Gödel, say, or the Quine–Putnam indispensability argument — they generally mean that numbers and sets are abstract, causally inert, mind-independent objects, and that each one is unique: there is exactly one number 7, not many interchangeable 7's floating around. But once I went back to the primary texts, Plato's own view looked more tangled than that label suggests. Aristotle reports in the Metaphysics that Plato actually posited a separate class of "intermediate" mathematical objects, distinct from both sensible things and the Forms, apparently because arithmetic and geometry need many equal or similar units (many 2's, many congruent triangles) in a way that doesn't sit well with each Form being one and unique. And in the Republic's Divided Line, Plato seems to rank mathematical reasoning (dianoia) below full dialectical knowledge (noesis) of the Forms, since mathematicians argue from unexamined hypotheses and lean on visible diagrams rather than climbing all the way up to the unhypothetical first principle of the Good.

So here's what I'd like help thinking through for my essay: if Plato were somehow briefed on today's platonism-versus-nominalism debate — including Benacerraf's worry about how we could ever know objects that are causally inert, and Hartry Field's nominalist attempt to do physics without quantifying over numbers at all — would he accept contemporary mathematical platonism as a faithful continuation of his own position, or would he think it quietly flattens distinctions (Forms versus intermediates, dianoia versus noesis) that he was careful to keep apart? I'd rather you reason from those specific, less-quoted pieces of the dialogues and of Aristotle's testimony than from the standard "Plato thought numbers are eternal abstract objects" summary, and I'd appreciate it if you flagged any spot where you think the comparison is more speculative than settled.
