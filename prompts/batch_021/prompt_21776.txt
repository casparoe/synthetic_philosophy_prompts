In my Philosophy of Religion elective, we spent a week on Molinism, the view devised by the sixteenth-century Jesuit Luis de Molina to hold together robust divine providence with libertarian free will. I think I have a reasonably solid grasp of the basic picture: God's "middle knowledge" consists of true counterfactuals of creaturely freedom (things like "if Peter were placed in circumstances C, he would freely deny Christ"), which God knows logically prior to His free decision about which possible world to actualize, and which then let Him providentially arrange history around people's free choices without causing those choices Himself.

Wanting to understand the view better than what we covered in class, I read further on my own, including some of William Lane Craig's replies to critics, and I keep running into what's called the "grounding objection," originally pressed by the philosopher Robert Adams. As I understand it, the objection is that these counterfactuals of freedom cannot have truth-makers: they are not necessary truths built into logic or God's nature, since the whole point of libertarian freedom is that the agent could have done otherwise in the very same circumstances; they cannot be grounded in the agent's actual choice, since in most cases the relevant agent and circumstance never actually get instantiated in the real world; and grounding them in the agent's character or psychological dispositions seems to reduce them to merely probable tendencies rather than the flat-out truths Molinism needs. Craig's usual move seems to be to deny that truths need this sort of independent "grounding" at all, but I cannot tell whether that is a genuine answer to the problem or just a way of talking past it.

Could you write me an essay that first explains the grounding objection clearly enough for someone without a background in metaphysics or truthmaker theory to actually follow the worry, then lays out the strongest Molinist replies (including, but not limited to, the "truths don't all need grounds" move), and finally reaches and defends an actual verdict on whether the objection succeeds in showing that there are no true counterfactuals of freedom for God to know? Please commit to a real position rather than concluding that reasonable people just disagree -- even a hedged verdict like "the objection fails, but only at a cost most non-Molinists won't accept" would be fine, as long as you argue for it.
