I'm an oncologist, and for the past several months I've been serving, at my bishop's request, as the physician member of the informative tribunal he convened for a local cause of beatification -- that of Mother Aurelia Conti, a religious sister who ran our diocese's hospital until her death in the 1960s. The tribunal is the panel of five that canon law calls for at this stage -- the bishop or his delegate, a notary, a clerk, the promoter of justice, and me -- charged with assembling the file on a possible miracle before it goes to the Dicastery for the Causes of Saints in Rome. I'm Catholic myself, but I'd never had to think hard about the epistemology of any of this until now. The case concerns a parishioner, then in her fifties, who was found to have pancreatic cancer that had already spread to her liver and was told she had months to live; her parish began a novena to Mother Aurelia, and within six weeks the woman's scans were clear. She is eleven years out now, with no further treatment and no recurrence, and a second oncologist I brought in independently agrees that nothing in the literature explains this. By the Church's own longstanding test for these cases -- the cure must be sudden, complete, and durable, and not attributable to any treatment she received -- the medical side of our file looks about as strong as these ever get.

Where we're stuck is philosophical rather than medical. Our promoter of justice, who studied theology before canon law and takes the office's older function as "devil's advocate" more seriously than the job now technically requires, won't sign off on forwarding the file until we address an objection he's borrowed straight from Hume's essay on miracles: that no testimony can be sufficient to establish a miracle unless its falsehood would be a greater miracle than the fact itself, and since it will always be antecedently more probable that our medicine is simply incomplete than that a law of nature was suspended, no quantity of scans or sworn statements -- however sincere -- could ever meet that bar. Our postulator disagrees just as sharply: she thinks this proves too much, since applied consistently it would make it irrational ever to revise a settled expectation on the strength of any evidence, however extraordinary, and she argues the Church's own criteria already do the epistemic work Hume thought impossible, more carefully than he gives anyone credit for. Both of them are arguing in good faith, and I don't think either one is a fool, which is precisely the problem.

The bishop wants the panel's written recommendation before we finalize the Acta this month, and he's asked me to draft it. Please write that recommendation for me: whether to certify the file as ready to forward, hold it for further follow-up, or decline to proceed, with reasoning that either resolves the dispute between my two colleagues or explains why the tribunal can reasonably leave it to Rome's theologians to settle -- and that is honest about which of them has the stronger argument, and what the other is nonetheless right about.
