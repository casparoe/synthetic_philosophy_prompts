I spent about thirty-five years running a comparative physiology lab before I retired, mostly looking at how different species' organ systems cope with oxygen deprivation, and since hanging up my lab coat I've gotten pretty into philosophy on the side, mostly bioethics stuff. What got me onto this particular question was my father-in-law being on a ventilator this past winter -- the neurologist ran through the apnea test and the brainstem reflex checklist right at the bedside, and it struck me how much of what we casually call "death" is actually a technical determination rather than the obvious, unitary biological event I'd always sort of assumed it was.

Since then I've been reading around -- Shewmon's papers, the President's Council on Bioethics white paper (2008, I think), some law review pieces on the Jahi McMath case -- and honeslty the more I read, the less settled the whole thing looks. So I'd like you to write me an essay that actually gets into that mess, rather than reciting "there are two standards, circulatory and whole-brain, and they amount to the same thing really." I want you to specifically wrestle with: (1) Shewmon's chronic "brain-dead" patients, some kept going for years, still growing, healing wounds, fighting off infections -- and what that does to the "the brain is what integrates the organism" idea that the whole-brain standard is supposed to rest on; (2) the fact that circulatory death for organ donation is typically declared after only a few minutes of a flat-line, on the grounds that the stoppage is "permanent" rather than strictly "irreversible" -- a distinction that, as someone who's watched plenty of successful late resuscitations in animal work, has always struck me as a bit of a dodge; and (3) what a case like Jahi McMath's -- dead by California's law, alive by New Jersey's, same body throughout -- is actually supposed to tell us about whether "brain death" tracks some real biological fact or just codifies a convenient line for transplant purposes.

Don't leave me with "well, reasonable people disagree" -- my old lab partner already gave me that answer and it's useless. I want an actual verdict, argued for: is there a single coherent concept of death underneath all this, and if so does whole-brain, higher-brain, or strict circulatory death actually get it right? Or is the honest conclusion closer to what Truog and Miller have argued -- that we should just drop the pretense of a "dead donor rule" and be upfront that what's doing the ethical work is consent, not the timing of death? Pick a side and defend it properly.
