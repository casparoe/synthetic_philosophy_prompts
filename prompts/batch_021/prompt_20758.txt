A few years back I did a big magazine feature on Project CETI, the whole "let's use machine learning to crack sperm whale communication" effort, and somehow that got me invited onto the small external advisory panel of a private foundation that funds a handful of similar bioacoustics-and-AI outfits. Normally it's a rubber-stamp gig, but this quarter's big renewal request has genuinely split the panel and I need to circulate a recommendation memo before we meet Friday.

Here's the situation: one of our grantees — a different team, working with a different whale population — rode the wave of public interest after that 2024 paper on combinatorial structure in sperm whale "codas" (the one everyone wrote up as whales having a "phonetic alphabet," way more distinct click-patterns than the couple dozen "words" that had been catalogued before). They're now asking for a much bigger grant to build a real-time model that goes beyond classifying codas and starts actually playing synthesized responses back to wild whales — basically a first attempt at two-way "conversation" with another species. Half the panel is thrilled and just wants the win of "humans talk to a whale." The other half, led by a semi-retired philosopher we somehow have on this board, keeps insisting the whole "conversation" framing smuggles in way more than the data can support. She keeps bringing up something called radical interpretation — Davidson, plus a bit of Quine's old "gavagai" problem — and this idea that you can't even fix what a signal means without some kind of triangulation between interpreters who are each able to catch the other one being wrong, and that we have zero reason to think that condition could ever be met between us and a sperm whale via a model trained on statistical correlations. She's not just doing reflexive anti-anthropomorphism hand-waving, and honestly some of it stuck with me, but most of the panel has never heard the name Davidson and isn't going to find the arguement persuasive just because of who's making it.

Could you draft the actual memo I'd bring to that meeting? I need a real recommendation — fund the interactive playback phase as proposed, decline the renewal, or fund it with specific conditions (say, they can keep doing the passive listening and statistical work but hold off on any "conversational" playback claims or interventions until certain things change) — not a "more research is needed" shrug. Lay out the philosophical objection clearly enough that panel members who've never read a word of philosophy of language could actually follow and argue with it, and whichever way you land, be honest about the strongest thing that could be said for the option you're not recommending.
