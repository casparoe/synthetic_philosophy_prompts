I sit on my university's small interdisciplinary research fund, which each year awards a handful of modest, no-strings-attached grants to speculative projects that would not survive review by an ordinary disciplinary panel -- which is presumably why a philosopher has a seat on the committee alongside the scientists. This cycle's most contentious application comes from a mathematician who wants support for what she readily admits is a purely internal piece of mathematics: an extension of a certain class of abstract algebraic structures with no known connection to anything empirical, pursued for nothing more than its formal elegance. Her application leans heavily on history. She points out that the non-Euclidean geometry Riemann worked out in the 1850s sat around as a mathematical curiosity for roughly sixty years before Einstein needed exactly that machinery to formulate general relativity, and that the group theory developed by algebraists with no thought of physics became, decades later, the essential tool for classifying elementary particles and even predicting new ones before they were observed. Her conclusion is that this pattern is common enough that funding mathematical elegance for its own sake is a reasonable bet on future payoff, not a favor the committee would be doing her.

Two of my colleagues are unpersuaded, for different reasons. One calls this the mathematician's survivorship story: nobody keeps a tally of the far larger number of equally elegant structures that led nowhere, so a handful of famous successes cannot tell us anything about the base rate, and dressing that pattern up as a "track record" mistakes anecdote for evidence. The other thinks the whole style of argument is suspect regardless of the statistics, since "this might prove useful in eighty years" is compatible with almost any piece of mathematics whatsoever, and a consideration that nothing could ever count against cannot be doing real work in deciding where a fixed pot of money goes this year. I need to circulate a one-page recommendation before we vote next week, and I would rather it stake out a real position than simply list considerations on each side. Could you help me think through who actually has the better of this argument, and draft the recommendation itself -- fund, decline, or fund with specific conditions -- with reasoning the losing side would recognize as a fair hearing of their view rather than a rehearsal of the winning one?
