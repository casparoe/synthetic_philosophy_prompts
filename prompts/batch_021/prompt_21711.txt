I have no formal training in philosophy, but over the past year I have been trying to think more rigorously about how to donate money effectively, and that has led me through a fair amount of amateur reading on the ethics of consequentialism. One issue I keep encountering, and have not been able to resolve to my own satisfaction, is the objection that consequentialists are "clueless" about the true effects of their actions once those effects are traced far enough into the future. What puzzles me is that the writers I have come across do not present this as a single difficulty: they distinguish between what they call "simple" cluelessness, which they seem to regard as ultimately harmless once properly understood, and "complex" cluelessness, which they treat as a genuine problem for the theory. As I understand it, the reason simple cluelessness is not supposed to matter is that the unforeseeable effects in question are just as likely, for all we know, to be good as to be bad, so that they cancel out and can be safely set aside when comparing options. What I cannot see is why the same move could not equally be applied to the complex cases, since there too our knowledge of the eventual outcome seems just as thin.

I believe the piece where I first came across this distinction was written by someone named Hilary Greaves, though I am not entirely certain I am remembering that correctly. In any case, could you explain, without assuming any prior background on my part, exactly what is supposed to distinguish these two varieties of cluelessness, and why the "canceling out" response is thought to dispose of the simple kind while leaving the complex kind untouched? I would also value your own assessment of whether this distinction genuinely rescues consequentialism as a usable guide for ordinary decisions, such as choosing between charities, or whether it merely narrows the scope of the original worry without actually answering it.
