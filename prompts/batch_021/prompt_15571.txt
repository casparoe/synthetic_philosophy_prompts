My nephew Idris is nine years old, and until this past winter he had never seen anything more than light and shadow. He was born with dense congenital cataracts that went undiagnosed for years at the rural clinic near where my sister was posted with an aid organization, and by the time a visiting surgical team finally caught it, he had spent his entire conscious life organizing the world through his hands and ears. The operation in January restored a functioning image to his retinas almost overnight, but "seeing," in the sense philosophers care about, has arrived far more slowly: for weeks afterward he would still reach out and pat a cup or a stuffed animal before admitting, often reluctantly, that he recognized it, as though sight and recognition were two separate achievements that had simply turned up in the wrong order.

I co-host a small philosophy podcast for curious non-specialists, and watching Idris relearn his own living room has turned Molyneux's Problem from a footnote in my undergraduate epistemology notes into something I now think about daily. Reading around the topic afterward -- including the sight-restoration research associated with programs like Project Prakash, where newly sighted patients have actually been tested on this kind of cross-modal matching -- made the question more pressing, not less. For next month's episode we've booked a philosopher of perception who has spent several years analyzing data from a similar program and who has argued in print that this research settles Locke's question in the empiricist's favor. I don't want the conversation to be the coffee-table version: Locke relays Molyneux's question, Berkeley answers no in the New Theory of Vision, Leibniz grumbles in the New Essays that reasoning might get the blind man there anyway, and everyone nods and moves on. Our listeners could get that much from a short video essay, and I suspect our guest could recite it in her sleep.

What I need is eight to ten interview questions that use what has actually been learned since to make that "settles the question" line progressively harder for her to lean on. I would like at least one question pressing on whether patients who only succeed after several days of visual experience are really answering Locke's question, which stipulates recognition immediately upon first sight, or a related but looser one; at least one on whether the well-documented immaturity of a newly sighted person's visual system -- poor acuity, unstable fixation, and so on -- is already doing part of the empiricist's work before any genuine cross-modal test has begun; and a closing question asking what result, if any, would count for her as evidence that Leibniz's confidence in reasoning was not entirely misplaced. For each question, please add a short note on the answer she is most likely to give, so that I have the follow-up ready rather than improvising it live.
