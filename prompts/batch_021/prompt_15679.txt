The teacher who has run our school's philosophy club for the last twenty years is retiring, and I've been asked, as this year's club president, to say a few words at her retirement reception. Her signature line, repeated to every new batch of members, is some version of "if something is true, it can in principle be known" -- a kind of stubborn optimism that no truth is ever locked away from us forever. Last fall the club spent three meetings on Fitch's paradox of knowability, which shows that this exact claim, taken as a strict logical principle, entails the absurd conclusion that every truth is already known by somebody -- which is obviously false, so the claim itself has to go. Roughly half the room will be club members and alumni who will catch this immediately; the other half are her family and colleagues from other departments, none of whom have heard of Fitch or ever will look him up. Write me a five-minute speech, to be read aloud, built around one real joke that turns on the actual logical structure of Fitch's argument -- not just "philosophers argue about weird things" -- that both halves of the room can laugh at, and that ultimately lands as a tribute to her optimism rather than a rebuttal of it.
