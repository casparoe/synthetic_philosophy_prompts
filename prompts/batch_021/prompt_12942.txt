I'm a PhD student in financial economics, and I've been sitting in on our department's market-design reading group, which spent last week's session on Milgrom and Stokey's no-trade theorem and its ancestor, Aumann's agreement result. As I understand the argument, if two parties start from an efficient allocation and it is common knowledge that they share a prior and are both rational, then any proposed bet motivated purely by private information unravels before it can be executed: the counterparty's willingness to take the other side of the trade is itself evidence that should make you revise your estimate, so there is no point in the resulting sequence of updates at which the trade remains mutually agreeable.

What I can't reconcile — and this came up again over lunch with a couple of labmates who work in behavioral finance — is how this squares with the near-universal enthusiasm, including among several people in my own cohort, for prediction markets as the premier device for pooling dispersed private information. They're used to forecast elections, geopolitical events, or, in an application I find particularly striking, how many published findings in psychology will actually replicate. The epistemic authority usually claimed for platforms like Polymarket or Kalshi rests on exactly the mechanism the no-trade theorem seems to forbid: individually rational participants, mutually aware of each other's rationality, betting against one another because each thinks they know something the current price doesn't yet reflect. Yet real prediction markets show substantial, sustained trading volume rather than the near-total silence the theorem seems to predict.

So it looks to me like we're stuck with one of two unattractive conclusions: either prediction-market participants are not actually rational in the sense the theorem requires, which undercuts the standard argument for trusting the resulting price, or the price's reliability doesn't in fact depend on the kind of purely information-driven trading the theorem is about, in which case I no longer understand what work "people are willing to bet on it" is doing in the usual defense of these markets. Is this really the dilemma it appears to be, or have I smuggled in an assumption from the idealized theorem that ends up doing more work than it should once we turn to actual markets?
