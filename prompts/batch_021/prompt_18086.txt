So a few months back I somehow got talked into joining the board of the co-op building I live in — twelve units, five of us on a volunteer board, none of us has any kind of legal or policy background, we just take turns because somebody has to sign the checks. Anyway we've got a mess on our hands that's had me loosing sleep. One of the other owners, who's lived here almost a decade without a single complaint against her, got outed on the neighborhood Facebook page as running a legal, home-based dominatrix business — strictly appointment-only, clients in and out the side door, everything arranged over text beforehand, she pays taxes on it as an LLC, nothing illegal going on as far as anyone's been able to find out. Three of the other four board members now want to use the "conduct objectionable to the co-op or its residents" clause in our proprietary lease (which is one vague sentence and has never actually been used before) to start termination proceedings against her. Their case is basically: it's degrading, they don't want to live over top of "that," and if it gets more attention then the one nosy local blog post it already got, it'll tank what all our units are worth and turn the building into a punchline. The rest of us (me included, most days) think that's exactly the kind of reasoning that could get used to push out literally any owner the majority happens to find distasteful for reasons that have nothing to do with how she actually treats her neighbors, and that "objectionable" can't just mean "some of us are grossed out by it."

We vote in a week and a half, and since I'm the one who's spent every night since this started reading state landlord-tenant law and old "morals clause" court opinions (none of which map onto us super cleanly), I got stuck writing the memo the board's actually going to vote off of. I need it to land on one clear recommendation — proceed with termination, issue a formal warning, or drop the matter entirely — with an actual timeline attached, not a wishy washy "reasonable minds differ." And I really don't want it to just wave off the property-value argument as pure prejudice and move on, because even my husband, who thinks this whole thing is a non-issue, admits that if resale values genuinely take a hit, that's a real cost to people who didn't sign up for it. Can you put that memo together for me?
