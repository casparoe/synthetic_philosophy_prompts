For my philosophy seminar on the epistemic foundations of game theory, we have spent the last two weeks contrasting Aumann's proof that common knowledge of rationality entails the backward induction outcome in the centipede game with Pettit and Sugden's well-known "backward induction paradox." Their paradox contends that the proof is self-undermining: it recommends an action at a given node on the assumption that common knowledge of rationality still holds there, even though the argument's own conclusion implies that node is never actually reached. I would like to see the strongest available defense of Aumann's side of this dispute. Please argue that the paradox does not show his derivation to be incoherent, and in making that case, address directly why treating rationality as a property of a complete, ex ante strategy — rather than as a disposition that is revised sequentially as the game unfolds — blocks the inference from "reaching that node would be surprising" to "no common-knowledge-sanctioned recommendation is available there." Please also state plainly what, if anything, this defense concedes to critics such as Binmore, who charge that it merely redefines rationality rather than vindicating backward induction as an account of how players actually reason during play.
