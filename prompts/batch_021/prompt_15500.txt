I'm the philosopher on my university's IACUC (the committee that signs off on animal research protocols). We've got a weird case up for renewal: a neuro lab wants to keep running a chronic nociceptive-sensitization study on Drosophila larvae -- repeated heat and pinch stimuli to induce something like hyperalgesia -- and since IACUC oversight (like the AWA) only formally kicks in for vertebrates, there's no actual rule stopping them. When one member objected, the PI's defense leaned on higher-order theories of consciousness: sure, the larvae have nociceptors and show robust avoidance and sensitization, but on the HOT picture none of that is *felt* unless there's also a higher-order state representing the first-order one, and nothing we know about fly neuroanatomy suggests they have the machinery for that. One commitee member finds this pretty convincing; another points out it's awfully convenient how a theory of consciousness always seems to exempt whatever animal we're already using. Can you write the actual memo I'd bring to the meeting -- a specific recommendation (approve as-is, approve with named changes, or send it back), the reasoning spelled out in terms that actually engage with the theory rather than just gesturing at it, and a fair statement of the strongest reason someone would give against whichever way you land.
