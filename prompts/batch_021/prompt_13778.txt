I'm a PhD student in philosophy of physics, and at our department's reading group last week, two of the regulars got into a long, unresolved argument about what Bell's theorem actually establishes. I'd like a considered outside judgment on their exchange, since I don't think either of them was simply right.

(1) Naomi opened by insisting that Bell's theorem proves nature is nonlocal, full stop: any theory reproducing the observed violations of the CHSH inequality must contain some form of action at a distance, and Bohmian mechanics is living proof, since it reproduces every prediction of ordinary quantum mechanics while being explicitly and unapologetically nonlocal.

(2) Felix objected that this moves too fast. Bell's proof requires premises beyond "no faster-than-light influence": that measurement outcomes are fixed by some complete local state, and, crucially, that these hidden variables are statistically independent of the experimenters' choice of measurement settings. Give up that last premise, measurement independence, as superdeterministic theories do, and you can keep locality and determinism while still violating the inequalities.

(3) Naomi replied that superdeterminism isn't a genuine option, since it requires the initial state of the universe to be tuned so that experimenters' "free" choices of settings always correlate with the hidden variables of whatever they are about to measure. She called this unfalsifiable in principle: any inconvenient correlation could be blamed on such a conspiracy, which would gut the evidential force of experiments generally, not just Bell tests.

(4) Felix countered that the charge of "conspiracy" begs the question. In a fully deterministic universe, of the kind classical physics already assumed, settings and hidden variables will be correlated via their common causal past whether or not anyone intends it; calling this conspiratorial, rather than simply a consequence of determinism, smuggles in the assumption that experimenters possess a libertarian freedom that no other variable in physics enjoys.

(5) Naomi granted that superdeterminism might be internally coherent but argued that Bohmian mechanics remains the more principled response, since its nonlocality is located in a precise, universal dynamical law (the guiding equation) rather than in an unspecified correlation between distant past states and future choices that would have to hold anew in every single experiment.

(6) Felix's rejoinder was that this doesn't vindicate "nonlocality is forced" either, since a third route -- denying that measurements ever yield single, definite outcomes, as Everettians do -- reproduces the statistics without any nonlocal dynamical law at all. The appearance of a nonlocal correlation, he said, arises only if you insist on comparing the outcomes at each wing as though both were uniquely actual, which is exactly what a many-worlds theorist denies.
