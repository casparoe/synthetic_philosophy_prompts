After thirty years running a neurophysiology lab, I've spent retirement reading philosophy of mind in earnest, and I now sit on the neuroethics working group that advises our hospital's neuro-ICU on disorders of consciousness. We must decide whether clinicians may cite two new tests — a TMS-EEG brain-complexity index (PCI) and Owen-style covert command-following on fMRI or EEG — when telling families of patients diagnosed with unresponsive wakefulness syndrome (formerly the vegetative state) that "someone may still be in there." One member, drawing on higher-order theories of consciousness, argues that both tests show at most rich first-order integration or goal-directed responsiveness, and nothing about whether the patient retains the higher-order representation of that very state which such theories require for it to be felt rather than merely processed, so citing either result as evidence of awareness overstates what we actually know. Another, who runs the unit, counters that we have never demanded such evidence for any conscious person we simply cannot ask, and that withholding it now just manufactures pessimism out of a theoretical scruple. Please draft the working group's written opinion on whether and how these tests should enter our family-counseling script, with a concrete recommendation that engages the higher-order objection head-on rather than tabling it for further research.
