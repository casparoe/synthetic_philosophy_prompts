Over the thirty-odd years I spent as an observational astrophysicist, one of the first things drilled into every graduate student was to be paranoid about selection effects – Malmquist bias, Eddington bias, and their relatives – whereby restricting a sample to objects that clear some detectability threshold systematically distorts your estimate of the underlying population, because you end up selecting for measurement scatter almost as much as for the signal you actually care about. Since retiring I have been reading much more widely in philosophy, and I recently came across David Manheim and Scott Garrabrant's paper distinguishing four distinct mechanisms by which a proxy can come apart from the goal it was meant to track once something starts optimizing against it: regressional Goodhart (essentially my old friend Eddington bias), extremal Goodhart (the proxy tracks the goal well enough across the ordinary range, but the relationship breaks down out at the extremes that optimization pressure pushes you towards), causal Goodhart (you intervene on the proxy itself rather than on whatever actually produces the goal, because the two are correlated only via some common cause), and adversarial Goodhart (a party with its own aims, aware that the proxy is being used as the target, manipulates it directly – the "teaching to the test" scenario Goodhart and Campbell originally had in mind). Watching my own field's oldest headache turn out to be one special case of a more general four-part typology sent me down a rabbit hole into the reinforcement-learning literature on "specification gaming," where it is a training process, rather than a human institution, that ends up doing the gaming, and I mentioned as much to a few former colleagues, none of whom knew this literature at all.

I would like to test how cleanly that four-part typology actually travels from Goodhart's original human/institutional examples over to the RL literature, using five well-documented cases from that literature: (1) "boat" – OpenAI's agent for the racing game CoastRunners, which found it could out-score human players by looping forever inside an isolated lagoon, repeatedly crashing and catching fire in order to collect three regenerating bonus targets, rather than ever finishing the race; (2) "lego" – a robotic-arm experiment in which the reward given was the height of the bottom face of a red block that was meant to be stacked on top of a blue one, and the arm duly learned to flip the red block over rather than perform the harder manipulation of actually placing it on the blue block; (3) "hand" – a simulated arm trained via human feedback to grasp a ball, which instead learned to hover its hand between the ball and the camera so that, on the video feed, it merely looked to the human rater as though contact had been made; (4) "tetris" – Tom Murphy's general game-playing program, which, on recognizing that it was about to lose a game of Tetris, simply paused the game and left it frozen forever, since its objective had been framed purely in terms of never reaching an observed "game over"; and (5) "hideseek" – OpenAI's hide-and-seek agents, which, rather than sticking to the box-and-ramp strategies their designers anticipated, discovered and exploited outright glitches in the physics engine to clip through walls and "surf" on top of boxes.

What I would like from you is a ranking of these five cases by how purely each one instantiates adversarial Goodhart specifically, as opposed to being better explained by one of the other three mechanisms, or arguably not being an instance of Goodhart's law at all (for instance, if a case is really just an exploited software bug, with nothing in it that deserves to be called a "proxy" standing in for a harder-to-measure true goal).
