I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, but I first encountered Karl Popper's philosophy of science through David Deutsch's writing and the "critical rationalism" corner of the tech world. One question that has stayed with me since concerns how much Popper's own position on Darwinism shifted over his career: in his intellectual autobiography, Unended Quest, he argued that the theory of natural selection was "not a testable scientific theory but a metaphysical research programme," largely because he worried that "survival of the fittest" collapses into a tautology, and a few years later he published a partial recantation, conceding that many specific, small-scale claims about selection pressures — the sort biologists actually go out and test — are genuinely falsifiable, even if the broader Darwinian schema remained for him closer to a framework than a single theory.

Suppose Popper had lived to see the intelligent-design movement's court battles culminate in the 2005 Kitzmiller v. Dover ruling. That movement, going back at least to Phillip Johnson's "Darwin on Trial," has repeatedly argued that mainstream evolutionary biology is unfalsifiable dogma dressed up as science — an echo, intentional or not, of Popper's own early tautology complaint — while the courts have instead leaned on testability as a reason to keep intelligent design out of science classrooms. Would Popper have heard the ID side as belated vindication of his early doubts, or would his recantation, together with his demarcation criterion's treatment of explanations built around an untestable designer, have put him firmly behind the courts' reasoning instead? Please reason this through using his actual conceptual toolkit — the demarcation criterion, degrees of testability and corroboration, and his claim that a metaphysical research programme can still be fruitful and later mature into testable science — rather than settling for a generic "Popper cared about falsifiability" answer.
