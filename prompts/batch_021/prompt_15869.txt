I'm reporting a feature for a science-and-ideas magazine on the more esoteric corners of AI-safety research, and for the past few weeks I've been trying to get my head around the literature on "acausal" threats: the worry that a sufficiently capable agent—a future AI, say—could effectively extort another agent it has never causally interacted with, simply because each can reason about what the other would predictably do and act on that reasoning in advance—the kind of updateless, precommitment-heavy logic that decision theorists in this literature favor. What's drawn me in is a specific proposed countermeasure known as "surrogate goals": rather than trying to make itself immune to threats, an agent deliberately cultivates a substitute value that it commits to defending exactly as fiercely as its real one, even though the substitute doesn't actually matter to it much—the hope being that a rational extortionist, with nothing to gain by distinguishing between the two, may as well aim the threat at the decoy. I've arranged an interview (guest unnamed, at her request) with a decision theorist who has done some of the most influential work developing this proposal, mainly in connection with worries about worst-case conflict between future AI systems, and I don't want her to simply restate the pitch when we talk.

Could you draft nine or ten interview questions that push on the mechanics and the standard objections rather than inviting a rehearsed summary? I want at least one question on the "why would the extortionist bother" problem—if a sufficiently sophisticated threatener can tell, or doesn't care, that the target is a decoy, what is actually doing the protective work; one on the worry that the whole scheme only functions if the agent comes to genuinely, if partially, care about the surrogate goal, which seems to relocate a smaller copy of the original vulnerability rather than remove it; one on how this sits against the more familiar decision-theoretic advice to simply never give in to blackmail in the first place; and one on how adopting a public, legible commitment of this kind interacts with the "commitment race" worry, in which each side has an incentive to lock in extreme strategies before the other does. For each of these, add a short follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give—for instance, if she retreats to "the credibility only has to be probabilistic, not perfect," push on where that probability estimate is even supposed to come from, absent any real-world trials to calibrate against. End with one question asking what would have to be true for her to conclude that surrogate goals were a bad idea after all.
