I'm working on a feature about a concept that keeps surfacing in AI-safety and rationalist circles: "acausal trade," the claim that two agents with no causal link at all — separated by distance, time, or even branches of a multiverse — can still benefit from cooperating, provided each can reliably reason about what the other, being similarly rational, would do. I'd like a dialogue between two decision theorists who already agree on one-boxing in Newcomb's problem, so the standard causal-versus-evidential fight is off the table. One argues that the logic behind one-boxing generalizes cleanly: what matters is the correlation between decision procedures, not causal contact, so having good reason to think a distant, unreachable agent reasons as you do is enough to make cooperating with it rational. The other insists there's a principled line being crossed: Newcomb's predictor is at least causally downstream of facts about you, which is what grounds your evidential leverage, whereas a genuinely acausal "trading partner" can only be stipulated, never evidenced — so the whole framework collapses into a flattering story the agent tells itself. Have them work out, concretely, whether this disagreement changes how a rational agent should respond to acausal threats as opposed to acausal offers. Each should engage the other's actual weak point rather than restate their own position, and neither should win simply by asserting their theory's name.
