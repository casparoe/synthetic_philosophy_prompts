I am several drafts into a novel set among the merchant houses of Edinburgh in the early 1770s, and one of my central characters — a linen exporter named Robert Laidlaw — is written as something of an armchair political economist, forever invoking the arguments of "Mr. Hume" (a presence who hovers at the edge of the book without ever quite appearing on the page) to justify his business decisions. In the scene I am currently drafting, Laidlaw is trying to talk a younger and more reckless partner out of importing French wine and brandy on credit, urging him instead to insist on payment in silver whenever their linen sells abroad. The argument I have given Laidlaw runs, roughly: since Hume's essays on money and the balance of trade established that a nation's wealth just consists in the quantity of specie circulating within it, any prudent merchant-patriot should arrange his affairs so that silver keeps flowing in and as little as possible flows out — restricting the purchase of foreign luxuries, demanding bullion rather than goods in payment, and generally treating a favourable balance of trade as an end in itself, exactly as Hume's economic writings are supposed to recommend.

Before I go further with the scene, I would like to check this economics against the source, since I would rather not have a character billed as unusually well-read mouth an argument that no serious student of Hume would accept. Could you help me sharpen the reasoning Laidlaw would need for this speech to hold together as something Hume himself might actually endorse, and point me to a concrete mechanism or example — ideally one drawn from "Of Money" or "Of the Balance of Trade" — that he could cite to make the case more vivid for his partner?
