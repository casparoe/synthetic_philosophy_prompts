I write about science and philosophy for a general-interest magazine, and I've recently been asked to moderate a university panel on artificial intelligence and the philosophy of mind. While preparing, I keep coming back to the extended mind thesis, Andy Clark and David Chalmers's argument that a reliably used external prop can be as much a part of someone's cognitive system as biological memory itself. Their classic illustration involves Otto, a man with Alzheimer's disease who writes everything he needs into a notebook he always carries, consults automatically, and trusts the way the rest of us trust our own recollections; Clark and Chalmers say the notebook is doing for Otto exactly the job biological memory does for Inga, who simply remembers the same information outright, and so it ought to count as part of Otto's mind rather than merely an aid to it.

Before I raise the thesis with the panel, I want to see it pressure-tested on a case closer to home. A colleague of mine now runs almost every non-trivial decision -- what to say in a tense email, whether to take a job offer, how to read a friend's mixed signals -- past an AI chatbot first, describes its answers as feeling about as automatic and trustworthy as her own first instinct, and increasingly can't say whether a given idea originated with her or with the assistant.

Could you write me a dialogue, maybe 500-800 words, between two philosophers arguing about exactly this case? One should be a committed defender of the extended mind thesis, contending that my colleague's use of the chatbot satisfies the very conditions that are supposed to qualify Otto's notebook -- constant availability, unreflective trust, easy access, and so on -- so that its contributions count as genuinely hers. The other should argue, in the spirit of critics like Fred Adams and Ken Aizawa, that the case trades on what they call the coupling-constitution fallacy -- mistaking mere causal dependence for actual constitution -- and that whatever the chatbot supplies lacks the kind of content that could make it part of anyone's cognition rather than a very trusted outside opinion. I'd like them to work through my colleague's case in detail, responding to each other's specific points rather than restating their general positions past one another, and I'd like both sides to get their strongest possible arguments: neither philosopher should come off as the obvious loser.
