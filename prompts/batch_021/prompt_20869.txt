I'm running a graduate seminar this term on moral responsibility, and we've just spent two sessions on the "dilemma defense" that Robert Kane, David Widerker, and Carl Ginet mounted against Frankfurt-style counterexamples to the principle of alternative possibilities. As I've reconstructed it for the class, the dilemma turns on the status of the "prior sign" that Frankfurt's counterfactual intervener must track: if the sign is causally sufficient for the agent's ensuing decision, the case only shows that a causally determined choice can lack alternatives, which is no news to the incompatibilist and so begs the question against her; if the sign is not causally sufficient, the agent retains a genuine indeterministic alternative at the very moment of decision, so the principle survives after all, just in a form restricted to alternative volitions rather than alternative actions.

A few of my students are eager to treat Mele and Robb's "buffer" cases, and Pereboom's later "blockage" refinements of his Tax Evasion case, as settling the matter in Frankfurt's favor, on the grounds that these constructions dispense with any monitored sign and so simply step outside both horns. I'd like a sharper statement of the skeptical rejoinder before I let that view carry the discussion next week. Please argue for the following specific thesis: that no way of specifying a buffer- or blockage-style case precisely enough to be genuinely conceivable can avoid reproducing, at the sub-personal or mechanistic level, exactly the disjunction that the dilemma defense exploits -- so that these constructions do not refute the dilemma defense but merely relocate it, leaving its proponents with substantially the same problem one level down. I'd rather you engage with what has to be true, at the instant the backup process takes over, about whether the agent's own indeterministic process could still have gone the other way, than rest on the general slogan that "no sign is needed."
