"Explain the Lucas–Penrose argument against mechanism, using Gödel's first incompleteness theorem. Then present what you take to be the strongest objection to the argument." (20 pts, upper-level undergrad)

Heres the student's answer, copied basically verbatim:

"The Lucas-Penrose argument tries to show the human mind can't be a Turing machine, i.e. mechanism is false. It leans on Gödel's first incompleteness theorem: for any formal system F that's consistent and strong enough to represent arithmetic, there's a Gödel sentence G_F, built via the diagonal lemma so it effectively says 'I am not provable in F,' and if F is consistent then G_F is true but not provable in F. Lucas's move is: suppose I am just some such F, running on neurons. I can construct my own G_F and see that it's true, even though F can't prove it itself. Since this works for whatever F you propose as a model of me, I'm not identical to any single formal system, so mechanism is false. Penrose runs essentially the same argument in The Emperor's New Mind and Shadows of the Mind, and adds that whatever lets the brain outrun formal systems must involve non-computable physics, which he speculates could be quantum gravity effects in microtubules.

The strongest objection is Putnam's: even granting all of this, I need to already know that whatever F I secretly am is consistent before I can run the Lucas move on myself, and Gödel's second incompleteness theorem says a consistent F that can express its own consistency statement can't prove that statement. So the argument needs a premise it can't supply. This is exactly the worry Gödel raised himself, in his 1951 Gibbs lecture, where he first laid out the argument and concluded, a bit more cautiously than Lucas, that the mind can't be a machine."
