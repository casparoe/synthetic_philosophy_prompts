As a novelist, I'm working out the psychology of a character whose courage is never a settled fact: either she possesses it and something keeps intervening to stop it from showing itself, or the disposition itself blinks in and out of existence exactly when she is tested. Trying to think this through rigorously sent me into the metaphysics of dispositions, specifically the distinction between finkish dispositions and masked ones as counterexamples to the simple conditional analysis. I can recite the textbook contrast — a fink (as in C. B. Martin's electro-fink) removes or installs the disposition's causal basis at the very instant of the triggering stimulus, while a mask leaves that basis fully intact and instead blocks the manifestation further downstream — but I don't really grasp why the distinction is supposed to be so consequential. Specifically, could you explain, with some precision, why David Lewis's reformed counterfactual analysis, which builds in an intrinsic causal basis, is generally taken to neutralize finkish counterexamples but not masking ones? I want the actual logical structure behind that asymmetry, not just confirmation that it holds.
