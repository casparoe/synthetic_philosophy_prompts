I've recently agreed to help run the judging for a small annual fiction prize that our regional writers' association awards to an unpublished short story. The mechanics are simple: five of us each read all forty or so submissions independently and privately mark every story either "advance" or "reject" to produce a longlist; the panel then meets to discuss the survivors and narrows the field to three finalists, among whom the winner is settled by a straightforward majority vote. At the planning meeting, one of my fellow judges—a statistician-turned-novelist—argued that we are forgoing accuracy by keeping the panel so small. Her reasoning invoked the Condorcet Jury theorem: so long as each of us is even slightly more likely than not to sort a given story correctly, she said, adding more independent readers to the longlisting stage should drive the group's collective accuracy toward near-certainty, which makes a panel of five look needlessly conservative next to, say, eleven.

I found this persuasive at first, but the more I turn it over the less sure I am that it transfers cleanly onto what we actually do. First, several of us trained at the same two or three MFA programs and read the same handful of journals, and our post-vote discussion, meant to sharpen our judgment, presumably also nudges our opinions toward one another's—both effects seem to cut against the independence the theorem needs. Second, the theorem seems to presuppose some fact of the matter about which story is "correct" to advance, in the way a jury's verdict tracks whether a crime actually occurred, and I am not convinced literary merit admits of a correct answer in that sense at all. Third, our final vote is a three-way pick among finalists rather than a binary in/out call, which feels like it might belong to a different strand of Condorcet's work entirely rather than to the jury theorem proper. I would be grateful if you could lay out the most important implications of the Condorcet Jury theorem for a small panel structured like ours: where the statistician's case for a larger longlisting panel actually holds up, where these three worries of mine genuinely undercut it, and whether the theorem, or anything in its vicinity, has real bearing on that final three-way vote at all.
