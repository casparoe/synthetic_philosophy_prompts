We just finished working through Galen Strawson's "Basic Argument" against moral responsibility, and it is my turn to grade. Since none of us has a background in the subject, I would like a more expert pass at this before I send my comments back, so that I am not unfair to the person who wrote the answer, and so that I do not wave through something incorrect simply because it is written with confidence.

The question that was set read: "State Galen Strawson's Basic Argument for the conclusion that no one is ever truly or ultimately morally responsible for what they do. Your answer should address: (a) what work the notion of being causa sui is doing in the argument; (b) whether the argument's force depends on whether determinism or indeterminism is true; and (c) what, if anything, Strawson takes the argument to imply about our ordinary practices of praise, blame, and punishment." Here, word for word, is the answer that one of the group's members, whom I will call Dave, turned in:

"Strawson's Basic Argument tries to show that 'true' or 'ultimate' moral responsibility — the kind that would make praise and blame deserved in the deepest sense, and not merely useful — is impossible for any agent whatsoever, human or otherwise. Whatever you do in a given situation, you do it because of the way you are, mentally speaking: your beliefs, desires, values, and general character. So if you are to be truly responsible for what you do, you must be truly responsible for being the way you are, at least in the relevant respects. But to be responsible for being the way you are, you would have had to choose your own character in light of some prior aims and values, and to be responsible for choosing it in light of those aims and values you would already have to be responsible for having them, which pushes the question back a further step. Since the regress cannot terminate in a choice made from nowhere, with no prior character to choose from, you cannot be the ultimate, self-caused source (causa sui) of your own character — and Strawson thinks this holds even for a hypothetical being that created its own nature outright, such as God, since any such act of self-creation would itself have to flow from a nature that the being had not chosen. Because you are not responsible for your character, you are not, in the deepest sense, responsible for what that character leads you to do.

Strawson takes this to be at bottom an argument against determinism, since it is determinism that guarantees that each stage of the regress is fixed by an earlier one; were some of our choices undetermined rather than fully caused by prior states, the chain could in principle be broken by a free, spontaneous act that was not simply the product of an earlier self, and that act could be the point at which a person first became truly responsible for her own character going forward. This is why the Basic Argument is usually classed alongside other hard determinist arguments, such as Derk Pereboom's, as a reason for thinking determinism rules out moral responsibility.

Extending his father P.F. Strawson's account of the reactive attitudes — resentment, gratitude, indignation, and the like — Galen Strawson takes the argument to show that these attitudes, however natural and psychologically ingrained, cannot be ultimately justified, and he concludes that praise, blame, and retributive punishment ought to be abandoned in favor of purely forward-looking, consequentialist reasons for how we treat one another. Read this way, the Basic Argument is really a piece of practical ethics wearing the costume of metaphysics, and its actual target is the institution of retributive punishment."
