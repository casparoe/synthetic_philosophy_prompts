I recently moved from engineering into the privacy policy group at the consumer app I work for, and this week I have to draft the internal position paper on whether to simplify our data-sharing consent screen. The strongest argument against simplifying it comes from our growth team, and it rests entirely on revealed preference: roughly 97 percent of users click "Accept All" without reviewing the options, almost none of the minority who do customize their settings ever loosen them again, and growth argues that this is exactly the kind of hard behavioral evidence that revealed preference theory was developed to treat as more authoritative than self-reported survey data, in which the same users describe real discomfort with our data practices. On their view, a stable, repeated choice is by its nature better evidence of what someone actually wants than anything that person says about themselves, which makes the survey results close to irrelevant. Before I put this argument in my paper, I want to check that I have the underlying economics right: does revealed preference theory really support treating consistent click-through behavior on a screen like ours as stronger evidence of users' genuine preferences than their own stated views, and if it does, is there any principled economic reason left to keep weighing the survey data at all?
