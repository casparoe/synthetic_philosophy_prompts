I've been chosen to give the student address at my graduation this spring, and after a term spent in a seminar on Keynes's non-economic writings, I want to build the speech around "Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren" -- specifically his claim that humanity's real, permanent problem was never scarcity itself but what to do with our freedom once scarcity is solved. I can't in good conscience pretend his fifteen-hour week ever arrived, and several of the professors who will be in the audience know the essay well enough to notice if I dodge that; at the same time, I don't want to give my family and the other non-academics in the crowd a lecture on late capitalism. I'd also like to bring in the more chastened Keynes of "My Early Beliefs," who came to doubt whether the Bloomsbury circle's faith in refined individual judgment could really extend beyond a small, cultivated elite. Could you draft a five-minute speech that holds this tension honestly, without resolving it into false cheer, and still leaves my class with something real to carry into an economy full of automation anxiety?
