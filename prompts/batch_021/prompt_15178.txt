I spent almost forty years doing experimental condensed-matter physics before I retired, and this past year I've fallen into philosophy pretty hard -- mostly podcasts, plus a little "philosophy for retirees" reading group that somehow talked me into co-hosting a monthly recorded conversation. The rabbit hole started when I reread Kelly's old 1956 paper on optimal betting for an information theory refresher and found out there's a live philosophical fight over it: whether betting to maximize long-run geometric growth (the Kelly fraction) is just one risk preference among many, the way Samuelson always insisted -- he even wrote one rebuttal using only one-syllable words, apparently just to mock how simple he thought the reasoning was -- or whether it's actually the uniquely rational policy once you take seriously that an individual only ever lives out one time-average trajectory and never gets to experience the ensemble average across all the parallel worlds where the bet could've gone differently.

Next month's guest for our little series defends that stronger claim, which she calls geometric rationality: full Kelly betting isn't just a defensible preference, its THE rational response to any repeated or compounding bet, full stop, because ordinary expected-value reasoning is quietly borrowing an ensemble average that no actual agent ever lives through. When people push back she likes to say they're "still thinking like an ensemble," smile, and move on to the next question, and I don't want to let her get away with that twice in the same conversation.

Could you draft me seven or eight interview questions, ordered so that dismissal gets harder to use as we go? I want at least one question that presses the Samuelson charge directly -- why isn't this just a log utility function with better marketing -- one on why real Kelly bettors and quant shops almost always run fractional Kelly rather than full Kelly given how ugly the drawdowns get, and one on what her framework even recommends for a genuinely one-off decision that can't be redescribed as part of a long repeated sequence. For each question, tell me the answer she's most likely to give and hand me a sharper follow-up keyed to it, and end the sequence with one question on whether anything could count as evidence against geometric rationality, or whether she thinks of it more as a theorem than as a claim that could turn out false.
