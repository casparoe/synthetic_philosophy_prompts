I've been reading Spinoza on my own for about a year now, no philosophy background at all really (I'm a dental hygienist), ever since I fell down a rabbit hole from that Einstein line about believing in "Spinoza's God." I found one of those free old ebooks that bundles the Ethics together with a couple of his other, way less famous books, and last week I finally worked my way to chapter 11 of the "Political Treatise," the one on democracy, which he apparently never finished because he died partway through writing it. I hit a passage in there that's been bugging me all week, and I don't really have anyone around to talk this stuff over with, so I'm hoping you can help me pick it apart.

The chapter opens by defining democracy as the government where "all, without exception," who obey the laws and are "independent," get a vote and can hold office, and it reads like the payoff line of the whole book. Then a couple sentences later "independent" turns out to already exclude women (along with slaves and kids), and when he raises the obvious objection against himself, this is what he actually writes (it's the old Elwes translation, which is what my copy has):

"But, perhaps, someone will ask, whether women are under men's authority by nature or institution? For if it has been by mere institution, then we had no reason compelling us to exclude women from government. But if we consult experience itself, we shall find that the origin of it is in their weakness. For there has never been a case of men and women reigning together, but wherever on the earth men are found, there we see that men rule, and women are ruled, and that on this plan, both sexes live in harmony. But on the other hand, the Amazons, who are reported to have held rule of old, did not suffer men to stop in their country, but reared only their female children, killing the males to whom they gave birth. But if by nature women were equal to men, and were equally distinguished by force of character and ability, in which human power and therefore human right chiefly consist; surely among nations so many and different some would be found, where both sexes rule alike, and others, where men are ruled by women, and so brought up, that they can make less use of their abilities. And since this is nowhere the case, one may assert with perfect propriety, that women have not by nature equal right with men: but that they necessarily give way to men, and that thus it cannot happen, that both sexes should rule alike, much less that men should be ruled by women. But if we further reflect upon human passions, how men, in fact, generally love women merely from the passion of lust, and esteem their cleverness and wisdom in proportion to the excellence of their beauty, and also how very ill-disposed men are to suffer the women they love to show any sort of favour to others, and other facts of this kind, we shall easily see that men and women cannot rule alike without great hurt to peace. But of this enough."

And then that's genuinely it, the whole book just stops right there, no summing up or anything. I looked it up afterward and apparently "but of this enough" really is the last sentence he ever wrote, mid-argument, before he died, which if true is a strange note for the book to end on.

What I can't work out is whether this argument actually holds together on its own terms, or whether he's just piling things up. He seems to be running three or four different moves at once here - plain historical track record, then the Amazons as a counterexample that he brings up and then kind of steps around instead of really answering, then something like "if women could rule we'd have seen it somewhere by now," then a completely seperate closing point about jealousy wrecking any mixed government - and I can't tell if he thinks these all back each other up or if he's stacking them because he isn't fully confident in any one by itself. But honestly the bigger thing nagging at me, and I've been chewing on this alot, is this: I thought the whole spine of his philosophy was that "nature" doesn't hand out value judgments, that a bigger fish eating a smaller one exercises exactly as much "natural right," in his own technical sense, as anything else does, and that you're specifically not supposed to read whatever has always happened as proof of what has to happen. So when he turns around here and says women "have not by nature equal right with men," basically because that's how it's always gone historically, is that him quietly breaking his own rule, or is it actually the predictable result of applying that very same rule - right just is power, so less power just literally means less right - to a place I really don't want it applied? I'd genuinely like to understand which one is going on, instead of just being annoyed about it.
