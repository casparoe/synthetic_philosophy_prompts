Here's what I've got:

Hegel's argument isn't that war is good the way a tyrant thinks war is good, its something narrower and stranger: that a state of permanent peace between nations would actually rot the ethical life of a people from the inside. He's got this image, I think its from the addition to paragraph 324 of the Philosophy of Right though don't quote me on the exact number, comparing it to the sea versus a pond - the sea stays healthy because winds keep it moving, while a pond with no current just goes stagnant. States are the same way for him. A state that never has to risk anything, that just lets its citizens sink into private life and property and comfort forever, loses its grip on the idea that anything could matter more than that comfort. War is the thing that reminds a people the state they belong to is worth more than their money or even their own lives, and for Hegel that reminder is itself doing ethical work, not just a tragic cost you pay.

This is also more or less his answer to Kant, who a few decades earlier had floated a federation of republics that would outlaw war between its members for good. Hegel's objection isn't "that's naive," its closer to structural: a federation like that is still just an agreement between separate sovereign wills, and an agreement between wills can always be broken by those same wills later, so it never rises above contingency into actual necessity. Without some judge standing above both sides the way a court stands above two citizens, disputes between states that can't be talked out just are the kind of thing that gets settled by force, and that's not a flaw in the system, that's what being a sovereign state means.

Where it gets less cartoonish, and the bit I actually want my officer to lean on, is that Hegel also builds limits into war on his own terms. He talks about how "civilized" nations still respect ambassadors in the middle of a war and don't send armies after family life or private citizens, only after the other state as a state. So on his account its not "anything goes" once war starts, it's specifically the state as a political unit that gets to risk itself and demand risk of its own citizens, not a free for all against the whole population on the other side.

I want him to actually believe all this, not just recite it, so be honest with me about where it still sounds like a lecture instead of a person talking.
