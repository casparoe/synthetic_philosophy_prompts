This came up at one of our regular Sunday dinners with the rationalist-meetup crowd and I still don't know what to think about it. My friend Owen is about to spend four days at one of those pricey "transformational intensive" retreats -- sleep deprivation, forced vulnerability circles, the works -- and he mentioned over dessert that he's currently about 20% confident his startup is fundamentally unworkable, but he'd bet good money that by the last night of the retreat he'll be at 80%+ confidence it's unworkable, 'because that's just what happens to everyone who does the program, regardless of how they walked in.' Our friend Sasha, whose read way more formal epistemology than the rest of us, jumped on this immediately, and the argument that followed is still bugging me.

(1) Sasha said: if Owen's already certain what his credence will be on day four, then van Fraassen's Reflection Principle says his credence right now, conditional on that certainty, has to equal 80% too -- refusing to move is refusing to fully trust a future self he claims to have complete information about, which is a strange kind of self-distrust from someone who isn't even disputing the prediction. (2) Owen said Reflection is supposed to model deferring to a future self's rational updating on evidence, and this isn't that: the shift will come from exhaustion and forced sharing circles, not from him learning anything new about unit economics, so moving now would just be letting the retreat win a round early. (3) Sasha came back with the Dutch book argument that's supposed to justify Reflection in the first place: if Owen won't move now while being certain what he'll believe later, a bookie can lay out bets across the two days that take his money no matter how things actually go -- and 'the process producing my future belief is untrustworthy' can't be a free pass from that, because if it were, literally anyone could dodge Reflection on any inconvenient future opinion just by declaring in advance that it'll come from bias instead of reasoning, and the principle would stop constraining anybody. (4) Owen said that's not what he's doing -- he's not calling the process unreliable out of nowhere, he's watched four different friends go into that exact program with four different starting opinions and come out all four converted to whatever the final exercise pointed at, independent of which of them actually turned out to be right -- that's an honest track record of the process being insensitive to the truth, and noticing that in advance is no different from knowing a coin is rigged and refusing to reason as if it were fair.

Neither of them budged, and I can see the shape of both arguments well enough that I've lost confidence I could settle it myself (I know this stuff mostly from LW threads and an SEP article, so go easy on me if I've smudged a detail somewhere).
