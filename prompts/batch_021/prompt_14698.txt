Had a long, kind of frustrating argument with my friend Dan last night about anthropic reasoning and neither of us actually budged. It started because he brought up Bostrom's "presumptuous philosopher" thing -- physicists have narrowed it down to two theories of the universe, equally supported by all the evidence so far, except one theory implies there are vastly more observers in existence than the other, and the philosopher struts in and says forget the experiment, self-indication assumption (SIA) already tells us which one's true, since its just way more likely we'd exist at all if that one's correct. Dan thinks that's basically a reductio of SIA -- nobody should get to out-reason the physicists from an armchair like that. I said sure, but self-sampling assumption (SSA) isn't free either -- it's the thing behind the doomsday argument, where just noting your own rough birth rank among everyone who's ever lived is supposed to shift your credence toward the human race ending relatively soon rather than blooming into some huge future population, and that feels every bit as unearned as the philosopher's move. Dan's comeback was that the doomsday argument at least stays answerable to future evidence, while the presumptuous philosopher gets to dodge empirical work entirely, so the two aren't equally bad. We went in circles for like an hour. Can you write an essay that actually settles this -- go through the strongest arguments for and against SSA and SIA, engage honestly with how each one handles both the presumptuous philosopher and the doomsday argument, and land on a real verdict instead of "reasonable people disagree."
