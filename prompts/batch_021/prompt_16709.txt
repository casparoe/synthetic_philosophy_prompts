During a reading group I'm part of that's working through classical Chinese philosophy in translation, our session on the Zhuangzi turned into a genuine argument between two members over how to read the famous butterfly dream passage that closes the chapter usually called 'Qiwulun' (often translated 'On Making All Things Equal'). It's the passage where Zhuang Zhou dreams he is a butterfly, flitting about happily, then wakes and cannot say whether he is a man who dreamed he was a butterfly or a butterfly now dreaming he is a man, before closing by naming the situation 'the transformation of things.' (1) Grace opened by arguing that this is basically Zhuangzi's version of the dream argument from Descartes' First Meditation: a compact piece of skeptical epistemology showing that, from the inside, waking and dreaming experience can't be told apart, so the fact that Zhuang Zhou cannot tell which he 'really' is amounts to the same worry Descartes raises about being deceived by dreams. (2) Owen pushed back that this maps the passage onto Descartes for no good reason: Descartes treats the indistinguishability of dreaming and waking as a problem to be solved on the way to something certain, whereas this passage never tries to certify either side as the real one -- it just names the boundary 'the transformation of things' and leaves it there, which suggests the point is about the relativity of perspectives and the dissolving of a fixed self, not about how we could ever know which state we're in. (3) Grace granted that the passage itself doesn't resolve anything the way Descartes does, but pointed out that earlier in the very same chapter Zhuangzi explicitly raises the worry that waking life itself might be a kind of dream we mistake for wakefulness -- the bit about someone who dreams of a feast and wakes up weeping, and about there being a 'great awakening' after which this whole life might turn out to have been a 'great dream' -- so the chapter clearly does traffic in real doubt about telling dreaming from waking, and the butterfly is just its most vivid packaging. (4) Owen answered that the 'great dream' passage shows the two texts share raw material -- dreaming, and not knowing which state is real -- but not that they are doing the same thing with it: Zhuangzi never turns that predicament into a method for reaching certainty the way Descartes does, he turns it into a reason to stop insisting on the distinction, so calling this 'the same move as Descartes' mistakes a shared image for a shared purpose. Neither of them budged, and I'd like an outside read on the actual exchange rather than just a verdict on who 'won.'
