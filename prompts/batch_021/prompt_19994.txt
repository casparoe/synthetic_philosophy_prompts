And here's what Priya wrote: "The reason a cheap signal doesn't work is that if it costs the sender nothing, a low-quality individual has just as much incentive to send it as a high-quality one does -- nothing is stopping a weak male from 'lying' and displaying the exact same signal as a strong one. Once that happens the signal doesn't carry real information anymore, so receivers should evolve to just ignore it and the whole system collapses. Zahavi's solution is that if the signal is costly enough, only genuinely high-quality individuals can actually afford to produce it, so it can't be faked and it stays honest.

For this to work, the cost has to be something a low-quality individual would find not worth paying, since the fitness hit outweighs whatever benefit they'd get, while a high-quality individual can pay it and still come out ahead overall. This was actually Zahavi's big breakthrough in his 1975 paper, where he worked out the math and showed this kind of costly signalling can be an evolutionarily stable strategy, which is what finally got a lot of skeptical biologists on board after years of people doubting it.

A handicap is different from an index signal, even though both are supposed to be hard to fake. The peacock's tail is actually a really good example of an index: its so metabolically expensive and such a predation risk that only a genuinely healthy, high-quality male can grow a big symmetrical one and survive, so peahens can basically read tail size as a direct readout of male condition.

For my examples: the non-human one is the peacock's tail like I just described, its costly enough that it reliably tracks male quality. For the human one I picked promposals -- spending real money, building some elaborate poster or setup, and publicly risking getting rejected is a genuine cost, and someone who isn't actually that into the person has no reason to pay it, so a big promposal credibly signals real interest in a way that just casually asking in the hallway wouldn't."
