Here is the student's answer in full:

"Standard expected utility theory says that a gamble g = {x_1, p_1; ...; x_n, p_n} should be valued at EU(g) = Σ_i p_i u(x_i), and requires a rational agent's preferences to satisfy the Independence Axiom: if two gambles agree on a common outcome mixed in at the same probability, one's preference between them shouldn't depend on that shared part. The Allais paradox shows that most people prefer a certain \$1,000,000 (L4) to the gamble {\$5M, 0.1; \$1M, 0.89; \$0, 0.01} (L3), while also preferring {\$5M, 0.1; \$0, 0.9} (L1) to {\$1M, 0.11; \$0, 0.89} (L2). It's a short algebraic exercise to show that no assignment of u(\$0), u(\$1M), and u(\$5M) can make EU rank L1 above L2 while also ranking L4 above L3 -- exactly the pattern Independence rules out.

Buchak's response is to keep the probability function p and the utility function u, but add a third component, a risk function r: [0,1] → [0,1] with r(0) = 0, r(1) = 1 and r non-decreasing, which reweights each outcome according to its own probability: REU(g) = Σ_i r(p_i) u(x_i). When r is convex the agent is risk-avoidant, since shrinking an outcome's probability shrinks its contribution to REU faster than it would under EU; when r is concave the agent is risk-inclined; and when r is the identity function the agent is globally neutral, which is just the EU-maximizing special case. Crucially, r operates on top of u rather than through it, so an agent's risk-avoidance needn't show up as diminishing marginal utility for money the way it must under ordinary EU theory.

This is also why the two functions can't just be collapsed into one another: global risk-attitude (how much weight to give worse versus better possibilities) and diminishing marginal value (how much a given outcome is worth in itself) are, for Buchak, conceptually distinct attitudes, and she needs separate functions to represent them separately. That said, I'd argue the separation is more a matter of representational transparency than strict necessity: since u, p, and r are all ultimately fixed by fitting one and the same preference ordering, an REU-maximizer's choices could in principle always be redescribed as the choices of an ordinary EU-maximizer with a suitably exotic utility function u*. On this view, the real payoff of REU theory is that it makes an agent's attitude to risk explicit and independently measurable, not that it rationalizes preferences that no EU function could rationalize at all.

Because of this, REU theory counts as normative in a way that Kahneman and Tversky's prospect theory does not. Prospect theory also transforms probabilities with a function shaped much like r -- an inverse-S curve that overweights small probabilities and underweights large ones -- but Kahneman and Tversky offer it as a purely descriptive model of how people deviate from rational choice, not as a claim about what rationality actually permits. REU theory is much closer in spirit to Quiggin's rank-dependent 'anticipated utility' theory, which already reweights cumulative probabilities by an outcome's rank in order to avoid violating stochastic dominance; Buchak's theory is best understood as a generalization of Quiggin's apparatus, backed by a representation theorem, and put to explicitly normative use: on her view, REU-maximizers aren't biased, they're simply attending to a genuine third dimension of rational choice that EU theory leaves out."
