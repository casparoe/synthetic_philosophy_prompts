**Question (20 points).** State Lewis's Principal Principle precisely, including the role played by admissible evidence. Explain why, given Lewis's commitment to Humean supervenience, "undermining futures" pose a problem for the Principle (the difficulty Lewis called "the big bad bug"). Finally, explain how the New Principle is supposed to resolve this difficulty, and briefly state whether you think it succeeds.

**Student's answer.**

According to Lewis's Principal Principle, a rational agent's credence in a proposition A, conditional on the proposition that the objective chance of A at t is x together with any admissible evidence E, should equal x: C(A | Ch_t(A) = x & E) = x. The admissibility clause is doing real work: without it the principle would be trivially false, since an agent with, say, a crystal ball that simply shows her how a chancy coin will land should not defer to the coin's forty-percent chance of landing heads. Historical information up to t and information about the chances themselves are Lewis's standard examples of admissible evidence, and in practice this is usually glossed as a distinction between the past, which is admissible, and the future, which is not: an agent may freely use everything she knows about how the world has gone so far, but not non-chance-mediated information about how things are going to go.

Lewis is also committed to Humean supervenience, on which chances, like laws, are not primitive but are fixed by whichever probabilistic system best combines simplicity, strength, and fit with the total pattern of occurrent fact across all of history — crucially, including the future relative to t, and not merely the past. This generates the difficulty Lewis calls "the big bad bug." Because the chance function in force at t is only fully fixed once the entire future course of history is in, there are, for any chance hypothesis Ch_t(A) = x, alternative continuations of history after t — "x-undermining" futures — that agree with actuality on everything up to and including t but that, were they to occur, would make some rival system best overall, and so would make some other function the true chance function at t. Since any genuinely indeterministic process must leave positive chance for a spread of different continuations, some undermining continuations should themselves receive positive chance from the very function they would undermine. But applying the Principal Principle to the proposition that some undermining future obtains seems to squeeze the chance of undermining toward zero on pain of contradiction. A chance distribution therefore appears forced to be nearly certain of its own future vindication, which sits badly with the thought that which system is best is supposed to be a wholly contingent, a posteriori matter.

Lewis's fix, laid out in the postscript to "Humean Supervenience Debugged" and there called the New Principle, is to stop conditionalizing on the proposition that the x-function obtains simpliciter, since that is exactly the proposition hostage to undermining. Instead, the New Principle conditionalizes on a rigidified specification of whatever chance function actually governs the world in question, without thereby asserting which of its possible continuations will be the one that actually transpires. Because the conditioning proposition no longer entails, via Humean supervenience, that no undermining future occurs, the chance of an undermining future is no longer forced toward zero, and the contradiction is blocked.

I think this is a genuine solution rather than a mere patch: it preserves the deferential core of the original Principle while relocating exactly the bit of content — which continuation is actual — that was causing the trouble. The cost is that "the chance function" now has to be picked out in a more theoretically loaded, quasi-rigid way than the plain descriptive reading Lewis's 1980 paper seemed to invite, which is a real, if bearable, departure from the reductive spirit of Humean supervenience.
