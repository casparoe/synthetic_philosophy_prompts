Its about how Timothy Williamson's "luminosity" idea relates to the KK principle -- the thesis that if you know P, you're automatically in a position to know that you know P.

As I understand it, luminosity is basically the more general idea: a condition is "luminous" if, whenever it actually holds, you're always in a position to know that it holds -- classic examples being stuff like feeling cold, being in pain, or something seeming a certain way to you. Williamson's famous move is to run a margin-for-error argument (using cases where some sensation changes so gradually that you can never pinpoint the exact moment it starts or stops) to conclude that pretty much nothing is luminous, not even our own mental states. And people treat this as also killing KK, since "knowing that P" is just another condition -- so if no non-trivial condition is luminous, then knowing isn't luminous either, and KK falls with it.

What I can't settle with my friends is whether KK is really nothing more than a special case of luminosity, so that accepting the anti-luminosity argument basically forces you to give up KK too, or whether KK is actually its own separate thing that deserves separate treatment -- like, it's had this whole independent life in modal epistemic logic (people just stipulating positive introspection as an axiom, going back to Hintikka-style stuff) totally apart from luminosity, and there are also these separate externalist worries about KK floating around (the idea that you might know P via some reliable process without having any way to know that the process is reliable, so you'd know P without knowing that you know it) that have nothing to do with vague sensations or margins of error. So could you actually work through how these two relate -- is KK just luminosity wearing a costume, or does it stand on genuinely different ground -- and then tell me straight up which of the two you think is in worse shape overall, rather than giving me a wishy-washy "well it depends" answer?
