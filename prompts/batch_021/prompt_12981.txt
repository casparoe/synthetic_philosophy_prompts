I spent most of my working life as a behavioural ecologist, watching starlings and jays choose between a small worm now and a bigger worm after a wait, so discount curves are second nature to me from the animal side of things. Since retiring I've joined a little philosophy reading group at the library, and the last two meetings we got completely bogged down in Book VII of Aristotle's Nicomachean Ethics, on akrasia. I think I've more or less got what Aristotle means by it (someone who judges, right there in the moment, that doing X is bad for them, and does X anyway, without simply being ignorant of the facts) — but its bugging me how that connects, or maybe doesn't connect, to hyperbolic discounting, which I know from a totally different angle.

Here's the specific thing I can't work out. In the discounting literature (Strotz, Ainslie, that whole tradition) the classic reversal — preferring $100 now over $110 in a month, but preferring $110 in thirteen months over $100 in twelve — just falls out of a perfectly well-behaved, locally rational discount function; nobody's reasoning has to fail for that to happen, it's just what a hyperbolic (as opposed to exponential) discount rate does across a sequence of "selves" at different times. But Aristotle, and later Davidson in his weakness-of-will paper, seem to want something stronger and weirder: an agent who goes on judging, right up until the moment of action, that the thing is bad, and does it anyway — presumably why Davidson needs that whole story about the mind being partitioned, so the contradiction isn't fully "in view" all at once. So is the hyperbolic discounter actually an akratic in Aristotle's sense, or are these two genuinely different phenomena that just happen to produce the same "knows better, does worse" behaviour from the outside? And where does someone like Holton fit into sorting this out, with his idea that weakness of will is really about failing to stick to your own resolutions rather than about acting against a judgement as such — is he effectively telling us the discounting people and the Aristotle people were never quite talking about the same thing? I'd like an explanation that doesn't presuppose I've already read the secondary literature; between Aristotle himself and a working scientist's feel for discount functions, that's about all I'm bringing to this one.
