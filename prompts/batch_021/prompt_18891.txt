The protagonist of the novel I am currently drafting — a self-made entrepreneur convinced he has willed himself entirely free of conventional morality — likes to invoke Nietzsche's "sovereign individual" as a kind of personal credo, treating the phrase as though it were a straightforward endorsement of his own self-image. Before deciding how much ironic distance to build into the characterization, I want to be sure I haven't misread the source myself. Here is the passage, from the Second Essay of On the Genealogy of Morals, section 2:

"Let us place ourselves... at the end of this tremendous process, where the tree at last brings forth fruit, where society and its morality of custom finally bring to light that to which they were only the means: we then find as the ripest fruit on its tree the sovereign individual, that being who resembles no one but himself, who has broken away from the morality of custom, the autonomous, supra-moral individual (for 'autonomous' and 'moral' are mutually exclusive), in short, the man of his own independent, long will, who is entitled to make promises."

Nietzsche goes on to describe this figure as carrying a proud, almost bodily awareness of the power and freedom he has achieved. Taken on its own, the passage reads as an unqualified celebration of self-mastery. Yet the rest of the essay treats the very machinery said to produce this figure — breeding, punishment, the internalization of cruelty as "bad conscience" — as a kind of sickness, and elsewhere in the book Nietzsche is deeply skeptical that any stable, willing self stands behind action at all. Is there a genuine textual basis here for reading the sovereign individual as Nietzsche's sincere ideal, or does its place in the argument support a more ambivalent reading, on which my protagonist's confident self-description would itself be a symptom of the disease being diagnosed rather than evidence that he has escaped it?
