A. J. Ayer's brush with death in 1988 has become a stock anecdote in discussions of near-death experience: while hospitalized, he suffered a cardiac arrest lasting several minutes and afterward described the episode -- a red light, an impression that space itself was somehow in the charge of beings responsible for administering it -- in his essay "What I Saw When I Was Dead," followed by a more cautious piece once the press had inevitably run with the idea that a famous atheist had glimpsed evidence of an afterlife.

A few of us got into an argument about this after a seminar on logical positivism, and I'd like your help settling it. What strikes me as philosophically interesting is Ayer's own verdict on the episode: he was adamant that it gave him no reason whatsoever to believe in God, a soul, or any religious picture of survival, yet he also conceded that it had somewhat weakened his confidence that his own eventual death would be the absolute end of him. That second concession is the part I want you to go after.

I'd like you to make the case that this concession sits poorly with Ayer's own standing philosophical commitments, and that, judged strictly by his own lights, the episode should have left his credence entirely unmoved. Recall that Ayer's verification principle does not demand conclusive proof for a statement to count as meaningful; it only requires that some possible observation be relevant to raising or lowering its probability -- his "weak" sense of verifiability, as against the "strong" sense he explicitly rejected as too restrictive to do any useful work. Recall too that Ayer was a lifelong physicalist about persons, with no patience for a soul or a mind capable of detaching from a functioning body. Please argue that even on this comparatively permissive, weak-verifiability standard, a few minutes of reversible cardiac arrest -- after which, by all indications, his brain resumed normal operation rather than having permanently and irreversibly ceased to function -- cannot constitute relevant evidence, even weakly, for the hypothesis that actually concerned him: namely, whether some subject continues to have experiences once the brain has permanently stopped functioning altogether. Consider, too, whether the episode might instead count as mild evidence for the opposite conclusion. You're welcome to draw on whatever you find useful from Ayer's broader treatment of personal identity and the mind-body relation, provided the central move -- treating reversible and irreversible loss of brain function as evidentially quite different situations -- remains the crux of the argument.
