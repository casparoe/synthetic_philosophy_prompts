So I've been on a bit of a Hobbes kick lately (no philosophy degree here, just a guy who read Leviathan cause someone on a podcast said it was "way more readable than you'd think for 1651"), and I got kind of stuck on this bit in chapter 5 where he says reasoning is basically nothing more than "reckoning" -- like it's literally just addition and subtraction of ideas, the same way arithmetic is addition and subtraction of numbers. That felt like a really weirdly modern thing for a guy to say a few centuries before computers existed. And then earlier in the book he talks about sensation and imagination as just being motion, caused by stuff physically pressing on our sense organs and rattling around inside us.

This got me wondering: if Hobbes came back today and someone explained functionalism to him -- the idea in philosophy of mind that a mental state like pain or belief is defined purely by the causal role it plays in a system (what causes it, what it causes), so it doesn't matter whether that role is played by neurons, silicon chips, or some weird alien goo, as long as the pattern of inputs and outputs matches (this "it doesn't matter what it's made of" part is the multiple realizability thing) -- would he be into it? Like, is "reason is reckoning" basically him inventing functionalism 300 years early, meaning he'd be totally cool with a computer reckoning it's way to real thoughts? Or does his actual materialism, where thought seems to be tied to specific matter literally being pushed and rattled around in specific ways, mean he'd actually side against the modern functionalists and insist the physical stuff has to be the right KIND of stuff, not just doing the right kind of job? My roommate thinks these two questions are basically the same thing and I'm not so sure. Curious what you think, and what Hobbes would've actually said.
