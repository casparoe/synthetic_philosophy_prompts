Here's what the student wrote, verbatim:

"The Continuum Hypothesis says that there's no set whose size is strictly between the size of the natural numbers and the size of the real numbers - i.e. no cardinality strictly between aleph-null and 2^aleph-null. Cantor proposed this back in the 1870s and spent years trying and failing to prove it.

Its independence from ZFC was established in two steps. First, Gödel showed in 1938 that CH can't be disproved from ZFC: he constructed the constructible universe L, a kind of minimal model of ZFC built up in stages using only definable operations, and showed that CH holds in L. So if ZFC is consistent, so is ZFC + CH. Then in 1963, Cohen invented the method of forcing to build a different model of ZFC in which CH is false, showing that ZFC + not-CH is equally consistent. So neither CH nor its negation can be proved from ZFC, assuming ZFC itself is consistent.

Really this is just a set-theoretic version of the same incompleteness phenomenon Gödel discovered in arithmetic: any axiom system strong enough to be interesting will have sentences that are true but unprovable, and CH is simply one more casualty of that fact, just dressed up in the language of sets instead of numbers. Since CH's status can't be settled by proof from the axioms, the natural conclusion - and one Gödel himself eventually accepted - is that CH just doesn't have a determinate truth value; asking whether it's "really" true ends up being a bit like asking whether the rules of chess are true. That's presumably also why comparatively little work has gone into CH specifically since the 60s, with set theorists moving on to other questions like large cardinals instead."
