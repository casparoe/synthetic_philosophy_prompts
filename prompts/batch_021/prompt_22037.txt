I spent my working life as an experimental physicist, and it's only in retirement that I've started reading philosophy in any serious way; foundations of mathematics turned out to be a natural entry point, since I had always been faintly uneasy, as a working scientist, about how casually one moves from "assuming no solution exists leads to a contradiction" to "a solution exists," without ever producing the thing itself. That unease eventually sent me to a secondhand copy of van Dalen's *Logic and Structure*, and a few chapters into the material on intuitionistic logic I ran into Glivenko's theorem, which has left me with a puzzle I can't quite resolve unaided.

As I understand it, Glivenko showed in 1929, in a paper explicitly framed as a commentary on Brouwer's logic, that for propositional formulas, A is a classical tautology if and only if its double negation ¬¬A is provable intuitionistically. The example that made this vivid for me is excluded middle itself: intuitionistic logic notoriously withholds a proof of A ∨ ¬A, yet apparently proves ¬¬(A ∨ ¬A) without difficulty. On its face this seems to license a much stronger conclusion than I suspect is warranted — namely, that classical propositional logic is simply intuitionistic logic wearing a double-negation disguise, so that the entire dispute between classical and intuitionistic logicians collapses into a verbal one, with every classical theorem surviving intact just beneath two negation signs. Yet I gather this cannot be the whole story, both because the result is explicitly restricted to propositional logic and, I'm told, does not carry over in any simple way once quantifiers are added, and because provability of ¬¬A intuitionistically is presumably not supposed to carry the same constructive meaning as a direct proof of A.

Could you first walk me through why ¬¬(A ∨ ¬A) is provable intuitionistically even though A ∨ ¬A is not — I'd like to see the derivation itself, not just be told the result — and then explain why the same trick of simply double-negating the whole formula stops working once we move to predicate logic, such that something more elaborate (I believe associated with Gödel and Gentzen) is needed instead. Finally, I would be grateful for your own view on the philosophical moral to draw: does the existence of such double-negation translations show that the intuitionist's rejection of classical reasoning is, at bottom, merely notational, since an encoded copy of classical logic survives inside the intuitionistic system regardless — or is there a principled reason why a formula's being ¬¬-provable does not put it on an epistemic par with its being provable outright?
