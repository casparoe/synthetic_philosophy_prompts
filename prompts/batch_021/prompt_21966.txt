A friend and I spent a long evening arguing about whether metaphysical grounding has to bottom out somewhere, and we never converged. She defended foundationalism about grounding — the view, pressed by writers like Jonathan Schaffer, that if grounding is meant to explain why the less fundamental obtains, a chain of grounds that descends forever without ever reaching something ungrounded explains nothing, so there has to be a fundamental level for anything to be grounded at all. I was defending the infinitist alternative associated with Ricki Bliss's work on "grounding without foundations": that the demand for a terminus imports, without independent argument, the same assumption Aristotle relied on when he argued that demonstration must ultimately rest on unproved first principles, and that since gunk (matter every part of which has further proper parts) is a live mereological possibility, an infinitely descending structure of grounds deserves the same presumption of coherence rather than being dismissed as automatically vicious. (We set aside the coherentist option, where the chain of grounds loops back on itself, since neither of us was tempted by it.) Write a dialogue of 500 to 700 words in which a Foundationalist and an Infinitist take up this exact disagreement. Have them work through a single running case — suppose our world turned out to be gunky — rather than trading slogans, and make sure each is forced to answer the other's strongest point: the Infinitist should have to say how anything gets explained at all if the explaining is perpetually deferred, and the Foundationalist should have to say why the demand for a bottom level is more than an artifact carried over from a different, epistemic regress problem. Both characters should argue with equal skill, and the dialogue shouldn't resolve neatly in either direction.
