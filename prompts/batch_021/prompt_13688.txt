A friend and I spent a good part of a long walk after a seminar on AI alignment arguing about the "Löbian obstacle" that shows up in discussions of self-modifying or self-trusting agents, and we never quite reconciled our positions. My friend, who has taken a course in formal logic and read some of the "tiling agents" literature associated with MIRI, maintained that the whole obstacle is essentially Gödel's Second Incompleteness Theorem restated in the vocabulary of AI safety: once one accepts that no sufficiently strong, consistent formal system can prove its own consistency, one has already grasped the entire reason an agent cannot fully vouch for a successor that reasons from the same axioms. I pushed back, on the grounds that Löb's theorem seemed to me to be doing something more general than that: it appears to say that a system cannot even adopt a blanket schema of the form "if I can prove P, then P is true" without the system collapsing into triviality, proving every sentence whether true or false, and that this failure has to do with more than just the single question of whether the system can certify its own consistency. Neither of us could pin down exactly where, or whether, the reduction breaks down, and we ended the walk no closer to agreement.

I would like your help adjudicating this properly. Could you compare Löb's theorem to Gödel's Second Incompleteness Theorem specifically as each bears on this self-trust problem? I would like to see, first, exactly how Gödel's theorem falls out of Löb's theorem as the special case in which the sentence under consideration is false, so that it is clear what is and is not being claimed when someone calls one theorem "just" the other; and second, what, if anything, the self-trust obstacle for successor or self-modifying agents demands over and above what is already secured once a system's inability to prove its own consistency has been taken into account. If it helps sharpen the comparison, feel free to bring in why the standard workaround in the tiling-agents framework -- having each successor reason from a strictly weaker proof system than its predecessor, rather than an equally strong or stronger one -- is better understood as a response to the Löbian side of this problem than to the Gödelian one. On balance, and with reasons, whose reading of the disagreement holds up better: mine or my friend's?
