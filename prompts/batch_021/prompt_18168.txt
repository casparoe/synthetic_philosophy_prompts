Okay so this has been bugging me for like a week now. I work at a small startup and we were running an A/B test on a new checkout button. After two weeks the results weren't quite significant (something like p=0.09), and my teammate said "let's just leave it running another week, more data can only get us closer to the truth, right?" That seemed totally reasonable to me too — like, how could MORE information ever make a conclusion worse? But then our data guy got weirdly worked up about it and said that checking your results periodically and stopping as soon as you cross the significance line basically breaks the whole test, and that technically you're supposed to fix your sample size (or atleast your stopping rule) in advance or you're kind of fooling yourself. I didn't really follow the logic though — if the extra week of data is real, honest data, why should it matter WHEN we decided to look at it or how many times we peaked before that? He also mumbled something about how there's a totally different school of statistics that wouldn't even run into this problem to begin with, since it thinks about "evidence" completely differently, which confused me even more.

For context, my roommate is doing a psych PhD, and when I mentioned this to her she said something like "oh yeah, that's basically part of why you can't fully trust a huge chunk of what gets published in psychology" — which is a way bigger deal than our dumb checkout button, so now I'm even more curious about the actual logic here rather than just getting a rule to follow blindly.

So, three things: is our data guy actually right that peeking-and-extending is a real problem even though the data itself is 100% real and honestly collected? Why would the timing of when you decided to stop matter, if the final numbers are the final numbers regardless of how you got there?
