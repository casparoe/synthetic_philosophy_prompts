I'm reporting a longer piece on how arguments about political legitimacy often smuggle in something closer to a description of raw power, and I keep coming back to a short, dense fragment from Pascal's Pensées that I think gets quoted far more often than it actually gets read. Almost everyone who cites it stops after the first couplet — justice without force is powerless, force without justice is tyrannical ("la justice sans la force est impuissante ; la force sans la justice est tyrannique") — and uses it as a slightly world-weary flourish before moving on to something else. But the fragment keeps going, and it's the second half that I can't quite get a handle on.

Here it is, in the rendering I've pieced together from the French (Pascal never polished these notes for publication, so different translators break up his run-on clauses quite differently):

"It is right that what is just should be obeyed; it is necessary that what is strongest should be obeyed. Justice without force is powerless; force without justice is tyrannical. Justice without force is contradicted, because there are always wicked men; force without justice stands accused. So we must join justice and force together, and for that, make what is just strong, or what is strong just. Justice is open to dispute; force is easily recognized and beyond dispute. So force could not be given to justice, because force contradicted justice and said that justice itself was unjust, and that force alone was just. And thus, unable to make what is just strong, we have made what is strong just."

What I'd like help untangling is the status of that last sentence. Is Pascal simply offering a resigned, almost anthropological diagnosis of how any actual political order has had to solve the unenforceability of justice — not so different from the picture Hobbes was drawing up in Leviathan barely a decade earlier? Or is the fragment doing something more specific to his own project, given that it belongs among the pieces he grouped under "the reason of effects," where his usual move is to first puncture the ordinary person's naive respect for law and custom before circling back to a subtler reason for obeying it regardless? I'm also trying to work out how this connects to the more overtly relativist fragment elsewhere in the Pensées, where he needles the reader with the thought that killing a man counts as murder on one bank of a river and heroism on the other: is the justice-and-force fragment making that same debunking point in a different register, or is it actually doing something more conservative — closer to a defense of settled authority than an unmasking of it? Any help separating these strands, including how they might relate to Pascal's broader distinction between the orders of body, mind, and charity, would be a real help in getting this section of the piece right.
