I'm a retired physicist (spent about thirty-five years doing spectroscopy, with a lot of curve-fitting along the way), and now that I've got time on my hands I've finally started reading philosophy properly instead of just absorbing it secondhand from popular science books. One thing that's really hooked me lately is algorithmic information theory, because Occam's razor was basically load-bearing in my old job every time I had to choose between two fits using AIC or BIC, and honestly I never had a rigorous justification for that beyond "well, everyone does it that way."

So over Christmas dinner my grandson Ben, who's doing a machine learning PhD, and his flatmate Aisha, who's doing an MSc in philosophy of science, got into a proper row about whether Solomonoff induction and Kolmogorov complexity really deliver what people claim for them: a rigorous formalization of Occam's razor. It went roughly like this:

(1) Ben opened by saying Occam's razor used to be hopelessly vague -- "prefer the simpler hypothesis" -- with no agreed notion of simplicity, but Kolmogorov complexity fixes that: define a hypothesis's complexity as the length of its shortest program on a universal machine, weight hypotheses by two-to-the-minus-that-length as a prior, and update on evidence via Bayes' rule. Thanks to the invariance theorem, he said, this measure is the same across universal machines up to a constant, so simplicity stops being a matter of taste and becomes an objective, well-defined quantity.
(2) Aisha shot back that "up to a constant" is exactly the problem, not a technicality to wave off: that constant is basically the length of the program needed to make one universal machine simulate another, and nothing bounds how large it can be. For any two specific hypotheses you actually care about, she said, you can rig a "universal" machine with a lookup table that flips their complexity ranking outright -- so the theorem only really bites asymptotically, not for the short, finite cases that make up basically every real scientific dispute.
(3) Ben answered that nobody picks machines adversarially in practice: the standard move is to restrict attention to "reasonable" universal machines -- plain encodings of ordinary programming languages, say -- and among those the constants stay small and the rankings don't flip. The bare logical possibility of a pathological machine doesn't undermine ordinary use, he said, any more than a language with one word for your pet theory undermines talk of simplicity generally.
(4) Aisha replied that "reasonable" is exactly where he's smuggling the problem back in, since there's no complexity-theoretic definition of a reasonable machine, only an appeal to which languages and notations humans happen to find natural -- which is the same contingent, practice-relative judgment the whole formal apparatus was supposed to replace. She added a seperate point too: Kolmogorov complexity is uncomputable, so even granting perfect objectivity in principle, nobody can ever actually compute it; real approximations like minimum description length require choosing a coding scheme by hand, which drags the subjectivity back in through the side door.
(5) Ben's comeback was that uncomputability doesn't sink the philosophical point, because plenty of idealizations in science (ideal gases, perfectly reversible processes) are unreachable in practice yet still explain why real approximations work as well as they do -- Solomonoff induction can play that role as a regulative ideal for something like MDL, even if we can only ever approximate it.

They both more or less talked past each other after that and roped me in to settle it, which I really don't feel qualified to do. Could you go through the exchange move by move and say who's got the better of each point -- they needn't be all-or-nothing -- flag anything you think both of them are missing, and then give me your own bottom-line take on the big question underneath it all: does the Kolmogorov-complexity/Solomonoff-induction story actually rescue Occam's razor as an objective principle, or does it just relocate the same old arbitrariness somewhere less visible?
