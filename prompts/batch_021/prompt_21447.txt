My closest friend from university turns forty next month, and I've been asked to give the toast at his birthday dinner. Some context that matters here: in our twenties he was the one who always outlasted everyone else at a party, but for the last six or seven years he's barely touched a drink, ever since he took up long-distance running and started seeing someone equally serious about it. Roughly a third of the guests will be people from that running-club world who have only ever known him sober, while the rest of us are the old university crowd who remember things rather differently. While hunting for toast material I ended up reading an old Penguin Classics copy of Plato's Laws, and found, to my surprise, that Plato actually sets out a fairly detailed age-graded policy on wine: none at all for boys, moderation through early adulthood, and then, once a man turns forty, real license to drink freely and get properly merry at communal feasts, on the grounds that it counters the crabbedness of old age and, oddly, helps loosen the shame that otherwise keeps older men from singing and dancing alongside the young. As far as I can follow it, this sits inside a stranger and more serious argument: that a properly supervised drinking party, run by someone who stays sober throughout, is a genuine test of a person's self-command, on a par with how facing real danger tests courage, and that this is exactly why it has to be supervised rather than left to run itself.

I'd like the toast built around this specific piece of Plato rather than a generic joke about the "big four-oh" being a license to start drinking again. Please use his actual reasoning -- why forty is the threshold he picks, and why he thinks a bit of controlled excess can serve a serious purpose rather than being mere indulgence -- to say something both true and funny about my friend crossing this particular line, in a way that will land for the running-club contingent who take his sobriety seriously without sounding, to the old crowd, like we're daring him back into his twenties. Keep it to the length of an actual toast, not an essay -- three or four minutes, read aloud -- and if you can find a graceful way to admit that Plato's whole scheme depends on having one sober person keeping watch over everybody else, that would be a nice note to end on, since that happens to be the job I've been handed tonight.
