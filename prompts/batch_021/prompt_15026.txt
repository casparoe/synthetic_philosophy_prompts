I'm a novelist, so normally the hardest writing problem I deal with is getting a scene to land, but my old grad school roommate Aaron just asked me to be his best man and I think this toast might actually be harder than a whole book. Aaron did his PhD in philosophy and wrote his dissertation on Sartre, specifically bad faith and radical freedom, and for the fifteen years since he's told anyone who'd sit still for it that most of what people say about love and commitment is a textbook case of self-deception. Every single Thanksgiving when someone asked him and Jess (his now-fiancee, a large-animal vet who has heard this lecture more times than anyone alive should have to) when they'd finally get married, his stock answer was some version of "we're not the kind of in-itself that has a wedding date," which used to kill at parties and is now, definately, a bit of a problem for him.

Here's my actual issue: all the normal toast material is off limits. I can't say he knew from the moment he saw her, or that they were meant for each other, because those are more or less the exact sentences he's built a career on calling bad faith -- pretending your freely made choice was actually fate, so you never have to own it. But I also don't want to hand him four minutes of Being and Nothingness with a cake at the end. What I'm after is a toast that uses his own philosophy honestly to get somewhere warm: it should play fair with facticity vs. transcendence and "existence precedes essence," it should take the in-itself line and hand it back to him at the end, and if you can manage it, I'd love one bit that touches on that part of his work where wanting to be loved turns out to be wanting someone to freely choose to be captured by you forever -- I seem to remember him ranting once about that being basically unstable by design -- without making it sound like a threat instead of a toast. The room's going to be half his philosophy department, who'll catch every reference, and half family who've politely sat through this stuff for a decade and will check out the second it turns into an actual lecture. So: four minutes tops, funnier than solemn, and it needs to survive having every philosophy line cut out of it and still work as a toast.
