I spent thirty-five years as an experimental physicist before I retired, and now instead of writing grant applications I fill my mornings with philosophy books, which my wife find pretty funny given I couldn't have told you what "epistemology" meant five years ago. My retirement reading group just finished Carlo Rovelli's *Helgoland*, and near the end he brings in Nagarjuna, the old Madhyamaka Buddhist philosopher, and what I've since read is called the catuskoti or tetralemma (a thing is not existent, not non-existent, not both, and not neither) to back up his relational reading of quantum mechanics -- the idea that particles don't have definite properties except relative to whatever system they're interacting with. A couple of the sharper people in my group got pretty worked up about this and decided that Nagarjuna basically derived, through logic alone two thousand years ago, the same "no intrinsic properties, only relations" picture that it took physicists until the 20th century to stumble onto, and that this counts as a genuine vindication of the emptiness doctrine, not just a nice metaphor Rovelli likes.

I don't have the vocabulary to push back on this properly myself, but something about it nags at me. Could you actually walk me through it: does the four-cornered logic really get you to "no intrinsic properties, only relations," or is that reading emptiness as a positive thesis about what ultimately exists in a way that flattens what Nagarjuna is actually doing with the tetralemma? And is it fair to frame Rovelli's move as physics finally catching up with something Buddhist philosophy had already nailed down, or is there a real category mistake being made somewhere in that comparison? I'd like you to really engage with the logical structure of the tetralemma and the two-truths business, not just hand me the standard "comparisons across cultures are always a bit imprecise" shrug.
