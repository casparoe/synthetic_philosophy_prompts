A friend and I spent most of last night arguing about a passage in Plato's Laws that neither of us expected to find there. Book 1 opens with the Spartan and Cretan speakers explaining why their own cities keep wine-drinking under tight restriction. The Athenian Stranger responds, surprisingly, by defending the Athenian practice of communal drinking parties, arguing that getting citizens mildly and safely drunk, under the supervision of a sober "symposiarch," is a good way to test and strengthen a person's self-control - on the model of how you find out whether someone has courage by exposing them, in a controlled way, to fear. My friend thinks this cannot be squared with the Republic, where Socrates treats being overcome by wine as a paradigm failure of exactly the virtue he wants the guardians to have, groups it together with being ruled by money or fear as something guardians must never fall into, and faults Homer for showing gods and heroes losing themselves at feasts and drinking bouts. On her view, the Laws passage is Plato making peace with a social institution he couldn't get rid of, at the cost of a real inconsistency in his moral psychology. I pushed back that deliberately and temporarily loosening someone's guard, in a controlled setting, in order to see whether their self-control holds, is not the same thing as being ruled by appetite, and might even follow naturally from what the Republic already cares about - but I couldn't fully explain why the Republic never so much as considers a test like this.

Could you write an essay that actually resolves this disagreement rather than just surveying it? Lay out the strongest version of both readings - that the Laws marks a real, motivated shift in how Plato thinks the appetites work and how virtue is best produced, versus that the two dialogues are compatible once we are careful about what "being overcome by wine" really means and about who each institution is designed for - and then commit to and defend one specific verdict about whether Plato's overall position is coherent. I would much rather you take a real, even if qualified, stand than conclude that reasonable readers of Plato could simply go either way.
