hey so in my x-phi seminar we've been going over the Knobe effect (the chairman/environment case - board chairman says "I don't care at all about the environment, I just want more profit," approves a program that either harms or helps it as a pure side effect, and people call the harm "intentional" but the help "unintentional" even though his stated attitude is word-for-word identical in both versions). Prof mentioned theres still no agreed explanation for why: Knobe's own line is that its about the moral valence of the side effect, Hitchcock & Knobe explain it via violation of statistical/prescriptive norms instead, and then theres a third one, Sripada's "deep self concordance" model I think, where what matters is whether the side effect lines up with the agent's stable values rather than the outcome's moral coloring per se.

Got me wondering - if you dropped Hume into this debate, whose side would he actually end up on? He's a sentimentalist about moral judgment, but he's also got that compatibilist argument that we can only praise or blame someone for actions that flow from some durable trait of character, not one-off or accidental stuff. Does that second commitment actually generate the same asymmetry Knobe found in the data, or does it predict something else entirely (like, maybe the chairman isn't really blameworthy for the harm either, since one offhand remark doesn't establish a "durable principle" of disregard for the environment)? Can you work through which of the three accounts fits best with a genuinely Humean picture - not just "he'd say sentiment matters more than reason," but actually tracing the mechanics through?
