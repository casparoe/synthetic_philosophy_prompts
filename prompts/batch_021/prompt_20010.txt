For my IB Extended Essay in philosophy, I've been working through Spinoza's political writings, and I keep running into a tension between two of his texts that, according to my supervisor, scholars still disagree about how to resolve. In the Theological-Political Treatise, Spinoza argues that democracy is the most natural form of the state, since it keeps power closest to the condition people were in before they entered into the social contract with one another; some of the secondary reading I've found, especially work by writers like Balibar and Negri who read Spinoza through his idea of the "power of the multitude," treats this as making him an early, almost radical theorist of democracy grounded in the people's own collective power rather than in any authority handed down to a ruler. But in the Political Treatise, which he was still writing when he died, the very last chapter he worked on is the chapter on democracy, and it breaks off partway through an argument for excluding women from citizenship — an argument resting on the claim that women are naturally, not just conventionally, subject to men's rule, since, on Spinoza's own reasoning, no society has ever had men and women governing as equals. That seems to sit awkwardly with the "radical democrat" reading, since it suggests that once Spinoza actually had to specify who counts as part of "the people" who hold power, his answer turned out to be considerably narrower than his abstract argument seemed to promise.

Could you write me a dialogue between two Spinoza scholars, perhaps on a conference panel, who disagree about how to weigh these two texts against each other? One should defend roughly the Balibar/Negri line: that Spinoza's underlying principle — that natural right simply is power, and that a multitude's collective power can never be fully handed over to a ruler — is a genuinely egalitarian and even radical idea, whatever Spinoza himself failed to draw out consistently. The other should push back that this is too generous a reading: that the exclusion of women is not some stray inconsistency Spinoza would obviously have corrected had he lived longer, but a direct application of that very same "right equals power" principle, which licenses inequality just as easily as equality once you assume, as Spinoza did, that power is in fact unequally distributed between men and women — so that the unfinished chapter reveals what his democracy actually amounted to, rather than being a mistake we can edit away. I'd like the two of them to genuinely engage with each other's specific points, including the question of how much interpretive weight an argument that was literally cut off mid-sentence should carry, rather than just talking past one another.
