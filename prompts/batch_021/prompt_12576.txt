So a coworker of mine is way into the AI safety / rationalist blog scene, and last week he dropped that old MIRI paper on "logical induction" into our team channel -- the one where instead of updating beliefs on new evidence the normal Bayesian way, you set up a kind of prediction market where a bunch of trader-algorithms bet on logical and mathematical statements (stuff like "the ten-thousandth digit of pi is even," or unproven conjectures), and the market's prices are supposed to function as your "beliefs" about them over time. I actually followed the mechanics ok-ish: any trader that's efficiently computable is allowed into the market to bet against the current price if it thinks that price is wrong, and the headline theorem is that no such trader can exploit the market for unbounded profit in the long run. That's basically pitched as the logical-uncertainty version of the classic Dutch book argument for why ordinary probabilities should obey the axioms.

This turned into a whole derailing argument with him instead of us doing actual work: I don't get why "no efficient trader can money-pump this market forever" is supposed to mean the market's price IS a justified belief, rather than just a neat property of a betting game. Isnt there a whole classic complaint about Dutch book arguments generally -- that avoiding a guaranteed loss is a pragmatic fact about you, not an epistemic reason to think your credence is actually rational or tracks truth? Feels like it should be even worse for logical induction, since the no-exploitation guarantee is only asymptotic -- I'm pretty sure the paper itself admits there's no computable bound on how long the market can sit there confidently wrong about something before it self-corrects. So "you can't be money-pumped (eventually)" seems totally compatible with being stubbornly, confidently mistaken for as long as anyone could care about.

Could you write me an essay that actually commits to an answer instead of hedging? I want you to weigh whether the logical induction framework's no-exploitation guarantee gives its market prices real standing as rational beliefs/credences about logical and mathematical claims, or whether that's a category error and the guarantee is really just a nice pragmatic/engineering property that doesn't by itself earn the name "belief." Take the "that's only pragmatic, not epistemic" objection seriously, give it's best reply a fair shake too, and then land on a definite verdict -- not a "well, it's complicated" non-answer.
