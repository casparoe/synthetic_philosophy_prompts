I spent almost forty years as an experimental condensed-matter physicist, and now that I'm retired I've fallen into a bit of a rabbit hole with philosophy of math, mostly by sitting in on a logic reading group at the university libary (my wife thinks this is a very strange hobby for a retiree, and she may have a point). We just spent two sessions on Gödel's incompleteness theorems, and something has been nagging at me ever since: everyone, including working mathematicians, seems to treat the consistency of something like Peano arithmetic, or ZFC, as basically settled -- even though nobody has ever proven it in any absolute sense, and by Gödel's own second theorem, no system strong enough to do real math can prove its own consistency anyway.

Someone in the group mentioned, almost as an aside, that a logician (Nelson? I think that was his name) put out an actual proof that Peano arithmetic was inconsistent as recently as 2011, and had to retract it a few weeks later once a fatal gap was found in it -- and that Voevodsky, a Fields medalist no less, spent real time near the end of his career genuinely worried about this same basic question. That combination is what's been rattling around in my head since. Here's what bugs me about it, as an old experimentalist: every theory I ever trusted in my working life was one I knew, at some level, to be "merely" an effective description -- good within a domain, earned through track record, and not something whose ultimate metaphysical status I needed settled before I used it. Nobody demanded proof that Newtonian mechanics couldn't secretly be false somewhere before letting engineers build bridges with it. Mathematicians seem to want something categorically different from their own foundations: not "reliable so far" but actual, guaranteed consistency, full stop, with the mere possibility of a hidden contradiction treated as basically unthinkable.

So here's what I'd like: write me a real argument FOR the position that this is a mistake -- that mathematicians should adopt the physicist's attitude toward their own axioms, treating something like ZFC as an effective theory in which we've earned justified, but not certain, confidence, rather than as a foundation whose consistency has to be beyond question before anyone's allowed to trust a proof. And please don't let the argument dodge the obvious disanalogy: a false physical theory is just wrong in some regime, whereas a real contradiction in a classical system lets you derive anything at all (the ex-falso-quodlibet problem, which I just learned about and am probably overusing now), so the "it only breaks at the edges" comfort I got as a physicist doesn't obviously transfer to logic. I want the argument to actually wrestle with that gap rather than quietly stepping around it.
