A friend of mine and I spent the better part of an evening arguing about Arrow's impossibility theorem, and I don't think either of us walked away persuaded. She had just read a piece claiming that the theorem shows that any talk of a coherent "popular will" is essentially a mathematical fiction: since no rule for aggregating individual preference rankings into a single social ranking can satisfy unrestricted domain, the Pareto principle, independence of irrelevant alternatives, and non-dictatorship all at once, majoritarian democracy is, in some strong sense, built on sand. I objected that this conclusion depends much more than it seems on independence of irrelevant alternatives: that condition, together with the theorem's restriction to purely ordinal preferences that cannot be compared across people, is precisely what manufactures the impossibility, and both are more contestable as fairness requirements than they first appear. Allow a rule to use cardinal, interpersonally comparable information about how strongly people prefer one option to another, rather than bare rankings, and you can construct aggregation procedures that escape Arrow's result cleanly. She wasn't moved: her rejoinder was that this escape is illusory, since it just trades a rigorously proven impossibility for the murkier and more manipulable problem of measuring and comparing utility across people — arguably a worse foundation for democratic legitimacy than the one it replaces, not a better one.

Since we couldn't settle this ourselves, I would like you to write an essay that does. Lay out the strongest version of each side: the case that Arrow's theorem reveals something close to an inescapable truth about the impossibility of faithfully aggregating individual preferences into a collective one, and the case that its force is largely an artifact of treating independence of irrelevant alternatives and an ordinal-only informational base as neutral, minimal conditions on fairness rather than the substantive and avoidable choices they actually are. Take seriously, rather than wave past, what is actually gained and lost by moving to a cardinal-utility framework in order to dodge the impossibility result. The essay should end with a real, defended verdict on how much philosophical weight Arrow's theorem can bear regarding the possibility of democratic decision-making, rather than a summary that simply restates both sides.
