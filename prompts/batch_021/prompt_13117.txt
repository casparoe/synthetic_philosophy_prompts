In my school's computer science elective, we spent a unit on automated and interactive theorem provers, and my teacher mentioned in passing that back in 2013 two logicians (I believe their names were Christoph Benzmüller and Bruno Woltzenlogel Paleo) actually fed Kurt Gödel's ontological proof for the existence of God into one of these systems and had it check every inferential step for errors. Curious, I read more about it afterward: the argument apparently starts from axioms about which properties count as "positive" and a definition of what it means to be "God-like," then uses modal logic (S5, I think) to derive that a God-like being necessarily exists. Several of the articles I found from that time were quite triumphant about it, treating the computer's confirmation that the proof contained no logical errors as evidence that Gödel had essentially mathematically demonstrated God's existence.

When I brought this up with a couple of friends in my philosophy club, one of them argued that since a computer cannot misapply a rule of inference, and since the computer confirmed the argument was fully valid, we basically have to accept the conclusion, the same way we'd accept a computer-verified proof of an ordinary mathematical theorem. I wasn't sure how to push back on this, since the reasoning seemed sound to me at first, even though it also felt a little too easy for something this significant to be settled so cleanly. While reading around, I also came across a reference to something called "modal collapse" as a supposed problem with Gödel's axioms, but I couldn't follow why it would matter if the derivation itself was error-free. Could you help me understand whether this computer-verified proof really does establish that God exists, and, if it doesn't, explain what the modal collapse issue actually is and why logicians take it so seriously?
