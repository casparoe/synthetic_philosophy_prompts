During my forty years as a condensed-matter physicist, I grew accustomed to a particular kind of question: when does a large collection of individually negligible perturbations sum to something qualitatively new, and when does it not? Since retiring I have been working through the ethics literature in something like the same spirit, and I keep returning to the aggregation puzzle that Eliezer Yudkowsky posed some years ago and that echoes older worries in Parfit, Taurek, and others: would you rather one person be tortured horribly for fifty years, or that 3^^^3 people (an almost incomprehensibly large number, in the up-arrow notation Yudkowsky used) each get a single dust speck in their eye? The "shut up and multiply" answer treats moral disvalue as an extensive quantity that simply adds up across persons and occasions, however thinly it is spread, much as energy adds up across the parts of a physical system; the opposing intuition treats severe suffering as something closer to a phase transition, a qualitative threshold that no accumulation of merely quantitative, trivial irritations can cross. Reading around the topic (Stanford Encyclopedia entries, a few papers, assorted blog exchanges), I've found that philosophers have proposed quite a few distinct ways of resolving, dissolving, or simply refusing this choice, but I don't yet trust my own judgment about which of these are actually strongest.

Could you rank the following positions on the torture-vs-dust-specks problem from most to least philosophically defensible, by your own considered lights rather than by how popular or well-known each one is? Please treat each entry in its strongest available form rather than as a caricature.

1. Unrestricted additive aggregation ("shut up and multiply"): choose torture, since summed disvalue across enough people always dominates a single severe harm.
2. Lexical priority for severe harms: no finite quantity of trivial harms can ever outweigh one sufficiently severe harm.
3. Taurek-style rejection of interpersonal aggregation: numbers of people affected shouldn't simply be added up against one another at all.
4. Scanlonian contractualism's complaint model: assess the choice by the single strongest individual complaint, not by any sum.
5. Negative utilitarianism and other asymmetric views that weight suffering-prevention far more heavily than minor-discomfort-prevention.
6. Threshold deontology: a right against severe harm holds unless outweighed by a comparably weighty aggregate, and dust specks never reach that threshold.
7. Prioritarianism: weight harms by how badly off the sufferer already is, rather than aggregating raw utility.
8. Parfit-style appeal to imprecise or "rough" comparability, denying that a determinate ranking of the two options exists.
9. Diagnosing the anti-torture intuition as a scope-insensitivity bias to be corrected, not honored.
10. Denying the transitivity of "morally worse than" across the relevant spectrum of cases, in the manner of Temkin- or Rachels-style continuum arguments.
