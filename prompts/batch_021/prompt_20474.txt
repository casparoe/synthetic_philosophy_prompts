"According to Maimonides in the Guide for the Perplexed, why did God command the ancient Israelites to offer animal sacrifices rather than simply prohibiting the practice outright? What does this account imply about the relative rank of sacrificial worship compared to prayer and intellectual worship of God? And here's the (paraphrased) answer one of my students gave:

"In Book III of the Guide, Maimonides explains that the ancient Israelites had grown up surrounded by the sacrificial worship of the Sabians and the Egyptians, who offered animals to celestial beings and to creatures they regarded as divine. Since it is not in human nature to abandon an entrenched form of worship all at once, God did not command the Israelites to give up sacrifice, but instead redirected it toward Himself alone, restricting it to a single sanctuary and a single priestly line, gradually weaning them away from idolatry much like a doctor weans a patient off a harmful habit rather than overhauling his whole regimen overnight. Because sacrifice is only useful as a stepping stone to the true end of religion, which is correct knowledge of God, Maimonides ranks sacrificial worship below prayer, and ranks prayer itself below the wordless intellectual worship practiced by the few who have achieved perfection. This account was famously attacked by Saadia Gaon, who argued that the sacrifices have real, inherent value and are not merely a concession to a primitive mindset. And since Maimonides treats the whole sacrificial system as a temporary accommodation to an immature stage of Israelite religion, it follows pretty directly that he expected the practice to be permanently discontinued once that immaturity had been outgrown, rather than restored."
