So I've recently fallen down the decision-theory rabbit hole -- turns out this is what retired chemists do instead of taking up gardening -- and I stumbled across Gregory Kavka's old toxin puzzle for the first time last week. Quick recap in case its useful: an eccentric billionaire tells you that if, by midnight tonight, you manage to form the sincere intention to drink a nasty (but harmless) toxin tomorow at noon, she'll have a million dollars wired into your account overnight, no strings attached -- and crucially, the payment depends only on the intention being real (verified by some infallible brain-scanning device), not on whether you actually go through with drinking the stuff the next day. Problem is, once midnight passes and the money's already sitting there, you have zero reason left to drink the toxin, and a rational person can presumably see that coming, which seems to make it impossible to form the intention honestly in the first place.

My philosophy discussion group (mostly other retired scientists plus one very patient ex-lawyer) spent an entire session arguing about how much of this depends on Kavka's exotic stage-dressing -- billionaires, mind-reading scanners, a toxin that's somehow both nasty and totally safe. Being an old experimentalist, my instinct is that if the puzzle is really about something structural -- good reason to intend X now, no reason at all to actually do X later, and you can see this coming ahead of time -- it shouldn't need any of that sci-fi scaffolding to show up somewhere. Could you construct an example set in an ordinary, realistic situation, with no mind readers or eccentric benefactors involved, that reproduces that same structure? And could you flag exactly which everyday feature is doing the job that the "verified intention" and "money already banked" bits do in Kavka's version? I'd like to know whether the puzzle survives the transplant into normal life, or whether it secretly needs the exotic apparatus to work at all.
