Over the past couple of months I've been working through Saul Kripke's short book on Wittgenstein and rule-following in my spare time. I have no formal training in philosophy -- a friend recommended it after we argued about whether a calculator can be said to "really" follow the rule for addition or just simulate doing so -- but I've read it twice now, along with a few articles I could track down, and one piece of the argument still refuses to click for me. I follow the sceptical setup well enough: since my past use of "plus" only ever covered a finite number of sums, nothing about what I've actually said or done rules out my having meant some deviant function (Kripke calls it "quus") that agrees with addition on every case I've computed but diverges on numbers I've never used, and no appeal to mental images, a felt sense of understanding, or dispositions seems able to close that gap -- dispositions are themselves finite, and in any case they only tell you how I would respond, not how I should. What I can't work out is the so-called "sceptical solution," which Kripke presents by analogy with Hume on causation: instead of finding the fact that makes "plus" mean addition, Wittgenstein is supposed to give up on there being such a fact at all, while explaining why our ordinary talk of meaning is still warranted because it plays a role within a community that can check an individual's usage against shared practice.

My trouble is that the very argument used to disqualify individual dispositions seems to apply just as well to the community's collective dispositions: a whole community's practice is also finite, could in principle misfire outside the situations it's actually been tested in, and by itself only tells you how the group would respond, not how it should. So either the community's agreement is meant to supply the same sort of fact-making, truth-conditional role that individual dispositions were disqualified from playing -- in which case the "sceptical solution" looks like a straight solution that has simply changed levels, from the individual to the group -- or it isn't playing that role, in which case I don't see what stops the whole picture from collapsing into "large groups of people happen to agree," which doesn't sound like an account of correctness at all. Could you walk me through how Kripke's Wittgenstein is actually supposed to avoid this dilemma: what exactly "warranted assertability" is doing that a truth condition doesn't, and why the move from individual to community is supposed to be immune to the very argument that sank the individual's case? I'd also be curious whether this counts as a recognized objection to the "community view" reading -- I have a vague memory of having run into something like it in secondary discussion, though I honestly can't place where -- and, if so, what the strongest reply on Kripke's behalf is supposed to look like.
