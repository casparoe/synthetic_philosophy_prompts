I've spent a couple of recent weekends down a metaphysics rabbit hole that started, of all places, in a LessWrong comment thread arguing about whether ordinary objects like chairs and countries are "really there" or just convenient high-level compressions of lower-level facts. That sent me first to the Stanford Encyclopedia and then to a stack of papers on material constitution, and the example that has stuck with me since is Gibbard's Lumpl and Goliath: take a lump of clay, Lumpl, and shape it into a statue, Goliath, so that right now the two share every part, arranged identically, occupying exactly the same region of space. The standard argument that they are nonetheless numerically distinct runs through Leibniz's Law: Lumpl could survive being squashed back into a ball, Goliath could not, so the two differ in their modal and persistence properties, so by the indiscernibility of identicals they cannot be one and the same thing -- even though, at this very moment, there is no physical difference between them whatsoever. I keep flip-flopping on which side of this strikes me as more absurd, which is usually my cue to ask for a dialogue instead of a summary.

So: I'd like a dialogue between two equally sharp philosophers who take opposite sides here. One is a constitution theorist in roughly Baker's or Wiggins's sense, who accepts that Lumpl and Goliath are distinct-but-coincident objects, related by constitution rather than identity, and is willing to live with two things sharing all their matter and parts. The other denies that there are really two objects in that region at all -- have them settle for themselves whether that's best cashed out via something like Gibbard's own contingent-identity move, a "dominant sortal" story on which the lump simply ceases to exist the moment the statue comes into being, or some other strategy, so long as they actually commit to one and defend it rather than just insisting "there's obviously only one thing here." I want real back-and-forth rather than two prepared speeches: somewhere in there, I want the constitution theorist pressed on what I've seen called the grounding problem for coincident objects -- if Lumpl and Goliath share every categorical property and every part throughout the whole time they coincide, what could possibly make it true that only one of them is essentially statue-shaped? -- and I want the one-object side pressed on how they get to deny Kripke's necessity of identity here without that move looking completely ad hoc. Neither of them should be allowed a cheap "this is just a confused, verbal question -- let's taboo our words and move on" exit; I want to see which package of commitments is actually less costly to hold, and I'd like to come away still genuinely unsure who won.
