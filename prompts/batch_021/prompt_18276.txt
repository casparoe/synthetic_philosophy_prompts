My sister Claire is getting married in six weeks, and she has asked me to give the toast — which would be a straightforward honor if she weren't finishing a PhD in metaphysics on persistence through time, and hadn't spent the better part of a decade making her actual views on the subject unmistakably clear to anyone who sits still long enough at Thanksgiving. She became a perdurantist as an undergraduate, won over by David Lewis's problem of temporary intrinsics — the puzzle of how one and the same enduring thing could be, without contradiction, bent while sitting and straight while standing. By the time she reached her dissertation, she had staked out a more specific position within that broad four-dimensionalist camp: stage theory, on which ordinary names like "Claire" strictly denote only the momentary stage that exists right now, so that claims about Claire at other times are made true not by that very thing existing then too, but by some suitably continuous earlier or later stage — a "temporal counterpart" — standing in for her, the way a merely possible person in another world can stand in for me without being me. She has told me, fondly but not entirely joking, that she will hear every line of the toast as data, and that she will wince if it implies that the person at the altar is, in the strict sense she has spent five years arguing for, the very same person who will be there for the fiftieth anniversary. That is actually a harder bullet for her to bite than it would be for an ordinary four-dimensionalist, who can at least say that one whole spacetime worm has both moments as literal parts; on stage theory, strictly speaking, nothing quite so tidy links the woman at the altar to whichever future stage turns out to be her counterpart fifty years on.

I tried drafting a version myself and ran it past our cousin, who is finishing his own PhD in logic, and he told me that every attempt he's seen — mine included — either turns into a break-up speech in disguise ("strictly, I can only promise to love today's stage of you") or quietly smuggles the endurantist picture back in through the side door, which he suspects Claire would notice and hold against both of us at the reception. I don't need the toast to contain a metaphysics lecture; most of the room, including our grandmother and the entire non-academic half of Daniel's family, has never heard the word "perdurantism" and shouldn't have to start now. But I would like it to be honest by Claire's own lights, and if there is a genuinely comforting thought available to someone who holds her view — I have a vague sense that eternalism, which she also accepts, might supply one, along the lines of every stage of the marriage, including the ones that haven't happened yet, being no less real than the wedding day itself — I would rather the toast find it than quietly paper over the difficulty.

Could you write this toast for me? It should run to about five minutes read aloud, work as a genuinely warm tribute rather than a seminar, and include at least one moment that will land as a real joke for the four or five philosophers in attendance — her advisor and old cohort — because they actually know the material, not a generic joke about philosophers being pedantic. Whatever else it does, it should not assert or imply anything about Claire's persistence through time that Claire, by her own philosophical lights, would consider straightforwardly false.
