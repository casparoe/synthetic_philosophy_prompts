I'm putting together a short paper on Fitch's paradox of knowability for a graduate seminar on anti-realism and verificationism, and before I send a draft to colleagues I'd like a second pair of eyes on the central section. As a reminder of the setup: the paradox threatens the semantic anti-realist's Knowability Principle (KP), ∀p(p → ◇Kp) — every truth is in principle knowable, where K abbreviates "it is known that" — once we add the modest premise that not every truth is actually known. The proof instantiates KP with the Moore-style conjunction "p ∧ ¬Kp": if this conjunction is ever true, KP says it is possibly known; but knowing a conjunction requires knowing both conjuncts, and knowing that one doesn't know p entails, by the factivity of knowledge, that one doesn't know p — so the supposed possible knowledge collapses into the flat contradiction "Kp and not-Kp." Since the conjunction can therefore never possibly be known, yet would have to be knowable if it were ever true, the only consistent option is that it is never true: every truth turns out, absurdly, to already be known.

Here is the section I'd like scrutinized: "Two families of response dominate the literature. Neil Tennant blames classical logic itself: the slide from 'it is never known that (p and p is unknown)' to 'every truth is known' relies on inferential moves an intuitionist rejects, so Tennant restricts KP to what he calls 'Cartesian' propositions and leaves the rest of classical semantics untouched. Dorothy Edgington instead keeps classical logic but rewrites KP using a rigidifying 'actually' operator, so that what KP demands is only that some knower, situated at some other possible world, be able to know that p actually obtains; because 'actually' fixes reference to the real world regardless of where the knowing happens, the act of knowing and the world the known fact is true in come apart, and the contradiction no longer goes through. My own view is that Edgington's fix, whatever its formal elegance, gives up more of the anti-realist's original thought than Tennant's does. Verificationism was supposed to tie the truth of p to the knowability of p, full stop, not to the knowability, from some merely possible vantage point, of a rigidified surrogate 'actually-p.' Tennant's restriction is admittedly ad hoc, carving out exceptions to KP with no independent motivation beyond avoiding paradox — but the ad hoc-ness stays confined to the domain of application, which propositions the principle governs, rather than seeping into the meaning of 'knowable' itself."

I'd appreciate comments on three things: whether my reconstruction of the proof and of the two responses is accurate as far as it goes; whether the charge against Edgington in that last part is fair, or whether I'm too quick to dismiss her solution as changing the subject; and whether there's a more promising response to the paradox, from either camp or elsewhere in the literature, that would upset the dichotomy I've drawn between them. I'd rather hear the objections now than from my colleagues.
