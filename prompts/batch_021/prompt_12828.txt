I'm putting together a graduate seminar on classical American pragmatism, and this week's session is devoted to a disagreement that I think gets flattened in most textbook treatments: the falling-out between Peirce and James over what truth actually is. The split was serious enough that Peirce, in his 1905 Monist essay "What Pragmatism Is," relabeled his own position "pragmaticism" — a name he said was ugly enough to be safe from kidnappers — specifically to distance himself from what James and F. C. S. Schiller had done with a term he felt he had originated decades earlier. I'd like to give my students something livelier than a side-by-side comparison chart, so I'm asking you to write a dialogue in which Peirce and James, imagined as meeting not long after the 1907 publication of James's Pragmatism lectures, hash the disagreement out face to face.

Please have the two of them move past the usual capsule contrast (Peirce: truth is the opinion fated to be agreed upon by a community of inquirers at the ideal limit of inquiry; James: truth is whatever works) and into the real pressure points between them. I want Peirce pressing James on whether tying truth to a belief's "cash value" and expedience collapses truth into mere usefulness, leaves no room for a belief that works beautifully yet is false, and gives up on the world's being what it is independently of what any of us happen to think of it. And I want James pressing back just as hard: that Peirce's "final opinion," secured only at an infinitely distant limit by an idealized community of inquirers, is a regulative fiction that gives a working believer no guidance today — little better, James might say, than a piece of theology dressed up as logic — and that Peirce's own scholastic realism about generals smuggles back in exactly the kind of truth-behind-the-facts that pragmatism was supposed to do away with. Both men are first-rate philosophers, so please don't let either one score an easy, one-sided win: have them actually engage each other's objections, and feel free to let the exchange end in the honest impasse where, historically, they more or less left things.
