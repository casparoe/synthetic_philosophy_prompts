For the past couple of years I've been teaching myself decision theory as a hobby, mainly because I rely on expected-value reasoning constantly in my own decisions - things like how much insurance to buy, how to weigh rare but severe risks, and how much weight to give low-probability, high-impact considerations when deciding where to donate money. Recently I came across the Pasadena paradox, a variant of the older St. Petersburg paradox, and it unsettled something I had been taking for granted. As I understand it, the gamble is set up so that the payoff on the n-th outcome is (-1)^(n+1) times 2^n/n, occurring with probability 1/2^n, so that the expected value works out to be the alternating harmonic series 1 - 1/2 + 1/3 - 1/4 + .... That series converges, but only conditionally, meaning that reordering the very same outcomes can make the sum come out to any number at all, or to no number whatsoever. That seems like a different, and in some ways more disturbing, failure than the merely infinite expectation you get from the St. Petersburg game.

Could you lay out the most important implications of the Pasadena paradox for how much weight I should give to expected-value calculations when I'm reasoning through decisions that hinge on small probabilities of extreme outcomes?
