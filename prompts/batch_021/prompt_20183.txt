I run a monthly reading group for graduate students on formal epistemology and decision theory, and last week's session was on Parfit's hitchhiker case and the puzzle of rational irrationality. Afterward, two of my strongest students stayed behind and got into a real argument that I haven't been able to stop thinking about, and I'd like a second opinion before I see them again. For context, in case it's useful: you're dying of thirst in the desert; a driver will take you to town only if she's confident you will pay her $1,000 once you arrive safely, and she's an excellent, though not infallible, judge of whether you're bluffing; once you're actually in town, paying does nothing further for you, since the ride has already happened.

Here is roughly how the exchange went. (1) Yuki said paying is straightforwardly irrational: standing in town, safe, the ride is a sunk cost, and handing over $1,000 for nothing further in return cannot be what a rational agent does at that point, whatever it was rational to want beforehand. (2) Marco replied that this proves too much: if that is correct, no purely self-interested agent could ever truthfully signal an intention to pay, the driver would detect the bluff, and every such agent would be left to die in the desert; since dying is obviously worse than paying $1,000, the disposition to pay must be the rational one to have, and an act that simply executes a rational disposition cannot itself be irrational. (3) Yuki answered that Marco was running together two different questions -- which policy is good to adopt, versus which act is rational given where you actually stand -- and that one can consistently hold that the disposition to pay is worth cultivating while denying that the isolated act of paying, once the ride is already banked, maximizes anything; she compared it to Ulysses's sailors, who keep him bound to the mast, but whose refusal to release him is not made rational by anything he is shouting at them in that moment. (4) Marco said the analogy cuts the other way: the sailors' refusal is rational precisely because it carries out the antecedently rational policy, with no further, freestanding fact about "the act itself" to be assessed apart from that policy -- and likewise, anyone who could still coherently wonder "but should I really pay?" while standing safely in town was never the kind of agent the driver would have picked up to begin with, so treating the payment as a fresh, isolated decision misdescribes the situation from the outset. Neither would concede, and it was late enough that I sent them both home still arguing on the sidewalk.

I would like your help adjudicating this properly. Please work through the exchange move by move, (1) through (4), saying who has the better of it at each step rather than simply declaring an overall winner, and flag explicitly any move that trades on an ambiguity or quietly begs the question. Then tell me whether you think the residual disagreement between Yuki and Marco is really a substantive disagreement about what is rational in this case, or whether they are talking past each other because they favor different, perhaps not fully compatible, standards of rational assessment -- one keyed to acts, the other to dispositions or policies.
