So I've fallen down a bit of a rabbit hole this week while researching a piece on the history of moral panic around sex and technology, after reading about Jeremy Bentham's secret writings on sex, the ones he was apparently too scared to ever publish. Turns out the same guy who gave us the panopticon and "the greatest happiness principle" also wrote a whole essay (I think it's usually referred to as "Offences Against One's Self") defending consensual "paederasty" — which in his era's usage just meant male homosexual acts generally, not what the word implies now — on the grounds that laws against it were based on pure antipathy and disgust rather than any actual harm to anyone. He apparently knew this was way too dangerous to publish (sodomy was still a capital crime in England at the time), and none of it saw daylight until historians started digging through his papers something like a hundred years after he died.

Anyway it's got me wondering what he'd say about stuff that obviously wasn't on his radar back in the 1780s. I'm curious specifically about two things: the whole OnlyFans/camming economy, and seperately, these AI "girlfriend"/companion apps that are suddenly everywhere. Bentham was weirdly rigorous about actually running things through his hedonic calculus — intensity, duration, certainty, propinquity, fecundity, purity, extent — instead of just going with gut reactions, and he had this whole framework where he was contemptuous of two things people mistake for moral principles: "asceticism" (the idea that pain and self-denial are good in themselves) and what he called the "antipathy principle," basically judging something wrong just because it disgusts you. He explicitly used that second one against the sodomy laws of his own day, arguing nobody could point to actual harm, just dressed-up disgust. So genuinely reconstruct how you think he'd reason through the OnlyFans case and the AI-companion case using his own categories, not just "he was a utilitarian so he'd say maximize happiness." Would he land in basically the same place as with the "unnatural acts" stuff, i.e. it's none of the law's business absent demonstrable harm to a non-consenting party? Or is there something about monetized/algorithmic mediation, or about one "partner" not even being sentient, that would actually move the calculus for him? I'd love an answer that takes his framework seriously enough to end up somewhere a little surprising, not just the CliffsNotes version.
