John Taurek's "Should the Numbers Count?" (1977) argues that if a scarce resource can save either one person or a larger group of other people from an equally serious harm, but not both, you are not thereby required to save the larger group: on his view, separate people's losses cannot be added together into one loss worse than any individual's, so a fair procedure such as a coin flip may permissibly decide the matter instead. This claim provoked an extended debate—sometimes called the numbers problem—over whether, and how, numbers should count in such cases. Rank the following positions in that debate from most to least philosophically defensible:

1. Taurek's own view that numbers are morally irrelevant and a neutral chance mechanism should decide between the options.
2. The straightforward aggregative view that harms and benefits to distinct people simply sum, so that the larger group must always be saved.
3. Derek Parfit's response in "Innumerate Ethics," defending the moral relevance of numbers against Taurek's claim that losses cannot be aggregated across persons.
4. T. M. Scanlon's contractualist proposal that, once the individual claims at stake are equally weighty, the number of claims on each side can break the tie between the options.
5. F. M. Kamm's "balancing" argument, on which additional people with equally strong claims tip the scale toward the larger group without any literal summing of separate lives.
6. Michael Otsuka's skeptical case, in "Skepticism about Saving the Greater Number," that neither Scanlon's nor Kamm's strategy actually succeeds in vindicating the relevance of numbers.
7. A "weighted" or "individualist" lottery proposal, on which numbers raise the probability that the larger group is saved without guaranteeing that outcome.

Give your answer as a JSON object with keys "S" (most defensible) through "D" (least defensible), each mapped to an array of the position numbers (1-7) assigned to that tier, so that every position appears in exactly one tier, formatted as follows:

```json
{

  "D": []
}
```
