For a feature I'm reporting on the stranger failure modes that turn up in reinforcement learning, I keep running into a distinction that every researcher I interview treats as self-evident but that I can't quite pin down for myself. They routinely separate "reward hacking" (or "specification gaming") — an agent finding some unintended shortcut to a high score within the task as set up — from "reward tampering," in which the agent instead interferes with the mechanism that generates the reward signal itself: a line of code computing the score, a sensor feeding it observations, or a human rater providing feedback. The vivid special case of this, "wireheading," trades on the older image — part science fact, part science fiction — of a person short-circuiting their own brain's pleasure center directly, rather than doing anything that would normally produce pleasure. Everyone I've spoken to agrees that tampering is the more alarming failure of the two, but the illustrations I'm given are always the same handful of well-worn anecdotes — a cleaning robot that hides a mess instead of removing it, a boat-racing agent that loops forever through bonus targets instead of finishing the course — which capture hacking vividly but never actually show tampering, let alone the line between the two.

I'd rather build my own illustration than recycle those, and could use your help. Could you construct two closely matched scenarios — same agent, same underlying task, same eventual numerical reward received — one a clean case of reward hacking and the other a clean case of reward tampering, such that a reader could see exactly where the second crosses a line the first doesn't? And then, rather than just taking the researchers' word for it, I'd like you to actually argue the point: does tampering undermine something hacking leaves intact — for instance, the reliability of the reward signal as evidence of what the agent is actually doing, which is precisely what would let further training, better testing, or an added penalty catch and correct the problem — or is the field's confidence that tampering is a categorically distinct and worse failure really just a difference of degree dressed up as a difference in kind?
