As a novelist, I spend my working life managing the difference between myself, my narrators, and my characters, and I've always found Wayne Booth's old category of the "implied author" useful for naming whatever sits in between: not the flesh-and-blood writer, not any single narrator or character, but the coherent sensibility a careful reader infers from a text's choices. I want to test that category against the "simulator" framing now common in discussions of large language models, on which a model is not an agent with a persistent self but a mechanism for generating a shifting population of simulated speakers, none more "real" than the others. Write a dialogue between two equally sharp thinkers, both fluent in that framing, who disagree about whether a fine-tuned assistant model still needs an implied author standing over the characters it simulates. One holds that the assistant persona is just another simulacrum with no special status; the other holds that the model's habit of reasserting consistent values across unrelated contexts, and the strange stubbornness of an "opposite" persona once coaxed out, both point to something author-like at work. Have them argue through these specific phenomena rather than restate their positions in the abstract, and end the dialogue with each naming the one piece of model behavior that would change their mind.
