A reading group that grew out of my local rationalist meetup has spent the last few months working through Nāgārjuna's Mūlamadhyamakakārikā in Jay Garfield's translation, and a disagreement broke out at the last session that nobody managed to settle. Owen opened by arguing that the catuṣkoṭi — Nāgārjuna's fourfold denial that a thing exists, that it doesn't, that it both does and doesn't, and that it neither does nor doesn't (his treatment of whether the Tathāgata exists after attaining nirvāṇa is the clearest case) — isn't some deeper logic but a bit of a cheat: once you deny "P" and deny "not-P," ordinary logic already treats "both" and "neither" as two names for the same leftover impossibility, so there aren't really four independent things being ruled out. Mira answered that the collapse only follows if you assume bivalence throughout, which is exactly what's in question: Nāgārjuna's four denials are about svabhāva, intrinsic or independent existence, not about existence in any old sense, and once a claim can fail to have a determinate status at all — the way "the present King of France is bald" is neither true nor false because it presupposes something false — "both" and "neither" stop collapsing into each other and become two distinct, substantive things to rule out. Owen replied that the presupposition-failure story might work for a loaded case like that, but Nāgārjuna runs the same fourfold denial over perfectly mundane things like motion and causation, where nothing about ordinary usage looks like it smuggles in a false presupposition, so Mira needs an independent argument that "exists" fails to apply to ordinary conditioned things, not just an analogy. Mira said that argument is exactly what the rest of the text is doing chapter by chapter, and added that treating certain existence-claims as neither affirmable nor deniable wasn't invented for this passage: earlier Buddhist texts already set aside questions like whether an enlightened person exists after death in the same fourfold way, rather than answering yes, no, both, or neither. Owen's last word was that granting all of that just relocates the problem: if ordinary things lack svabhāva, and that's why bivalence fails for claims about them, it's unclear whether that very claim — that nothing has svabhāva — itself has svabhāva, and Mira hadn't said where the regress is supposed to stop. Neither of them budged, and I don't know the text or the logic well enough to referee it myself.
