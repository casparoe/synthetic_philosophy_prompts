A friend and I spent a long evening arguing about whether large language models have genuine internal representations, prompted by Anthropic's work extracting human-interpretable "features" from Claude's activations using sparse autoencoders. The detail I keep coming back to is the feature-splitting phenomenon they document: a feature that reads as a single concept in a smaller autoencoder — their example is a "San Francisco" feature that splits into two features in a 4-million-feature dictionary and eleven in a 34-million-feature one — keeps fragmenting as the dictionary grows, and no reconstruction metric can tell researchers which grouping is the "right" one. My friend wasn't troubled by this: she compared it to how "dog" and "poodle" are both real categories at different grains, so a finer feature set simply refines a coarser one without threatening the reality of either. I suspect the analogy fails, since biological and folk categories answer to some independent standard that keeps them stable across observers, whereas here the grain is fixed only by a hyperparameter the researcher happens to pick. Please make the strongest case you can for my side — that this sensitivity to an apparently arbitrary design choice gives us good reason to doubt that these features track representations already determinately present in the network, rather than structures we impose on it through the analysis itself — and explain specifically why the dog/poodle analogy fails to rescue the realist position.
