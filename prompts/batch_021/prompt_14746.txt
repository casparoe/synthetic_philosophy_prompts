I teach an introductory epistemology course, and in the unit on skepticism I have, for years, followed our department's standard anthology in pairing Descartes' dreaming argument from the First Meditation with the butterfly dream passage that closes the "Qiwulun" (often translated "Discussion on Making All Things Equal") chapter of the Zhuangzi, presenting the two as versions of essentially the same argument. Zhuang Zhou wakes from dreaming he was a butterfly and cannot tell whether he is a man who dreamed he was a butterfly or a butterfly now dreaming he is a man — which seems to exploit exactly the kind of subjective indistinguishability between dreaming and waking that Descartes later uses to motivate doubt about whether our present experience, whatever it is, can be trusted to reveal how things really are. Since Zhuangzi apparently arrived at the same skeptical impasse a couple of millennia earlier, several of my students this term wanted to know why he never developed anything resembling a cogito, or any comparable strategy for recovering certainty, later in the text, the way Descartes does across the rest of the Meditations.

I fumbled the answer in class, saying something vague about different traditions asking different questions, which did not satisfy anyone, including me. A colleague who works on classical Chinese philosophy raised an eyebrow when I described the comparison to her afterward, which is partly why I would like to think it through more carefully before the topic resurfaces. What is the most illuminating way to explain, to students who come to this material through the Cartesian tradition, why Zhuangzi never follows up the butterfly dream with anything like a search for indubitable foundations? I would also welcome your sense of whether this is best understood as an interesting asymmetry between two philosophers pursuing a shared project with different degrees of success, or whether it points to something mistaken in how I am setting up the comparison in the first place.
