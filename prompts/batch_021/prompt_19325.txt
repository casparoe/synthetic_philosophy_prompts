I'm a PhD student in statistics, but this term I'm auditing a formal epistemology seminar in the philosophy department, and van Fraassen's "Judy Benjamin problem" has more or less taken over the response paper I owe the seminar next week. Since it's obscure enough that I shouldn't assume anyone has it at their fingertips: Judy is parachuted into a region split evenly into Red and Blue Territory, each of which is further split into a Headquarters Company area and a Second Company area, so her prior over the four resulting cells is uniform at 1/4 apiece. A garbled radio message gets through: "If you're in Red Territory, the odds are 3:1 that you're in the Second Company area" -- nothing else comes through, and in particular nothing at all is said about Blue Territory. The natural first thought is that a message entirely about the internal makeup of Red Territory shouldn't move Judy's credence that she's in Blue Territory even slightly off its prior of 1/2. And yet the rule van Fraassen himself favored for cases like this -- closely related to minimizing relative entropy against her prior subject to the new constraint, and which converges, as the stated odds are pushed toward certainty, to simply conditionalizing on the material conditional "if Red then Second Company" -- forces exactly such a shift: working it through, her credence in Blue Territory actually rises to about 0.53 rather than sitting still at 0.5. Grove and Halpern, and later Douven and Romeijn, took this as decisive evidence that the rule is simply wrong and that any acceptable treatment has to leave Judy's credence in Blue fixed; others have preferred to bite the bullet and give up the invariance intuition instead.

I'd like to see this fight actually staged rather than summarized the way I just did. Could you write a dialogue -- picture two formal epistemologists hashing it out over coffee after the seminar, something like six to ten substantive exchanges apiece -- between someone who defends the invariance intuition (Judy's credence in Blue has to stay at exactly 1/2, on pain of the update rule having conjured information the message never contained) and someone who defends the shift as the correct, if uncomfortable, upshot of trying to model genuinely conditional evidence probabilistically at all? I want the two sides evenly matched and genuinely responsive to one another rather than talking past each other: the invariance defender should be pressed to say what principled rule actually delivers invariance without just stipulating it, and the shift-defender should be pressed to explain why we should trust an update that seems to manufacture information about a territory the message never mentions. They don't need to reach agreement by the end, but I'd like it to be clear exactly where the disagreement bottoms out.
