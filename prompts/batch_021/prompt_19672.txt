I'm a physics PhD student and my dissertation touches on the hole argument in GR, which means I keep bumping into this thing everyone calls "Leibniz equivalence" — it goes back to Leibniz's actual correspondence with Samuel Clarke, where he argues that if you shifted the whole material universe five feet to the left, or swapped east for west, literally nothing would be different, because God could have no sufficient reason to prefer one arrangement over the other, so they aren't really two distinct possibilities at all. Fine, I got that part. But rereading the primary texts for my intro chapter sent me sideways into Leibniz's identity of indiscernibles, and from there into Max Black's 1952 paper where he has you imagine a universe with nothing in it but two perfectly identical spheres — same size, same mass, same everything, floating in otherwise empty symmetric space with nothing else around to tell them apart. Black's whole point is that they're obviously two things, not one, even though he claims nothing could ever qualitatively distinguish between them.

Here's what's actually been bugging me, and my advisor just gives me a look when I bring it up because I'm supposed to be finishing chapter 2: Black's setup is structurally almost the exact same move Leibniz makes against Clarke — a symmetric situation where nothing would look or behave differently if you swapped the two halves — except Leibniz uses that kind of symmetry to conclude there's only one real possibility, and Black uses it to conclude there are definately two real objects. So which way does Leibniz's own system actually cut here? Would he say a universe like Black's literally couldn't exist, since his sufficient-reason-plus-maximal-variety picture of creation rules out God ever making two things exactly alike with nothing to individuate them? Would he just bite the bullet and say the "two" spheres are really one thing counted twice? Or does he get an escape hatch through the monads — since every monad is supposed to be its own unique living mirror of the universe, maybe he'd say Black's thought experiment only touches extended bodies, which for Leibniz aren't fully real anyway, so it never actually reaches the real metaphysical claim. I don't want the standard SEP-style rundown of the PII debate in general — I want someone to actually run Leibniz's own machinery, PSR, relational space, monads, phenomena bene fundata, all of it, directly against Black's specific spheres and tell me where it holds up and where it breaks.
