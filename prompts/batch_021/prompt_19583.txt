Last night a friend and I got into a long argument about Arrow's impossibility theorem, specifically the Independence of Irrelevant Alternatives (IIA) condition. Her position was that IIA is really just the ordinary consistency requirement from individual rational choice theory — that removing an option nobody would have chosen anyway shouldn't reverse your preference between two options that remain — merely relocated from a single chooser to a social welfare function. My position was that this undersells what IIA does in Arrow's framework, since it constrains how the social ranking of a pair responds across different preference profiles, rather than how one fixed preference ordering behaves as the menu changes, and that this difference is exactly why the impossibility result is substantive rather than a truism. Neither of us could settle it. Could you compare Arrow's IIA with the independence and consistency conditions used in individual choice theory — for instance, Sen's properties α and β, or the Weak Axiom of Revealed Preference — and say precisely where Arrow's version is doing something stronger or different? I'd also like to understand, given that comparison, why a condition that sounds like plain consistency ends up disqualifying procedures such as the Borda count, which most people would consider perfectly reasonable.
