I am deep into a novel set on the eve of a naval engagement, and one of my point-of-view characters — the fleet's chaplain, a former logic tutor who has drifted into a bleak fatalism — needs to deliver a sermon arguing that tomorrow's battle, whatever its outcome, is already settled beyond anyone's power to alter it. Rather than have him simply gesture at "fate," I wanted to give him a real argument, so I went back to what I understand is the original source of this whole puzzle in Western philosophy: the ninth chapter of Aristotle's *De Interpretatione*, on statements about the future. I can follow the broad shape of the chapter's ambitions, but one move near the end has me stuck, and the notes in my secondhand Oxford paperback (whose translator's name has worn off the spine) are no help at all. The relevant stretch reads:

"A sea-battle must either take place to-morrow or not, but it is not necessary that it should take place to-morrow, neither is it necessary that it should not take place, yet it is necessary that it either should or should not take place to-morrow. ... What is, necessarily is, when it is; and what is not, necessarily is not, when it is not. But not everything that is, necessarily is; and not everything that is not, necessarily is not. For to say that everything that is, is of necessity, when it is, is not the same as saying unconditionally that it is of necessity."

What I cannot reconstruct is how that last move is supposed to defeat the fatalism the chapter set out to block, rather than simply restate it in fancier dress. Aristotle seems to want to keep the disjunction "there will be a battle, or there will not" necessarily true, while denying that either half of it is, right now, determinately true or false on its own — but if neither half yet has a truth value, I do not see what entitles him to call the disjunction necessary rather than merely eventual. And the distinction he leans on, between a thing "necessarily being, when it is" and a thing being "necessarily, full stop," sounds to my ear like a restatement of "the past is fixed and the future is open," which is exactly the conclusion he needs to earn, not something he can assume against his own fatalist. Is there a genuine piece of logical work being done in that last sentence — something about how the two claims place the word "necessary" differently that I am simply not seeing — or is this an actual soft spot in the argument that a sharp rival character could legitimately exploit against my chaplain? I would like to understand this properly before I put words in either of their mouths.
