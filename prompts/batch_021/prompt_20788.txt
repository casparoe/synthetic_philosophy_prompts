For my AP European History class, we just wrapped up the Scientific Revolution unit, which included excerpts from Galileo's 1615 Letter to the Grand Duchess Christina alongside a short overview of the "conflict thesis" that nineteenth-century historians like Draper and White popularized, the idea that science and religion have been locked in more or less continuous warfare since antiquity. A friend in my class and I both came away thinking the letter is essentially the founding document of that war from the science side, since Galileo seems to argue flatly that whenever a demonstrated fact about nature conflicts with the literal words of scripture, the demonstrated fact should win, which sounds like exactly the sort of declaration of independence from religious authority that would make the Church nervous enough to eventually put him on trial.

I want to build my response paper around that reading, but one detail from the excerpts is giving me pause. Galileo doesn't just declare scripture irrelevant to questions like the earth's motion and move on; he spends real effort on passages such as the Joshua story, where the sun is commanded to stand still, carefully working out why that episode actually fits a moving earth better than a fixed one, rather than simply setting it aside as outside scripture's jurisdiction. If his real position were that scripture has no authority over this kind of question, I'm not sure why he'd bother with that close reinterpretation at all. So is it actually fair to characterize the letter as science's declaration of independence from scriptural authority, or is there some qualification Galileo places on when a scientific claim earns the right to override a literal reading of scripture that I'm missing?
