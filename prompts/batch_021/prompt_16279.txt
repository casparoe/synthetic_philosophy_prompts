So this happened at a philosophy of biology reading group I run, and its been bugging me since. We were working through Zahavi and Grafen on the handicap principle, and two of my grad students, Nora and Ben, got into it and never really got it resolved.

(1) Nora's opening move: the whole point of the handicap principle is that the ornament has to actually cost the signaler something substantial — real survival risk — otherwise nothing stops a low-quality male from growing one too and the signal just collapses.

(2) Ben pushed back: that's not actually what Grafen's ESS model requires. All that has to hold is that the *marginal* cost of extra signal is steeper for a low-quality individual than a high-quality one — a genuinely top-quality male can end up paying very little at equilibrium. What has to be expensive is the counterfactual of a low-quality male faking it, not the honest signal itself, so "costly signal" really means "costly to fake."

(3) Nora shot back that if the top male is basically paying nothing, Ben has quietly swapped Zahavi's real biological claim (burning off surplus vigor, an actual handicap) for a bare game-theoretic label — on those terms cheap talk counts as a "handicap" too, so long as some off-path deviation is expensive.

(4) Ben answered that this is too quick: the cost schedule decreasing with quality is still a substantive, testable claim, and the reason tails look burdensome for everyone in practice isn't the strategic cost that maintains honesty (which can in principle be small) but the efficacy cost of just making the signal detectable at all — and Nora is running those two together, which is the exact mistake later critics pinned on Zahavi's own verbal version of the theory.
