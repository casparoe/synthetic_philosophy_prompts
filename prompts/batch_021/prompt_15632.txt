So over Thanksgiving my aunt Carol and her husband Doug got into an argument I haven't stopped chewing on, partly because I'd read Schellenberg's hiddenness stuff for my phil of religion seminar like four days earlier (I'm doing a PhD in philosophy, mostly epistemology, but got roped into this seminar) and the fight mapped onto it almost embarrassingly well. Background: Carol left the church about ten years ago after what she describes as a long, sincere attempt to keep believing — spiritual direction, retreats, the works — and just never got enough evidence to sustain it; Doug's still a pretty committed Presbyterian. This came up over pie and somehow turned into a real argument instead of the usual "let's not talk religion at dinner," and I ended up just listening while everyone else cleared plates.

Carol's opening move was that if God is supposed to be perfectly loving, God would want an actual two-way relationship with anyone capable of one, and you can't have a relationship with someone you don't even believe is there — so a genuinely loving God would make sure anyone who isn't actively fighting the idea winds up believing. Her own case, she said, is exactly this: she wanted it to be true, searched in total good faith for years, and simply didn't get enough to go on, and that bare fact (not any sadness or hardship it caused, just the plain fact of nonbelief despite not resisting) already counts against God existing. Doug came back that love can't just mean maximum evidence — if God settled the matter the way the table in front of you is settled, belief would basically be forced on her, and that kind of overwhelming proof would crowd out the freely-given trust that's supposed to make the relationship worth having, so some distance is actually necessary for the good God is after. Carol said that mixes up belief and trust: you can be rationally sure someone exists while still being completely free to decide whether to love or follow them, so a loving God could've given her enough to believe without touching her freedom to respond however she wanted, which means the distance move doesn't get God off the hook. Doug's comeback surprised me: maybe she isn't as nonresistant as she remembers, he said, since people are amazing at deceiving themselves, and resistance — fear of what belief would demand, wanting to stay in control of her own life — can be running underneath a search that feels completely open and sincere from the inside. Carol got kind of annoyed and said that move is suspiciously convenient: if any case of searching hard and coming up empty can just be relabeled secret resistance after the fact, then "real nonresistant nonbelief doesn't happen" can never be tested against anything, which is less an explanation than a way of guaranteeing you never lose. Doug's last word was that it's not invented on the spot — if you already have independent theological reasons to think humans are estranged from God in ways they can't fully see themselves, then expecting some hidden resistance is a prediction of the view, not a rescue for it.

That's where it got dropped, since my uncle needed help with the dishwasher. i keep flip-flopping on who was actually landing punches here versus just asserting things, and would rather have a real assessment than my own vibes. Could you go through the exchange move by move — Carol's opening argument, Doug's distance-and-freedom reply, Carol's belief-vs-trust rebuttal, Doug's hidden-resistance move, and Carol's unfalsifiability charge plus his reply to that — and say who's got the better of it at each step, rather than just declaring an overall winner? Also curious whether there's a cleaner way of resolving this in the philosophy of religion literature that neither of them landed on.
