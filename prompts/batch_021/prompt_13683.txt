I've spent years reading Less Wrong and the EA Forum, where "shut up and multiply" functions as something close to an article of faith: if you can save five strangers or one, you save the five. John Taurek's 1977 paper "Should the Numbers Count?" is, to my mind, the most serious attempt on record to deny this, and I've never seen it argued out loud by someone who actually holds the view, against an opponent of equal ability, rather than waved off in a footnote.

Write a dialogue between two philosophers who disagree about the following case: you can save either one stranger or five other strangers from the same fate (drowning, say), but not both, and you have no other relevant connection to anyone involved. The first philosopher defends Taurek's conclusion: numbers alone give you no additional reason to save the five, since there is no one, and no impersonal point of view, for whom "losing five" is a worse loss than "losing one" — there are only separate people, each of whom loses exactly what the lone stranger loses. The second holds that the five are owed better than a fifty-fifty chance, and should press the standard replies to Taurek — such as Parfit's rebuttal in "Innumerate Ethics," or the contractualist move of replacing a coin flip with a lottery weighted by numbers — rather than simply insisting that more deaths must be worse.

Steer the exchange toward this specific crux: Taurek's own proposal, absent any other difference between the parties, is to flip a coin between the two options (save-the-one versus save-the-five), on the grounds that no single claim outweighs any other. The second philosopher should object that this is the wrong fair procedure: a lottery with one ticket per person would instead give the five-person group a five-in-six chance of being saved rather than one-in-two. Have the two work out together whether adopting that per-person lottery already concedes that numbers count after all, or is still compatible with Taurek's original argument. Each speaker should respond to the other's actual last move rather than simply restate an opening position, and the dialogue should not tip its hand about which side has the better of the argument.
