I was re-reading the Yudkowsky/Soares "Functional Decision Theory" paper the other night (I lurk on LW and the alignment forum way too much) and got stuck again on the Parfit's Hitchhiker case. You're dying of thirst in the desert, a driver who's basically a perfect lie-detector says she'll give you a lift into town only if she's convinced you'll pay her a decent chunk of money once your safe (I've seen the amount given as anywhere from $100 to $1000, doesn't matter which). She reads you as sincere, drives you in, and now comes the part everyone argues about: once you're actually standing in town with your wallet, do you pay up? Straight causal decision theory says no way — the ride already happened, handing over cash now has zero causal effect on a choice she already made, so you'd just be burning money for nothing — but obviously a predictor good enough to see that coming would never have picked you up in the first place, so the "rational" CDT agent is the one who dies in the sand. This is basically the engine behind what Parfit calls (I think? might be misremembering the exact term) "rational irrationality" somewhere in Reasons and Persons — the idea that it can be rational to make yourself into the kind of agent who, at the crucial later moment, does something that isn't rational "in itself."

What I'd like is a dialogue between two people standing in town right after the drop-off: one a die-hard causal decision theorist refusing to open his wallet, the other defending Parfit's move (they can talk like a resolute-choice theorist, a Gauthier-style constrained maximizer, or an updateless/FDT type from the LW-sphere — whichever voice lets them make the case best, your call). I don't want either side to be a pushover. The CDT guy should get to really press the "you can't let an already-settled fact about the past hold your present wallet hostage" line, plus the move where he points out that some environments just unfairly punish correct in-the-moment reasoning, which isn't itself evidence the reasoning is wrong (like a demon that tortures anyone who doesn't believe 2+2=5). And the other side should get to push back hard with a "why-ain't-cha-rich" style objection — that a theory of rationality which predictably leaves you worse off, by your own lights, from any standpoint before you knew how things would turn out, has some explaining to do — and should make the case that this isn't some exotic corner case but basically the same structure as ordinary promise-keeping and trust. Have them actually respond to each other's specific points instead of talking past one another, and don't feel like you need to wrap it up with some tidy resolution — it's fine if it just ends in genuine disagreement.
