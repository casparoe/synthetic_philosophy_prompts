And here's what he sent back:

Updateless decision theory (UDT), which Eliezer Yudkowsky introduced on LessWrong as a refinement of his earlier timeless decision theory (TDT), rejects the idea that a rational agent should condition its decision on whatever it has already observed. Instead, an updateless agent commits in advance to the policy -- a full mapping from possible observations to actions -- that has the highest expected utility according to its prior, and then just executes whatever that policy says once it actually makes an observation.

(a) In the counterfactual mugging, before the coin is flipped the two candidate policies are "pay if asked" and "refuse if asked." Paying nets an expected value of 0.5(10,000) + 0.5(-100) = $4,950, versus $0 for refusing, so the optimal policy is to pay. A UDT agent therefore pays the $100 even after learning the coin came up tails, because it is simply following through on the policy that was already optimal from the point of view of its prior -- it doesn't re-derive the decision in light of the new information.

(b) CDT and EDT, applied without any prior commitment, will not pay. CDT reasons that the coin flip is already settled and paying now cannot causally influence a coin that landed in the past, so paying is a pure loss. EDT reasons similarly here, since the coin is exogenous and uncorrelated with anything else about the agent, so learning "tails" plus choosing to pay gives no evidence of any compensating benefit -- the heads-branch payout is simply not on the table anymore. Both would therefore have liked to commit to paying beforehand (since that has higher ex ante value) but renege once they're actually in the tails branch. This is exactly the dynamic inconsistency UDT is built to avoid: since Omega is a perfect predictor, it's really your willingness to pay in the tails-branch that causes Omega to have put you in a position to win the $10,000 in the heads-branch back when the coin was flipped.

(c) UDT one-boxes in Newcomb's problem for a similar "evaluate the whole policy against the prior" reason: given a highly accurate predictor, the ex ante expected value of the one-boxing policy (~$1,000,000, weighted by predictor accuracy) exceeds that of the two-boxing policy (~$1,000 plus a small chance of the extra million), so UDT commits to one-boxing and just does it. EDT also one-boxes, but for a related-but-distinct reason: EDT conditions on its own act as evidence, so choosing to one-box is treated as evidence that Omega predicted one-boxing and filled box B, making one-boxing look better in conditional expectation. CDT two-boxes because it treats the contents of box B as causally fixed by the time you choose, so two-boxing dominates -- you get whatever is in B either way, plus the extra $1,000 from box A -- and CDT has no way to let the correlation between prediction and action count in its favor.
