In my seminar on Ubuntu and relational conceptions of personhood, we've spent the last few weeks working through the exchange between Ifeanyi Menkiti (1984) and Kwame Gyekye (1997) on the metaphysics of personhood. Menkiti holds that personhood is not conferred all at once but achieved by degrees, through progressive incorporation into community and the gradual acquisition of moral maturity, so that a child, or an adult who has failed to meet communal ethical demands, counts as less of a person than one who is fully integrated. Gyekye rejects this "radical communitarianism" precisely because a degreed conception of personhood seems incompatible with ascribing individual and community "equal moral standing." His rival "moderate communitarianism" therefore insists on some inherent, non-relationally-conferred basis for personhood that secures individual rights independently of communal achievement. Most of the secondary literature I've read treats Gyekye's rights-based objection as more or less decisive against Menkiti. Please argue the reverse: that Menkiti's fully processual, degreed account of personhood is compatible with robust individual rights, without covertly reintroducing the kind of inherent, pre-social personhood that Gyekye takes to be unavoidable.
