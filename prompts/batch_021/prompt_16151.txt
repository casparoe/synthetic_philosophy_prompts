David Hume's essay "Of the Balance of Trade" argues against mercantilist fears of a persistently negative trade balance by describing what later economists dubbed the price-specie-flow mechanism: an outflow of gold and silver lowers a nation's domestic price level, making its goods and labor cheaper and more competitive abroad, which draws the specie back in, so that trade imbalances are self-correcting and tariffs designed to secure a "favorable" balance amount to needless interference. Suppose Hume were told two things he could not have known: that virtually all major currencies today are pure fiat money, exchanged at floating or managed rates rather than backed by metallic specie, and that a country such as the United States has run large, persistent trade deficits with a partner such as China for decades, with no sign of the automatic correction his mechanism predicts. Reconstruct his argument precisely enough to identify which of its premises are actually doing the work, and then argue whether he would treat the modern case as vindicating the mercantilists he was refuting, or whether he would still resist their conclusions on different grounds -- perhaps by drawing on his broader case against the "jealousy of trade." Be explicit about which parts of his mechanism carry over to a fiat-currency world and which do not.
