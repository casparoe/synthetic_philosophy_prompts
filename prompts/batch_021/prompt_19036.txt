I'm the community representative on my hospital's Just Culture committee, which reviews serious safety events and decides, under our written policy, whether a lapse counts as blameless human error, "at-risk" behavior calling for coaching and system fixes, or recklessness warranting formal discipline. Our current case: a well-regarded night-shift ICU nurse silenced a monitor alarm that had already sounded falsely dozens of times that shift, and missed a real deterioration in her patient as a result; the unit was two nurses short, as it often is on nights. She had a smaller, similar near-miss two years ago on an equally short-staffed, alarm-heavy shift, which her manager handled informally at the time. Half the committee calls this her second strike and wants formal remediation aimed at her personally, on the grounds that a genuinely careful nurse does not let this happen twice regardless of the noise. The other half invokes the well-known studies on bystanders — seminary students who stepped past someone in distress once they were rushed, passersby who only stopped to help a stranger pick up dropped papers right after finding a dime — and argues that almost any nurse on that unit, that night, would have done exactly what she did, that holding her personally responsible is a textbook fundamental attribution error, and that only redesigning staffing levels and alarm thresholds will actually prevent a repeat. Please draft the short recommendation memo I'll bring to next week's meeting: classify the event under our policy, propose a concrete response with a timeline, and take the losing side's strongest point seriously rather than dismiss it.
