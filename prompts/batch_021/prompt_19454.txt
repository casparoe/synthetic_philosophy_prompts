I've been slowly working through some old formal-epistemology material on Bayesian treatments of conditionals — exactly the sort of thing that would have made a great Less Wrong post back in the day — and I keep getting stuck on the same fork in the road. The thesis in question (due to Ernest Adams, and independently defended by Robert Stalnaker) says that the probability of an indicative conditional "if A, B" is just the conditional probability P(B|A); I'll follow the literature and call this the Equation. David Lewis's 1976 triviality proof shows the Equation can't hold in full generality: a couple of steps of conditionalizing an arbitrary credence function on B and on not-B are enough to force P(B|A) = P(B) for that very function — i.e., to make A and B independent according to it — which is absurd if the Equation was ever supposed to track genuine dependence rather than mere coincidence.

As far as I can tell, this leaves two live escape routes, and they trade off against each other in an interesting way. Either indicative conditionals really do express ordinary truth-conditional propositions, in which case you have to deny that the Equation is "resilient" — it might hold relative to one context or one credence function, but has no business surviving arbitrary conditionalization — or conditionals aren't truth-conditional propositions at all (a "no truth value," or NTV, position), in which case the Equation is really about assertability rather than probability of truth, at the cost of giving up the ordinary law of total probability for sentences that contain conditionals.

I'd like you to write me a dialogue between two philosophers of language holding these two positions — call them the propositionalist (thinking along Stalnaker/van Fraassen lines) and the NTV theorist (thinking along Adams/Edgington lines) — set right after their reading group has worked through Lewis's proof together and found themselves agreeing on the mathematics but not on what to conclude from it. Have the propositionalist press the NTV theorist on how a sentence that isn't truth-apt can still be sensibly negated, embedded as the antecedent of a further conditional, or conjoined with other sentences while still supporting ordinary-looking chains of reasoning — essentially a Frege-Geach problem for conditionals — and have the NTV theorist push back with whatever real resources they can muster, while turning the tables to press the propositionalist on how much unexplained pragmatic machinery, Gricean or otherwise, has to be doing the work of making the Equation look true if it was never more than a coincidence local to a single credence function. Both characters should be equally capable and should respond to the specific point just made rather than cycling back to prepared talking points — I'd rather see the disagreement narrow and sharpen over the course of the exchange than watch either side win by default. A page or two is plenty.
