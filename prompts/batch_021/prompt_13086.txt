And here is the student's answer:

"Hume famously denied that there are any necessary connections between distinct existences: for him, the world contains just one event following another, with no further cement holding them together. Contemporary Humeans about laws inherit this picture. On the crudest version, the regularity theory, a law of nature is simply a true, exceptionless generalization: it is a law that all Fs are G just in case, as a matter of fact, every F is a G. The trouble with this view is well known: it cannot distinguish laws from accidents. That every sphere of enriched uranium is less than a mile in diameter looks like a law, tied to critical mass, whereas the parallel claim about spheres of gold is true only by cosmic accident; yet both are exceptionless universal generalizations, so the naive theory cannot tell them apart.

David Lewis's Best System Analysis is designed to fix this. Rather than counting every true regularity as a law, Lewis proposes that we consider, for the entire actual pattern of particular events (what he calls the Humean mosaic), all the possible deductive systems that could be used to describe it, and ask which system best combines simplicity of axioms with strength, or informativeness. The regularities that show up as theorems of this best system are the laws. The gold generalization isn't needed to make the system stronger and doesn't earn its keep in terms of simplicity, so it drops out as merely accidental, while the uranium generalization, entailed by more basic and systematically central physical principles, survives as genuine law. So on Lewis's view, lawhood is still nothing but a pattern of events, just a specially privileged pattern, and laws remain fully reducible to the non-modal facts.

Armstrong (along with Dretske and Tooley) rejects this reduction. On his view, it is a law that Fs are G not in virtue of any pattern among first-order particulars, but because there holds, at the level of universals, a further relation of natural necessitation between F-ness and G-ness, standardly written N(F,G). Because this necessitation is grounded in the essential natures of F-ness and G-ness themselves, two worlds could match perfectly in every categorical, non-modal respect and yet differ in their laws, since the necessitation is doing real metaphysical work over and above the pattern of events rather than merely summarizing it. This lets Armstrong explain the regularity instead of merely redescribing it -- the Fs are G because F necessitates G -- and it lets the theory underwrite counterfactuals about Fs that never actually occur, which a bare pattern arguably cannot do.

The most influential objection to this necessitarian picture is what's called the inference problem. Even granting that some relation N(F,G) holds among universals, all we ever actually observe are its particular instances -- this F and this G, that F and that G, and so on for some finite stretch of history -- we never observe N itself. So the necessitarian ends up in exactly the same position as the regularity theorist: neither has any way to license the further step from the Fs and Gs examined so far to the claim that all Fs whatsoever, including ones never yet encountered, are G. Positing N does not close this inferential gap, since N is itself only ever known through its finitely many instances; the extra layer of universals buys no additional entitlement to generalize beyond our evidence.

On balance I find the Best System Analysis more defensible. It draws a principled line between laws and accidents without committing us to a mysterious relation among universals that does no independent explanatory work, and Ockham's razor favors the view that gets by with less. Armstrong's necessitation may sound like it explains the regularities, but since we only ever cash it out by pointing back to the very regularities it was meant to explain, the explanatory gain looks illusory."
