So this happened at drinks with two friends from my old CS program, Denny and Faye. I'd fallen down a wikipedia hole about philosophers and death (as you do), and got kind of obsessed with the A.J. Ayer story -- the guy was basically THE hardcore atheist logical-positivist type, spent his career arguing that talk of God or an afterlife isn't just false but literally meaningless because there's no way to verify it. Then near the end of his life he choked on something in the hospital, his heart actually stopped for a few minutes, and afterward he wrote this whole piece about seeing a red light that felt like it was "in charge of the universe" and some beings who'd botched running the place, and said the experience had (something like) slightly weakened his confidence that death is just the end, even though he never actually became religious about it. I brought this up as a fun fact, thinking nothing of it, and Denny and Faye ended up going back and forth on it for a solid twenty minutes while I just sat there -- I write backend code for a living, philosophy of religion is not really my department.

Here's roughly how it went (reconstructing from memory so the exact wording's off, sorry):

1. Denny: the whole point of that story is that Ayer's own experience made him doubt the position he'd built his career on. If a guy that hardcore comes back going "huh, that shook me," that's about as strong an admission against your own theory as you're gonna get.
2. Faye: that's not quite what got shaken though. From what she remembered, his actual argument was more specific than "no afterlife" -- something like, your body getting reconstituted later is at least a meaningful claim, just one with zero evidence, while a soul floating around with no body attached isn't even a coherent claim to begin with. So a gut-level wobble about survival doesn't automatically mean disembodied-soul talk suddenly made sense to him.
3. Denny: doesn't that prove his point anyway though -- if an experience can shake your confidence that "this is the end" at all, you must have some working idea of what "not the end" would even mean for you. You can't get rattled out of a position you think is literal gibberish.
4. Faye: you can have a vivid, weird experience while your heart's stopped and go "huh, what was that" without that requiring "my soul survives with no body" to suddenly be a well-formed hypothesis to you. He himself apparently chalked a lot of it up to his brain running on fumes, oxygen-wise. Being puzzled by your own mind misfiring isn't the same as granting that the concept behind the fright actually parses.
5. Denny: sure, but he published a whole piece about it, and only walked a bunch of it back later in a follow-up, presumably after his philosopher friends gave him grief. That timeline reads less like careful reasoning and more like backpedaling once it got embarrassing.
6. Faye: or the first piece got written fast because a magazine wanted something punchy right after a famous atheist nearly died, and the follow-up was just him being more precise once people ran way further with it than he'd meant. Clarifying an overstatement isn't the same as caving to peer pressure.

Neither of them budged, and honestly by round three I'd lost track of who was even winning.
