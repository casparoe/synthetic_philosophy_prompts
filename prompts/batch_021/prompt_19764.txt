Ok this is a weirdly specific story but it happened on a camping trip and I can't stop replaying it — probably because it's basically the higher-order evidence stuff from my own research (phil PhD, it's my subfield), except messier and with mushrooms.

My friend Wren, a longtime forager, filled a basket of what she was sure were chanterelles — the right ridges, the smell, all the marks she always checks. Her partner Oskar, who'd just read about foraging accidents, told her to toss it anyway: the jack-o-lantern lookalike apparently fools people specifically when they're already excited about a big haul, since feeling sure makes you skip checks you'd normally run — and Wren, it turned out, had texted the group chat "SO many chanterelles out here!!" before she'd even finished the ID.

(1) Wren reran the ID in her head and said everything still pointed to chanterelle, so she didn't see what her mood had to do with it.
(2) Oskar said that's exactly what you'd expect her to feel if the bias were real — feeling careful isn't the same as being careful.
(3) Wren said fine, but then no amount of rechecking could ever reassure her of anything, which can't be a sane policy.
(4) Oskar said she doesn't have to treat it as all-or-nothing — just discount her confidence to match the error rate for foragers in her situation, since eating them at full confidence anyway is basically believing something while believing you're not entitled to.
(5) Wren shot back that Oskar's asking her to believe the mushrooms are fine while distrusting her own reasoning that they're fine, which isn't obviously coherent either.

Everyone survived, for what its worth, but I keep flip-flopping on who had the better of this. Can you go through it move by move, say who's got the stronger case at each step, and flag whatever you think both of them are missing about how higher-order evidence and epistemic akrasia are supposed to work?
