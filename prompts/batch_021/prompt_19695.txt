I have been preparing a graduate seminar on the philosophy of technology and existential risk, and this week's session centers on John von Neumann's short 1955 essay "Can We Survive Technology?," published in Fortune roughly two years before his death. Its central argument is that technology was shrinking the world, both geographically and politically, faster than political institutions could adapt to the change, and that there was probably no permanent, once-and-for-all solution to the dangers this produced, only a continuing sequence of ad hoc, opportunistic decisions taken crisis by crisis, for as long as we managed to keep making the right ones. Around the same period, Stanislaw Ulam recalled, in his memorial tribute to von Neumann, that von Neumann liked to speculate about technological progress accelerating toward an "essential singularity" in human affairs, beyond which life as we currently know it could not continue, an anecdote that later found its way into a good deal of subsequent writing on the technological singularity.

I would like your help reconstructing how von Neumann, retaining his characteristic intellectual temperament (the game-theoretic and adversarial cast of mind, the Cold War-era hawkishness on nuclear strategy, the direct personal involvement in both weapons policy and the birth of programmable computing), might have reacted had he lived to see today's debates over advanced AI. Would he have been sympathetic to proposals for a moratorium on frontier AI training runs, to international verification regimes modeled on nuclear arms control, or to some licensing scheme for powerful models, or would the diagnosis of his own essay, that there is no durable fix and only successive crises to be managed, have led him to doubt that any deliberate, coordinated slowdown was achievable, or even the right goal to pursue in the first place? I would especially value your reasoning on whether he would have treated the risks of AI as fundamentally continuous with the nuclear case he knew so intimately, or as a different kind of problem calling for a different posture. Please build your answer from his documented views and habits of thought, rather than from generic present-day "AI safety" talking points simply attached to his name after the fact.
