So this one's actually about my own workplace and not a story I'm writing (though now I kind of want to write about it after, haha). I'm on the governance committee of the small journalism co-op I work for -- about 18 of us, employee-owned, we put out a science/tech newsletter plus do some reporting. We've been deadlocked for months over whether to spend next year's budget and our one new hire on a YouTube/video push, or just double down on the newsletter and paid subs, and it's gotten kind of personal between two factions. A couple of members who are really into rationalist blog stuff proposed we try Robin Hanson's futarchy idea -- the "vote on values, bet on beliefs" thing -- instead of just settling it with a normal vote at the next general meeting: first vote on a metric (probably trailing 12-month revenue, or maybe a blended revenue-plus-retention index), then run two conditional prediction markets among the membership -- "conditional on Path A (video), what will the metric be in 12 months," "conditional on Path B (newsletter), what will it be" -- and go with whichever path the market prices higher.

Half the committee thinks this is a great way to defuse a fight that's really about ego and factional loyalty, force people to actually put a number on their confidence instead of just arguing louder in meetings, and let whoever genuinely understands the video-versus-newsletter economics best move the price. The other half thinks it's basically a fig leaf: that picking "revenue" as the metric already answers the harder, values-laden question of what kind of outlet we want to be; that beliefs and values aren't nearly as seperable as Hanson's slogan implies once you're the one who gets to pick the metric; that 18 people plus maybe a handful of outside traders is nowhere close to a real market; and that since the same people betting are the people who'll be implementing whichever path wins, you get weird incentive problems that Hanson's original pitch, aimed at national policy where traders and bureaucrats are mostly different people, never really had to reckon with. I have to write the committee's recommendation memo for the full membership vote next week -- something concrete (run it as proposed, run a scaled-down pilot version, or scrap it and just vote normally), with a timeframe attached, that actually engages the "this is epistemics theater" objection rather than just waving it away. Can you draft that memo for me?
