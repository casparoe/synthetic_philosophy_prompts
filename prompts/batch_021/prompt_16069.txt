Q3 (20 pts): State I. J. Good's theorem on the expected value of a cost-free experiment for a Bayesian expected-utility maximizer, and sketch why it's true. Then assess this claim: "Since a rational Bayesian agent can never expect, in advance, to be made worse off by gathering more evidence before acting, ideal rationality requires gathering as much evidence as possible before every decision." Does Good's theorem actually establish this? Say what the theorem shows and what it doesn't.

Student's answer:

I.J. Good showed that for an agent who is an expected utility maximiser and who will update on the evidence by strict conditionalization, the expected utility of observing a cost-free experiment and then choosing the best act in light of it is never less than the expected utility of just choosing the best act right now, using her current credences. In other words, the option to look at more evidence before you decide is never worth less than zero to you.

The proof goes via a convexity argument. Your expected utility if you act now is just the best of the expected utilities available to you given your prior. If you get to see the evidence first, you'll pick, for each possible outcome of the experiment, whichever act maximises expected utility given the updated credences, and by the law of total probability your prior credence in each state is just the probability-weighted average of your posterior credences across the possible outcomes of the experiment. Because "take the max" is a convex operation, Jensen's inequality tells us the max of the averages is never more than the average of the maxes, so pre-committing to update and then optimise can only help. This is really a claim about expected-utility, i.e. risk-neutral, maximisers specifically: it's the assumption that the agent maximises a linear function of probabilities that lets the convexity argument go through, and if she instead maximised a risk-averse, concave transform of expected utility, the relevant value function need not come out convex and the guarantee could fail.

Does this support the claim in the prompt? Almost, with one qualification. The theorem assumes the evidence is free to gather; it says nothing about whether it's worth your while to pay for a costly experiment. Once we set that aside, though, the theorem does the rest of the work: since looking things up never lowers your expected utility, a rational agent has no basis, grounded in the value of information, for declining a free look at the evidence, so ideal rationality does require taking in as much of it as she possibly can before acting.
