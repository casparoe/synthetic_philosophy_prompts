I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and I'm putting together a segment on the small revival of interest in Simone Weil's wartime essay on political parties — the piece usually translated as "On the Abolition of All Political Parties," written in London in 1943 while she was attached to the Free French. My guest is a political theorist who has argued in print that the essay deserves to be read as a serious argument rather than a wartime curiosity: Weil defines a party by three features — a machine for manufacturing collective passion, an apparatus for exerting organized pressure on the private judgment of each member, and an organization whose first and eventually only real object is its own unlimited growth — and concludes that parties cannot be reformed, only abolished, if politics is to leave any room for individuals to discern truth and justice for themselves. Given how much current commentary blames hyperpartisanship, rather than parties as such, for democratic dysfunction, I expect she'll be pressed on just how radical she really wants to be, and I don't want the segment to stop at a restatement of the thesis.

Could you draft eight to ten questions for this conversation, pitched at an audience that already knows the essay? I'd like at least one question that puts Schattschneider's famous claim that modern democracy is unthinkable without parties directly to her; one that presses the Madisonian worry from Federalist 10 that removing the causes of faction is a cure worse than the disease Weil is trying to treat; one that asks whether Weil's own three-part definition doesn't equally indict the syndicalist unions and resistance networks she herself belonged to, unless she can specify what exempts them; and one that traces the demand for solitary, undistracted attention to truth back to her more mystical writing, asking whether a model of judgment built for contemplatives can really be generalized to mass democratic politics. For each, please add a brief follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give — for instance, if she retreats from "abolition" to "stripping parties of formal privileges" like ballot access and whip discipline, I'd like the follow-up ready that asks why that concession isn't simply the halfway reform Weil explicitly refused to settle for.
