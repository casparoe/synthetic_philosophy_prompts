I've never studied philosophy formally, but I spend a good deal of my free time reading about it on my own. A couple of people in an online discussion group I take part in got into a long back-and-forth a while back about Max Black's old thought experiment with the two spheres, and I've never been able to decide who came out ahead. For anyone unfamiliar with it: Black imagined a universe containing nothing but two spheres, alike in every way down to their size and material (I don't remember if he ever gave exact figures, but the point is that nothing about either one, described purely in itself, tells them apart) and sitting some fixed distance from one another, and he used the example to argue against what's usually called the principle of the identity of indiscernibles — the idea that anything sharing every property with something else has to simply be that other thing, not a second thing alongside it.

The argument between my two acquaintances went, roughly in this order. (1) He said the thought experiment settles the matter, since the spheres are exact duplicates of one another in every respect and yet there are plainly two of them, so sameness of properties obviously doesn't force sameness of identity. (2) She said that's too quick, because the spheres aren't actually complete duplicates: each one stands in the relation "being some fixed distance from a sphere" to the other, and that's a real difference in their situations that the "they're indiscernible" framing papers over. (3) He replied that this doesn't work, because that relational description is symmetric and perfectly general — equally true of the first sphere and the second — so it never lets you say "this one, and not that one, has such-and-such feature." To use it to single one sphere out from the other, you'd already need to be able to tell them apart, which is exactly what's in question. (4) She answered that there's still something in the relation even so: whatever it is, it's the kind of relation that never holds between a thing and itself — nothing is ever "some fixed distance from" itself. So the mere fact that it holds at all, between whichever two things we're calling the spheres, shows there are two objects and not one, even though it can never tell you which is which. (5) He came back and said that's circular: to say the relation "holds between the two of them but not of either one taken on its own," you already have to be assuming there are two separate things for it to hold between in the first place. If there really were only a single sphere, there would be nothing there for anything to relate to — so her move only works by quietly helping itself to the very plurality it was supposed to establish.

They left it there, and I've been turning it over ever since without reaching a verdict of my own.
