For my IB Philosophy extended essay, I've been working through the literature on the conditional analysis of dispositions, and I've gotten stuck trying to move past the textbook cases. I understand C. B. Martin's finks well enough: the classic example of a dead wire hooked up to a sensor that makes it live the instant a conductor touches it, so that "if touched, it would shock you" comes out true even though the wire has no standing disposition to shock anyone at all. I also understand why Mark Johnston's masks are supposed to be the bigger problem for David Lewis's Reformed Conditional Analysis: in a masking case, like a fragile vase packed in styrofoam or a lethal poison neutralized by an antidote, the object keeps its disposition completely intact (the vase is still just as fragile, the poison just as toxic), and yet the manifestation still doesn't occur, because whatever is doing the masking interferes somewhere downstream in the causal process instead of touching the disposition's categorical basis the way a fink does. My teacher explained that this is exactly why Lewis's patch, which requires the categorical basis to persist through the process leading to the manifestation, still can't rule masks out.

What I haven't managed to do yet is invent one of these cases myself. Every time I try, my classmates point out that I've either smuggled in a fink (something that actually alters the disposition's basis) or picked a "disposition" whose categorical basis is too vague for the mask/fink distinction to even get a grip. Could you construct one original example of a masked disposition — not the vase-in-styrofoam or poison-and-antidote cases I already mentioned, and ideally about something other than a physical object's fragility or toxicity — and then walk through exactly why the categorical basis is left fully intact in your case (which is what makes it a genuine mask rather than a fink), and what minimal change to the scenario would turn it into a fink instead?
