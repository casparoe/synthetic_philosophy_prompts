Over the past year I've moved from academic work in mechanism design into a role at a small firm that builds autonomous negotiation agents for supply-chain contracts: software that issues and responds to offers, deadlines, and occasionally threats (such as "walk away" clauses) without a human approving every message. During a recent internal safety review, an outside consultant we'd brought in from the AI safety world warned us against ever giving these agents the capacity to publish binding, verifiable precommitments -- something like a smart contract that locks in a reservation price -- and invoked what she called the "commitment races problem" as the reason. She moved on before I could get more than the one-line version, and what I've since pieced together from adjacent reading has left me with the classic Schelling-style intuition that being able to commit is a good thing in bargaining: whoever can credibly bind themselves to a hard line usually gets the better split. What I don't understand is why that logic is supposed to flip from an advantage into a hazard once both sides are sophisticated enough to anticipate each other's incentive to commit.

My impression is that the worry isn't the boring "race to build better commitment technology first" sense of the word, but something about the order and timing of commitments themselves -- including, apparently, a notion of committing "early" in something like logical time rather than in calendar time -- and about why obvious-seeming fixes, such as "just commit to something reasonable" or "commit to bargain in good faith," don't actually dissolve the underlying dynamic. Could you explain the commitment races problem in enough depth that I could defend it to a skeptical colleague: what precisely breaks down once each party can reason about the other's incentive to pre-commit, why the problem is expected to get worse rather than better as agents become more capable of transparent or verifiable self-binding, and what the main proposals for defusing the dynamic -- as opposed to merely describing it -- actually amount to?
