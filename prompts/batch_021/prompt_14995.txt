Now retired after some forty years as an experimental condensed-matter physicist, I have finally found time to read the philosophy I always meant to get to, and a small reading group I recently joined has been working through the history of statistical mechanics. This sent me back to Boltzmann's own running dispute over the H-theorem: Loschmidt's objection that a time-reversible microscopic dynamics cannot ground an irreversible macroscopic law, and then Zermelo's recurrence objection two decades later, which Boltzmann met by insisting that entropy increase is only overwhelmingly probable rather than strictly necessary. What struck me is that, pressed to defend this statistical reading, Boltzmann floated something close to an anthropic argument of his own: perhaps the universe at large sits mostly in thermal equilibrium, and the orderly, low-entropy patch we happen to inhabit is simply an enormous, exceedingly rare downward fluctuation away from that equilibrium — the only sort of region in which observers like us could ever find themselves.

It seems to me that this is essentially the ancestor of what present-day cosmologists call the "Boltzmann brain" problem, except that the modern version turns the argument against itself. In a universe dominated by a positive cosmological constant, the de Sitter vacuum settles into a fixed, nonzero Gibbons-Hawking temperature and persists indefinitely, so given enough time it will spontaneously fluctuate into every possible configuration, including isolated brains complete with fabricated memories of an entire cosmic history — and it will do so incomparably more often than it produces whole galaxies and evolved observers through ordinary structure formation. Combined with something like a self-sampling assumption over all observers who will ever exist, this appears to force the conclusion that a "typical" observer, right now, is overwhelmingly more likely to be such a freak fluctuation than a product of the last fourteen billion years of cosmic history — a conclusion almost everyone treats as a reductio of the cosmological model rather than as a live epistemic possibility.

What I would like from you is a serious, textually grounded reconstruction of how Boltzmann himself might have reacted to this descendant of his own proposal, rather than a generic guess about what a nineteenth-century physicist would supposedly make of modern cosmology. He had nothing resembling Bostrom's self-sampling machinery, but he did hold quite definite views on the reality of probability distributions over ensembles, on atoms and unobservable microstates (contra Mach's phenomenalism and Ostwald and Helm's energeticism), and, later in his career, on the evolved and contingent status of our basic cognitive categories. Given those actual commitments, would Boltzmann have accepted the typicality reasoning that gives the modern Boltzmann brain argument its force, treating it as a genuine problem to be answered on its own terms, or is there something specific in his statistical and epistemological outlook that would have let him resist the inference from "fluctuations of this kind are astronomically more numerous" to "I should expect to be one of them"? Please commit to a specific answer and defend it, rather than simply listing considerations on both sides.
