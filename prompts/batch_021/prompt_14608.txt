Since retiring after forty years running a laboratory in immunology, I have been attending a small philosophy discussion circle, where I have somewhat unexpectedly become the designated grader for our occasional written exercises. Before returning marked work, I like to check my own judgment against an independent read, so let me give you the question I set on Molyneux's problem, together with one member's submitted answer, and ask you to grade it in my place.

Question (20 points; five per part). William Molyneux's puzzle asks whether a person, born blind and taught by touch alone to distinguish a cube from a globe, would be able to say which is which by sight alone the moment his sight was restored. (a) State Locke's answer and his reasoning. (b) State Berkeley's answer, and explain how his reasoning compares with Locke's. (c) State Leibniz's reply to Locke, and its rationale. (d) Summarize what the Project Prakash sight-restoration studies found, and discuss how much weight that evidence can bear on the original philosophical question.

Submitted answer. Molyneux's question is really asking whether the ideas of shape we get from sight and from touch are connected by nature or only by experience. Locke says no: he holds that there is no necessary or resembling connection between a visual idea and a tactile one, so the two kinds of experience are entirely heterogeneous, and any link between a given look and a given feel can only be forged by the custom built up through repeated experience. Berkeley reaches the same verdict for essentially the same reason, agreeing that sight and touch are heterogeneous senses whose ideas have nothing in common. Leibniz disagreed with both: he thought the newly-sighted man could work out which shape was which on the very first look, not through any resemblance between the seen and the felt, but through reasoning -- a cube presents the eye with sharp edges where its surface breaks off in a new direction, while a globe's outline varies continuously, and a mind that already grasps geometrical relations could infer from this difference which body must be the cube. Modern sight-restoration studies bear this out empirically: in the Project Prakash program, children born with dense congenital cataracts were given sight-restoring surgery, and when tested on Molyneux's exact task shortly afterward, they were unable to match the shapes they saw to the shapes they had only ever felt, confirming that Locke and Berkeley, not Leibniz, had it right. Interestingly, the same children's matching ability improved within about a week, showing how quickly the missing association gets learned once vision becomes available.
