For a seminar on twentieth-century political thought, I just read Simone Weil's 1943 essay on political parties, sometimes translated as 'On the Abolition of All Political Parties,' written in London near the end of her life while she was working for the Free French. It argues that parties should be abolished outright, not reformed, because a party is essentially a machine for manufacturing collective passion, pressuring each member's private judgment into conformity, and treating its own unlimited growth as its one real purpose, ahead of truth or the common good. That describes the club I've chaired all year uncomfortably well: we run a slate in student government elections and whip our senators to vote the ticket once we've taken a position. I'm a graduating senior giving the closing remarks at our end-of-year banquet, handing the chair to a sophomore in front of the alumnus who founded the club and the first-years who joined this year. I can't in good conscience give the usual 'keep growing, carry the torch' send-off, but I can't stand up and repudiate everything he built in front of people who trusted me either. Write the remarks: they should run about four minutes, stay honest to what Weil actually argues rather than reading like a seminar recap, and still work as a genuine toast that sends the room off warmly.
