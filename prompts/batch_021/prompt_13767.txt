A friend of mine, Priya, and I got into a long argument this week that I still haven't been able to shake. It started after a dinner with our wider friend group, at which one member, Elena, spent a good five minutes reprimanding another friend, Noah, in front of everyone for canceling on a trip at the last minute, calling him unreliable and inconsiderate of other people's time. The complication is that Elena cancels on people about as often as Noah does. Her defense, when I raised this with her afterward, is that her cancellations are always forced on her by genuine emergencies -- a sick parent, a work crisis -- while Noah's tend to be a matter of mood, so in her mind she isn't doing the same thing at all. Priya thinks this is beside the point: in her view, once you make a habit of failing to show up for people, you lose the right to lecture anyone else about it, however good your own reasons sound to you. I kept wanting to say that Elena might simply be right that flaking without a good reason is worse than flaking with one, but Priya kept returning to the point that Elena would be furious if anyone extended her the same leniency she refuses to extend to Noah. Neither of us left that conversation satisfied, and we've since traded two more rounds of it by text.

What I would like from you is not a verdict on who won, but help getting clear on the moving parts of the disagreement. Is there a genuine difference between Elena's complaint about Noah being correct and Elena herself being entitled to voice it to him -- or does a fair complaint simply become unavailable to you once you behave the same way yourself? Does it matter whether Elena genuinely, rather than just conveniently, believes her excuses are a different kind of thing from his, as opposed to knowing on some level that she is holding him to a standard she wouldn't accept for herself? And would anything change if a third friend who never cancels on anyone said the exact same words to Noah, or if Elena had prefaced her criticism by admitting her own record first? I would rather come away understanding what is actually going on, philosophically, when someone is said to have "lost the right" to criticize, than simply settle who had the better of the argument.
