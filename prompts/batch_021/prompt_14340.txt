Using the Wilt Chamberlain example, reconstruct Nozick's argument for the claim that 'liberty upsets patterns.' Here is the student's response:

"Nozick sorts principles of distributive justice along two independent axes. A principle is historical if whether a distribution is just depends on how it came about; an end-result (or current time-slice) principle instead judges a distribution solely by its structure at a moment, so that two distributions with the same profile of holdings are equally just regardless of their history. A principle is patterned if it requires holdings to track some natural dimension -- moral merit, need, effort, IQ -- so that, like end-result principles, justice can be read off the current distribution by checking it against that dimension. Nozick's entitlement theory is historical and unpatterned: a holding is just if it arose through a just acquisition or a voluntary transfer, or through rectification of a past injustice; no formula fixes in advance how much anyone should end up with.

The Wilt Chamberlain example is meant to show that any patterned principle, once achieved, cannot stay achieved if people are free to act on their holdings. Suppose D1, a distribution satisfying some favored pattern such as equality, is just. Wilt Chamberlain's contract entitles him to 25 cents from each admission ticket; over a season, a million fans each choose to pay it, leaving him with $250,000 and producing a new distribution, D2, that no longer fits the pattern. Since D2 arose from the admittedly just D1 through nothing but a chain of individually voluntary transfers, the entitlement theory counts it as just, even though whoever wants to preserve the original pattern must now either forbid such 'capitalist acts between consenting adults' or keep confiscating Chamberlain's earnings. Either way, Nozick concludes, the pattern can only be maintained by continuous interference with people's choices about resources they already justly hold -- liberty upsets patterns. This tells against Rawls's difference principle specifically, since it too fixes a pattern (shares tied to the position of the worst-off) that free exchange will inevitably disturb.

The argument also establishes a positive conclusion: because most actual holdings are patterned by illegitimate acquisitions somewhere in their history, it shows that taxation to rectify those particular injustices remains justified, even where taxation to re-impose a pattern is not."
