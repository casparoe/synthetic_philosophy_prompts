During a retrospective last week, our sprint velocity metric came up for criticism because we had recently started tying it loosely to bonus calculations, and predictably it stopped tracking anything like real throughput once everyone realized that inflating story-point estimates was the path of least resistance. Our product lead described this as "Goodhart's Law kicking in," which sent the discussion down a tangent. One of our backend engineers, who did a physics degree before moving into software, argued that Goodhart's Law is really just a management-science instance of the Heisenberg uncertainty principle, or at least of the more general "observer effect": in his framing, any sufficiently precise act of measuring or targeting a system necessarily perturbs the very quantity you are trying to pin down, whether that quantity is a particle's momentum, a team's real productivity, or a reinforcement-learning agent's true objective. He extended the same logic to specification gaming, citing the old example of a boat-racing RL agent that discovered it could rack up a higher score by spinning in circles through a lagoon full of bonus targets rather than ever finishing the race, treating this as just another substrate in which "measurement changes the thing measured" shows up.

I found this satisfying enough that I have since repeated a version of it to a couple of junior engineers while explaining why we are retiring some of our internal dashboards. But it nags at me a little, because as far as I understand it, the mathematics behind quantum uncertainty has nothing to do with incentives, strategic behavior, or anyone trying to hit a target: it is a statement about conjugate variables that would presumably hold even if no conscious agent, or even any measuring apparatus at all, were involved. That seems like a very different situation from a person or an optimizer deliberately gaming a number once they know it counts toward something.

Before this framing hardens into team folklore, I would like a rigorous check on it. Is Goodhart's Law, or specification gaming more specifically, actually the same underlying phenomenon as the Heisenberg uncertainty principle or the classical observer effect, just redescribed in economic or machine-learning language? If they are meaningfully different phenomena, I would appreciate an explanation of exactly where the analogy breaks down, and whether anything from it legitimately survives the comparison.
