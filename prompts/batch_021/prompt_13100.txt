I'm a PhD student working on modal metaphysics, and for a paper on de re modality I'd like to see one particular technical exchange played out as a full dialogue rather than compressed into a footnote. Write a dialogue between a Lewisian counterpart theorist, who holds that individuals are worldbound and related across worlds only by similarity relations, and an equally sharp defender of genuine transworld identity in the spirit of Kripke and Plantinga.

Build the exchange around two mirrored objections, each pressed by one side and genuinely answered by the other rather than deflected. First, the transworld identity theorist presses Kripke's Humphrey objection: since it is a mere counterpart, not Humphrey himself, who wins the election in the other world, counterpart theory seems to misdescribe what we mean when we say Humphrey could have won. The counterpart theorist must answer on Lewis's own terms -- via context-sensitive counterpart relations -- and defend that answer against the charge that it is just an ad hoc patch. Second, the counterpart theorist presses Lewis's problem of accidental intrinsics against transworld identity: if Humphrey is strictly identical across worlds, and some intrinsic property such as shape is incompatible across the worlds he occupies, then either he bears contradictory properties or intrinsic properties must be relativized to worlds, threatening to turn them into disguised relations. The transworld identity theorist must respond, and the counterpart theorist should then argue that this cost is no cheaper than what counterpart theory pays with its own context-relative similarity relation.

Neither character should simply restate an opening position -- each move should engage the specific point just made -- and the dialogue should end with both stating plainly which cost they are willing to live with and why they judge the other's worse, without either side conceding outright.
