Okay so I've been going down an AI safety rabbit hole for like the past month (blame Robert Miles's youtube channel) and I got stuck on this thing called the shutdown problem. On the surface it sounds simple -- you want an AI that won't fight you if you try to switch it off, but also won't try to get itself switched off on purpose either -- but apparently it's actually brutal to formalize, and there's this whole beef between two camps on how to solve it that I can't fully wrap my head around. One camp is the old-school MIRI "utility indifference" idea from that 2015 Corrigibility paper (Soares, Fallenstein, Armstrong and Yudkowsky, i think?), where you basically bolt together two different utility functions with a correction term so the agent ends up not caring whether the button gets pressed. The other camp is this newer stuff by Elliott Thornley that I found through the alignment forum, where instead the agent is built to have straight-up INCOMPLETE preferences -- it just doesn't rank "get shut down" against "keep going" at all -- and it follows some rule called timestep dominance instead of normal expected utility maximizing, which is supposedly what lets it dodge the money-pump argument that usually gets used against incomplete preferences.

Both of them should clearly know their decision theory and neither should be a pushover for the other -- honestly I kind of want the dialogue to end without a clean winner, because that's sort of where I'm at too.
