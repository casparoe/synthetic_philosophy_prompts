I sit on the small grants committee of a suffering-focused EA foundation that funds wild-animal-welfare research. We've received a proposal -- the kind of thing that gets litigated regularly on the EA Forum -- to fund an immunocontraception field trial in an overabundant population of a fast-breeding small mammal (voles, say) at a reserve we already partner with. The justification offered is that most of the animal's offspring die within weeks of birth from starvation, exposure, or predation, so suppressing births plausibly reduces the population's net suffering even though it also means fewer lives lived. Two committee members who otherwise share our mission are resistant. One thinks the argument, if accepted, gives us no principled stopping point before endorsing suppression across most wild vertebrate life, and takes that as a reason to reject a premise rather than the conclusion. The other is less worried about the philosophy than about how little confidence we can have in the welfare estimates involved, and about what funding this would signal to a field that already treats us as an easy target. Draft the memo I'll circulate before next week's vote: a recommendation -- fund, decline, or fund with specific conditions -- with reasoning that actually engages both objections rather than just restating the expected-suffering calculation.
