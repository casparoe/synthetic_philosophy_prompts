I've been on the volunteer board of our little town science museum for about six years now (I run a hardware store for my actual job, I just ended up on the board because I've been a science nerd since I was a kid), and this Thursday we're voting on whether to approve the text for a brand new permanent exhibit hall our education director wants to call "Built For A Reason." She hired an outside writer to do the panels and honestly a lot of it's fun, but pretty much every placard follows the same formula: "Why do men have nipples? Scientists think it helps fathers bond with infants," "Why do we have chins? Researchers believe it helped with chewing tougher foods," "Why do we blush? It likely evolved to signal submission and keep peace in the group." Every single trait gets its own tidy little story about the specific job it evolved to do, which is exactly what the hall's name is promising visitors before they even walk in.

A few years back I picked up a used copy of one of Stephen Jay Gould's essay collections, and I remember he was really down on this kind of thinking. He had this whole bit about "spandrels" — like the leftover triangular spaces you get between an arch and the rectangular frame around it in old churches, which aren't there because an architect wanted them, they're just what's left over once you put a round arch in a square opening, and only afterward does someone paint a nice mosaic in there so it looks like it was planned all along. His point was that biologists do the same move: they notice a trait, invent a plausible-sounding adaptive story for it, and basically treat the story as confirmed just because it sounds tidy, when really the trait might just be a side effect of something else developing (I'm pretty sure the nipples one was actually his own favorite example, since apparently that tissue shows up before a fetus even develops sex-specific stuff, so it's not "for" anything in males). We don't have any actual written policy at the museum about how much "scientists aren't totally sure" hedging our exhibits are supposed to include, so I feel like this vote is gonna set a precedent way beyond just this one hall.

I'm the one who has to write up the recommendation for Thursday, and our options are basically: approve the text as is, send it back with specific required changes, or pull the "why we evolved this" framing from the whole hall and have her redo it from scratch. I don't want to be the guy who read one book one time at the lake house and ruins a fun exhibit little kids are gonna love, especially since I know there's a real case that a museum can't hedge every placard with "we're honestly not sure," that gets boring fast and is kind of a cop-out too — deciding something is just a leftover byproduct instead of an adaptation is ALSO a specific claim that needs it's own evidence, not some safe default you get to fall back on for free. Could you write the actual short memo I'd bring to the meeting? I want it to land on one real recommendation, with concrete next steps and who's responsible for what, not a wishy-washy "well there are two sides" — but I also want it to seriously grapple with the strongest reason to leave the exhibit mostly alone, not just steamroll that side to make the recommendation sound clean.
