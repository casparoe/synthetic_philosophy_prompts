I'm a novelist finishing a book narrated by a stride pianist making his way through 1920s Harlem, and my publisher has asked for a short critical afterword to accompany it. That afterword has taken shape around W. E. B. Du Bois's essay "Criteria of Negro Art," partly because my narrator has a scene late in the novel in which a magazine editor rejects pages from his memoir for being "the wrong kind of true," and I want the afterword to have earned that scene rather than merely gestured at it. My editor has already warned me that the argument is shaping up "too in the weeds" for a general readership, but I'd rather get it right before worrying about making it presentable.

Here is the argument as it currently stands. Du Bois's famous claim that art "is propaganda and ever must be" tends to get read as a bare demand that Black artists subordinate craft to political usefulness. That isn't quite his position, I think: what he actually seems to be claiming is that beauty and truth converge, so that an honest portrait of Black life is automatically propaganda against a civilization built on the lie that such life is ugly — no distortion required, because the propaganda is just the truth doing its ordinary work once prejudice stops suppressing it. I trace this back to the "double consciousness" of The Souls of Black Folk: the "second sight" behind the Veil, available only to those who see the nation from both inside and outside it, is what supposedly guarantees Black art this privileged, undistorted relation to truth in the first place. The seam, though, shows fairly quickly: the identification only works if "the truth" has quietly been pre-filtered to mean dignifying truth, and Du Bois's own record suggests he never fully trusted that filter to come off. In the same year as "Criteria," he ran a Crisis symposium anxiously asking whether portraying Black criminals and the sordid side of Black life handed ammunition to segregationists; two years later, he reacted with real revulsion to Claude McKay's Home to Harlem, a novel that took his "truthful portrayal" license at face value and spent it on exactly the material his theory seemed, in principle, to sanction.

Before I commit to this as the afterword's spine, I'd welcome comments on it. First, is the double-consciousness derivation a fair reconstruction of what licenses the truth/beauty/propaganda identification in "Criteria of Negro Art," or am I supplying an argument Du Bois never actually makes there? Second, and this is the question I keep circling back to: does the McKay episode expose something philosophically unstable in the identification itself — some way truth and beneficial propaganda can come apart as a matter of principle, rather than just of Du Bois's personal squeamishness — or am I catching one man being inconsistent and dressing it up as a discovery about his theory? And third, is there a more obvious weakness in the argument that I'm skating past because the parallel to my own novel is too convenient?
