ok so I'm drafting a novel with a washed-up concert pianist as the narrator, and I fell down a tolstoy rabbit hole for research -- specifically "What Is Art?", which honestly should come with a warning label. there's a passage in ch. 15 I've reread like four times now and still can't tell if its brilliant or just contradicts itself. after pages of building up "infection" as the one true test for whether something's art at all, he writes:

"If a man is infected by the author's condition of soul, if he feels this emotion and this union with others, then the object which has effected this is art; but if there be no such infection... then it is not art. And not only is infection a sure sign of art, but the degree of infectiousness is also the sole measure of excellence in art. The stronger the infection the better is the art, as art, speaking now apart from its subject-matter, i.e. not considering the quality of the feelings it transmits. And the degree of the infectiousness of art depends on three conditions:—(1) On the greater or lesser individuality of the feeling transmitted; (2) on the greater or lesser clearness with which the feeling is transmitted; (3) on the sincerity of the artist... Thus is art divided from not art, and thus is the quality of art, as art, decided, independently of its subject-matter, i.e. apart from whether the feelings it transmits are good or bad. But how are we to define good and bad art with reference to its subject-matter?"

so by his own rules right there, whether something's art at all, and how good it is as art, comes down entirely to individuality/clarity/sincerity, with subject-matter explicitly set aside. but this is the same guy who elsewhere trashes War and Peace as bad art, even though it's about as sincere, individual, and infectious as prose gets. is "good/bad art" basically a second, separate scale for him -- a moral verdict on the feelings a work spreads, bolted on after the "is it art" question is already settled -- and if so why call the losing side of that second test "bad art" rather than something like "art aimed at a bad end"? trying to figure out if there's a real seam in the argument right where those two scales are supposed to connect, or if i'm missing something already in this passage that resolves it.
