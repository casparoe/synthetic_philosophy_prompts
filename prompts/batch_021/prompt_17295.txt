The question, as I set it, was: "In 'Internal and External Reasons' (1979), Williams distinguishes internal from external reason statements and argues that the external reading cannot do the work its defenders want from it. (a) State the distinction, including the role played by the agent's subjective motivational set S. (b) Reconstruct Williams's central argument for thinking that external reason statements either collapse into internal ones or fail to be genuine reason-giving claims at all. (c) Present one influential objection to that argument — from McDowell, Korsgaard, or Scanlon — and say, as precisely as you can, how Williams would most likely respond. (Parts (a), (b), and (c) are worth 5, 7, and 6 points respectively; the remaining 2 points are for clarity and for accurate use of the wider literature.)"

Here is the student's answer, transcribed exactly as submitted:

"Williams's basic distinction is between two things we might mean by 'A has a reason to φ.' On the internal reading, this is true just in case some sound deliberative route runs from an element of A's actual desires and self-interested aims — what Williams calls A's subjective motivational set, S — to A's φ-ing; the statement is relativized to, and would be falsified by the absence of, some such element in A. On the external reading, the statement is supposed to hold of A regardless of S: even an agent who has, and could reach by any amount of correct reasoning, no motive whatever connected with φ-ing is still said to have a reason to do it.

Williams's argument against the external reading turns on what a reason has to be capable of doing: if A has a reason to φ, this has to be able to explain, or be part of the explanation of, A's φ-ing when A is rational and aware of the reason. An internal reason statement meets this condition automatically, since the sound deliberative route to φ-ing is, precisely, a route through some existing element of S. An external reason statement, by contrast, would have to explain A's φ-ing while granting that nothing in A's actual psychology need be engaged by it — which Williams thinks is impossible without smuggling in some new element of motivation, at which point the statement was really an internal one all along, just for a newly enlarged S. So external reason statements are either false, or disguised ways of saying something other than what they appear to say, such as expressing the speaker's own values.

This is essentially a generalized version of a point Philippa Foot had already made against Kant: her 1972 paper argued that morality's demands are categorical imperatives, holding independently of the agent's desires, and Williams later broadened this same claim about the categorical status of practical 'oughts' from morality specifically to reasons as such, while sharpening the argument for why any putative categorical reason must fail.

McDowell's response denies that motivation must always be traceable to some element of S already present prior to deliberation. On his more Aristotelian picture, a virtuous agent's clear perception of a situation can itself constitute the reason and supply the motivation, without that motivation needing to be present, even implicitly, beforehand. Williams could reply that this only relocates the difficulty: either the perception engages some antecedent sensibility of the agent, in which case it is internal after all, or it is meant to move any agent irrespective of sensibility, which reintroduces exactly the unexplained motivational power that made external reasons puzzling in the first place."
