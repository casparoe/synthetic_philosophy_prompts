Over the past few weeks I have been reporting on the recurring controversies surrounding new and revised entries in the DSM, most recently the debate over whether "prolonged grief disorder" deserves recognition as a genuine psychiatric condition rather than a pathologized version of ordinary mourning. In the background reading for the piece, I kept encountering Ian Hacking's notion of the "looping effect of human kinds," which is often invoked, sometimes rather loosely, to explain why psychiatric categories seem to behave differently from categories in the natural sciences: classify someone as having a disorder, the thought goes, and the classification can change how that person thinks, feels, and behaves, which then feeds back into how clinicians understand and revise the category itself, turning the "kind" into a moving target rather than a fixed thing being discovered.

What I have not been able to work out from the secondary literature is how this idea differs, if it differs at all, from the older sociological notion of labeling theory associated with Howard Becker and Edwin Lemert, which likewise holds that labeling someone deviant, as a "criminal" or an "addict," changes their self-understanding and behavior in ways that can entrench the very deviance being named. Hacking clearly regarded himself as doing more than restating that tradition in new vocabulary, developing his own apparatus of "human kinds," "interactive" versus "indifferent" kinds, and using multiple personality disorder as his central case study. For a sidebar to the piece, I would like a fairly precise comparison of the two ideas: where do looping effects and classic labeling theory genuinely overlap, and in what specific respects does Hacking's account do more, or something different, than the labeling-theory story it grew out of? I would be especially grateful if the discussion addressed why Hacking apparently thought a new concept was needed, rather than a straightforward psychiatric application of Becker's sociology of deviance.
