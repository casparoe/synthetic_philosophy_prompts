I'm writing a novel whose central figure is an aging composer who discovered Schopenhauer in his youth and has spent his career trying to render The World as Will and Representation in orchestral form, and in one pivotal scene he defends his method to a skeptical pupil—so before I draft the dialogue, I want to be sure the philosophy behind his defense is sound rather than merely atmospheric.

I already have the headline claim well in hand: that music is unique among the arts because it bypasses the Platonic Ideas and copies the Will directly. What I need explained more carefully is the finer-grained analogy Schopenhauer draws, in Book Three, between the voices of a composition—bass, the inner harmony parts, and melody—and the successive grades of the Will's objectification, running from unorganized nature through plant and animal life up to self-conscious human striving. Specifically: he treats the bass's slow, rule-bound motion as akin to the rigidity of inorganic causation, and melody's greater freedom of movement as somehow tracking human striving, yet he is elsewhere a strict determinist about the phenomenal exercise of will, human action included—so what work is "freedom" actually doing in that comparison? I'd also like the mechanics of his suspension-and-resolution-as-desire-and-satisfaction analogy unpacked, and where you think this whole four-part correspondence is weakest as philosophy rather than as evocative metaphor.
