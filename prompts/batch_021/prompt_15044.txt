A few months ago I bought a ticket in a large lottery. After the drawing had taken place but before the winning numbers were made public, I told a friend, quite flatly, that my ticket had lost. She asked how I could possibly know that when the numbers had not even been announced. I admitted that I did not strictly know it, but said it was true all the same, since my odds of winning were only about one in several million. That disagreement led me to read about what philosophers call the norms of assertion -- the question of what standing a claim must have before you are entitled to state it outright, rather than merely hedge. The main division seems to be between those who hold that the standing required is knowledge (you may assert only what you know), and those who hold that it is simply truth (you may assert whatever is true, known or not), with a further camp maintaining that sincere belief alone, justified or not, already suffices. I would like you to write a dialogue between a philosopher who defends the knowledge requirement and one who defends the truth requirement, set at the exact moment of my exchange with my friend, in which each tries to win the other over to their own verdict on my case and directly answers the strongest objections the other raises, rather than talking past one another. Neither should get the better of the other too easily.
