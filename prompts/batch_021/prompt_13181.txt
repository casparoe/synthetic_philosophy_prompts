Here's the question he got:

"Explain what philosophers mean by 'moral trade.' Illustrate with an example of two people who disagree morally but could both still benefit from trading. Then raise one serious objection to the idea and say whether it succeeds."

And here's his answer:

"Moral trade is when two people with different moral beliefs make a deal so that each of them ends up doing more of what they personally think is good. It works a bit like ordinary trade in economics: two countries can both be better off giving up something they want less for something they want more, even though neither country's underlying preferences change. The same logic applies to morality - instead of each person spending all their own time and money only on the cause they personally care about, they specialize, since an action can be cheap for me to do but valuable to you, and vice versa.

For example, say Alice thinks abortion is seriously wrong but isn't bothered by factory farming, while Bob thinks factory farming is seriously wrong but has no strong feelings on abortion either way. If Alice agrees to go vegetarian and Bob agrees to donate to a crisis pregnancy center, each of them has done something that costs them little (since they don't personally think it's required) but that the other person cares about a lot. Through trades like this, the two of them basically agree to shift their moral positions closer to one another, meeting halfway so that both sets of values end up a bit more satisfied than before.

The most obvious objection is an incentive problem: since the terms of a trade depend on how strongly each side says it feels about an issue, people might be tempted to exaggerate their convictions to get a better deal, kind of like bluffing in a negotiation. I don't think this really undermines moral trade though, because even if someone bluffs a little, both sides still end up doing more of what's good than they would have otherwise, so the trade is still worth making."
