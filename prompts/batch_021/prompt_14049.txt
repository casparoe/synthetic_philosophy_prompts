I've been reading through Pascal's Pensées on my own time. I have no formal philosophy training — just an engineer's habit of wanting to trace an argument down to its foundations — but I've gotten stuck on one particular fragment, which is usually grouped with the pieces Pascal wrote on what he called the "reason of effects." Here it is:

"Justice, force. It is right that what is just should be followed, and necessary that what is strongest should be followed. Justice without force is powerless; force without justice is tyrannical. Justice without force is contradicted, because there are always wicked men; force without justice is accused. We must therefore combine justice and force, making what is just strong or what is strong just.

Justice is open to dispute; force is easily recognizable and beyond dispute. So it has not been possible to give force to justice, because force has contradicted justice and claimed that it alone was just. And so, unable to make what is just strong, we have made what is strong just."

My first instinct was to read this as a flat "might makes right": a descriptive shrug about how power dresses itself up as legitimacy. But that sits awkwardly with the opening lines, which treat the union of justice and force as something that ought to have happened, and only arrive at force-as-justice as a fallback once that union proves impossible. Pascal elsewhere in the Pensées also argues that ordinary people are right to obey established laws simply because they are established, without asking whether the laws are actually just, since tracing any law back to its real origin would only expose its arbitrariness and invite revolt.

I can't tell how these pieces are meant to fit together. Is Pascal making an actual normative argument for submitting to established authority — something like: since true justice can't be reliably identified in practice, treating force as justice is the least-bad option available — or is the fragment really a piece of theological diagnosis, evidence of what human reason does once you take the Fall seriously, dressed up in the language of political theory? And is there a real difference between those two readings, or do they collapse into the same position once Pascal's larger apologetic aims in the Pensées are taken into account?
