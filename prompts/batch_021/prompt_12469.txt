So this quarter I'm taking a Democratic Theory seminar, and we just spent two whole weeks on what my professor calls "the boundary problem" — basically the puzzle of how you can democratically justify who counts as part of "the people" in the first place, since any vote on that question already presupposes an answer to it. We read this old Whelan piece from the 80s (I think it's literally titled something like "Prior to Democracy") where he points out the chicken-and-egg thing — you need to already know who the demos is before you can hold a vote on who the demos is — plus a Goodin piece defending an "all-affected interests" principle and an Abizadeh piece arguing no state really has the right to unilaterally control its own borders, since border enforcement coerces people who never got a vote on it.

Anyway, last week in discussion section the professor gave us a case study about a made-up country she calls Ruritania that's holding a referendum on whether to slash immigration and deport a bunch of long-term undocumented residents, with only citizens allowed to vote, and it turned into a real argument between two people in my group, Marcus and Ilana, that ran way past when we were suppose to move to the next question. Marcus's opening position was that obviously only citizens vote, full stop — that's just what self-government means, and if outsiders got a say too then citizens wouldn't really be governing themselves anymore. Ilana shot back that this just assumes the very boundary that's in dispute, since the people with the most at stake in the referendum are precisely the migrants who'd get deported, and calling their exclusion "self-government" doesnt make it not question-begging. Marcus said fine, but if "who's most affected" is the standard then practically everyone on the planet is affected by Ruritania's immigration law somehow (relatives abroad, labor markets, refugee flows elsewhere), so Ilana's principle either hands the whole world a vote or it isn't really about being "affected" at all. Ilana adjusted: she said the relevant group isn't "everyone affected" but specifically everyone Ruritania's border agents will actually coerce — stop, detain, or deport — which is a much narrower class than "affected" but still includes the migrants while excluding some random person three countries over. Marcus came back that this just relocates the circularity: to know whose coercion "counts" you already need to know where Ruritania's jurisdiction ends, and that's exactly the boundary being fought over, so the coercion principle presupposes an answer rather than deriving one. Ilana's last move was to basically bite the bullet — fine, it's circular, but maybe that's just the actual shape of the problem, and at least her view admits the circularity instead of quietly assuming, like Marcus's does, that the present-day citizenry is obviously the correct unit with no justification needed at all.

I could not for the life of me figure out who was actually winning this, and the professor just smiled and said "good, sit with that" before ending section, which was not super helpful in the moment. Could you go through Marcus and Ilana's exchange move by move and say who's got the better of it at each step, not just an overall winner, and flag anywhere one of them is equivocating, begging the question, or missing an obvious comeback the other side could've used? I'm also curious what you make of the bigger picture underneath their fight: are "citizens only," "all-affected interests," and "all-subjected-to-coercion" genuinely three different principles here, or do they secretly collapse into each other once you push on them hard enough?
