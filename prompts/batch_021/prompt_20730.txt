Across a long career in experimental thermodynamics and statistical mechanics, I trained myself to distrust any proposed perpetual-motion machine that cycles a system back to its exact starting state while extracting something of value at every step; experience teaches that the cost is merely hidden, not eliminated. When I began reading decision theory in retirement, the money-pump argument against cyclic preferences (A preferred to B, B to C, yet C to A) struck me as a philosophical version of exactly this device: an agent trading around such a cycle can be drained of resources indefinitely while always ending up back where it began. I have since learned, however, that Edward McClennen's notion of "resolute choice" is meant to block the pump without restoring transitivity: the agent forms a plan at the outset and honors it at every subsequent decision node, even where the preference consulted locally at that node favors deviating — and, unlike Ulysses binding himself to the mast, without ever removing the tempting option from the menu. My instinct is that this merely stops the crank from turning rather than showing the mechanism is coherent. I would like you to argue against that instinct: please make the strongest case you can that resolute choice constitutes a genuinely independent and rational form of diachronic coherence, rather than a disguised precommitment device, so that an agent whose preferences are synchronically cyclic can nonetheless count as diachronically rational when choosing resolutely. Please also explain why this defense is even needed, given that a "sophisticated" backward-induction chooser can evade the very same pump without ever having to act, at the moment of choice, against a preference actually held at that moment.
