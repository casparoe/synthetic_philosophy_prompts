Over dinner last week, a friend and I fell into a long argument about supertasks that neither of us managed to resolve, and it has been nagging at me ever since. We had both been reading around the philosophy of infinity for a small reading group we run, and I opened with Thomson's lamp: a lamp switched on, then off, then on again, each toggle taking half as long as the one before it, so that infinitely many switches are completed within a single minute, at the end of which we are asked whether the lamp is finally on or off.

My friend countered with what she considers an equally unsettling case: the Ross–Littlewood "balls and vase" problem, in which ten numbered balls are placed into a vase and one is withdrawn at every stage of a similarly compressed, infinite sequence of steps. If the balls are withdrawn in increasing numerical order, the vase turns out to be provably empty once the whole sequence has run its course, even though the number of balls inside it grows without bound at every finite stage along the way. Her view is that the two puzzles are essentially the same trick told twice, both meant to show that supertasks are conceptually incoherent and can be dismissed on those grounds alone.

I am not convinced. My instinct is that the vase problem, unlike the lamp, actually yields a well-defined and derivable answer once the withdrawal rule is fully specified, so that whatever is troubling about it has more to do with the labeling and ordering of the balls than with any genuine breakdown of logic, whereas the lamp seems to resist a determinate answer however carefully the setup is elaborated. Could you help settle this properly, with an actual comparison of the two thought experiments rather than two separate summaries? I want to know whether they rest on the same underlying confusion about infinite processes, or whether they are diagnosing two genuinely distinct problems -- one about the indeterminacy of a supertask's outcome, the other about its sensitivity to how the objects involved are individuated -- and, if the latter, whether that difference tells us anything about which of the two, if either, poses a real obstacle to the possibility of completing infinitely many tasks in finite time.
