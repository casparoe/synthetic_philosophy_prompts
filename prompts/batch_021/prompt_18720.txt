About a month ago I started a part-time research assistantship for a philosopher who works on formal epistemology and longtermist ethics, and my very first assignment was to get up to speed on evidential cooperation in large worlds (ECL): the idea, due mainly to Caspar Oesterheld's work on multiverse-wide cooperation via correlated decision making, that if the universe or multiverse contains astronomically many causally disconnected civilizations, evidential decision theory gives each of us reason to act more cooperatively toward the aggregate of other agents' values, since our disposition to do so is evidence that relevantly similar agents elsewhere are disposed to do likewise — a kind of gains from trade without any actual causal contact, and supposedly more modest than full-blown acausal trade since it doesn't require anyone to simulate anyone else. Since I came to this from a physics background rather than a philosophy one, the part of the picture that kept nagging at me was the phrase "large worlds" itself, and after a couple of weeks of reading I drafted the notes below toward a short memo for my supervisor. I would very much appreciate an outside pair of eyes on the argument before I hand it in. Here is the core of what I have so far:

My worry is that ECL's persuasiveness depends on quietly sliding between "large" and "infinite," without paying the price that either reading imposes. The whole motivation for thinking my present choice is meaningfully correlated with enough other agents to be worth sacrificing some of my own values seems to require an enormous population of relevantly similar decision-makers; a merely large-but-finite population doesn't obviously generate anything ECL's proponents would consider interesting gains, as opposed to a dressed-up restatement of ordinary impartial altruism. But once we grant that the relevant population is effectively infinite, the expected-value comparisons ECL needs — "cooperating produces more expected value than defecting" — run straight into the familiar problems of infinite aggregative ethics: sums of value over infinitely many agents can fail to converge, or can be reordered to favor either option, so "more expected value" may not even be well defined. And picking out which agents count as "similar enough" to be correlated with me looks like it needs a measure over an infinite space of possible minds, which strikes me as no less arbitrary than the measure problem cosmologists run into when assigning probabilities in eternal inflation. So ECL seems to face a dilemma: the relevant world is either too small for the correlation argument to have real bite, or large enough that the very machinery used to weigh cooperating against defecting no longer delivers a determinate verdict.

What I would like your comments on is, first, whether this is a genuine problem for ECL as its defenders intend it, or whether I am illegitimately running together the reference-class question (who is correlated with me) and the aggregation question (how do I compare infinite sums of value) in a way a more careful formulation would keep apart; second, whether existing responses to infinite-ethics worries already defuse the aggregation half of the dilemma in a way I am not crediting; and third, whether this is already a well-worn objection in the ECL literature that I should simply be citing rather than presenting as though it were my own. I would rather find out I have reinvented a known wheel now than after I have sent this to my supervisor.
