I am plotting a multigenerational novel about a wealthy American family whose fortune originated in a nineteenth-century land seizure from an indigenous community, and I want two of my characters—a libertarian-minded estate lawyer and a historian consulting on a museum exhibit about the seizure—to have a genuinely rigorous disagreement about whether the family's present holdings are justly theirs. The lawyer leans on Robert Nozick's entitlement theory, and specifically on the Wilt Chamberlain argument, to insist that nobody has standing to redistribute the estate according to some independently attractive "pattern" of fairness, since free exchanges proceeding from a just starting point can generate literally any distribution whatsoever, however unequal. The historian counters that entitlement theory itself contains a third principle, rectification of past injustice, which is supposed to govern cases exactly like this one, and that actually applying it seems to require the sort of counterfactual, patterned reasoning about what the distribution would look like had the seizure never happened—precisely the kind of reasoning the Wilt Chamberlain argument treats as illegitimate when Rawlsians invoke it on behalf of the difference principle.

Before I trust myself to write this dialogue so that it holds up for philosophically literate readers, I would like you to compare the logic of the Wilt Chamberlain argument with the logic that the rectification principle actually demands of us, and tell me whether the tension between them amounts to a real inconsistency at the heart of Nozick's entitlement theory, or only an apparent one that dissolves on closer inspection. I also seem to recall, perhaps imprecisely, that Nozick himself half-concedes the difficulty somewhere in Anarchy, State, and Utopia, suggesting that in our ignorance of the exact historical facts we might treat a patterned principle like the difference principle as a rough proxy for what rectification would require in practice. If I am remembering that correctly, I would like to know whether leaning on such a proxy commits the entitlement theory to more than the rest of it can really afford, and, ultimately, how a careful reader of Nozick ought to adjudicate between my lawyer and my historian.
