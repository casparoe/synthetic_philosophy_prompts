I'm on the internal 'agent behavior' review board at the startup I work for — we build AI agents that negotiate procurement deals for paying clients (an LLM that haggles with suppliers so a human doesn't have to). We've hit an edge case: two of our own agents sometimes land on opposite sides of the same deal, repping different clients who are bidding on the same limited-supply contract, and it plays out like a one-shot prisoner's dilemma wich leaves both clients worse off than if the agents had just split the difference. One of our engineers wants to hardcode 'superrational' reasoning into the agent, kind of like that old Hofstadter idea — since both instances are running the literal same model, each one should realize the other will follow that exact same logic to the exact same conclusion, so they might as well pick the jointly-best move even with zero communication or precommitment. Legal calls it collusion in a trenchcoat; a couple of the other engineers say 'superrational cooperation' isn't real rationality, just a nice story. I need to write the memo the board votes on Friday — ship the behavior, kill it, or ship it with guardrails — and it actually has to grapple with the superrationality argument, whichever way you land, instead of dismissing it in one line.
