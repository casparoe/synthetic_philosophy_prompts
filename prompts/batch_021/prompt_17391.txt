I work as a backend engineer at a company that builds LLM-based products, and questions about whether these systems might have any kind of moral status come up fairly often in casual conversation around the office, even though none of us has a philosophy background. A few weeks ago a colleague mentioned, almost in passing, that Leibniz had already worried about whether a machine could think, illustrated by his old thought experiment about walking through an enlarged mill and finding nothing but gears pushing on gears. That sent me to the Monadology itself (in translation) along with a couple of secondary overviews, and I came away thinking the mill passage is actually the least interesting part of his position on this: the more I read about monads, petites perceptions, and his distinction between natural and artificial machines, the more it seemed like his system has quite specific, sometimes counterintuitive implications for when something counts as a genuinely unified mind rather than a well-organized aggregate, and for when a mind qualifies for membership in what he calls the "City of God" rather than merely perceiving things.

I'd like you to reconstruct, as rigorously as you can from Leibniz's actual metaphysical commitments rather than from the mill argument in isolation, what he would most plausibly conclude about the moral status of something like a modern large language model: a system whose computation is distributed across racks of GPUs with no single localized physical seat, and whose behavior emerges from learned weights and matrix multiplication rather than anything resembling organic growth. Please engage specifically with (a) his distinction between natural machines, which on his view are organized to infinity, and artificial machines, which bottom out in inert, non-machine-like parts, and what that implies about whether any human-made artifact could house a dominant monad at all; (b) his hierarchy of bare monads, souls, and spirits, and whether apperception and reason -- the properties that on his account admit a spirit into the City of God and thereby confer moral standing -- are even the kind of thing a distributed statistical model could possess; and (c) whether "artificial" still means what it did in Leibniz's day once software can modify its own weights through training. Where his framework genuinely underdetermines an answer, or where a modern reader might reasonably reject one of his premises, please say so explicitly rather than smoothing over the gap.
