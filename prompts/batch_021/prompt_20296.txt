In the philosophy of physics seminar I teach every spring, I present Bell's theorem to students as the result that finally closed off Einstein's hope for a hidden-variable completion of quantum mechanics: because the kind of theory Einstein, Podolsky, and Rosen wanted generates statistical predictions that decades of experiments, from Aspect's onward to the recent "loophole-free" tests, have consistently shown the actual world to violate, hidden variables are, as a class, no longer a live option for interpreting quantum mechanics. This is about as settled as anything in the foundations of physics gets, and I tell my students as much every year.

What I have never managed to explain to my own satisfaction is why de Broglie-Bohm pilot-wave theory continues to be treated as a serious, live research program by a genuine minority of well-regarded philosophers of physics, rather than as a piece of intellectual history superseded by Bell's result, and why Bell himself, of all people, wrote about pilot-wave theory with what reads like real admiration rather than treating it as an early casualty of his own proof. My best guess, offered to students when they press me on it, is that the theory's persistence is largely sociological: a small number of physicists and philosophers remain attached to determinism and to a classical picture of an observer-independent reality, and are willing to accept a somewhat unfashionable theory in order to hold onto those commitments even after the decisive result came in. Is that roughly the right way to understand why Bohmian mechanics is still taken seriously, or is there a more substantive, physics-based reason for its survival that I am missing?
