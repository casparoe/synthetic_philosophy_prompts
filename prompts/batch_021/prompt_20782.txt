Before he died, my grandfather set up a trust for his grandchildren that none of us can touch until we each turn sixty, drafted so tightly that not even a unanimous vote among every living beneficiary can unlock it early, whether for a medical emergency or anything else. According to my mother, he had watched two of his own brothers squander their inheritances in middle age and decided that no one in the family, including an imagined younger version of himself, could be trusted to judge that question fairly in the moment. Every living beneficiary, now in our forties and fifties, agrees the rule has outlived its purpose, but my mother points out that this very unanimity is exactly the condition he designed the trust to survive, since that is precisely when he thought people talk themselves into bad decisions. I cannot tell whether there is a real principle behind what he did or whether we are simply letting one stubborn man's control issues outlive him. Is there a coherent way to decide when an arrangement like this is genuinely justified, rather than simply being one person's will imposed on people who never agreed to it?
