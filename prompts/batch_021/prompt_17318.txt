I've volunteered with my town's rural fire brigade for about six years now. I never went past high school and don't have any background in this kind of thing - I run the parts counter at the local auto shop during the day - but a hypothetical our training officer walked us through at a recent debrief has stuck with me for weeks, and nobody I've brought it up with since has been able to talk me out of how uneasy it makes me feel.

The situation he described was this: during a fast-moving grass fire, our one available truck gets calls from two directions at almost the same moment. On one side, an elderly man living alone on an outlying property refused to evacuate earlier and now has the fire bearing down on his house with no way to get himself out. On the other side, five neighbours in a small cul-de-sac are trying to load their cars, but the fire has already cut off their only exit road, and they need the truck to hold it back long enough for them to get clear. There's time and water enough to reach one side, not both, and whichever side doesn't get the truck will almost certainly be lost before anyone else can reach them. Everyone else in the room agreed at once that the truck goes to the cul-de-sac, since that's five lives against one, and our officer added that this is the same reasoning behind how we're taught to triage patients or ration supplies whenever there isn't enough to go around: help as many as you possibly can.

I suggested, half-joking, that it might actually be more honest to flip a coin between the two calls instead, so the old man has some chance rather than none, and got laughed at for it. But I haven't been able to drop the idea. It isn't that I think his life outweighs the other five - I don't think that either - it's more that something feels wrong about treating his one life as though it just gets cancelled out the moment there happen to be more people on the other side, as if lives were the kind of thing you could add up and compare like that. I'd like to know whether my crew was simply right and I'm being sentimental about it, or whether there's an actual argument behind my discomfort - and if there is one, what it would mean in practice for how a crew in that position ought to decide.
