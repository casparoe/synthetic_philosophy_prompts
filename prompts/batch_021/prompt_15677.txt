Over dinner last night, a friend and I ended up in a surprisingly long argument about what I'd assumed was a simple probability question, and I still don't think we resolved it. It started because her sister runs a small business making custom cube-shaped planters, and the manufacturer she uses can produce any edge length between 20 and 50 centimeters, but there's no record of which sizes within that range customers actually tend to order. Purely as a puzzle, we stipulated that all we know about the next order is that its edge length falls somewhere in that interval, with nothing more to go on. The question we posed to ourselves was: given only that, what's the probability that the next planter's edge length turns out to be under 35 centimeters, the midpoint of the range?

My instinct was that, with no further information, the edge length itself should be treated as uniformly spread out over the interval, which puts the answer at exactly one half. My friend pushed back, pointing out that there's no obvious reason edge length is the quantity we should treat that way rather than, say, the planter's volume (what actually determines how much soil it holds) or its surface area (roughly what determines how much material it takes to produce). Sure enough, when we assumed a uniform spread over volume instead and translated that back into a statement about edge length, we got a distinctly different probability than one half, and a third, different answer again when we started from surface area. Each starting point seemed just as reasonable as the others, and neither of us could come up with a non-arbitrary reason to prefer one over the rest. We eventually gave up out of frustration, but it's stayed with me since.

So I'd like your take: is there actually a defensible, principled way to decide which quantity should be treated as evenly spread out when reasoning from this kind of bare-bones ignorance, or is the question simply underdetermined as we've posed it? And if it really is underdetermined, what does that imply about the broader habit of assigning equal probabilities to possibilities whenever we lack information to distinguish them — is that habit rescuable in some more careful form, or does an example like this suggest it doesn't really make sense in general? I'd rather have an answer that engages with the actual difficulty than one that just shrugs and says probabilities are subjective.
