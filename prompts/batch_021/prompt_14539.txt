Confucian role ethics keeps coming up as the hardest case in a piece I'm reporting on how different ethical traditions handle conflicts between loyalty to kin and impartial justice. On the reading associated with interpreters like Roger Ames and Henry Rosemont, a person isn't an individual who happens to occupy roles and separately "has" virtues, but is instead constituted by their relational roles — son, minister, friend — cultivated through practices like filial deference. While reporting the piece I sat in on a graduate seminar on classical Chinese philosophy, and two of the students got into an argument during the break that never really got resolved before class resumed.

The trigger was two textbook passages. In the Analects (13.18), the Duke of She boasts that a man in his state was so upright he testified against his own father for stealing a sheep; Confucius answers that in his state, father and son instead "cover for" one another, and that this mutual concealment is itself a form of uprightness. In a companion passage in the Mencius (7A35), a student asks what the sage-king Shun would do if his own father, Gusou, killed a man. Mencius's answer: the minister of justice would simply arrest Gusou, and Shun neither would nor could stop him — but Shun would then treat the throne as no better than a worn-out sandal, secretly carry his father on his back, and flee to live out his days happily as a fugitive by the sea, forgetting the empire entirely. (1) The first student took the two stories together as proof that Confucian role ethics can't really adjudicate between conflicting roles at all — that whenever a family role and a civic or judicial one genuinely collide, family simply wins, so "role ethics" is just kinship particularism in fancier dress. (2) The second objected that this overreads the Mencius passage especially: the law runs its course on Gusou either way, since Gao Yao arrests him regardless of what Shun does. All Shun's flight shows is that he won't go on personally occupying an office he can no longer hold in good conscience once his own father is its target — a claim about which role a person may occupy at once, not a ranking of family over law as such. (3) The first student replied that the "worn-out sandal" line undoes that defense: Mencius doesn't merely permit Shun's flight, he presents it as the happy, admirable outcome, and a tradition truly neutral between roles wouldn't celebrate a ruler discarding his post rather than finding some way to stay and see justice done. (4) The second answered that this proves too much — almost any ethics with more than one genuine duty will sometimes have to praise someone for accepting a forced, tragic trade-off — and added that the two passages actually pull in opposite directions: Confucius never even entertains the son testifying, whereas Mencius has the father arrested regardless. If anything, that runs against the "family always wins" story the first student was telling.

Neither student budged, and it's nagged at me since. I'd also value your take on anything both of them are overlooking about what these two passages can and can't show about Confucian role ethics as a theory.
