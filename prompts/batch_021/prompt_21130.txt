I do multi-agent RL / logistics-optimization stuff for my day job, and a coworker sent me a paper a few weeks ago (full disclosure, I only really got through the intro and skimmed the rest) about something called "safe Pareto improvements," and it's been rattling around in my head ever since. From what I can piece together, the setup is: two sides in some standoff each hand the actual decision-making off to an agent, and neither side knows the other's true strength or resolve. If you just tell your agent "get me the best deal you can," that uncertainty alone can make both agents play aggressive and stumble into a disaster that calmer, fully-informed versions of themselves would've easily avoided. A "safe" Pareto improvement is supposedly a way of changing the rules your agent plays by - agreed on in advance, before anyone's cards are shown - so that no matter how the hidden info actually shakes out later, both sides end up at least as well off as they would've been by default. The "safe" part is that it doesn't depend on trusting the other guy or guessing the right probabilities - it's supposed to hold up in every possible case.

This reminded me a lot of the dark-forest logic in The Three Body Problem, where civilizations destroy each other because there's no way to safely signal or verify peaceful intent - except a safe pareto improvement feels almost like the anti-dark-forest move, the actual trick that gets you out of that trap instead of into it. I'd love a short story written in that same "hard sci-fi that's secretly a game theory paper" spirit, but built around this idea: two rival powers (nations, corporations, or the AI systems representing them, whatever works better for you) heading toward a genuinely catastrophic conflict over some newly contested resource, where the turn in the story isn't the usual brinkmanship-and-who-blinks-first but someone actually proposing or discovering a concrete safe Pareto improvement to how the two side's negotiating agents are instructed. Please don't hand-wave it as "and then they found a clever compromise" - I want the actual mechanism visible on the page, some sense of why it's guaranteed rather than just a lucky guess, and ideally some real tension from the fact that these commitment tricks can apparently backfire just as easily - I read that if two agents each lock in an aggressive demand before either side can verify the other one did too, you end up with the worst outcome instead of the best one.

Doesn't need a tidy or happy ending, and you don't need to over-explain the theory in the narration itself - I'd rather it trust the reader (and honestly, part of why I want this is to see if I even understand the idea correctly by watching it dramatized). Something under 3000 words is fine. If it helps, maybe tell it from the POV of someone fairly low-level on one side, like the engineer who actually maintains the negotiating system, rather than the general or CEO making the final call - that's the kind of vantage point I'd actually recognize from my own job.
