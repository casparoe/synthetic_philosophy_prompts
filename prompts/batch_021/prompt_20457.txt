I am taking a seminar this term on Frege's philosophy of logic and language, and a few weeks ago our professor mentioned, almost as an aside, that Frege kept a private diary in 1924, the year before he died, full of virulently antisemitic, anti-parliamentary, and nationalist opinions — material that stayed obscure for decades and that Michael Dummett apparently found disturbing enough to write about in the preface to one of his own books on Frege. That aside sent me down a rabbit hole in the secondary literature, and I ended up writing a short response paper for our seminar's discussion board that tries to say something more specific than the usual "does the diary taint the logic or not" framing everyone seems to default to. I would like some candid comments on it before I post it. The paper is below.

Discussions of the diary tend to split into two camps: those who treat it as philosophically inert, since anti-psychologism is precisely the doctrine that a proof's validity cannot depend on the prover's character, and those who treat it as a moral stain that should make us read the technical work more suspiciously. I want to try a third, more specific line, built around a coincidence I don't think is usually taken seriously enough to be worth arguing about, but that I think survives being taken seriously. In "Der Gedanke" (1918), Frege argues that thoughts belong to a "third realm" — in his own German, if I have this right, "ein drittes Reich" — distinct from both the outer world of physical things and the inner world of private ideas, precisely because a thought's truth cannot depend on any thinker's psychology, nation, or race. Not many years later, in Weimar nationalist writing, "das Dritte Reich" starts to name something else entirely: a longed-for political order superseding the exhausted alternatives of monarchy and parliamentary republic. I don't think Frege borrowed the political phrase, or that nationalist writers were reading "Der Gedanke" — the resemblance is almost certainly independent convergence on an old piece of German cultural furniture (the idea has real pedigree, going back at least to Joachim of Fiore's three ages) rather than any direct line of influence.

What I want to argue is that the convergence is interesting anyway, because it isn't merely verbal. Frege's mature philosophy keeps reaching for exactly this shape of move: a binary that seems to leave an intolerable remainder, resolved by positing a third thing that isn't really a compromise but a way of denying that any legitimate plurality was ever there. The inner/outer divide leaves thoughts homeless, so he adds a third realm rather than conceding that logic might be answerable to psychology after all. Against Hilbert, he cannot accept that there might legitimately be more than one consistent geometry answering to the word "true," so it has to be that Hilbert has confused sense with reference, rather than that Frege's own picture of a single true geometry is optional. And the 1924 diary reaches for the same shape again: German political life is not, for Frege, legitimately plural — Weimar's parties, or Jews within the German nation, are not a real plurality to be represented or accommodated — so the diary keeps gesturing at a resolving figure or order that would restore the single correct arrangement. On this reading the diary is not caused by the logic, and the logic is certainly not an argument for the politics, but both look like symptoms of the same low tolerance for conceding that a given domain might legitimately contain more than one right answer.

The obvious objection is that this is too cute by half: "Reich" is a completely mundane German word (the animal kingdom is the "Tierreich"), so leaning on the coincidence risks exactly the kind of numerology serious intellectual history is supposed to resist, and a philosophically serious version of the parallel shouldn't need the wordplay at all — it should survive on the structural point about intolerance of plurality by itself, with the terminological echo demoted to a mnemonic rather than treated as evidence of anything.

I would appreciate comments on three things in particular: first, whether the "third realm / Third Reich" coincidence is worth keeping at all, or whether my own objection in the last paragraph is decisive and I should cut it and lead with the structural point instead; second, whether I have Frege's actual positions right, especially the bit about Hilbert, which I suspect I am compressing too aggressively; and third, whether the "low tolerance for plurality" reading strikes you as genuine philosophical insight or as an unfalsifiable pattern that could be told about almost any absolutist thinker, logical or otherwise, regardless of their actual politics. I am not looking for a rewrite, just an honest sense of where the argument is weakest.
