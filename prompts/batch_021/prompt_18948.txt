Now retired after a career in observational cosmology, I have lately filled my time with philosophy, and I keep returning to the literature on anthropic reasoning, since it revisits concerns about selection effects that occupied much of my working life. I understand the basic opposition between two positions: the Self-Sampling Assumption (SSA) taken alone, on which one reasons as though randomly drawn from the observers who actually exist within one's reference class, and SSA supplemented by the Self-Indication Assumption (SIA), on which hypotheses entailing more observers become proportionally more likely before any evidence is even brought to bear. What I have not seen properly dramatized is the trade-off between the two. Please write a dialogue between a philosopher who accepts SSA alone and one who accepts SSA+SIA, in which the latter presses the former on Carter and Leslie's Doomsday argument, and the former presses back with Bostrom's Presumptuous Philosopher. Each should be made to explain, under the other's specific pressure, why the counterintuitive consequence their own view accepts is more tolerable than the one they are attacking — neither may simply retreat to rejecting anthropic reasoning altogether.
