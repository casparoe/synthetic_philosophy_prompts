I had a long, unresolved argument last week with a friend who works as a machine-learning engineer, which started with whether large language models have genuine common sense and ended, unexpectedly, with Daniel Dennett's old fable about a robot named R1. R1 is sent to retrieve its spare battery from a room that also contains a time bomb sitting on the same wagon as the battery, and gets blown up because it never derives that pulling the wagon out will bring the bomb along with it. Its successor, R1D1, is built to deduce the consequences of its actions first, and is still working through the endless list of true but useless implications of its own plan -- starting with the fact that pulling the wagon will not change the colour of the walls -- when the bomb goes off anyway. A third model, R2D1, is built to sort relevant from irrelevant implications before acting on any of them, and is destroyed in turn, because sorting turns out to be exactly as unbounded a task as the original deducing was. My friend's position was that this whole problem has been quietly dissolved by scale and statistics: a model trained on enough text simply weights relevant considerations far more heavily than irrelevant ones, in practice, without anyone specifying in advance what counts as relevant, so treating the frame problem as an open philosophical wound now seems to her a bit like still fretting over whether bumblebees can really fly. I pushed back with two older objections that I don't think she ever fully defused: Fodor's argument that fixing what is relevant to a belief requires something like unbounded access to everything else you believe -- what he called being "isotropic" and "Quinean" -- which is exactly the sort of process his own modularity thesis says cannot be handed to any encapsulated, rule-governed system; and Dreyfus's older rejoinder that relevance only ever shows up for a creature already absorbed in a world of practical cares, so that no amount of disembodied pattern-matching over text could really solve the frame problem rather than merely get away with not noticing it most of the time.

Could you write me a short story, growing out of that argument, that dramatizes the frame problem for a reader who has never heard of it -- without simply re-running the Dennett fable, which I have now basically told you myself? It doesn't need to involve a robot at all; a triage nurse, a chess player in time trouble, a spy improvising a cover story, or an animal loose in an unfamiliar habitat would all serve, provided the crisis in the plot turns on the character's difficulty bounding what is relevant, rather than on their simply lacking some crucial fact. I would be glad if the story left room for both halves of the disagreement above: some sense of why a reasoner might drown in true-but-useless implications the way R1D1 did, and some sense of why a body, a history, or a set of practical stakes might be what keeps a mind from ever getting that lost in the first place -- without turning into an essay that spells this out. Please do not resolve the underlying debate on our behalf; an ending that stays honestly uncertain about whether the character's way of coping with relevance amounts to a real solution, or just a way of not noticing the problem, would suit the actual state of the argument better than a tidy moral.
