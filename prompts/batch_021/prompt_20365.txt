I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, but I work on distributed systems, where epistemic logic is a standard tool of the trade. In that setting, an agent's knowledge at a given global state is defined as whatever holds in every global state it cannot distinguish from its own, and "cannot distinguish" just means "assigns the agent the same local state." Because sameness of local state is a genuine equivalence relation, positive introspection -- Kp implies KKp, the KK principle -- falls out as a free theorem of the semantics, which is why puzzles like the muddy children problem can simply take it for granted. I recently came across Timothy Williamson's argument that no non-trivial condition is "luminous" (you can be in a state, even the state of knowing that p, without being positioned to know you're in it), which he builds from a margin-for-error, sorites-style argument and deploys specifically to reject the KK principle for knowers like us. I'd like you to compare how the KK principle functions in these two settings -- a stipulated theorem for the idealized agents of epistemic logic, versus a substantive thesis about human self-knowledge that, according to Williamson, turns out to be false -- and connect this to his more general luminosity thesis, of which KK is meant to be a special case. Does his argument expose a hidden empirical assumption buried in the distributed-systems formalism, or is there a principled reason the margin-for-error reasoning can't get a foothold there at all? Please be precise about exactly where the two notions of "indistinguishable" part ways.
