A dinner conversation with a close friend has turned into something I can't stop turning over in my head. Maya is convinced that my habit of buying clothes from fast-fashion retailers is seriously wrong, given what we both know about the conditions in the factories that make them; I'm equally convinced that her diet — she eats meat freely and treats my vegetarianism as a personal quirk rather than a moral position — contributes to a great deal of needless animal suffering. Neither of us has budged the other after several rounds of the usual arguments, so, half as a joke, Maya proposed something different: she'd go vegetarian for six months if I committed to buying only secondhand or certified ethically-made clothing for the same period. Neither of us would be doing this because we'd come around to the other's view; we'd each simply be acting, in one domain, the way the other already thinks we should, in exchange for the other doing likewise. A friend who studies philosophy tells me this general pattern of bargaining across moral disagreement has its own name and literature — "moral trade" — and that its legitimacy is treated as a genuine open question, quite separate from the first-order rights and wrongs of meat or fast fashion.

Before Maya and I decide anything, I'd like to understand the disagreement about moral trade itself, rather than get a verdict on our particular case. Could you write a dialogue between two philosophers who take opposing sides on it — one who defends such trades as a rational, perhaps underused, response to persistent and reasonable moral disagreement, treating them as something like a Pareto improvement relative to each party's own values, and another who regards them as a subtle form of moral corruption, closer to the sale of indulgences than to legitimate compromise? I'd like the defender to be pressed on hard cases rather than easy ones — whether granting someone a concession in exchange for their compliance makes you complicit in whatever they go on to do with that concession, whether the arrangement rewards exaggerating one's convictions in order to negotiate better terms, and whether a trade that counts as a mutual gain relative to two incompatible moral views could still be a net loss relative to whichever view, if either, happens to be true. The critic should equally have to explain why self-interested motivation is disqualifying here when it doesn't seem to undermine the sincerity of ordinary promises, treaties, or contracts entered into for mixed reasons. I'd like the two of them to genuinely engage with each other's specific objections in turn rather than restate their opening positions, and it's fine if the exchange ends without either side conceding.
