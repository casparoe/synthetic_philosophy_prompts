I'm working on a piece about how physicists and philosophers of science talk about probability in contexts like quantum mechanics and radioactive decay, where the propensity interpretation is popular: the idea that a probability describes an objective physical tendency of a chance setup to produce a given outcome, rather than a long-run frequency or a degree of belief. In my research I keep running into references to Paul Humphreys' 1985 paper "Why Propensities Cannot Be Probabilities," which is supposed to show that propensities can't actually satisfy the ordinary axioms of probability theory. As far as I can tell, the trouble has something to do with inverting conditional probabilities via Bayes' theorem, but I can't work out why that would cause special difficulty for the propensity view specifically, since frequentists and subjectivists use Bayes' theorem just as freely. Could you walk me through the core of what's come to be known as Humphreys' paradox, and explain what exactly makes it bite for propensities in particular, in a way that the other interpretations escape?
