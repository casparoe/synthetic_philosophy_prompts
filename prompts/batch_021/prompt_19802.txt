so in my philosophy elective we just started a unit on classical Indian philosophy and covered the Nyaya school's list of "pramanas," basically the officially recognized ways you can gain real knowledge. one of them is called upamana (comparison), and the teacher gave the classic textbook example: a city guy who's never seen a gavaya (some wild-ox looking animal) gets told "it looks kind of like a cow," then later he's actually out in the forest, sees an animal that looks like a cow, and goes "oh, THAT must be a gavaya." Nyaya says that moment of recognition is its own seperate source of knowledge, distinct from perception, inference, and testimony.

my friends Theo and Mia would not let this go on the bus ride home. roughly how it went:

Theo: this is one pramana too many, nothing new is actually happening. the guy just remembers being told about the resemblance (testimony), perceives an animal that matches (perception), and infers "matches the description, so this is it" (inference). thats three ordinary things stacked, not a fourth kind of knowing.

Mia: none of those three actually gets you the thing that happened though, which is learning that the WORD "gavaya" applies to THIS animal. perception just gives him fur and horns, not a word. and it cant be inference either, since Nyaya says inference runs on a general pattern you've already seen hold a bunch of times, like smoke and fire -- theres no backlog of observations here, its a one-shot recognition.

Theo: "matching a verbal description to a thing in front of you" is a totally normal thing memory and perception do together all the time, like matching a missing-dog flyer to a stray you see on the street. we dont need a whole new faculty of knowledge for that.

Mia: the flyer already gives you a picture to match against though. all this guy had was a sentence comparing gavayas to a totally different animal, applied to something he'd never mentally pictured before. linking the word to this new object for the first time IS the gap between the testimony he had and the perception he's having, and neither one closes it alone.

Theo: ok but if "this combo technically produces a new-ish kind of output" is enough to justify its own pramana, you could mint one for basically any two-step cognition you like. what's the principled stopping point that doesn't also let in a hundred pramanas nobody wants?

they made me pick a side on the bus and I genuinely had no idea. can you go through Theo and Mia's exchange move by move and say who's got the better argument at each point -- not just an overall winner -- and also say whether there's something important about what upamana is supposed to explain that they're both missing?
