So I spent almost forty years as a research chemist, and now that I'm retired I've been filling the time reading philosophy, which I never had patience for back when I was working. Lately I fell down a rabbit hole on the early Society for Psychical Research (yes, that's a real thing — founded in 1882 by a bunch of serious academics), because I couldn't figure out why a genuinely rigorous philosopher — the same Sidgwick people know from utilitarian ethics — would spend decades of his life on telepathy experiments and sittings with mediums. I dug up the address he gave at the Society's first general meeting, and there's a passage near the end I keep rereading without quite getting a handle on. Here it is:

"Scientific incredulity has been so long in growing, and has so many and so strong roots, that we shall only kill it, if we are able to kill it at all as regards any of those questions, by burying it alive under a heap of facts. We must keep "pegging away," as Lincoln said; we must accumulate fact upon fact, and add experiment upon experiment, and, I should say, not wrangle too much with incredulous outsiders about the conclusiveness of any one, but trust to the mass of evidence for conviction. The highest degree of demonstrative force that we can obtain out of any single record of investigation is, of course, limited by the trustworthiness of the investigator. We have done all that we can when the critic has nothing left to allege except that the investigator is in the trick. But when he has nothing else left to allege he will allege that.

We shall, I hope, make a point of bringing no evidence before the public until we have got it to this pitch of cogency. I think it is desirable on various grounds, but one ground is, I think, this: It is due to the private families or private circles of friends whom we hope to persuade to allow us to take part in their experiments, not to leave the subject or the medium of the phenomena—when we have convinced ourselves, by our own methods, of the genuineness of the phenomena—to bear alone the injurious suggestions of any incredulous materialist who may find it needful to attack our experiments. We must drive the objector into the position of being forced either to admit the phenomena as inexplicable, at least by him, or to accuse the investigators either of lying or cheating or of a blindness or forgetfulness incompatible with any intellectual condition except absolute idiocy."

What trips me up is that last move. He sounds almost satisfied that he's cornered the skeptic into a dilemma — admit the phenomena, or accuse the investigators of lying, cheating, or idiocy — like the argument has actually reached a real resting point. But couldn't a determined skeptic just keep taking that second horn forever, no matter how much evidence piles up, since "you're lying or deluded" is always logically available to say? In chemistry we settle disagreements by rerunning the reaction, not by vouching for each others character, so this move feels foreign to me. Is Sidgwick making a legitimate epistemological argument here about where reasonable doubt has to stop, or is "we have done all that we can" just a graceful way of admitting the whole thing has collapsed into a personal dispute about who to trust? Genuinely curious what's going on logically in this bit.
