I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, but I've been asked to give the best man speech at my friend Dan's wedding next month, and there's one part of it I'd like to get right rather than fudge. Dan and I met in a Christian fellowship group in college, back when he was, if anything, more devout than the rest of us. Sometime in his mid-twenties he read his way into what he calls "the hiddenness problem" -- he name-drops a philosopher named Schellenberg -- and it's what moved him from committed believer to a settled, low-drama agnostic. As he's explained it to me, and I'll admit I'm probably rounding off some corners, his point was never the standard "there's too much suffering for a good God to exist"; he gets genuinely annoyed when people flatten it into that. His version was narrower: a God who truly wanted a relationship with him would have made sure that Dan, who was looking sincerely and not just going through a phase, ended up with something to go on, and instead he searched for years and came up with nothing, and he came to think the silence itself was the evidence. He's also quick to say there are serious responses to this -- something about hiddenness protecting our freedom to choose belief rather than being coerced into it -- that he takes seriously without finding persuasive.

Here's my actual problem. Dan's fiancée, Claire, has grandparents who raised her and are quite devout, and they'll be at the head table, a few seats down from a cluster of Dan's old philosophy-club friends who will absolutely notice any reference to this. Dan has told me, half-joking, that he'd hate it if the toast made it sound like he "just hasn't found God yet" or is secretly still searching, since that isn't his actual position and it would flatten something he's thought about carefully for years; but he also very much doesn't want a philosophy-of-religion detour at his own wedding, and I don't want Claire's grandparents to feel like the room is quietly laughing at their faith. What I'd love, if it can be done well, is for the one moment that touches on this to turn into something warm about the marriage itself -- along the lines of: whatever Dan concluded about a God who stays silent no matter how hard you look for him, he has never once left Claire wondering where she stands with him.

Could you draft the toast? It's a reception toast, about five minutes long, delivered right before the cake is cut, for a room of roughly 150 people split between Dan's more secular, philosophically inclined friends and Claire's more traditionally religious family. It should be funny and mostly about the two of them as a couple, not a lecture -- but I'd like that one turn handled with real care: get Dan's actual position right rather than a softened version of it, and make it land warmly for both halves of the room.
