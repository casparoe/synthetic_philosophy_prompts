Two of my graduate students got into a genuinely good argument after a reading group on Singer's "Famine, Affluence, and Morality," and I haven't been able to settle it since. It started because a mutual friend of theirs, an adjunct who has pledged ten percent of her income to effective charities, admitted over lunch that she thinks the argument actually commits her to far more than that, and that she isn't willing to give it.

(1) Mara said this shows consequentialism can't be right: a theory that requires each of us to keep sacrificing until our own marginal utility matches the person we could help demands a life almost no one could live, and a theory that counts nearly every life as seriously wrong has gone wrong somewhere.

(2) Dan said that conflates the theory of right action with a standard of blame: a consequentialist can hold that the act is wrong while denying the agent is much to blame, given ordinary excusing conditions like weakness of will and a lifetime of partial socialization. Demandingness shows we should be careful moving from "wrong" to "blameworthy," not that the theory is false.

(3) Mara answered that this just relocates the problem: a standard of rightness that virtually no one could follow, and that treats a stranger's minor discomfort as on a par with your own child's education, has stopped tracking anything we'd recognize as a moral requirement -- which is close to Williams' complaint that consequentialism reduces the agent to a mere channel for impartially produced value, alienated from his own commitments.

(4) Dan pointed out that other theories are just as demanding once their own duties are triggered -- an absolute duty not to break a serious promise, or a duty of easy rescue, can cost someone dearly in the right circumstances -- so the real difference may be that consequentialism's demands are triggered constantly rather than occasionally, not that they are distinctively heavy, and that difference needs an argument of its own.

(5) Mara replied that Scheffler himself felt he had to graft an agent-centered prerogative onto a consequentialist axiology from outside, and that a permission justified mainly by the acceptable verdicts it yields is exactly what "ad hoc" means.

(6) Dan didn't accept that: every moral principle needs some non-arbitrary way of fixing how much it requires, and specifying the size of a prerogative is no more ad hoc than commonsense morality specifying how costly a "reasonable" rescue can be.

Neither budged. Please go through the exchange move by move and say who has the better of it at each numbered point -- not just who wins overall -- and note anything you think both of them are missing. Close with a short final verdict.
