Three months ago my mother received a terminal diagnosis, and my family has since split quietly over how honest to be with her about it: my father softens every scan result before repeating it to her, my sister insists we may withhold things but must never actually assert anything false, and I have caught myself simply rephrasing the truth rather than answering directly when she asks how bad it really is.

Trying to work out where I actually stood, I went back to Augustine's two treatises on lying, De Mendacio and Contra Mendacium, expecting some moderate, common-sense Christian position and instead finding one of the strictest prohibitions on falsehood in the history of ethics. For Augustine, to assert what you believe to be false with the intention of deceiving is sinful regardless of motive: he explicitly denies that it is permissible to lie even to protect an innocent person from someone meaning to kill them, ranking this kind of "helpful" lie among the least grave in his catalogue of lies but a sin all the same, and by his own logic a lie told only to spare a dying woman from distress would fare no better. What struck me is that none of this rules out silence, or misleading someone by what you decline to say -- the line he insists on is at assertion itself, not at every way of managing what another person comes to believe. The conclusion, if not the framework, reappears almost unchanged in Kant, who centuries later denied, against Benjamin Constant, that you may lie even to a murderer asking where your friend is hiding -- a verdict many of Kant's own admirers treat as an embarrassment rather than a discovery -- and which Dietrich Bonhoeffer explicitly repudiated while he himself was helping to hide people from the Gestapo.

I would like an essay that treats this as a live question rather than a historical curiosity. Lay out the strongest case for Augustine's (and Kant's) absolutism about lying, including why the assertion/silence distinction might be doing real philosophical work and why "just this once, for a good enough reason" may be exactly the exception a workable rule against lying cannot survive, and then lay out the strongest case against that absolutism, drawn from cases like the murderer at the door or my mother's. Rather than leaving the matter tied, commit to an actual verdict -- it needn't be "lying is always fine" or "never"; a carefully qualified rule is a perfectly good answer -- about whether, and by what principle, some lies are genuinely permissible, and defend it as an argument rather than a shrug.
