I cover the history and philosophy of science, and I'm drafting a column on Isaac Newton's theology that I'd like a second opinion on before filing it. I'd appreciate comments on three things: whether the historiographical point in the third paragraph is a fair distinction or pedantic hair-splitting, whether the closing parallel is a real continuity a working historian would recognize or a flattering anachronism I'm projecting onto him, and any factual claim you think is wrong, overstated, or too technical to survive without further throat-clearing for a general readership. The draft follows.

Everyone who has read a Newton biography knows the punchline: the man who held the Lucasian chair and gave physics its laws was, in private, a committed heretic who denied the Trinity — a fact usually deployed for the cheap irony that he held this view as a fellow of Trinity College, Cambridge, and got away with keeping it quiet partly because a royal dispensation, obtained around 1675 with the help of his predecessor Isaac Barrow, had exempted the Lucasian professorship from the usual rule that Cambridge fellows take holy orders.

That trivia undersells how specific Newton's heresy actually was. Around 1690, in a manuscript he sent to John Locke titled "An Historical Account of Two Notable Corruptions of Scripture," Newton didn't just register doubt about the Trinity; he built a case from manuscripts and patristic citations that the two verses doing the heaviest lifting for the doctrine — 1 John 5:7's "three that bear witness in heaven," and the reading of 1 Timothy 3:16 that has "God," rather than "who" or "which," manifest in the flesh — were absent from the earliest Greek witnesses and unquoted by early Fathers who had every polemical reason to cite them had they existed. He arranged through Locke for anonymous publication on the Continent, then lost his nerve and asked him to call it off; the piece wasn't published under his own name until long after he died.

Historians working on this material tend to resist collapsing it into "Arianism." Newton never used the word for himself: he understood his project not as reviving a fourth-century heresy but as stripping away a fourth-century corruption to recover a purer, pre-Nicene Christianity, and he reserved particular contempt for Athanasius, whom he accused, in a manuscript devoted to exactly this charge, of having manufactured the whole controversy through forged documents and mob intimidation rather than honest argument. Whether the Christology he arrived at — a Christ who pre-exists but is created and subordinate to the Father — is best described as Arian, some near relative of it, or an idiosyncratic reconstruction of his own, is a genuine live dispute among specialists, not just a missing label.

My argument is that this is the same evidentiary temperament that produced the Principia, aimed instead at scripture: distrust of transmitted authority, insistence on tracing a claim back to its earliest witnesses, and a willingness to sit on a well-supported private conclusion for decades rather than publish it without what he considered sufficient proof. That caution was not paranoia: going public could have cost him his career and civil standing, exactly as it did his own successor in the Lucasian chair, William Whiston, expelled from Cambridge in 1710 for saying in print what Newton only ever said in manuscripts he kept in a drawer. What I can't tell from inside the piece is whether the parallel to his scientific method reads as a real continuity in how he thought, or as a tidy unity I'm imposing on a life most Newton scholars treat as two largely separate rooms.
