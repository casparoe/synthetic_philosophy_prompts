And here is the answer one member of the group (I'll call him Marcus) turned in:

"The Doomsday Argument trades on the idea that I should treat my own position in the sequence of all humans who will ever be born as a random draw rather than assume I occupy a special spot near the beginning. That move is licensed by the Self-Sampling Assumption: absent other information, you should reason as though you were a randomly chosen member of your reference class — here, all humans past, present, and future. The idea was put into these terms by Nick Bostrom in the early 1990s, as part of a broader project of showing how self-locating information could bear on questions like extinction risk, and it was this framing that John Leslie subsequently popularized in The End of the World. Given that somewhere between 60 and 100 billion humans have been born so far, my own birth rank is itself a piece of data, and Bayes' theorem tells us how to use it: compare a hypothesis on which only 200 billion humans will ever live to one on which 200 trillion will. A birth rank of 100 billion is an unremarkable, middling draw under the first hypothesis, but it would be an extraordinarily early one under the second — comparable to drawing one of the first few tickets out of a drum that holds two million. Since the data is much less surprising under the low-population hypothesis, Bayes' theorem shifts the posterior probability toward that hypothesis relative to whatever prior you started with. It's the same structure as the classic German tank problem, where a low serial number on a captured tank is evidence that the total production run was small.

Richard Gott's reasoning about the Berlin Wall, which he visited in 1969 and wondered how much longer it would stand, is a relative of this argument rather than the same argument in different clothing. Gott's version assumes that your moment of observation is a random draw from the wall's total lifetime and derives a confidence interval for its remaining lifespan directly from how long it had already stood, with no need for an explicit prior over rival hypotheses about the wall's ultimate lifespan. Carter and Leslie's version, by contrast, is doing genuine Bayesian updating over a set of hypotheses about total population size. Both trade on a Copernican refusal to treat your own vantage point as special, but only one of them is doing that through explicit Bayesian conditionalization.

The Self-Indication Assumption is, if anything, the deeper principle underneath all of this: it holds that, given that you exist at all, you should favor hypotheses on which more observers exist, since your own existence is less surprising under those hypotheses. That is exactly why a low birth rank counts as evidence for a smaller total population in the first place — SIA is what licenses treating your own existence as informative, and the Self-Sampling Assumption is best understood as the corollary that tells you which observer, within whatever population turns out to be correct, you should expect to be. So contrary to how it's sometimes presented, SIA doesn't cut against the Doomsday Argument; it's the engine underneath it, and the two assumptions end up pulling in the same direction once you condition on your own birth rank actually being unremarkable rather than special.

A further objection, independent of any of this, is the reference class problem: the argument's conclusion depends entirely on which population of beings counts as 'observers relevantly like me,' and there's no non-arbitrary way to fix that boundary. Restrict the class to Homo sapiens and you get one number; widen it to any entity capable of running this very argument, or any observer anywhere, and you get a different one, with no principled way to adjudicate between them.

My own verdict is that the argument is a legitimate illustration of how self-locating evidence can move a probability distribution around, but it is weaker than its popular reputation suggests: it shifts a prior, it doesn't hand down a date, and given how contested both the prior and the reference class are, I would treat its practical import as fairly small."
