I'm working on a novel where one of my characters survived a plane crash as a kid and still white-knuckles every flight, even though he knows the statistics cold and lectures other people about how flying is safer then driving. I brought this up at my critique group cause I wasn't sure if it read as believable or just contradictory, and it turned into a side-argument between two other people there, Priya and Oskar, about what fear even *is*. I wrote down the exchange and want a real move-by-move adjudication, not just "good point, both of you."

(1) Priya's opening move: fear is basically an evaluative judgment -- to be afraid of something IS to judge, at some level, that its dangerous to you. So if my character genuinely believes flying is safe, him being terrified on the plane isn't "complex," it's just bad writing -- either he doesn't really believe the stats, or he isn't really afraid, just twitchy.

(2) Oskar pushed back: recalcitrant fear is totally ordinary -- people are scared of the dark, or freeze on a glass skywalk, while flat-out believing there's no danger. Fear is more like a perception than a judgment, he said: it presents the plane as dangerous the way an illusion presents two equal lines as unequal, and you can perceive it that way while believing otherwise, no contradiction needed.

(3) Priya's comeback: fine, but then what makes it fear *of* danger, rather than some bodily flutter that happens to get set off by turbulence? Perception theory smuggles in the "aboutness" -- fear being about danger -- without earning it, since real perception represents low-level stuff like shape and color, and "dangerous" isn't the kind of thing eyes or guts track the way they track shapes.

(4) Oskar's response: that's backwards -- perception represents plenty that isn't raw shape and color (a face just looks angry to you, a slope just looks too steep to ski), so there's no reason an evaluative property like dangerousness can't be perceived directly too, especially if fear evolved specifically to track danger the way vision evolved to track edges.

They ended up just repeating themselves and looking at me to settle it, which I can't. Also tell me if there's some third way of thinking about emotions they're both ignoring, one that would've changed the shape of the whole argument.
