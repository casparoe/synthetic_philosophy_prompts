After forty years running an experimental condensed-matter lab, I retired a couple of years ago and have been using my newfound free time to work through philosophy rather than referee reports. A friend recently lent me Carlo Rovelli's popular book on the nature of time, and it sent me down a rabbit hole into the dispute between presentists, who hold that only the present moment and its contents are real, and eternalists, who hold that past, present, and future are all equally real, differing from one another only as "here" differs from "there." What strikes me, coming from a physics background, is how quickly this metaphysical dispute becomes entangled with the interpretation of special relativity: since simultaneity is relative to one's inertial frame, two observers in relative motion can disagree, without either being mistaken, about which distant events are happening "now" relative to some event here. This is essentially the point behind the argument that Rietdijk and Putnam made in the 1960s, later dramatized by Penrose's "Andromeda paradox," which many philosophers of physics take to show that there is no objective, frame-independent present, and hence that relativity favors a four-dimensional "block universe" over presentism.

Naturally, this got me wondering how Newton himself would have reacted, had he somehow lived long enough to absorb Einstein's theory and its philosophical reception. Newton was quite explicit that absolute time exists in its own right and "flows equably," as he put it in the Scholium to the Principia, independently of any bodies or their relations — about as full-blooded a presentist notion of a single, universal "now" as one could want. Do you think he would simply have conceded that the relativity of simultaneity refutes this absolute time, and gone over to something like the eternalist block universe? Or would his declared refusal to "frame hypotheses" about unobservable causes — his famous hypotheses non fingo — have made him just as uncomfortable with the alternative some presentist philosophers favor today, so-called neo-Lorentzian interpretations, which reproduce all of relativity's empirical predictions while positing a single, undetectable privileged frame underneath them, leaving him genuinely torn between his physics and his own methodological scruples? I would value your reconstruction of which of these commitments you think would ultimately have won out, and why.
