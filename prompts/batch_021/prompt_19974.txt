I'm a novelist spending this year as writer-in-residence at a small college, and the position comes with a seminar of my own design, which I called "The Metaphysics of Music." I proposed it partly to force myself to finally read Schopenhauer properly for a novel I'm drafting about a concert pianist who is going deaf, and partly because the college didn't ask too many questions about my qualifications to teach it. That second part is now a problem: for the midterm I asked students to explain why Schopenhauer thinks music has a wholly different relationship to reality than the other arts do, and I now have a stack of answers I don't trust myself to grade properly. I ran the strongest of them past a philosopher friend over dinner; he skimmed it, pronounced it "basically sound," and went back to his wine, which didn't reassure me much, since I suspect a novelist and a distracted dinner guest are about equally likely to be won over by confident, well-built sentences regardless of what the argument underneath them is actually doing.

The question on the midterm read: "In Book 3 of The World as Will and Representation (see especially §52), Schopenhauer argues that music occupies an entirely different position among the arts from painting, sculpture, or poetry, and has a far more powerful effect on us than any of them. Explain what he means by this. Your answer should address (a) the role the Platonic Ideas play in his account of the other arts, (b) his analogy between the voices of a musical composition and the grades of nature, and (c) what this metaphysics implies for program music and for the practice of setting words to music."

Here is the student's answer in full, exactly as submitted: "For Schopenhauer, the other arts are valuable because they let us contemplate the Platonic Ideas -- the fixed grades in which the will objectifies itself -- apart from the distortions of individual desire and the principle of sufficient reason. Architecture presents the lowest grades of the will's objectivity, the conflict between gravity and rigidity; sculpture and painting present the Ideas of plant and animal life and, at their highest, of the human body and character; poetry, culminating in tragedy, presents the Idea of the self-conscious, rational will in its suffering. Music, on Schopenhauer's account, belongs at the summit of this same series: of all the arts it presents the Idea of the will most purely and completely, which is why its effect on us is so much stronger than that of the others. This is also why he takes seriously Leibniz's remark that music is nothing more than an unconscious exercise in arithmetic, the mind counting without knowing that it counts: for Schopenhauer this is essentially correct, and it is what explains why music can be grasped immediately and completely by anyone, without concepts or training of any kind. He develops the point through an analogy with the different voices of a composition. The bass, the lowest and slowest-moving voice, corresponds to the lowest grade of objectification, unorganized and inorganic nature; the middle voices correspond to the plant and animal kingdoms; and the melody, the free, quickly-moving top voice that continually digresses from and returns to the keynote, corresponds to the deliberating human will, whose satisfactions are always temporary and are followed at once by new desires. Because music presents this restless structure of willing directly, with no represented object standing between them, Schopenhauer treats texted music warily: setting words to music, he suggests, forces its universal language into the service of one particular story, so that even a fine song or opera is really only tolerated by music rather than demanded by it. He is on much firmer ground, though, in his complaint against 'tone-painting' -- music that imitates a specific external event like a storm or a battle -- since this, he argues, drags music down to the level of the very particular phenomena it should transcend. The theory had a long afterlife: Wagner discovered Schopenhauer in the 1850s and adopted this metaphysics of music as a description of his own art, a debt audible from Tristan und Isolde onward."

What I'd like from you is the grade I couldn't confidently produce myself: a letter grade, and a rubric formatted as JSON, with one entry for each of the following five aspects of the answer -- the general theory of Ideas as it applies to the other arts, the specific relationship between music and the will, the analogy between the voices of a composition and the grades of nature, the handling of Leibniz's remark about music and arithmetic, and the treatment of program music and of setting words to music. Each entry should carry a score out of 4 (twenty points in total) and a comment of a sentence or two that I could show the student without embarrassing either of us -- including, especially, anywhere a sentence sounds authoritative but is quietly doing something the underlying text doesn't support.
