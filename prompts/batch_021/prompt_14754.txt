A friend and I spent most of a long drive back from a decision-theory reading group last weekend arguing about the Pasadena paradox, which one of that week's papers had mentioned only in passing, and neither of us walked away persuaded. For reference, the game is this: toss a fair coin repeatedly until it first lands heads; if that happens on the n-th toss, you receive (-1)^(n+1) x 2^n/n. Summed in the natural order, by number of tosses, the expectation is the alternating harmonic series 1 - 1/2 + 1/3 - 1/4 + ..., which converges only conditionally, to ln 2, so by the Riemann rearrangement theorem those very same terms can be reordered to sum to any real number whatsoever, or to diverge to positive or negative infinity, without changing anything about the underlying gamble itself.

My friend's position was that this is a non-problem: once you accept, as many decision theorists already do on independent grounds (following Menger's and Arrow's arguments that any utility function satisfying the expected-utility axioms must be bounded), that admissible utility functions have to be bounded, the expected-utility series becomes absolutely convergent, rearrangement stops mattering, and the Pasadena game reduces to just another unremarkable cousin of the St. Petersburg game. I felt this move was too quick, but I couldn't pin down exactly why on the spot, and I'd rather not lean on a hazy memory of how Nover and Hájek's original paper handles this exact reply, since I'd probably misremember or flatten their actual argument. So I'd like your help doing this properly: please construct the strongest possible case, built from first principles, that mandating bounded utility functions does not actually dispose of the problem the Pasadena game raises for decision theory. Engage directly with the absolute-convergence point rather than talking past it, and argue against the most defensible version of my friend's view, not a weaker one.
