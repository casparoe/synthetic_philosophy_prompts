The assignment was built around this question:

"In section 6 of 'Computing Machinery and Intelligence' (1950), Turing works through nine objections to the claim that a machine could be said to think. The ninth and last is the Argument from Extra-Sensory Perception. In a few paragraphs: (a) explain why Turing does not simply dismiss this objection; (b) explain exactly what is supposed to go wrong with the imitation game if ESP is real; (c) explain how the 'telepathy-proof room' is meant to fix it; and (d) briefly assess whether this fix sits comfortably with how Turing handles the other eight objections."

Here is the response one member submitted:

"Turing doesn't wave the ESP objection away, which is what a modern reader expects. He concedes the statistical evidence for telepathy is 'overwhelming' and calls the objection 'quite a strong one,' and he admits that once you accept that much, 'it does not seem a very big step to believe in ghosts and bogies' — so on his own logic he ends up committed to the existence of ghosts as well as telepathy, which is an odd place for a founder of computer science to land.

The actual problem for the imitation game is that a human witness with real telepathic ability could pick up information no ordinary conversation would give them. Turing's own example is a card-guessing test: the interrogator asks what suit a card belongs to, and a telepathic human answers correctly far more often than chance would allow while the machine can only guess at random. If the interrogator uses questions like that, the human gets unmasked as human (or the machine as machine) by a paranormal skill that has nothing to do with thinking, which defeats the point of the test.

Turing's patch is to seal both competitors in a 'telepathy-proof room,' which he compares to stopping a competitor from listening at the wall while the interrogator talks to himself. Block the telepathic channel and the game goes back to being about conversation alone.

Where this sits badly with the rest of the paper is that Turing's usual method is to refuse to settle questions about hidden, unobservable inner states — that is exactly his move against the Argument from Consciousness, where he brackets the mystery of subjective experience rather than resolve it. With ESP he does the reverse: he takes a hidden, unobservable faculty at face value on statistical grounds and redesigns his experiment around it. That is a real inconsistency in how much weight he puts on evidence he cannot observe directly."
