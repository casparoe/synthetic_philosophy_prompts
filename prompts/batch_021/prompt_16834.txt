In my Philosophy of Technology seminar this term, we have spent several weeks working through the literature on so-called "responsibility gaps" in automated decision-making. We started with Andreas Matthias's 2004 argument that learning systems whose behavior even their own designers can no longer fully predict create a gap that traditional, control-based notions of culpability cannot bridge, and then moved on to Filippo Santoni de Sio and Giulio Mecacci's more recent proposal that this is not really one problem but at least four distinct ones — gaps in culpability, moral accountability, public accountability, and active responsibility — each traceable to different technical, organizational, or legal causes.

The discussion became far more concrete for me a few weeks ago, when a close friend was flagged by our university's automated remote-proctoring and plagiarism-detection system for "anomalous behavior" during an online final. When she appealed, no one in the chain could really explain why the model had flagged her: her professor said he simply trusted the software's output, the academic-integrity office said they only ever see a flag and a confidence score rather than a reason, and the vendor declined to disclose how the underlying model reaches its decisions, citing trade secrets. She was eventually cleared, but only after several stressful weeks, and the whole episode left me wondering whether anyone had ever really been in a position to be held responsible for the false accusation, or whether we had simply watched one of the gaps from our seminar readings play out in real time.

Could you describe what you take to be the most important implications of the responsibility-gap literature for a case along these lines? I would be especially interested in your view on which of Santoni de Sio and Mecacci's four gaps, or which combination of them, is doing the most work in a scenario like automated proctoring; whether frequently proposed remedies such as mandatory explainability requirements, strict or vicarious liability schemes, or the "meaningful human control" framework genuinely close the relevant gap rather than merely relocating it to a different point in the chain; and what practical lesson, if any, a student in my friend's position should draw about where to direct demands for accountability the next time something like this happens.
