I write on science and ideas for a general-interest outlet, and I've spent the last few weeks reporting a long-form piece on how different intellectual traditions cope with the idea that a well-formed question might not have a straightforward yes-or-no answer. That reporting is what led me from Graham Priest's work on paraconsistent logic to his co-written argument, with Jay Garfield, that Nāgārjuna's Mūlamadhyamakakārikā is best read as embracing genuinely true contradictions at the limits of thought. Next week I'm recording an hour-long interview with a scholar of Madhyamaka Buddhism whose forthcoming book argues the opposite: that the catuṣkoṭi's fourfold rejection of a thing's existing, not existing, both, or neither was never meant to breach classical logic at all, because each of the four 'horns' denies that the thing has svabhāva — own-being, essence, independent existence — rather than assigning or withholding an ordinary truth-value. Whenever an interviewer presses her on whether this leaves excluded middle and non-contradiction intact, she reaches for some version of the line "that's a diagnosis of reification, not a truth-value assignment," and I don't want to just let her repeat it on air.

Could you draft eight to ten interview questions, each with a short follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give, that make that line progressively harder for her to fall back on? Please work in at least one question on the plain incredulity this kind of move provokes — the sense that refusing all four options, as in the chapter applying the schema to whether the Tathāgata exists, doesn't exist, both, or neither after death, is nihilism or evasion dressed up as logic — and one on how the fourfold negation of svabhāva is supposed to do more than either quietly reject excluded middle or just restate that things are dependently originated. I'd also like one built on Nāgārjuna's own line in the Vigrahavyāvartanī that he has "no thesis" to defend, pressing whether that claim escapes the tetralemma or merely defers the contradiction to a meta-level; one on 'the emptiness of emptiness' — whether declaring everything empty of essence doesn't quietly grant emptiness itself the singular, essence-like status of being the one ultimately true description; and one that weighs her reading against the two-truths doctrine, since "conventionally existent, ultimately empty" looks a lot like exactly the third horn — both existent and non-existent, under different descriptions — that the tetralemma is supposed to rule out. Close the sequence with a question about what textual or argumentative evidence would actually be enough to move her toward the Priest-Garfield dialetheist camp. I'd rather skip anything generic like how she got drawn to Buddhist philosophy; I want every question to turn on the real pressure points in this debate.
