Last night a friend and I argued for nearly two hours over whether it even makes sense to keep resenting someone once you take seriously the possibility that determinism is true. I kept retreating to a broadly Strawsonian line: that the propriety of resentment, gratitude, and indignation doesn't rest on any prior verdict about libertarian free will, but on facts about interpersonal life so basic that we couldn't give them up even if we tried. My friend's rejoinder was that this only explains why we react as we do, not why the reaction is warranted. I'm interviewing a philosopher on my podcast next week who has built her career defending exactly this kind of Strawsonian naturalism about moral responsibility, and I don't want the conversation to just retrace "Freedom and Resentment." Draft eight interview questions that push past mere restatement of the view. I want at least one question, each paired with a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give, on: (1) whether the excuse/exemption distinction can really handle hard cases like chronic addiction or severe childhood deprivation, where it's unclear whether we're excusing the act or exempting the person; (2) Susan Wolf's asymmetry worry, that we're far readier to excuse bad conduct traced to a determined character than to withhold credit for good conduct traced to one; (3) the charge, pressed by Derk Pereboom and by Galen Strawson (P. F. Strawson's own son, and a hard incompatibilist), that inferring "the reaction is justified" from "we can't help having it" just smuggles in a naturalistic fallacy; and (4) whether a view on which responsibility is ultimately constituted by our actual reactive practices can still say a community is simply wrong to resent people more harshly because of their race, poverty, or disability.
