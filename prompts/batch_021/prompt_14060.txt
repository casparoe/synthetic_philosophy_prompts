I'm drafting a novel in which the antagonist is a neural monitor implanted in my protagonist: it stands ready to force a particular decision the instant she shows any sign of choosing otherwise, though because she always chooses as intended on her own, it never actually has to act. I know this is essentially Frankfurt's classic case against the principle of alternative possibilities, in which a manipulator, Black, stands ready behind an agent, Jones, without ever needing to step in. I want the novel's central question — whether my protagonist is complicit in something she was, in some sense, never able to avoid — to rest on real philosophical ground rather than a plot convenience, so I need to properly understand the standard objection to such cases, sometimes called the dilemma defense: whatever prior sign tips Black off must either be causally sufficient for Jones's decision, which covertly assumes the very determinism that libertarians about free will reject, or it isn't sufficient, in which case some "flicker of freedom" survives for Jones right up to the moment of choice. Could you walk me through this dilemma at a gut level rather than formally, explain how later "buffered" versions of the case, such as Mele and Robb's, are meant to escape it, and tell me candidly whether you think they actually succeed? I would rather build my device around a real position in that debate than around an evasion of it.
