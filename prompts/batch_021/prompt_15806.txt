I spent my working life as an experimental physicist, and only in retirement have I begun reading philosophy in a systematic way. One puzzle that quietly nagged at me throughout my career is Wigner's 1960 observation on the "unreasonable effectiveness of mathematics": that formal structures developed for their own internal, often aesthetic reasons, with no physical application in mind, repeatedly turn out to describe nature with startling precision, and that this fact deserves an explanation distinct from the more basic question of why nature is law-governed at all. Reading further, I came across Hamming's 1980 rejoinder, which tries to explain the effect away through selection effects (we notice, and even define as "science," whichever problems happen to be mathematically tractable) and through the evolutionary tailoring of our mathematical intuitions to the world we inhabit, though he stops short of declaring the puzzle fully resolved. Then there are more metaphysically ambitious responses, such as Tegmark's mathematical universe hypothesis, which dissolves the question by identifying physical reality with mathematical structure itself rather than treating mathematics as merely a description of it. I would like an essay that takes this dispute seriously on its own terms rather than simply retelling Wigner's original anecdotes: weigh the deflationary accounts against the more metaphysically committal ones, and close with a definite verdict on how much genuine mystery is left once the dust settles, and why.
