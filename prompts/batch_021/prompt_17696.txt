This term I'm taking a seminar on the ethics of war built around the debate over what the literature calls the "moral equality of combatants" — between the traditional view, defended by Walzer in *Just and Unjust Wars*, that soldiers who fight cleanly are moral equals regardless of which side's cause is just, and the revisionist challenge in McMahan's *Killing in War*, which holds that a combatant fighting for an unjust cause does wrong by killing even when she fights entirely within the rules. For our capstone assignment, each of us has been assigned a case to bring before a hypothetical Armed Forces Ethics Review Board, a body with discretionary authority over personnel petitions that existing regulations don't clearly settle.

My case, which generated more disagreement in section than any other when we workshopped them last week, concerns Captain Elena Reyes, a decorated infantry officer who has petitioned for reassignment away from her unit's current combat deployment. She is not a pacifist — she completed two earlier combat tours without objection and says she would deploy again to a war she judged just — and she has no complaint about how this particular war is being fought; her objection is narrowly that sustained independent study has convinced her the decision to fight it at all was unjust, and she does not want to go on killing people in its name. This is what the literature calls "selective conscientious objection," and it falls into a gap the current regulations don't anticipate, since they recognize objector status only for those opposed to war "in any and all forms." The Board's charter lets it deny the petition outright, grant a non-precedential reassignment to non-combat duties, or refer the matter upward with a recommendation to revisit the policy.

Could you draft the roughly one-page memo I would submit as my contribution to the Board's decision? It should settle on one concrete disposition rather than deferring the question, and its reasoning should engage the philosophical substance rather than just restating the regulation. Whichever way you rule, I would like it to confront the strongest objection from the other side directly: if you accommodate Captain Reyes, you will need to answer worries about the epistemic reliability of any individual soldier's judgments about jus ad bellum and about what happens to discipline and cohesion once soldiers start sorting wars into just and unjust on their own authority; if you deny her, you will need to answer the revisionist's case that someone honestly convinced she is helping wage an unjust war has real — perhaps decisive — moral reason not to keep doing so, regardless of what the regulation currently entitles her to.
