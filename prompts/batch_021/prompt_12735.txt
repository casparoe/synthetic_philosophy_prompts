I cover science and ideas for a national magazine, and for the past three years I've also sat on the five-person board of a small nonprofit that pays legal fees for journalists fighting subpoenas and contempt charges. We now have a genuinely hard case in front of us. A freelance reporter we support published a story, built on leaked internal review files, showing that a mid-sized pharmaceutical company withheld adverse-event data from a late-stage trial for several months before disclosing it to regulators. The company has since sued the still-unidentified employee who leaked the files and subpoenaed our reporter to name her source; when she refused, the judge held her in contempt and gave her until the end of the month either to comply or to begin serving jail time pending appeal. The board meets next week to decide whether we fund a public campaign and full appeal on her behalf, or quietly help her negotiate a narrower compliance that keeps her out of jail but breaks the promise she made her source.

The board is split down the middle, and one trustee, a retired appellate judge, keeps steering us back to Pascal — not the aphorism everyone half-remembers, that justice without force is powerless and force without justice is tyrannical, but the harder argument underneath it: that because people generally obey laws only insofar as they believe them just, openly declaring a particular lawful order undeserving of obedience is one of the more dangerous moves an institution can make, since that is exactly the mechanism by which settled authority everywhere eventually comes apart, whatever the merits of the case that prompts it. She isn't using this to argue simply against funding the defiance; she wants the board to feel the full weight of that cost, on the theory that we will decide badly if we only ever reach for the tidy half of Pascal's line.

Could you draft the short memo I'll circulate to the board before that meeting? I need one clear recommendation among the two options above, or a better third one if you see it, with a concrete timeline for what we do and by when. I need the reasoning to take both halves of Pascal's argument seriously: the half that makes funding open defiance look like exactly the justice-over-force stand the trust exists to support, and the half that should unsettle a room of people who care about the rule of law once they consider what it means to publicly underwrite a fight against a lawfully issued order. Please don't let the memo rest on the one-liner; it should show its work on the less comfortable argument, since that is the part actually dividing the board.
