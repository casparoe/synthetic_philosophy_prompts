I've been down a rabbit hole of old LessWrong and MIRI-adjacent posts on "program equilibrium" lately - the result that if two agents can read each others source code before playing a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma, they can actually end up cooperating, even with zero repeated interaction. The part that really got me was the FairBot construction, where you dodge the obvious "I simulate you simulating me simulating you..." regress by having each bot do a bounded search for a proof, in something like Peano arithmetic, that the other bot cooperates with it, and then Lob's theorem forces the handshake through. It feels like a genuinely enormous result: the whole folk-theorem tradition says you need repetition and the shadow of the future to sustain cooperation, and here you get it in a single round just from mutual transparency.

So here's my confusion. If literal code-transparency is enough to dissolve the Prisoner's Dilemma, why do people treat this as a cute logic-puzzle result instead of, like, THE solution to real-world coordination failure? Couldn't states just credibly publish their actual decision procedures for stuff like arms control or climate treaties and get the same guaranteed cooperative equilibrium FairBot gets, instead of relying on flimsy treaties and inspection regimes? I brought this up with a friend who does more formal game theory than me and she just said "sure, but DefectBot-vs-DefectBot is also a program equilibrium," which I didn't really follow - I thought the whole point was that transparency selects for cooperation. Is there a real reason this doesn't generalize the way it seems like it should, beyond the trivial "humans aren't computer programs" objection? It feels extra relevant now given all the talk of AI agents negotiating on our behalf, where actual code or weight transparency might not even be that far-fetched.
