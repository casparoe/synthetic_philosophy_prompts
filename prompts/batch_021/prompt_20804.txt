I'm a software engineer with no real background in philosophy, but over the past year I've fallen into opera fandom -- largely thanks to a colleague who dragged me to a student production -- and I've been working through Bernard Williams's essay collection "On Opera" on the train ever since. Last month two friends of mine, Marcus and Elena, joined me for a performance of Verdi's Il Trovatore, and the argument they got into at dinner afterward has stuck with me, because I think they both said something right and I can't work out how to score it.

(1) Marcus said he found it impossible to take the drama seriously: the plot turns on a decades-old baby swap that leaves two brothers unknowingly trying to kill each other, the whole backstory gets dumped on the audience in one dense narration before anything has actually happened onstage, and the characters keep bursting into gorgeous, symmetrical melodies while being tortured or awaiting execution -- none of which resembles how anyone would actually behave, so the conventions undercut whatever emotional truth the piece is reaching for. (2) Elena, who'd gotten further into the Williams essays than I had, replied that this is exactly the sort of objection to opera that Williams thinks rests on a mistake: judging sung drama by the standard of everyday psychological naturalism misunderstands what the form is doing, because the singing isn't a stylized stand-in for speech that needs excusing, it's the dramatic action itself -- a direct rendering of passion. Faulting Trovatore for being unlike real behavior under torture, she said, is like faulting a fresco for its figures' impossible poses. (3) Marcus answered that this proves too much: if any implausibility is automatically licensed by "that's just the convention," no opera plot could ever be criticized as drama, yet opera lovers themselves treat Trovatore's opening exposition as a notorious mess, worse than the plotting of something like La Bohème -- so some standard of dramatic coherence is clearly still in play, and by that standard this really is weak drama, not merely non-naturalistic. (4) Elena agreed a standard survives, but relocated it: not plausibility, but continuity of feeling -- whether the sequence of arias and confrontations lets you track and inhabit a coherent arc of passion. On that standard, she said, most of Trovatore holds together just fine; what people actually mock is Ferrando's opening narration, which is a failure of pacing and dramaturgy, not a failure of plausibility, and that's a different complaint from the one Marcus started with. (5) Marcus said this quietly gives away the store: if incoherence of some kind can still be a legitimate complaint against an opera, then his original objection was never the naive demand for photographic realism that the "category mistake" charge in (2) accused him of, and Elena's Williams-flavored rebuttal only ever answered a complaint he wasn't making.
