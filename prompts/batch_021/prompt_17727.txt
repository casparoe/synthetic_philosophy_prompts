I've been on a bit of a Turing kick after an argument on a rationalist discord about which parts of "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" people actually read versus just cite for the imitation game setup. Almost nobody talks about the last objection he deals with, the ESP one — he doesn't just wave it away, he says the statistical evidence for telepathy is basically overwhelming, leans on Soal's card-guessing experiments with Basil Shackleton as his main support, and then seriously proposes patching the imitation game with what he literally calls a "telepathy-proof room" so a psychic human contestant can't unfairly give away that they're not the machine. It's definately one of the weirder passages in the history of the field and it just gets cut whenever people excerpt the paper for a class.

What's been bugging me is the timing of it all. Turing died in 1954, so he never found out that Betty Markwick went back through the Soal-Shackleton score sheets in 1978 and showed they'd been doctored — the exact evidence he called overwhelming turned out to be fraud. And he obviously never saw the sequel decades later, Bem's 2011 "Feeling the Future" precognition study and the whole replication-crisis circus that followed (Wagenmakers' Bayesian reanalysis, the failed replications JPSP didn't even want to publish, the whole Yudkowsky "parapsychology is the control group for science" framing that's popular on LW now).

Given that this is also the guy who ran an explicitly Bayesian, sequential evidence-weighing scheme (bans and decibans) against Enigma traffic during the war, so he clearly had strong personal standards for not getting fooled by noisy data — I'm curious what you actually think he'd have done with both of these developments if he'd lived to see them. Does the Markwick fraud plus the Bem saga read like the kind of thing that would've made him recant the ESP section outright, or is there a plausible version of him that updates on the specific frauds and failures while still keeping some credence in psi because of everything else he gestures at in that passage? And if you ran his own deciban-style bookkeeping on Bem's data, what do you think it spits out? I'd rather you actually engage with what he wrote and the epistemic character he showed elsewhere than just assert "he was a rigorous scientist so obviously he'd become a skeptic."
