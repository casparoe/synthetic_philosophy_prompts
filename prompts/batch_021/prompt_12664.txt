I am several chapters into a historical novel set at a great Buddhist monastic university in seventh-century India, modeled loosely on Nalanda, and I have finally reached what I intend as the book's intellectual centerpiece: a formal public debate between my protagonist, a young Madhyamaka monk trained in Nagarjuna's dialectic, and the university's senior Naiyayika scholar, who is trying to have the boy's teaching of emptiness banned from the grounds. I have been reading through modern commentaries on the Mulamadhyamakakarika and the Vigrahavyavartani while drafting this chapter, because I want the debate to hold up against that material rather than lapsing into the vague "everything is interconnected" version of emptiness that circulates in popular treatments; one of my beta readers is a philosophy graduate student who will notice immediately if either side is a pushover.

Could you draft this as a tight five- or six-turn dialogue, stripped of narrative scaffolding? Have the Naiyayika open with the standard charge that the catuskoti is not a deep logic but an evasion: to say of some X that it is neither existent, nor non-existent, nor both, nor neither, is to abandon the law of excluded middle, and that the Madhyamika faces a dilemma either way — if the thesis that all things lack svabhava is itself exempt from that very emptiness, the school is inconsistent, and if it is not exempt, the thesis cancels itself and asserts nothing.

Have the monk answer using resources genuinely internal to the tradition — the distinction between conventional and ultimate truth, the idea that emptiness is itself empty and is offered therapeutically rather than as a rival metaphysical thesis, and the reductio strategy of showing each of the four horns collapse when applied to any proposed cause or self — and then have him turn the pressure back onto the Naiyayika by pressing on how the pramanas that ground his opponent's confidence are themselves established, without either an infinite regress of pramanas validating pramanas or a special pleading that exempts the pramanas from the very demand for grounding they impose on everything else. Neither character should fold early or land a rhetorical hit that would not actually work on a real opponent; each should concede ground exactly where his tradition's real vulnerabilities lie and recover exactly where it has genuine resources. I would rather end on a beat where neither side has clearly won but the terms of what would count as winning have shifted, than on a decisive knockout.
