I'm a PhD student in political philosophy working on epistemic arguments for democratic and market institutions, and while drafting a dissertation chapter I've spent the last couple of weeks stuck on a tension in Hayek's thought that I can't resolve on my own. His case for markets in "The Use of Knowledge in Society" and throughout Law, Legislation and Liberty is fundamentally epistemic: prices aggregate the dispersed, tacit, local knowledge that no planning board could ever collect, and this is exactly why he treated any attempt at technocratic optimization toward an explicit social objective as a "pretence of knowledge," to borrow the title of his Nobel lecture. And yet Hayek was clearly willing to engineer quite deliberate, artificial institutions when he judged existing democratic ones defective -- most strikingly his proposal, in The Political Order of a Free People (the third volume of Law, Legislation and Liberty), for a separate "Legislative Assembly" whose members would be elected only once, in their mid-forties, by their own age cohort, to serve a single non-renewable term of around fifteen years, precisely so that lawmaking would be insulated from party competition and organized pressure groups. (I'm reconstructing the details of that proposal from memory, so do flag it if I've gotten the specifics wrong.)

What I'd like from you is a genuine reconstruction of how Hayek would probably have reacted to Robin Hanson's proposal for "futarchy": elected representatives vote on a measurable welfare metric, and all substantive policy choices are then settled by conditional prediction markets betting on which option would maximize that metric. On one reading, futarchy looks like a natural extension of Hayek's own epistemics -- a decentralized, incentive-compatible mechanism for aggregating dispersed beliefs about consequences, exactly the sort of task he thought markets, and only markets, could perform well. On another reading, it runs straight into his insistence that a free society is a "catallaxy" rather than an "economy," possessing no single agreed scale of ends against which outcomes could be ranked, and into the same Nobel-lecture warnings against treating any quantitative aggregate as a legitimate target of policy. I would like you to work through both sides of that tension in some depth, rather than settling for the easy line that Hayek liked markets and so would have liked this one too, and to say, with reasons, where you think he would ultimately have come down -- drawing on the specific commitments of his later constitutional writing rather than only the knowledge-problem essay that everyone already cites.
