I'm finishing a longform explainer on the "scalable oversight" problem in AI safety for a general science readership — the worry that as AI systems keep improving, human evaluators will eventually be unable to tell whether a given output is actually correct, honest, or safe, so any training signal built on human approval risks rewarding whatever merely looks good to the overseer rather than what is actually good.

Researching the piece, I've interviewed several alignment researchers, and each turns out to be partial to a different proposed fix: pitting two copies of a model against each other in an adversarial debate before a judge who can't verify the underlying facts directly; decomposing hard questions into sub-questions that a human-plus-assistant team can answer and reassembling the results recursively, as in iterated amplification; training a reward model with AI-assisted human feedback that is itself refined recursively; treating oversight as an iterative interrogation modeled loosely on a prediction market ("market making"); trying to elicit a strong pretrained model's latent competence using only weak or partial supervision; and, in the most recent work I've come across, training a generator against a deliberately adversarial "sneaky" counterpart so that its outputs stay checkable by a much weaker verifier. Every researcher is fluent in the theoretical case for their favored scheme and noticeably vaguer about how it degrades once the gap between overseer and overseen system gets large — whether that means a dishonest debater burying a flaw inside an argument tree too costly to unpack, a reward model being gamed in ways no human would notice, or a decomposition strategy quietly discarding whatever never separated cleanly into sub-questions to begin with.
