I'm a software engineer with no background in philosophy, but for the past three years I've served as one of two alumni representatives on my undergraduate university's honorary degree committee, which vets nominees before sending a formal recommendation to the Board of Trustees ahead of commencement. This year's leading nominee is a class-of-1974 alumnus who went on to become prime minister of an allied country I'd rather not name, where he's remembered as the man who ended a fifteen-year civil war within eighteen months of taking office. He did it by ordering sustained strikes on the rebellious region's power stations, irrigation dams, and grain stores — targets chosen, by his own later account, specifically to make ordinary civilian life in that region unsustainable and so break support for the insurgency, rather than to hit the fighters directly. He has never walked this back: in a memoir excerpt included in our committee packet, he writes that he knew exactly what the campaign would do to the civilian population before he signed the order, and that he would sign it again, because the war ended eighteen months later instead of dragging on for another decade.

One committee member, a retired philosophy professor, says she'll resign rather than co-sign the recommendation, and has forwarded the rest of us Elizabeth Anscombe's 1956 pamphlet opposing Oxford's own honorary degree for Truman, insisting the two cases are close enough in structure that we're obliged to reason about them the same way. Two other members are unmoved: they point out that our nominee never personally targeted a single individual — he approved a strategy on the advice of his general staff, several institutional layers removed from anyone's actual death — and that an independent legal review after the war found the campaign proportionate under the laws of armed conflict in force at the time, whatever one thinks of it in hindsight. I described the disagreement to a couple of engineer friends over dinner and got two completely confident, completely opposite verdicts inside a minute, which is roughly where the committee stands too.

I don't trust myself to referee this without help, since I don't know Anscombe's argument well enough to tell whether it actually reaches a case like this one or whether the professor is just pattern-matching on 'atomic bomb' and 'war criminal.' Could you draft the roughly one-page memo I need to circulate to the full committee before next month's vote? It should reach a clear recommendation — advance the nomination as submitted, decline it, or advance it only with some specific accompanying statement — and it should genuinely work through Anscombe's reasoning on its own terms, including whether it has anything to say about the 'he only signed off on a strategy' and 'the numbers came out favorably' objections. Please don't just dismiss the professor as a purist or treat 'just war theory' as a black box that neither side has to open.
