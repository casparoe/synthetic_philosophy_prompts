The question was: "Reconstruct Earman and Norton's hole argument, including the role played by active diffeomorphisms and the 'dragged-along' metric g′ = d∗g. Under what assumption about the nature of spacetime points does the construction generate a threat to determinism, and how does Einstein's own use of a similar construction in 1913–1915 bear on the modern debate? Finally, present and evaluate one strategy for resisting the argument's conclusion. (20 points)"

Here is the answer:

"Einstein's field equations are generally covariant: if (M, g_ab) is a solution and d: M → M is any diffeomorphism, then (M, d*g_ab) is also a solution, since the equations make no reference to any particular coordinate system or background structure. Earman and Norton exploit this to build the hole argument. Pick a region H of the manifold that contains no matter (a 'hole'), and let d be a diffeomorphism that acts as the identity everywhere outside H but smoothly differs from the identity on the interior of H. Dragging the metric along by d produces a new metric g′ = d*g that agrees with g everywhere outside H but disagrees with it inside H, and by general covariance both (M, g) and (M, g′) are equally good solutions.

Whether this is troubling depends on what spacetime points are. If you are a manifold substantivalist — if you think the points of M have their own identity and existence independently of the fields defined on them — then g and g′ describe two distinct possibilities that agree on everything up to the boundary of H and yet disagree inside it, so nothing about the history of the world outside H fixes what happens inside. Since H can be as small as you like and located anywhere, general relativity is therefore an indeterministic theory.

This sharpens a worry Einstein himself had already confronted in the years before he completed the theory: working through a version of the same construction around 1913, he took it to show that any acceptable gravitational field equations had to be generally covariant, a requirement he finally satisfied with the field equations he published in November 1915.

The main strategy for blocking the argument is to deny that g and g′ represent different possibilities in the first place — Earman and Norton's own preferred response, sometimes called Leibniz equivalence. A more conservative variant, sophisticated substantivalism, tries to keep spacetime points as genuine substances while still denying that diffeomorphic models are physically distinct, by rejecting the assumption that a point's identity across models is a further fact over and above its role in the metrical structure. This seems like a reasonable way to get determinism back without collapsing into relationalism, though critics have pushed on whether denying that kind of 'primitive identity' leaves substantivalism with any real content."
