So in my formal epistemology seminar this week we covered Good's theorem on the value of information, and I get the math but not really the intuition behind it. The result is basically: if you're a Bayesian who's actually going to act on whatever you learn, then looking at a piece of free evidence before you decide can never make your expected utility worse then just deciding blind -- info is never expected to hurt you, only help or be neutral. Our professor's proof leaned on the fact that "the value of acting optimally given your posterior" is a convex function of the posterior, so Jensen's inequality basically does all the work, but that felt like an algebra explanation rather than an intuitive one to me. Is there a way to explain why this has to be true that doesn't just restate the convexity/Jensen step in different words -- like, what's actually going on underneath that makes peeking at free evidence never a mistake in this sense?

What's actually bugging me is how this squares with all the "ignorance is bliss" examples people bring up. I mentioned the theorem to my roommate (who's a stats major) and she immediately brought up her aunt, who didn't want to learn her genetic test results for a disease with no treatment, since there was nothing she'd do differently either way and she just didn't want to carry that around. Then there's the classic ruined-surprise-party case, and I also vaguely remember reading that in poker or negotiations, knowing more about the other side can actually leave you worse off once you factor in how they'll play differently around you. Are any of these genuine counterexamples to Good's theorem, or is each one quietly failing some assumption the theorem needs (maybe the "decision" isn't well defined, or it's not really a single-agent problem, or ignorance itself has payoff value)?
