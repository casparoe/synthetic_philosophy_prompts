My philosophy department's hiring committee has nine members. For years, once we had narrowed the search to two finalists, each member cast a private vote before any discussion took place, and whichever candidate received the majority was offered the position. This year, our new chair proposed that we go around the table in order of seniority instead, each voicing a preference aloud before we voted, in hopes that open discussion would lead to a better decision. The committee ended up unanimous, and several colleagues take that as proof the new procedure works better, since anyone who disagreed was free to say so. What troubles me is that the discussion seemed to settle almost immediately behind whichever candidate the first couple of senior speakers preferred, with the rest of us largely falling into line rather than exercising independent judgment. I suspect, without quite being able to justify it, that our nine-to-nothing verdict actually carries less evidential weight than our old five-to-four splits did. Is my suspicion warranted, and if so, where exactly does my colleagues' reasoning go wrong?
