A friend of mine has a seven-year-old son, and last weekend she and I spent much of a dinner arguing about a decision she had made with him that neither of us could quite resolve. She had warned him that if his room was not clean by five o'clock, she would cancel the trip to the amusement park they had been planning for weeks. Five o'clock arrived, the room was still a disaster, and she cancelled the trip anyway, even though by then cancelling could no longer get the room cleaned and simply left them both unhappy for the rest of the weekend.

My view was that once the deadline had passed, following through on the threat served no purpose: nothing about that particular moment gave her any remaining reason to cancel anything, since nothing she did next could change the state of the room, so she might as well have quietly let the trip go ahead and spared them both the disappointment. She countered that if she had backed down, her son would learn that her warnings carry no real weight, and that every future warning would lose its force as a result. I can see why that matters as a general policy, but what I cannot get past is the puzzle at the level of that single decision: standing there at five o'clock, she had, on her own account, no reason tied to that specific moment to act on the threat at all, since whatever work it could do had already succeeded or failed by then.

So, which of us has the stronger position? Was she being irrational to act on a threat that had already become pointless in itself, purely for the sake of a future benefit that this particular instance of compliance could not itself secure? Or does a threat like this work at all only if she had genuinely intended, from the very start, to follow through no matter what, with no exceptions for moments exactly like this one - and if that is right, what would it actually take for an intention like that to be sincere, given that in the moment itself she had every reason to let it go?
