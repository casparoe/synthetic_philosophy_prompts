I'm a novelist about two-thirds of the way through a manuscript, and I've hit a scene I keep rewriting because I'm no longer sure what kind of disagreement it actually depicts. Two former colleagues, estranged for a decade over an old professional betrayal, run into each other at a retirement dinner and fall back into an argument they never finished: whether the cairn that hikers have been adding stones to for thirty years on the ridge behind their old department building is a monument—a genuine memorial object with its own identity and history—or whether there is really nothing there but stones arranged monument-wise, with "the monument" being a convenient fiction laid on top. I minored in philosophy a long time ago and still remember enough to be dangerous, so when I went looking for how a philosopher would classify this kind of quarrel, I ran into the metaontology literature on "verbal disputes"—Eli Hirsch's quantifier variance, David Chalmers' method of elimination—and now I'm worried that my characters' big confrontation might, by the lights of that literature, turn out to be a textbook case of two people talking past each other rather than a real disagreement about the world.

What I'd like from you is a gut-level, non-technical walkthrough of how a philosopher would actually go about telling whether a dispute like this one is verbal or substantive. Specifically: what would it take for the disagreement to survive Chalmers' move of stripping the word "monument" (or "object," or "exists") out of the debate and having each side restate its position using only vocabulary both already accept, and why someone like Ted Sider thinks that test can't be the end of the story, since there might still be a fact of the matter about which candidate meaning of "exists" actually carves reality at its joints rather than being just one equally serviceable option among many. I don't need the formal apparatus of the debate, just enough of its underlying logic that I, as the person who gets to decide what's actually true in this story's world, can honestly choose whether to write these two men as unwittingly agreeing with each other or as genuinely, irreducibly at odds.
