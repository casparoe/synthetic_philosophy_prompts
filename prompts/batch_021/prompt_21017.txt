At my company, two of our senior backend engineers, Lena and Marco, got into a surprisingly heated argument during an incident retro, and it's been bugging me ever since even though I have no formal background in philosophy. The setup: after a nasty production outage, both of them independently dug through the same dashboards and logs and each arrived at a probability for the root cause. Lena came out at around 80% confidence that it was connection-pool exhaustion (versus a downstream API timeout), while Marco landed at around 30%. This wasn't a case of one of them being obviously more senior or more careless -- we keep a rough calibration scoreboard from past postmortems, and their track records over the last couple of years are close to identical. So when they compared notes, the disagreement itself, rather than the bug, became the argument. I looked into it afterward and gather this maps onto a real debate about "conciliationism" versus "steadfastness" in the face of peer disagreement, which only made me more curious what a careful answer would look like, since I clearly can't referee it myself.

Here's roughly how the exchange went, reconstructed as best I can from what people told me afterward. (1) Lena argued that since they'd examined the same evidence and have equal track records, it would be self-serving for either of them to just keep their own number, and that the only defensible move was for both estimates to shift toward each other rather than staying anchored where they started. (2) Marco pushed back that they hadn't actually weighed the same evidence the same way: he'd separately cross-referenced the timeout spikes against deploy timestamps, something Lena hadn't done as carefully, and that this kind of hidden asymmetry is exactly why supposedly clean "peer disagreements" are rarer in practice than the tidy cases used to motivate the idea. (3) Lena replied that if his cross-referencing were as decisive as he was making it sound, he should be far more than 70% confident, not just moderately over half -- and she pointed out that, going by their calibration history, the feeling of "I found the detail that clinches it" has been wrong about as often as it's been right for both of them, so his appeal to a private clincher shouldn't move her estimate at all. (4) Marco then escalated to a different kind of objection: he said Lena's whole argument was self-undermining, since it depends on a specific, contestable philosophical claim -- that shared evidence plus equal track records obligates convergence -- and the two of them plainly don't agree about that claim either; so by her own logic, discovering that he disputes the very principle she's invoking should make her less confident in that principle, which pulls the rug out from under the argument she's using it to make.

Nobody at the retro wanted to adjudicate this, and I said I'd think about it and get back to them, which was a polite way of admitting I had no idea. Please go through moves (1) through (4) and rate how defensible each is, on a scale from -2 (clearly the wrong move) to 2 (clearly the right move), giving your answer as a JSON object of the form {"1": <int>, "2": <int>, "3": <int>, "4": <int>}. Underneath the JSON, add a couple of sentences justifying each score, and then, separately, tell me whether Marco's move (4) actually succeeds in undermining Lena's position, or whether a conciliationist has a principled way to block that kind of regress without just begging the question -- and if you think there's something important about peer disagreement that neither of them is seeing, I'd like to hear that too.
