I spent about thirty five years running a sensory ecology lab -- mostly moths, and how a male locks onto a female's pheromone plume from a hundred meters out -- before I retired, and these days I fill the hours reading philosophy of mind instead of writing grant applications. Lately I've gone down a rabbit hole on teleosemantics, the idea that you can pin down what a mental state (or a receptor, or a signal) is "about" by looking at its evolutionary proper function rather than some free-floating intrinsic meaning. I liked it immediately because its basically the same logic I used for decades to explain animal signalling systems, but the more I read Millikan on "proper function" and "Normal conditions," the more I keep tripping over what's usually called the disjunction problem: a detector selected for tracking X will often also fire on some confound Y, and it's genuinely unclear what fact about the selection history is supposed to fix whether the content is "X," or "X-or-Y," or some coarser category that covers both.

Every worked example I can find in the reading is some version of the frog snapping at flies-or-BB-pellets, or Millikan's bacteria orienting to magnetic-north-or-whatever-that-correlates-with-in-the-northern-hemisphere, and at this point I've basically got the standard "Normal conditions" reply memorized, even though I'm not sure I actually buy it. So here's what I'd like from you: invent a brand new example -- a made-up organism or artificial sensor is totally fine, it doesn't need to be real biology -- where this disjunction/indeterminacy problem really bites, laid out in enough detail that I can see exactly which two or more candidate contents are equally well supported by the very same selection history. Then walk through why appealing to proper function, Normal conditions, or whatever the current best Neander-style fix is supposed to be doesn't actually settle which of those candidates is the correct one. I want a case that genuinely strains the teleosemantic toolkit, not just a relabelled frog.
