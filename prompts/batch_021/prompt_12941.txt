I write about behavioral economics and decision theory for a general-interest magazine, and next week I'm interviewing a decision theorist for a feature on how policymakers are being urged to rethink choices made under 'unknown unknowns.' She has built her academic reputation defending maxmin expected utility -- the Gilboa-Schmeidler view that a rational agent facing genuine ambiguity should rank options by their worst-case expected payoff rather than by an expected value computed from a single best-guess probability. In interviews she reliably retreats to Ellsberg's original urns, which makes for a clean soundbite but lets her dodge everything that has happened to the theory since 1989. Draft seven or eight questions that don't let her rest there. I want at least one that forces her to confront bets with three or more outcomes, where critics such as Machina have shown that maxmin and its main rivals can rank options in ways very few people find intuitively acceptable; one that presses the classic Raiffa objection -- that an agent free to randomize between betting on red and betting on black from the very same ambiguous urn can thereby manufacture a bet with a known fifty percent chance of winning, which seems to dissolve the very ambiguity her model treats as decision-relevant; and one that makes her explain why ranking by the worst case counts as a theory of rationality rather than a dressed-up version of pessimism. For each, add a short follow-up keyed to the deflection she's most likely to try.
