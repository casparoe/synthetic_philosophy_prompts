I cover science and ideas for a living, and I'm working on a piece about the intellectual pedigree of Robin Hanson's "futarchy" — his proposal that citizens vote on which welfare metric a government should maximize, then let conditional prediction markets decide which policy will actually maximize it, a scheme he sums up as "vote on values, but bet on beliefs." It strikes me that the scheme's two halves land squarely on the two results the Marquis de Condorcet is best remembered for: his Jury Theorem, which shows that majorities of independent, only moderately competent voters converge reliably on the truth as a group grows larger, and the Condorcet paradox, which shows that majority rule over preferences can cycle indefinitely with no stable outcome at all. Suppose Condorcet could examine a real, functioning futarchy market today. Would he see betting markets as an advance on jury-style aggregation for the "beliefs" half, given that traders watch prices and each other rather than judging independently, and that who bothers to trade is self-selected rather than random? And would he conclude that the "values" half has genuinely escaped his paradox, or simply relocated it into the choice of metric and market design? Please ground your answer in Condorcet's actual arguments about probability, reason, and enlightened self-government, rather than a generic gloss on his optimism about progress.
