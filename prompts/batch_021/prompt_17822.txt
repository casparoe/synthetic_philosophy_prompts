I spent about forty years as a physicist before I retired a few years back (mostly condensed matter, nothing philosophical about it at the time), and lately I've been working my way through Popper for fun. Falsifiability didn't really surprise me -- that's basically how you're trained to think about a hypothesis in the lab anyway. But then I hit his stuff on "verisimilitude" and it stopped me in my tracks a bit. The idea, as I understand it, is that even though a theory is false, it can still be "closer to the truth" than some other false theory, which seems obviously right to me as a physicist -- Newtonian mechanics is technically false but its clearly more true than whatever Aristotle had going, and that kind of comparison seems to matter a lot for how we tell the story of scientific progress. Popper tried to make this precise using what he called a theory's "truth content" and "falsity content" (roughly, the true and false statements it logically implies), and said theory A has more verisimilitude than theory B when A's truth content contains B's and A's falsity content is contained in B's, with the inclusion being strict somewhere.

Here's the bit that's been bugging me. I gather that Tichy and Miller each independently proved, back in the 70s, that on Popper's actual definition this basically can't ever happen for two false theories -- one false theory can never come out more verisimilar than another, no matter how obvious it seems to ordinary intuition that one is closer to the mark. I mentioned this to an old colleague of mine (a chemist, so no more of a philosopher than I am) and he agreed it sounded like it should be a real problem for comparing rival false theories of, say, chemical bonding, but neither of us could picture why the proof actually works. Every summary I've found just asserts the result rather than showing it. So what I'm hoping you can do is build me a concrete, small example -- a toy one is completely fine, doesn't need to be real science, just two clearly false "theories" or predictions about some simple made-up situation -- where any ordinary person would say one is obviously closer to the truth than the other, and then actually walk through the truth-content/falsity-content bookkeeping so I can see exactly where Popper's official definition fails to give the answer intuition gives, rather than just being told that it does.
