My employer recently restructured its retirement plan, and everyone in my position now has until the end of next month to make an irreversible choice: keep the traditional defined-benefit pension, which guarantees a fixed monthly annuity for life, or accept a lump-sum buyout that we would have to invest and manage ourselves from then on. The financial planner our HR department brought in to advise us was quite direct about the arithmetic: under any reasonable set of assumptions about future market returns, the expected value of the buyout clearly exceeds that of the annuity, and my persistent preference for the guaranteed payment is, in her words, simply "leaving money on the table" out of risk aversion. Wanting to know whether that instinct was really just a bias I should train myself out of before the deadline, I have spent the last few weeks reading about decision theory under risk, which is how I first came across Lara Buchak's work on risk-weighted expected utility.

As I understand it, Buchak developed this theory largely in response to the Allais paradox, to argue that preferences violating the independence axiom of ordinary expected utility theory are not thereby irrational; her risk function is meant to capture a rational, global attitude toward risk that a person's utility function for money alone cannot represent. When I described my situation to a friend finishing a philosophy PhD, she agreed this sounded like exactly the right tool for the job. That has only reinforced my sense that ordinary expected utility theory is the framework in which my preference for the annuity counts as an error to be corrected, and that appealing to something like risk-weighted expected utility is precisely what would vindicate sticking with the guaranteed annuity as rational rather than as a costly bias. Before I commit to a decision I cannot undo, I would like to understand whether that is really the right way to put Buchak's theory to work here, and, if it is not, what, within these two frameworks, would actually be required to show that my preference is rational rather than a mistake.
