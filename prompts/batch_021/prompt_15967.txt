Earlier this year, a consumer DNA test led me to an online registry for donor-conceived siblings, where I found dozens of half-siblings I never knew I had, all conceived with the same sperm donor my parents used decades ago; the registry's own figures suggest the true number could be considerably higher still. Trying to work out how surprised I should be by this, I've fallen into the dispute between the Self-Sampling Assumption (SSA) and the Self-Indication Assumption (SIA) in anthropic reasoning, and Bostrom's presumptuous philosopher case against the latter. It strikes me that the inference "I exist as one of this donor's children, so he probably fathered a great many" has exactly the structure of the presumptuous philosopher's move from his own existence to the higher-population cosmology, which makes me suspicious of it — yet I can't find a principled reason to prefer SSA instead, given how much its verdicts depend on an arbitrarily chosen reference class. Write an essay that lays out the strongest arguments for and against SSA and SIA as general norms for updating on one's own existence, and that treats the presumptuous philosopher as a genuine cost of SIA rather than a debater's trick. Reach a specific, defended verdict on which principle — or which qualified version of one — is actually correct, rather than retreating to an even-handed non-answer, and close by stating plainly, on your preferred view, whether the mere fact of my being one of this donor's children should have shifted my prior toward "prolific donor" even before I consulted the registry.
