Question set for the students: "Explain Alison Hills's distinction between moral knowledge and moral understanding, and how she uses it to argue for an asymmetry between deferring to testimony on moral questions versus non-moral ones. Is her argument convincing?"

Here's one student's answer, word for word:

"Hills argues that there's an important difference between merely knowing that an action is right or wrong and understanding why it is right or wrong. Moral knowledge is just having a true, justified belief about a moral fact - e.g. knowing that lying to your friend was wrong. Moral understanding is deeper: it means grasping the reasons that make the act wrong, being able to apply that reasoning to new cases, and seeing how it fits with your other moral beliefs. Hills thinks testimony is a perfectly good way to acquire moral knowledge - if a trusted friend tells you a business practice is exploitative, you can come to know it's wrong on the strength of their word, just as you could come to know a fact in chemistry by taking a chemist's word for it. But she denies testimony can give you moral understanding, since understanding has to be worked out for yourself; you can't just be handed it.

This grounds an asymmetry between moral and non-moral testimony. If you take a doctor's word that a drug will cure you and act on it, there's nothing deficient in what you did - knowledge was all you needed. But if you do the right thing only because someone told you it was right, without understanding why, Hills thinks your action is missing something, since acting well requires more than getting the right answer, it requires being sensitive to the reasons yourself. McGrath develops this point further, arguing that testimony-based moral action is defective because it bypasses the agent's own moral sensitivity and treats moral questions as if they had answers you could just look up, like facts in chemistry.

I think Hills is largely right that understanding needs more than testimony can give you, but I'm not sure the asymmetry is as sharp as she makes out, since deferring to an expert on a big non-moral practical decision, like whether to have a risky surgery, can also feel like its missing something if you don't understand your own reasons for consenting."
