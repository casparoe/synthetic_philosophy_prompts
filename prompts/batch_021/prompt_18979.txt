I'm a PhD student in philosophy of religion, and this argument broke out during my department's reading group on providence and free will, right after we'd worked through the relevant sections of Molina's Concordia alongside some of the secondary literature on middle knowledge. Two of the other regulars, Sofia and Marcus, got into a real back-and-forth about the grounding objection, and since I was running the session I stayed out of it, but I've been turning it over since and honestly can't tell who came out ahead.

Sofia opened by pressing the standard grounding objection: counterfactuals of creaturely freedom (CCFs) like "if Judas were placed in circumstances C, he would freely betray Christ" are supposed to have their truth values fixed logically prior to God's decree to create anything at all, but at that point nothing exists to make them true -- no actual Judas, no actual C, nothing. Marcus answered that the same demand would sink truths most of us accept without a second thought: on presentism, truths about the remote past have no present truthmaker either, since the past no longer exists, yet nobody concludes that all such truths are unfounded -- so either the objection proves too much, or "grounding" isn't doing the work critics think it's doing. Sofia replied that the cases aren't symmetric: a past-tense truth is at least anchored in a history that was actual, however long gone, whereas a CCF about an uninstantiated circumstance is never anchored in anything actual, at any time, since the relevant possible course of events may never occur. Marcus came back that this whole exchange presupposes every truth needs a truthmaker in the first place, which is itself a substantive and contestable thesis; a Molinist could instead be a modest primitivist about the truth-values of just this one class of subjunctives, in the same spirit as deflationists who reject truthmaker theory across the board. Sofia's comeback was that this doesn't rebut her point so much as pay for it: helping yourself to primitivism about contingent counterfactual truth, solely to rescue one doctrine from a problem it would otherwise have, is exactly the kind of ad hoc move that should count against a theory, whatever you call it.

Could you go through this move by move -- Sofia's opening objection, Marcus's presentism analogy, Sofia's asymmetry reply, Marcus's primitivism move, and Sofia's ad hoc charge -- and say who has the better of it at each step, rather than just handing down an overall winner? I'd also be curious whether there's a stronger version of either position that neither of them actually put on the table.
