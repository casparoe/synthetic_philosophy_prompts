Here's the student's answer, verbatim: "The sceptic asks what fact about my past use of '+' makes it the case that I meant addition rather than quaddition, where x quus y = x+y for x,y<57 and =5 otherwise. Since I've only ever performed finitely many additions, all of them fall inside the range where plus and quus agree, so the evidence available to me is compatible with either interpretation and I can never really know for sure which function I meant. Appealing to dispositions doesn't rescue us: my dispositions are also finite (ask me about numbers large enough and I'll die of old age before answering), and in any case dispositions can't capture the normative fact that I ought to answer 125 rather than something else — a disposition just describes what I would do, mistakes included. Kripke's sceptical solution grants that there's no fact of the matter and reframes meaning-talk around assertability conditions: I count as meaning plus by '+' as long as my answers keep agreeing with the rest of the community, and it's this agreement that licenses people to trust my arithmetic, correct me when I slip up, hire me as a cashier, etc. So in the end what I mean by '+' just is whatever the community would say, which is also why an isolated Robinson Crusoe can't mean anything by a private '+' — there's no one around for him to agree or disagree with. I'd say this is a genuine solution rather than a dodge: it delivers everything we actually wanted from a theory of meaning (predicting and justifying our practices of correction) without any spooky extra fact, so the paradox dissolves rather than lingers."
