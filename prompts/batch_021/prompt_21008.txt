I'm several drafts into a novel set in London between 1930 and 1933, centred on a marriage between two minor figures on the fringes of the Bloomsbury set who have each adopted, with rather different degrees of real conviction, the "new morality" that was being argued over so publicly at the time. To get the actual grain of those arguments right, rather than the flattened modern shorthand of "free love," I've been working through Bertrand Russell's *Marriage and Morals* (1929), a battered copy of which my more intellectually serious character keeps on her bedside table and produces whenever her sister-in-law starts in on the sanctity of the home. I want this character to be able to restate Russell's reasoning accurately and in her own idiom during an argument scene, rather than just waving at his conclusions, and there's one passage, from the chapter on patriarchal systems, that I've now read several times without feeling I've actually got hold of how it works as an argument.

The passage runs: "The purely instinctive element in jealousy is not nearly so strong as most moderns imagine. The extreme strength of jealousy in patriarchal societies is due to the fear of falsification of descent. This may be seen in the fact that a man who is tired of his wife and passionately devoted to his mistress will nevertheless be more jealous where his wife is concerned than when he finds a rival to the affections of his mistress. A legitimate child is a continuation of a man's ego, and his affection for the child is a form of egoism. If, on the other hand, the child is not legitimate, the putative father is tricked into lavishing care upon a child with whom he has no biological connection. Hence the discovery of fatherhood led to the subjection of women as the only means of securing their virtue—a subjection first physical and then mental, which reached its height in the Victorian age." I can follow each sentence on its own, but I can't reconstruct how they are meant to lock together into a single line of reasoning. The wife-and-mistress example seems, on the face of it, to cut against the ordinary assumption that jealousy simply tracks love, since the man in it loves the mistress and not the wife, yet is more jealous over the wife; and then the remark about "ego" and "egoism" arrives without much explanation of how it bears on the example just given. Could you walk me through how Russell means the illustration to secure his opening claim that jealousy is not truly instinctive, how the ego/egoism sentence is doing real argumentative work rather than serving as a rhetorical flourish, and, above all, what is actually licensing that "Hence"—how the whole chain is supposed to add up to the conclusion that the discovery of paternity, specifically, is what drove the subjection of women? I would like my character's paraphrase to be something a careful reader of Russell would recognise as fair, rather than a plausible-sounding gloss that quietly skips the hardest step.
