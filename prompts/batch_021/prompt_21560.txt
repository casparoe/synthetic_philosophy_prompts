Question (20 points). The fitting-attitude ("buck-passing") analysis holds that for an object to be good just is for it to have natural properties that provide reasons to adopt a pro-attitude—to desire, admire, or favor—toward it. Using the well-known "evil demon" case, explain the "wrong kind of reasons" problem this analysis faces, and briefly describe one strategy that has been proposed for handling it.

Student answer:

According to the buck-passing analysis defended by Scanlon, to call something good is just to say that it has properties that give us reasons to have a pro-attitude toward it, such as desiring or admiring it; goodness itself drops out as a separate property and is analyzed away in favor of reasons. The trouble is that not every reason to desire something seems to track its value. Kavka's well-known demon case makes this vivid: an evil demon will torture you unless you come to desire a perfectly ordinary saucer of mud. You now have a compelling reason to desire the mud, yet nobody thinks this makes the mud good. Philosophers describe this as an object-given reason, since the demon's threat is still about a particular object—the mud—so the target of our desire has not changed even though our motive for desiring it has. This is what makes the case so troubling for the buck-passer: if any reason to favor an object counts, the mud comes out good, which is absurd.

One influential response, associated with the buck-passers themselves, is to restrict the reasons that count to those grounded in the intrinsic, evaluatively relevant properties of the object—the mud's texture, composition, and so on—rather than in the consequences of holding the attitude, such as avoiding torture. This preserves the spirit of the analysis while excluding demon-style cases, though critics have worried that specifying which properties are "evaluatively relevant" without smuggling in the concept of goodness is not obviously possible.
