Last week, a friend of mine who spends a great deal of time reading about negotiation theory and, more recently, AI strategy, drew me into a long argument that we never quite managed to resolve, and I would like your help settling it. He was extending an idea he attributes to Thomas Schelling: that in any bargaining situation, the side that manages to bind itself irrevocably to a position first effectively wins, since a rational counterpart has no real choice but to adapt to whatever fait accompli has already been created. He is fond of the classic illustrations of this point—burning one's own boats before a battle so that retreat is no longer an option, a general who disables his own capacity to retreat in order to make an attack credible, a "doomsday" retaliation device that removes any option of backing down from a threat—and from these he draws a general policy recommendation: any agent that expects to negotiate with others in the future, whether a person, a firm, a state, or, his favorite case, a future AI system facing another AI system, should try to commit to its preferred terms as early, as publicly, and as irreversibly as it possibly can, on the grounds that the earliest and most binding commitment simply wins.

When I objected that this logic seems to quietly assume that the other side is not doing exactly the same thing at exactly the same time, he conceded that a race to commit first was possible in principle, but waved it away as a minor complication rather than a genuine problem for his general rule. I did not have a firm enough grip on the underlying game theory to explain why that dismissal struck me as too quick, and we left the disagreement unresolved. Could you help me work out, in reasonably precise terms, what actually happens once more than one party can play the "commit earliest and hardest" strategy at the same time, and whether there is a more defensible version of Schelling's insight about the strategic value of commitment that survives once that possibility is taken seriously?
