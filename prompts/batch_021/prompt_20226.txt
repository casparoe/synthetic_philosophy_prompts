During the discussion section for a graduate seminar on philosophy of mind, two of my classmates, Talia and Devon, became locked in a disagreement about the conceivability argument that none of the rest of us felt qualified to settle. It began with (1) Talia restating the core argument from Chalmers: a molecule-for-molecule physical duplicate of a conscious person who nonetheless lacks any phenomenal experience whatsoever seems ideally conceivable, and since ideal conceivability is our best guide to metaphysical possibility, such "zombie worlds" are possible; but if they're possible, the physical facts alone never necessitate the phenomenal facts, so physicalism about the mind is false. (2) Devon objected that this proves too much, invoking Kripke's point that conceivability can mislead us whenever a posteriori necessities are in play: before the relevant chemistry was known, "water is not H2O" also seemed perfectly conceivable, yet water is necessarily H2O, so a physicalist can likewise hold that some yet-unspecified psychophysical identity is a necessary truth we simply don't grasp a priori, and that the seeming conceivability of zombies is exactly the same kind of illusion.

Talia's response was (3) to invoke Chalmers's own two-dimensionalist rejoinder: the water case only works as a counterexample because "water" has a primary intension that different substances could satisfy depending on how the actual world turns out, whereas phenomenal concepts, on her view, refer directly to experience rather than via any such contingent, world-dependent description, so their primary and secondary intensions coincide and no Kripke-style "twist" is available to the physicalist here. (4) Devon countered with the phenomenal concept strategy, granting that our phenomenal concepts really are cognitively special — direct and recognitional rather than descriptive — but insisting that this special cognitive profile can itself be given an entirely physical explanation, one that would predict exactly the illusion of a conceivable zombie world without there needing to be any further, non-physical fact behind it. Talia closed the exchange with (5) a dilemma: either that cognitive profile is itself something derivable a priori from the physical facts, in which case the same reasoning should let us derive the phenomenal facts too and the explanatory gap ought to close on reflection (but it stubbornly doesn't), or it can't be so derived, in which case physicalism hasn't actually been secured for the mental, only relocated into an unexplained fact about concepts.
