I recently started structuring lending products for a decentralized-finance (DeFi) protocol, and at a family dinner my great-uncle, a retired classics teacher, delighted in reminding me that Aristotle called lending at interest the most unnatural way of acquiring wealth, since money exists only to facilitate exchange and ought not to "breed" more of itself -- he even had the etymological jab ready that tokos, the Greek word for interest, literally means offspring. Trying to construct a reply, I went back to Aristotle's own texts and found that this seemingly tidy verdict sits awkwardly next to a far less settled discussion of money in Nicomachean Ethics V.5: the claim that money stands in for chreia, or need, letting radically dissimilar goods -- Aristotle's example is that five beds equal one house -- count as commensurable, even though he explicitly denies that such goods are really commensurable at all and treats the equation as no more than a workable convention. Some readers, Marx among them, take this as Aristotle straining toward, but lacking the resources to name, some genuine common substance underlying value; others take chreia to show that Aristotle already held a basically subjective, demand-driven theory of value that needs no further common substance at all.

Write a dialogue between two present-day interpreters of the Ethics passage, one defending each reading. They should be evenly matched, engage closely with each other's textual evidence rather than talking past one another, and work out together what their disagreement implies for the usury argument: on a purely demand-driven theory of value, is Aristotle's case against interest simply confused, or does an objective theory of value fare any better at explaining what exactly is wrong with money "breeding"? Let the dialogue end with each side's strongest objection still standing, rather than a tidy resolution.
