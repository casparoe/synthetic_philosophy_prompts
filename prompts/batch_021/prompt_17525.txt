A friend and I spent most of a long train ride arguing about something that started as a throwaway comment in her philosophy of mind seminar. I had just encountered McCarthy and Hayes's original "frame problem" in my AI course: the difficulty, within a logic-based planner, of specifying which facts remain true after an action without writing an explicit "frame axiom" for every single fact that the action leaves unchanged -- a problem that, as I understood it, has at least partial technical fixes, such as Reiter's successor-state axioms or McCarthy's own circumscription. My friend insisted this isn't really the interesting frame problem at all, and pointed me to Dennett's essay "Cognitive Wheels," where his robots R1, R1D1, and R2D1 each fail in turn -- not because nobody told them where the bomb was, but because they either fail to notice the side-effects of their own actions or get stuck working out which of their countless beliefs are even relevant to the task at hand. Her claim was that this is the real, much deeper frame problem: a general epistemological puzzle about bounded relevance-detection facing any finite reasoner, and that the logic-notation version I'd learned is at best a narrow special case of it.

I pushed back that this looked like a bait-and-switch: McCarthy and Hayes posed a specific, comparatively tractable representational problem, and relabeling every hard question about relevance and common sense with the same name doesn't show they're the same problem -- it just borrows the original problem's air of solvability for a much harder, arguably open-ended one. She thought that objection had things backwards: treating the two as unrelated is exactly what misses the point, since the representational problem only ever looked tractable because it quietly assumed away the very relevance question Dennett is pointing at. Neither of us budged, and we ran out of train before we ran out of argument.

Could you help settle this? Please compare the original, technical frame problem from formal AI with Dennett's broader epistemological frame problem. I'd like you to say specifically in what ways the McCarthy-Hayes version is a genuinely special, more tractable case, and in what ways, if any, my friend is right that it is ultimately just a symptom of the deeper problem Dennett describes. Please commit to an actual verdict on whether Dennett's generalization is a legitimate philosophical move or one that subtly conflates two different problems, rather than simply laying out both sides.
