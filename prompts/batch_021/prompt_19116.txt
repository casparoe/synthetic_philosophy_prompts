I've been reporting a feature on why some social conventions can flip almost overnight—tipping norms, forms of address, how we greet each other post-pandemic—while others stay locked in place for centuries even when they look arbitrary or mildly costly to the people who follow them. Chasing down the philosophical background has led me to two accounts of convention that seem to pull in genuinely different directions, and before my next interview I'd like to understand precisely how they relate.

The first is David Lewis's analysis in Convention: A Philosophical Study, on which a convention is a regularity in behavior that solves a recurring coordination problem and persists because it is common knowledge within the population that everyone conforms, that everyone expects everyone else to conform, and that everyone prefers to conform given that the rest do. For Lewis, that common-knowledge condition is doing real work: it is supposed to be what turns a bare behavioral regularity into something people can be said to follow, rather than merely instantiate. The second is Brian Skyrms's evolutionary game-theoretic treatment, which takes some of the very same sender-receiver signaling games Lewis used to model linguistic convention and shows how a stable signaling system can emerge instead through replicator dynamics or simple reinforcement learning, with no individual agent ever representing the coordination problem, let alone attaining common knowledge of how the others will act.

Could you compare these two accounts for me in some depth? I'm especially interested in whether Skyrms's evolutionary story actually undercuts Lewis's claim that common knowledge is a necessary condition on convention, or whether it is better read as describing a related but distinct phenomenon—a behavioral regularity that only counts as a convention in some thinner sense than Lewis intended. I'd also like your own considered judgment rather than a neutral summary of both sides: which picture does the better explanatory work on real human norms that persist despite being arbitrary or inefficient—deference norms, spelling conventions, and the like—and is there a class of cases (say, a one-off ad hoc agreement versus a slowly settled cultural practice) where one framework is clearly the right tool and the other simply fails to apply?
