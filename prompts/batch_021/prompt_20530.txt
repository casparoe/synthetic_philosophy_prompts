I'm writing a long-form piece for our science desk on the hard limits of human knowledge — the kind of feature that moves from Heisenberg's uncertainty principle through Gödel's incompleteness theorems and, my editor insists, ought to culminate in Fitch's paradox of knowability as the philosophical capstone. As I understand it, Fitch's 1963 proof does for epistemology what Gödel did for arithmetic: in a few lines of elementary modal logic, it guarantees that at least one truth must forever elude any knower, however idealized, and it's widely credited with having delivered a fatal blow to verificationist and anti-realist theories that try to define truth in terms of what we could, in principle, come to know.

What I need for the piece is something readers can actually picture. Could you help me settle on a good concrete example of the sort of fact that Fitch's argument shows must be permanently unknowable — the kind of thing I could set alongside "events beyond the cosmological horizon" or "the exact position and momentum of a particle" as an illustration of a hard epistemic limit? I'd also like a tight paragraph I can adapt explaining why, given that the proof has been sitting there since 1963, serious philosophers still defend verificationism or anti-realism about truth at all, rather than conceding that the position was refuted in a single afternoon of formal logic.
