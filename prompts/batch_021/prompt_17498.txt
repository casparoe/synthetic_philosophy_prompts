I'm putting together the relations-and-facts unit for my grad metaphysics seminar, and I had a long back-and-forth with a colleague over coffee last week about how seriously we should still take Bradley's regress -- neither of us fully convinced the other, so I want an actual essay out of you rather than a quick verdict. Quick refresher in case its been a while since you looked at this: Bradley's argument (from Appearance and Reality) is that if a relation R genuinely relates two terms A and B, it can't just sit there next to them like a third item in a list -- it has to actually connect to each of them, and that connecting itself looks like it needs a further relation, which needs its own, and so on forever. He ran this together with a companion worry about how qualities could ever be individuated without relations to tell them apart, and how relations could relate anything without already-distinct qualities to relate -- and used the whole package to argue that the ordinary "terms-plus-relation" picture of a fact is incoherent, which became one of his main levers toward monism (only the Absolute is fully real, since any relational structure short of that dissolves once you press on it). People have tried plenty of things to block the regress since: Russell's early distinction between a relation occurring "as a term" versus occurring "as actually relating," Frege's idea that concepts are inherently unsaturated so no external glue is needed, Armstrong's frank primitivism about the unity of states of affairs, and various more recent moves (Kit Fine and Jeffrey King have both said interesting things in the neighborhood, from pretty different angles).

So here's the actual assignment: write me a real essay, not a survey. Explain why the regress is supposed to be vicious rather than just an infinite-but-harmless structure, engage properly with at least two of the proposed stoppers above (bring in others if you know them), and say concretely where each one succeeds or fails. Then commit to a verdict instead of hiding behind "there are considerations on both sides" -- something in the spirit of "the regress is genuinely vicious, primitivism about unity is the least costly way out, and this falls well short of vindicating Bradley's monism" would count as landing somewhere, and so would the opposite conclusion, as long as you actually argue for it. Bonus if you address whether the unity-of-the-proposition problem (the Russell/Frege/King version, about how a sentence's meaning hangs together rather than how a worldly fact does) is really the same puzzle underneath or a genuinely separate one -- I've never settled that one myself.
