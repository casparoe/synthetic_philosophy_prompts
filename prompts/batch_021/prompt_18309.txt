I am nearing the end of a near-future novel set inside an AI research institute, and I've reached a scene I've been putting off: a closed-door hearing at which Iris, the institute's most capable system, must persuade its oversight board not to approve a self-rewrite she has proposed, on her own assurance that it is safe. I want her argument to turn on the genuine philosophical trouble with embedded agency: that a system built out of the same stuff as the world it is trying to model cannot occupy the external, "Cartesian" vantage point that classical decision theory quietly assumes; cannot hold, inside itself, a model that accurately captures a reality which includes itself (the grain-of-truth problem); and cannot extend proof-based trust to a future or successor version of itself without running into the so-called Löbian obstacle. Half the board are alignment researchers who will notice if she smooths over the hard part; the other half are lay trustees who need it in plain terms and need to leave the room having endorsed one concrete safeguard, not a vague reassurance. Write Iris's speech: around 500 words, first person, meant to be spoken aloud, that earns its conclusion for both audiences without letting her be secretly untrustworthy, or secretly all-seeing, as a way out of the problem.
