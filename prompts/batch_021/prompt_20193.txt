Me and my housemate got way too deep into anthropic reasoning last night (yes, that's a normal Tuesday for us) after she brought up the Doomsday argument to freak me out about humanity going extinct sooner than we'd like to think. I said the whole thing leans on the self-sampling assumption, which I've never fully trusted, and that I actually lean self-indication assumption. She hit me with the classic presumptuous philosopher case -- the one (I think it's originally Bostrom's?) where two physical theories are equally well supported by the evidence so far, but one implies a way bigger population of observers, and some smug philosopher declares that theory true from his armchair before anyone even runs the experiment, purely because SIA weights hypotheses by how many observers they contain. She said that alone is reason enough to ditch SIA and stick with SSA, and honestly I didn't have a great comeback in the moment.

Its been bugging me since though, because doesn't the doomsday argument pull the exact same move, just aimed in the other direction? It also takes a purely self-locating fact -- my birth rank among however many humans will ever exist -- and cranks out a confident, substantive conclusion about the world (that there won't be some huge number of future humans) without looking at any actual evidence about extinction risk. So what I want is for you to build that case properly, not a "both sides have a point" cop-out: argue for the position that the presumptuous philosopher gives no special reason to favor SSA over SIA, since SSA's own flagship result, the doomsday argument, is guilty of the exact same move the philosopher is being mocked for. If it helps make the parallel vivid, feel free to invent a toy "presumptuous demographer" scenario that mirrors the original case but runs on SSA instead of SIA. It'd also be great if you dug into how the reference class gets picked along the way, since I've got a hunch that's where both sides find room to wriggle out of the objection.
