I have no formal training in philosophy, but I have spent a long time working through Hume's essay "Of Miracles" (Section X of the first Enquiry) on my own, mostly because it keeps coming up in religious-skeptic debates I follow online. I have become convinced that the popular one-line summary of the argument — a wise person proportions belief to the evidence, and testimony for a miracle can never outweigh our uniform experience of nature's laws — papers over a real tension in the text. Below are my notes toward an essay making that case, and I would like your comments before I try to turn it into something more finished.

As I read it, the essay runs two different arguments together. The first, in Part I, is close to definitional, an "in-principle" argument: a law of nature just is what uniform, exceptionless experience establishes, so that experience counts as a full "proof" against any reported violation, and the best any contrary testimony can do is produce a stand-off between two proofs that simply cancels out. Nothing about any actual miracle report needs to be examined for this argument to reach its conclusion. The second, in Part II, is an "in-practice," empirical argument: Hume gives four separate, contingent reasons why no reported miracle has in fact come with testimony good enough to believe — never enough witnesses of the right character and standing, our natural "passion of surprise and wonder," the fact that such reports cluster among uneducated peoples and credulous ages, and the way rival religions' miracles cancel each other out as evidence.

My worry is that these two arguments pull in different directions, and that Hume's own footnote about the reported cures and convulsions at the tomb of the Jansenist deacon François de Pâris — in Paris, in the early 1730s, if I have the history right — shows the seam. I think he raises the case precisely because it is awkward for him: the testimony came from a large number of witnesses, some of real social standing, gathered in a sophisticated city, examined by hostile observers who had every reason to discredit it and could not. By his own Part II standards, this looks about as good as miracle testimony gets, and yet he treats it as obviously not to be believed. That leaves two uncomfortable options: either the in-principle argument was doing all the real work and the in-practice criteria were never independent tests of anything, or there is some further criterion the Paris case fails that Hume never states, and I cannot see what it could be that would not also threaten ordinary historical testimony he is happy to accept elsewhere.

I gather, secondhand, from summaries rather than the original, that John Earman argued in a book that Hume's argument, once reconstructed in properly probabilistic terms, does not actually establish the strong in-principle conclusion he is usually read as wanting, and that Peter Millican has tried to defend a more charitable reconstruction against that charge. I cannot tell whether my Abbé Paris worry is just that dispute restated crudely, an old objection with a known answer, or a misreading of what the footnote is actually doing.

Could you comment specifically on: (1) whether my two-argument split is a fair, standard reading of Section X rather than something I have imposed on it; (2) whether the Abbé Paris footnote can really bear the weight I am putting on it, or whether I am misunderstanding its role in the essay; and (3) whether the tension I think I have found is genuine, and if so, whether it has already been resolved in the Earman–Millican exchange or elsewhere.
