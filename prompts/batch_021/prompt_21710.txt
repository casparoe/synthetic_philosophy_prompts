At work we had a whole debate blow up over something that seemed like a dumb implementation detail and somehow turned into an actual metaphysics argument (nobody called it that at the time). We run a document management system for a compliance-heavy client, and our storage layer dedupes files by content hash to save space -- if two uploaded PDFs are byte-for-byte identical, we store one blob and just point two records at it. A junior dev on my team argued this is obviously fine because "if they're identical there's no meaningful difference between them," but our compliance lead pushed back hard: two uploads from two different custodians on two different dates, even with identical bytes, are NOT the same document for audit purposes, and silently treating them as one looses information the law cares about. I sided with the compliance lead but couldn't really articulate why beyond "it just feels wrong," and after that a friend who did a philosophy MA told me this is basically a real-world flavor of the identity of indiscernibles debate, and pointed me at Max Black's old thought experiment about two iron spheres floating alone in an otherwise empty, symmetric universe.

I read up on it a bit over the weekend (enough to get that Black is arguing against Leibniz's principle, and that theres some genuinely tricky back-and-forth about whether relational properties like "being a mile from the other sphere" can individuate the two spheres, or whether that move secretly cheats by assuming they're already distinguishable), but I have zero formal philosophy training and I'm positive I'm missing the good parts. Could you walk me through the most important implications of this debate -- Black's spheres specifically, not just the "no two things share every property" slogan version -- for how I should think about identity and "sameness" in systems like ours? I'm especially curious whether the debate actually hands the compliance lead any real ammunition, or whether philosophers are basically answering a totally different question than the one our argument was about, and our engineering intuitions just don't map onto it as cleanly as my friend made it sound.
