Two people in a decision-theory group I'm part of got into it over a coin-flipping game someone brought up, apparently a real thought experiment called the Pasadena paradox. Rules: flip a fair coin till it first comes up heads; if that happens on flip n, you get paid (-1)^(n+1) * 2^n / n dollars -- so +$2 if heads on the very first flip, -$2 if it takes two, growing in size and flipping sign forever after that. (1) He summed the payoffs in the obvious order, n = 1, 2, 3..., got the alternating harmonic series, which converges to ln 2, and said the games worth about 69 cents to play. (2) She said thats cheating -- the series of absolute payoffs is just the harmonic series, which diverges, so the sum only converges "conditionally," and she half-remembered from calc that a conditionally convergent series can be reordered to add up to literally anything, or blow up to infinity, or never settle at all -- so ln 2 isn't THE value of the game, just an artifact of one arbitrary ordering choice. (3) He said the order by n isn't arbitrary, its the order the flips actually happen in, and defining a value as the limit of partial sums in that order is exactly what we do for any infinite process -- no different from a Riemann sum for an integral. (4) She said the order flips happen in is a fact about the coin, not about the probability distribution, which is what an expectation is supposed to summarize -- and added that even playing the game a million times and averaging your winnings wouldn't reliably drift toward 69 cents either, since infinite expected absolute payoff is exactly the condition under which long-run averages are known to never settle down. (5) He said if her standard kills the expected value here, it kills the expected value of a ton of less exotic-looking gambles too, so maybe the moral is "patch the theory for weird cases," not "this game truly has no value."

Go through their exchange move by move and say who's right (and who's missing something) at each step, then give your own final take on whether the game actually has a well defined expected value.
