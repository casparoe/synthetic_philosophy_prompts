Question (15 pts): The "Löbian obstacle" is often cited as the reason a formal agent can't fully trust a successor (or a future version of itself) that reasons the same way it does. (a) State Löb's theorem and explain how it creates a problem for an agent that wants to adopt the policy "take action A whenever I can prove that A leads to good outcomes." (b) How does Löb's theorem relate to Gödel's Second Incompleteness Theorem? (c) Describe one strategy from the self-modifying-AI literature meant to get around the obstacle, and note a limitation it doesn't fully overcome.

Student's answer:

(a) Löb's theorem says that for a theory T with its own provability predicate Prov_T, if T proves "Prov_T(P) → P" for some sentence P, then T already proves P outright - a theory can't get away with promising in advance "whatever I prove about P will turn out true" unless it's already got P nailed down. This is bad news for an agent that wants to formalize "take action A whenever I can prove Good(A)": if that policy is backed by a general schema like Prov(Good(A)) → Good(A) for every A, Löb's theorem says the agent's own proof system would have to already prove Good(A) for literally every A on the spot. Since it obviously can't do that in advance for every possible action, the schema doesn't buy the agent any real assurance - it either does nothing (for actions already provably good) or, if pushed through anyway, threatens the soundness of the whole system.

(b) This is really just Gödel's Second Incompleteness Theorem wearing a different hat - both are formal versions of the fact that a sufficiently strong consistent theory can't certify its own trustworthiness. Gödel's version says PA can't prove Con(PA); Löb's theorem is the provability-logic form of that same result, which is why people cite Gödel's theorem and Löb's theorem more or less interchangeably when explaining why a proof-based agent can't fully trust its own future verdicts.

(c) One proposed fix (the "tiling agents" approach) is to stop asking the agent to trust an exact copy of itself and instead have each successor run on a proof system one rung down an ordinal ladder from its predecessor - the current agent can prove a reflection principle about the next one because the next one is strictly weaker, and Löb's theorem only stops a theory from vouching for itself or something at least as strong. Since you can always step down to a smaller ordinal, the lineage of successors can keep doing this indefinitely, so the whole chain of self-modification ends up licensed by genuine proof rather than a leap of faith at any point.
