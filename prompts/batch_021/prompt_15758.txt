For years I've noticed something that bothered me: if a tyrant threatens to make my life miserable unless I come to genuinely admire him, I now have every reason in the world to admire him, yet he hasn't become one bit more admirable for it. I recently learned that philosophers have a name for this -- the 'wrong kind of reasons' problem -- and that it's a serious difficulty for so-called fitting-attitude or 'buck-passing' theories of value, which try to explain what makes something good, admirable, or desirable in terms of there being reasons to want, admire, or otherwise favor it. The trouble, apparently, is saying what separates these 'wrong kind' reasons from ordinary ones -- something to do with whether a reason is grounded in the object itself or merely in the state of holding the attitude toward it -- without quietly presupposing the very notion of value such theories were meant to explain. Could you walk me through why that distinction has turned out to be so hard to draw cleanly, using the actual proposals philosophers have made, and tell me whether any of them really escape the circularity, or whether the whole project is just stuck?
