I'm running a graduate seminar on philosophy of psychiatry this term, and a couple of weeks ago we spent a whole session on Hacking's work on looping effects -- the "looping effects of human kinds" piece, plus the relevant bits of Rewriting the Soul and Mad Travelers. Afterwards, one of my sharper students cornered me after class arguing that looping effects aren't really a matter of degree -- either a category loops (the classification and the classified population visibly co-evolve through feedback) or it doesn't, full stop. I definately think that's too tidy, and that some of Hacking's own examples are much more clear-cut cases of looping than others, so I want to build a little ranking exercise to bring to next week's seminar and settle this properly (or at least generate a good argument).

So here's what I'd like from you: take the following eight categories and sort them into tiers by how strongly and clearly they exhibit a genuine Hacking-style looping effect historically, where "genuine" looping means the act of classifying visibly changed the self-understanding/behaviour of the classified population in a way that then fed back into revising the classifcation itself (rather than the category just picking out some pre-existing, static kind). The categories: dissociative identity disorder (multiple personality), the 19th-century fugueur/"mad traveler" diagnosis, autism, ADHD, homosexuality as a pre-1973 psychiatric category, PTSD, antisocial personality disorder/psychopathy, and premenstrual dysphoric disorder.

Please give me the answer as a JSON block with tiers from S (clearest, most textbook cases of looping) down to C (weak or contested cases -- ones that look superficially loopy but that you think don't really fit Hacking's mechanism), like this:

```json
{
  "S": ["category - one-line reason", ...],
  "A": [...],
  "B": [...],
  "C": [...]
}
```

Don't feel obliged to spread the eight evenly across tiers -- if you think five of them are genuinely S-tier and three are C-tier, say so. And if you basically agree with my student that this whole tiering exercise is a category error, feel free to tell me that too, though I'd still like you to attempt the ranking regardless.
