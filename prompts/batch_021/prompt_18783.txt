(a) (10 points) Explain each philosopher's position and the central argument each gives for it. (b) (5 points) Explain the regress problem that anti-reductionists press against reductionism. Here is the student's answer, verbatim:

"Hume is the paradigm reductionist about testimony: he denies that testimony is a basic, sui generis source of justification on a par with perception or memory, and instead treats our reliance on it as itself a form of inductive inference from experience. Because we have repeatedly observed a constant conjunction between what people report and how things turn out to be, we come to expect testimony to track the truth much as we expect fire to burn, so testimonial justification is parasitic on prior perceptual and memorial evidence of witnesses' reliability. This is why Hume held, as a general rule for evaluating any piece of testimony, that no report should be believed unless its falsehood would be even more improbable than the fact it reports: testimony is just weighed evidence in a probabilistic calculus, no different in kind from any other empirical sign.

Reid rejects this picture. For Reid, testimony is an original source of knowledge, underwritten by two principles of our constitution: the principle of credulity, the speaker's built-in disposition to represent things as they are, and the principle of veracity, the hearer's complementary disposition to accept what is told to them. Because the two principles are paired by nature, a hearer is prima facie entitled to accept testimony without first mounting an inductive argument for the reliability of testimony in general, which is just as well, since any such argument would itself have to lean on testimony about other people's track records, threatening the regress or circularity that anti-reductionists say reductionism cannot obviously avoid.

Fricker tries to split the difference with local reductionism: she agrees with Reid that no single global inductive argument for the reliability of testimony in general is needed, or even available, but unlike Reid she still insists that on each particular occasion the hearer must have some local, non-testimonial grounds, such as cues to the speaker's sincerity and competence, for trusting that speaker, so acceptance is never quite the unearned default that perception is for Reid. I think this succeeds as a genuine middle position: it escapes the regress Reid presses against Hume without granting testimony the same unconditional standing Reid wants to give it.

Applied to the juror: since both witnesses are presumably competent and sincere by default, the juror has no non-testimonial grounds for preferring one over the other, and so, absent any corroborating evidence, ought to suspend judgment rather than credit either one outright."
