Over a long career as an experimental physicist, I developed a fondness for results that squeeze surprisingly strong conclusions out of a handful of innocuous-looking axioms, which is presumably why Harsanyi's aggregation theorem was the first piece of welfare economics that really grabbed me after I retired and started reading philosophy in earnest. The theorem says, roughly, that if every individual's preferences over lotteries satisfy the von Neumann-Morgenstern axioms, if society's preferences over those same lotteries satisfy the axioms too, and if society is indifferent between any two lotteries whenever every individual is, then the social utility function must be an affine combination of the individual utility functions. When I first worked through the proof with a small circle of retired colleagues who now meet monthly to read philosophy together, it struck all of us as a near-knockdown axiomatic vindication of utilitarianism, arrived at without the word "utilitarianism" ever being mentioned.

Our enthusiasm has since cooled. Amartya Sen has argued that the von Neumann-Morgenstern utilities being aggregated are, in the first instance, just numerical stand-ins for choice under risk, and that treating them as interpersonally comparable measures of welfare -- addable across people in the way utilitarianism requires -- imports an assumption the axioms themselves do nothing to license. Separately, Peter Diamond's well-known example seems to show that the theorem's innocuous Pareto-indifference condition quietly rules out any concern for fair process: when an indivisible good must go to one of two people alike in every relevant respect, a coin flip that gives each an equal chance seems fairer than simply handing the good to one of them, yet any aggregator constrained to be a weighted sum of individual utilities must be indifferent between the two policies, since both yield the same expected utility. Some responses try to rescue the theorem by folding a taste for fair procedure into the individual utility functions themselves, but it isn't obvious to me that this move is available without already conceding the main point.

I would like an essay that works through this dispute properly and comes down somewhere, rather than leaving matters hanging the way our reading group did. Please explain what Harsanyi's axioms do and do not entail, give the Sen and Diamond objections their due, weigh the main strategies for rescuing a utilitarian reading of the theorem, and then argue for a definite verdict: on balance, does Harsanyi's theorem give us good reason to accept utilitarianism as the correct approach to social choice under uncertainty, or do these objections show that its apparent force trades on conflating distinct senses of "utility"?
