I've spent the last few evenings back in the scalable oversight rabbit hole, rereading Irving, Christiano, and Amodei's original "AI safety via debate" proposal alongside the later material on the obfuscated arguments problem, and I keep getting stuck on the same tension. The original pitch leans heavily on a complexity-theory analogy -- something like the intuition behind IP = PSPACE, where a computationally weak verifier can be brought to trust the outcome of a claim adjudicated between two unboundedly capable, mutually adversarial provers, on the theory that an honest prover should always have some cheap, checkable rebuttal available against any dishonest move by the other side. Barnes and Christiano's write-up on obfuscated arguments makes me doubt that the premise that a cheap rebuttal always exists survives contact with debaters who can spread a load-bearing falsehood across thousands of individually plausible substeps, none of which is wrong enough on its own for the honest debater to profitably contest inside a bounded number of rounds. Cross-examination, recursive decomposition of claims, and the more recent "doubly-efficient" debate protocols are all supposed to help, but I've never actually seen anyone stress-test whether those patches touch the underlying problem or just relocate it.

Could you write me a dialogue between two people hashing this out at a rationalist meetup: one who still thinks debate, suitably patched, is our best available route to scalably overseeing systems smarter than their judges, and another who thinks the obfuscated arguments problem, combined with the basic fact that a debate optimizes for what persuades a bounded judge rather than for what's true, means debate cannot be fixed into something that actually scales. I'd like the two of them to be equally sharp and technically fluent, with no strawmanning on either side, and I want genuine engagement rather than two monologues -- each character should be forced to respond to the other's strongest specific point (the complexity-theory motivation and its limits, what an honest equilibrium actually requires game-theoretically, whether recursive decomposition or doubly-efficient variants dissolve the obfuscation worry or merely push it up a level, and the persuasiveness-versus-truth objection) rather than restating their opening position with different words. Ideally each of them concedes or complicates something at least once over the course of the exchange, so that by the end a reader can tell whether the disagreement is mostly about the game-theoretic math, mostly empirical (about what real human judges and real language models will actually do), or mostly about background priors on how badly optimizing an AI against human approval tends to go wrong.
