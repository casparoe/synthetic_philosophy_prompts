I'm a junior on my high school's debate team and one of five student members of our Honor Council, which hears academic-integrity cases and decides, by majority vote, whether a student is "responsible" for violating our Academic Integrity Code. The Code defines responsibility conjunctively: a student is responsible only if the Council finds (a) that the submitted work contains a substantial amount of AI-generated content beyond what the assignment's citation policy allowed, and (b) that the student knew, or reasonably should have known, that this required disclosure under our AI-use policy. Our bylaws simply say the Council "decides by majority vote" and never specify whether that means a single vote on the ultimate question of responsibility, or separate votes on (a) and (b) that then get combined using the Code's own rule.

We ran into exactly this gap last week in a matter I'll refer to only as Case 6, since we're supposed to discuss pending cases by number rather than by name. The five of us are the only voting members; our faculty advisor guides discussion but doesn't vote. We've always taken a single holistic vote on "responsible or not," but this time the advisor suggested voting on (a) and (b) separately first, to be careful about each part of the definition, since the AI-use policy is still new. When we did, three of us voted yes on (a) and three voted yes on (b), but not the same three people, and as far as I can reconstruct, nobody voted inconsistently with their own view of the rule: each person's individual bottom-line ballot matched exactly what their own (a)-and-(b) votes implied for them. Combine the two element-level majorities using the Code's own rule, and Case 6 comes out "responsible." Tally our five individual bottom-line ballots directly, and it comes out "not responsible," three votes to two. Same five people, same rule, same meeting, opposite answers, depending only on which of two apparently reasonable counting procedures is used. The respondent's appeal window closes in a week, and we meet again this Thursday to decide what to do, both about this case and about our procedure going forward.

I'm not asking you to weigh in on the merits of Case 6 itself; that's genuinely for us to judge. I would like the short memo I'll circulate to the Council and our advisor before Thursday, recommending (1) how we should treat Case 6 given the split, and (2) what voting procedure we should formally adopt for multi-element cases from now on, with reasoning the rest of the Council could actually follow rather than a philosophy lecture. One of my debate teammates thinks this must be related to Arrow's impossibility theorem, which came up once as an aside in econ class, but I'm honestly not sure that's the same kind of problem, and I would like the memo to say if it isn't. Please take the strongest argument for whichever procedure you end up not recommending seriously rather than waving it away, and tell us honestly whether you think some procedure could avoid this kind of split for good, or whether we should expect to be managing this trade-off case by case from now on.
