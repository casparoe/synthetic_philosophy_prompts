I'm a PhD student in epistemology writing a dissertation chapter on the KK principle (the claim that knowing that p entails knowing that one knows that p) and Timothy Williamson's argument against the luminosity of mental states — roughly, the claim that being in a mental state doesn't guarantee being in a position to know that one is in it. Williamson treats the surprise exam paradox not as a free-standing puzzle about self-reference or common knowledge, but as a special case of his margin-for-error diagnosis of failures of self-knowledge: the backward-induction argument that generates the "surprise" tacitly assumes the students have exact, luminous knowledge of their own evolving epistemic state from day to day, an assumption that margin-for-error considerations are meant to rule out quite generally. Please describe the most important implications of this diagnosis for the broader anti-luminosity argument. I'm especially interested in whether a case built on an explicit, publicly announced deductive argument, rather than on a gradually varying condition like feeling cold or judging a stick's length, can really do independent work in motivating margin-for-error epistemology, or whether it merely illustrates a thesis that has to be established on other grounds.
