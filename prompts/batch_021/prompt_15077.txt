In my school's "Logic and Paradoxes" elective, we just spent a week on Kurt Gödel's modal ontological argument for God's existence, which our teacher presented as the "serious," rigorous cousin of the more famous versions from Anselm and Descartes. As I understood it, the argument treats being "positive" as a primitive but well-behaved property, adds axioms saying that positive properties are closed under entailment and that being "God-like" (having every positive property) is itself positive, derives from this that a God-like being is at least possible, and then, by also treating necessary existence as a positive property and running everything through the S5 system of modal logic, concludes that a God-like being necessarily exists. I read that Gödel himself never tried to publish or publicize the proof while he was alive, which struck me as a little strange for something this rigorous, but I figured that's just a fact about his famously private personality rather than about the argument itself. What really convinced me this was more than clever wordplay, though, is that I also found out some computer scientists fed these exact axioms into automated theorem-proving software a few years back and had it confirm, mechanically and symbol by symbol, that the argument is valid: the conclusion truly does follow from the premises with no gaps. Given that Gödel is one of the two or three most important logicians in history, and that a computer with no theological stake in the matter double-checked every inferential step, I don't see what's left for a skeptic to object to besides simply not liking the conclusion.

A friend of mine who reads a lot of philosophy told me that the same axioms also generate something called "modal collapse," where it turns out that literally nothing in the universe is contingent anymore, but when I pushed her on why that counts as a real objection and not just a fancier way of rejecting a premise you find inconvenient, she couldn't quite explain it and we dropped it. I have to give a short presentation on this argument next week, and I don't want to either overclaim that a religious question has basically been settled by logic or wave away a genuinely valid proof just because its conclusion makes people uncomfortable. So I'd like to know: once an argument has been checked this rigorously, by both a legendary logician and a machine, is there anything legitimate left to dispute, and is my friend's modal collapse point actually different in kind from simply refusing to accept a premise?
