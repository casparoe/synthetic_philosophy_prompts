so in my econ class we did the centipede game as an activity — you start with $2 in a pot, and on your turn you either "take" (end the game and grab about 2/3 of whatever's in the pot) or "pass" (the pot doubles and it becomes the other person's turn), capped at 6 rounds. my friend Devon was paired against Maya and took immediately on turn 1, no passing at all. we'd literally just learned about backward induction that same week, and afterward at lunch they argued about it for like 20 minutes and I honestly can't tell who actually won.

Devon's arguement was that taking immediately is the only rational move, because you're supposed to reason backward from the end: at round 6 whoever's turn it is would rather take than pass, so at round 5 the other person should expect that and take instead, and so on all the way back to round 1. Maya said fine, but he never even gave her a shot, and she probably would've passed at least twice, so his "rational" move just left money on the table for both of them. Devon said that's irrelevant — if she were actually rational she'd take the second she got the chance too, he just moved first.

then Maya made the point that actually got me thinking: she said his whole round-6 argument only works if it's common knowledge that both of them will always play the "rational" move, but the second either of them ever passes even once, that's already false. so if they'd kept passing a few more rounds, there's no real reason backward induction still tells you what's "rational" from that point on, since the thing it needed to assume already broke. Devon shot back that this is true of literally any prediction in game theory the moment someone deviates, you can't just throw the whole framework out over one deviation. Maya said fine, but then he cant have it both ways — backward induction supposedly "forced" his hand on round 1 through that exact same chain of reasoning, so if the chain breaks down later on, it's not obviously safe at the very start either.

can you go through this move by move and say who's actually right at each point (not just who wins overall), and whether there's something bigger both of them are missing?
