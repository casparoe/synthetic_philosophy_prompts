I supervise a small reading group of PhD students working through Hobbes's natural philosophy this term, mainly De Corpore alongside Douglas Jesseph's book on Hobbes's long-running dispute with John Wallis over squaring the circle. After last week's session, two of the students, both drafting dissertation chapters on Hobbes, got into a disagreement that outlasted the room booking, and both have since asked me privately what I think. I'd like an outside view before I answer either of them.

Adrian's opening move was that Hobbes's refusal to admit error -- he offered new 'solutions' to the quadrature for roughly twenty-five years, each refuted by Wallis or his allies, right up to his death -- shows a man out of his depth: someone who, on Aubrey's telling, only fell for Euclid at forty, and who then wouldn't yield even when shown his mistakes in detail. Calling this 'philosophically motivated,' Adrian said, is generosity Hobbes didn't earn; it was wounded pride hardened into a program, and a good part of why the Royal Society never admitted him. Naomi answered that the errors aren't free-floating: they follow from commitments Hobbes never dropped -- that nothing exists without magnitude, so a 'point' can't be the truly unextended thing Euclid's definitions need; that a line is only the path of a moving point; and that geometry alone yields certainty because we construct its objects ourselves rather than inferring the hidden causes of effects we merely observe. On that view, Wallis's indivisibles and infinite series looked less like progress than a retreat from genuine demonstration into symbol-pushing, so Hobbes's refusal to fold reflects a real disagreement about what proof requires, not mere vanity.

Adrian granted the link to Hobbes's materialism but didn't think it helped: a theory of certainty that keeps generating refuted results isn't redeemed by its pedigree, and being sincerely motivated is compatible with being wrong in a way that costs you your credibility. Naomi's comeback was that losing to methods which only later became standard doesn't show a position was confused while the argument was live, and that this was no side hobby: the same idea, that certainty requires construction, underwrote Hobbes's political philosophy, where civil science could be as demonstrable as geometry precisely because a commonwealth, like a circle, is something we make rather than find. I couldn't adjudicate this on the spot, and I'd rather not referee two students I'll be supervising for years by just picking a side. Could you go through the exchange move by move and say who has the better of it at each stage, rather than only declaring an overall winner -- and tell me whether there's a more defensible position neither of them reached, since I suspect they've forced this into 'crank or genius' when the truth might fit neither box?
