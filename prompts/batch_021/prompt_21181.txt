In my graduate seminar on material constitution this term, we recently moved past the familiar Leibniz's Law argument for the puzzle of the statue and the clay — the observation that the statue and the lump of clay composing it seem to differ in their modal and historical properties despite sharing, at every moment they coexist, all the same proper parts arranged in exactly the same way — and into the sharper difficulty sometimes called the grounding problem, following Karen Bennett's work on spatiotemporally coincident objects: if the constitution theorist is right that the statue and the lump are two numerically distinct, coincident objects, then something has to make it true that the statue, and not the lump, would be destroyed by being pounded back into a shapeless mass, yet on the constitution theorist's own account there is no categorical, non-modal difference between the two objects at any time they coexist that could serve as that ground. A few of my students have concluded that this tells decisively against the whole "two objects, one place" picture, in favor of views that deny there is ever any real coincidence to begin with, and I've been trying to figure out for myself whether that conclusion is too quick.

Rather than adjudicate the matter on my own, I'd like to see the dispute played out. Please write a dialogue, set in a sculptor's studio, between two metaphysicians who happen to be present while an apprentice prepares to pound a clay statue — call it Goliath — back into a shapeless lump of clay for reuse. One character is a constitution theorist in the tradition of Wiggins and Baker, who holds that right now, on the pedestal, there are two coincident objects, the statue Goliath and the lump (call it Lumpl), and that only Goliath will be destroyed by the pounding. The other defends what's often called Michael Burke's "dominant kinds" solution, on which there is only ever one object on the pedestal at a time — at present, simply Goliath — so that no second, numerically distinct lump has been quietly coinciding with the statue all along, waiting to outlive it.

Have the dominance theorist press the constitution theorist with the grounding problem in the form sketched above, and have the constitution theorist press back by asking where, exactly, the surviving lump is supposed to come from once the pounding is finished if no lump was already there — and whether the "dominance" of being a statue over being a lump of clay isn't itself just the kind of unexplained, stipulated-looking fact that the dominance solution was supposed to spare us from positing. Each philosopher should take the other's strongest objection seriously and try to answer it rather than simply restating their own view, and please don't let the dialogue tip its hand about who, if anyone, gets the better of the exchange.
