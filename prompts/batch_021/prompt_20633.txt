I'm a political science PhD student working at the intersection of formal theory and judicial politics, and a case I'd been reading about for an unrelated project—Ramos v. Louisiana, which struck down Louisiana's and Oregon's old non-unanimous conviction rules—pulled me into a question I thought the Condorcet Jury Theorem would settle cleanly, and now I'm not sure it does. As I understand the theorem, if each of n jurors independently identifies the correct verdict with probability greater than one-half and votes sincerely for whichever verdict they privately believe is correct, then the group's probability of reaching the correct verdict under majority rule exceeds any individual juror's own accuracy once there are more than a couple of jurors, and climbs toward certainty as the jury grows larger (with the mirror-image collapse toward zero if competence dips below one-half). I'm fairly confident I have that part right.

What I can't get past is an extension of it that seems obviously true to me, but that a formal-theory paper my advisor pointed me to treats as a mistake. It seems clear that a unanimity requirement for a guilty verdict should only make wrongful convictions rarer than majority rule does: to wrongly convict an innocent person under unanimity, every single juror has to be independently wrong, rather than just a bare majority of them, and that gap should widen, not narrow, as juries get bigger. But the paper argues that once jurors are modeled as rational actors who condition on the fact that their own vote is pivotal, rather than as people who simply report their private signal, unanimity rules can make wrongful convictions more likely than non-unanimous ones, not less. I've worked through the argument more than once and it still feels like it must be exploiting some quirk of the model rather than identifying a real problem with the underlying probabilistic logic—switching the decision rule from majority to unanimity shouldn't change what any individual juror privately believes about the evidence in front of them. Is there something genuinely wrong with how I'm extending the jury theorem, or is this the kind of result that says more about the model's assumptions than about how real juries would behave?
