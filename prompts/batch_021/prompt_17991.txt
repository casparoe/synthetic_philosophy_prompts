I have no formal training in biology or economics, but I have spent the past year working through everything I can find on signaling theory in my spare time: starting with Zahavi's own writing on the handicap principle, then Grafen's game-theoretic defense of it, and eventually the extensions people like Robin Hanson have made to human behavior. The more examples I collect, the more I suspect "costly signal" gets used as an umbrella term for several distinct mechanisms that merely resemble one another on the surface.

As I currently understand it, a signal counts as a strict Zahavian handicap only if its cost falls disproportionately on lower-quality signalers, so that cheating does not pay. That differs from three other mechanisms I keep running into: an index signal, whose reliability comes from being physically impossible to fake regardless of cost; a Fisherian runaway trait, which can spread through mate preference alone without tracking any real underlying quality; and what I've been calling a commitment signal, whose cost mainly demonstrates loyalty or cooperative intent to a group rather than revealing a stable personal quality.
