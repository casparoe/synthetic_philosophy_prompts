"Popper's demarcation criterion holds that a theory only counts as scientific if it's falsifiable — if we can specify, in advance, observations that would count against it. Popper argued that the theory of natural selection fails this test in its most general form: 'survival of the fittest' comes close to tautological, since fitness is usually just defined as differential survival and reproduction, so 'the fittest survive' risks collapsing into 'those who survive, survive.' Worse, almost any trait we observe can be given a plausible after-the-fact selective story, so it's hard to specify beforehand what observation would actually falsify the theory. For these reasons, Popper originally grouped Darwinism together with Marxism and Freudian psychoanalysis as his three standard examples of theories that look scientific but aren't. Later in his career, though, he fully recanted: at a Darwin-anniversary lecture in the late 1970s he said he had changed his mind, and from then on treated natural selection as a properly falsifiable, testable theory on the same footing as an ordinary law of physics. These days philosophers of biology mostly treat the whole tautology worry as a dated artifact of an overly narrow Popperian picture of science, rather than a live problem for evolutionary theory."
