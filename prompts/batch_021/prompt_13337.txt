I run a philosophy podcast that doubles as a teaching tool for my early modern seminar, and next week's episode features a guest who has built her reputation defending what's sometimes called the "in-principle" (as opposed to "in-practice") reading of Part I of Hume's "Of Miracles": the view that the opening argument is meant to stand on its own as a near-a-priori case against ever crediting testimony for a miracle, which would make Part II's four empirical grounds for distrusting actual miracle reports -- the dearth of sufficiently reputable witnesses, the psychology of wonder, the prevalence of such tales among "ignorant and barbarous" peoples, and the mutual cancellation of rival religions' miracles -- something closer to icing than to load-bearing argument. My students got stuck last week on a wrinkle I'd like her pressed on: Hume describes the situation as a contest between opposing "proofs," yet elsewhere he defines a proof as evidence admitting of no doubt or opposition, which seems to make weighing one proof against another close to incoherent.

Please draft eight to ten interview questions that don't just invite her to restate the reading. I want at least one question built around that "proof versus proof" wrinkle; one on the Indian prince who refuses to believe reports of ice, and why that case is supposed to come out differently from a miracle report even though both pit testimony against a lifetime of uniform experience; one on the multiplication-of-witnesses objection -- the Bayesian-flavored complaint, associated with critics like John Earman, that a number of independent and only moderately reliable witnesses can jointly swamp even a very low prior in a way Hume's framing doesn't seem to anticipate; and one asking her to say what would be left of the essay if Part I really does the job on its own, given that Hume still went on to write Part II. For each of these four, add a brief follow-up question keyed to the reply she's most likely to give, so the conversation doesn't stall at the first answer. Round out the remaining questions however you see fit, as long as they engage the actual structure of Hume's argument rather than inviting generalities about miracles, testimony, or religious skepticism.
