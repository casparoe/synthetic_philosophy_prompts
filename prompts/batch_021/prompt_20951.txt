ok so this happened at my AI safety reading group and i still can't decide who won. we'd just gone through the debate paper (Irving/Christiano/Amodei) plus the obfuscated arguments writeup, and two people in the group, Ana and Marcus, argued for like twenty minutes over whether debate actually holds up as a scalable oversight scheme once you take the obfuscated arguments problem seriously.

1. Ana said it's basically handled now: cross-examination forces debaters to stay consistent across branches of the argument tree, so a liar spreading an error across a thousand plausible substeps eventually contradicts themselves. Combine that with doubly-efficient debate making the recursive-narrow-to-the-disputed-substep trick cheap for both sides, and honest play wins for the same reason the judge doesnt need to be as powerful as the debaters -- basically the IP=PSPACE move.

2. Marcus said that conflates two different failure modes: cross-examination catches logical inconsistency, but obfuscated arguments was never about debaters contradicting themselves -- it's about a bounded judge being unable to tell which one of a thousand plausible substeps is actually load-bearing. Recursive narrowing also assumes disagreements bottom out in something cleanly checkable, which is fine for formal problems but not obviously true for "this plan is safe."

3. Ana said "not obviously true" isn't an argument that decomposition fails, just an admission it's unproven -- and debate doesn't need the guarantee to hold everywhere, just often enough that self-play pushes toward honesty on the cases that matter. Plus no competing scalable oversight scheme is obviously safe from the same worry.

4. Marcus said "nothing else does better" isn't evidence debate specifically works, and you can't lean on the PSPACE-style completeness result for confidence while conceding its key premise might fail for exactly the claims we care about.
