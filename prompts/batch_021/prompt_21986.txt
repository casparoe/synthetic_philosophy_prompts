A friend and I spent an evening arguing about the statue-and-clay puzzle, and I'd like a second opinion on the move I made near the end. The usual version lets you separate the lump from the statue by pointing to a time, before the sculptor began, when the lump already existed and the statue did not. To close off that escape route, I proposed a tightened case: the clay is built up directly into the finished shape, so the lump and the statue begin to exist at the same instant, occupy the same place, share exactly the same matter throughout, and are eventually ground into a fine powder and scattered in a single event, so both cease to exist at exactly the same actual moment. At that point, I argued, there is no actual property left for the statue to have that the lump lacks, so Leibniz's Law delivers identity: "the lump" and "the statue" are just two names for one object. My friend objected that they still differ modally: the lump could have survived a gentler mishap, say being squashed into a shapeless ball rather than pulverized, and continued to exist as a lump, whereas the statue could not have survived any reshaping at all. I replied that a merely possible divergence isn't a property of the actual object that sat on the pedestal, so it shouldn't be able to outweigh total actual coincidence. She had no quick reply, but I'm not fully convinced I was right either. Does my tightened case actually settle the puzzle in favor of there being just one object, or was I too quick to set the modal point aside?
