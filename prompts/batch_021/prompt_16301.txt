I am several drafts into a speculative novel whose central conceit hinges on a single fracture: at a pivotal point in the story, the physical regularities that have governed my invented world since its creation begin to change, subtly at first and then decisively. Objects that have always fallen now sometimes drift instead; a substance that has decayed at a fixed rate for millennia starts decaying at another. My editor's note on the relevant chapter was that it currently reads as "magic wearing a lab coat," and she isn't wrong. I would like the rupture to register as a genuine metaphysical event rather than a plot convenience, which sent me down a rabbit hole trying to understand what philosophers actually mean by a "law of nature," since it turns out my premise means something quite different depending on which theory of lawhood one holds.

As far as I can piece together, one long tradition, descending from Hume and worked out rigorously by Ramsey and David Lewis, treats a law of nature as nothing more than the most economical and powerful summary of everything that ever actually happens across the whole history of the world: there is no further glue of necessity binding cause to effect, only a pattern that earns the title "law" by being remarkably simple and strong. The opposing tradition, associated with philosophers like Armstrong and with the various dispositional essentialists, denies that this is enough, insisting that laws must be grounded in some further fact — a relation of necessitation holding between universals, on one version, or powers built into the very identity of the properties involved, on another — which explains the pattern rather than being read off it after the fact.

What I cannot work out on my own is which of these two pictures, if either, actually leaves room for a world's laws to change while that world remains recognizably the same world, with a genuine before and after — and which one instead quietly threatens to make my premise either empty (nothing has really changed; we simply hadn't noticed the right pattern yet) or outright incoherent (whatever changed cannot have been a law, or whatever it now governs cannot really be the same property as before). Could you walk me through how a committed Humean and a committed anti-Humean would each try to accommodate, or would instead resist, a scenario like mine, and tell me plainly whether one side ends up in noticeably better philosophical shape than the other once you press on it?
