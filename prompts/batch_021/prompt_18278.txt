My younger sister -- a violinist -- is getting married next month to a cellist she met three summers ago at a chamber-music program in Vermont, and I have been asked to give the toast, presumably on the theory that a decade of writing about science and ideas for a living qualifies me to handle the philosophy angle. The angle in question is our father: a retired professor whose entire career amounted to one long, unfashionable defense of Schopenhauer, and whose single published monograph argued that Schopenhauer was exactly right that music has a completely different status among the arts than painting or poetry does -- that it doesn't represent anything the way they do, but is instead a direct, unmediated copy of the Will itself, which is supposedly why a minor chord can undo you in a way no still life ever could. For thirty years this has been the family joke: say a piece of music "gives you chills" at dinner and you get the unabridged version of Book Three of The World as Will and Representation, solicited or not.

I want the toast to actually use this idea, properly, since my sister and her fiancé built their whole relationship around music and my father will be sitting at a table with three or four of his old philosophy colleagues who will notice immediately if I get it wrong. Here is the difficulty I cannot resolve on my own: Schopenhauer's claim about music is genuinely moving, and I want it to be the emotional center of the toast, but the same philosopher argued, with equal seriousness, in his essay on the metaphysics of sexual love, that the feeling of having found one irreplaceable person is not what it seems from the inside -- that it is really the species' will-to-live using the illusion of personal happiness to engineer well-matched offspring, no transcendence involved. My father knows that essay as well as he knows the one on music, and would regard it as a kind of intellectual dishonesty if I quietly borrowed the beautiful claim about music while pretending he had never said anything so deflating about love.

I do not need the toast to relitigate the metaphysics of sexual love in front of a hundred and fifty guests who mostly have no idea who Schopenhauer is and simply want a nice toast about my sister -- but I would like it to be able to survive that tension being pointed out, rather than pretend it isn't there, and ideally to turn it into something affectionate rather than awkward. Could you write me that toast? Three or four minutes, warm enough for the cousins as well as the string section, precise enough that my father's old colleagues nod instead of wince, and honest enough about what Schopenhauer actually thought -- about both music and love -- that my father would recognize it as something he might have said himself.
