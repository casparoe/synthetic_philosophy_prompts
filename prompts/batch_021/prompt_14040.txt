I spent thirty-five years running a physiology lab before retiring and finally getting around to the philosophy I'd always meant to read "someday." Lately I've been captivated by Émilie du Châtelet's Discours sur le bonheur, partly because she was a scientist too (her Institutions de Physique argued, against the Cartesians, that force is proportional to velocity squared rather than velocity itself — most of the way to what we now call kinetic energy), and partly because her advice on happiness turned out to be far stranger and more specific than the generic Stoic wisdom I'd braced myself for. Below is a short reflection I've drafted on one strand of her argument, and I'd value your comments on it.

Du Châtelet's essay sits several claims side by side that don't obviously belong together. She insists that happiness requires real passions rather than their suppression, and she singles out the taste for study as the most reliable pleasure of all, since, unlike the pleasures of gambling or love (both of which she admits to indulging, gambling especially), it doesn't depend on fortune and doesn't wear out with use. But her boldest claim, in a phrase I'm probably mangling slightly in translation, is that one must actively cultivate a susceptibility to illusion: much of what makes life sweet, on her account, survives only so long as we decline to examine it too closely.

That struck me as a strikingly modern thought, and I've tried, probably too eagerly, to line it up with two pieces of psychology I only know at second hand: findings that mildly self-flattering distortions of one's own prospects correlate with better mental health, and the observation that we habituate to almost every pleasure except open-ended activities like learning, which sits well with her privileging of study. But if she's right, and if that psychology is right, both face the same puzzle. An illusion you have deliberately diagnosed and then prescribed for yourself, in the very essay meant to expose it as an illusion, looks like a contradiction in terms. Can one knowingly cultivate a belief for its usefulness, precisely because it is false, without the knowing dissolving the usefulness? Du Châtelet doesn't pause over this; I am not convinced the psychologists have fully resolved it either.

I'd welcome your comments on three things in particular: whether I have represented du Châtelet's actual argument fairly rather than flattening it into something more convenient; whether the parallel to modern happiness research illuminates her position or merely flatters it; and, above all, whether the "self-aware illusion" puzzle is a genuine problem for her account of happiness, or one that dissolves once stated more carefully.
