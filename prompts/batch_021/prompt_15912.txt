For the past month I've been using a commitment-contract app that automatically sends money to a cause I despise whenever I skip a scheduled gym session, precisely because I know from experience that my evening self reliably overrules whatever my morning self decided. I've now let it charge me twice anyway, hitting snooze both times while sincerely telling myself that tonight is the exception. Trying to make sense of this, I've been reading about hyperbolic discounting, and I can't decide whether it actually explains weakness of will or just gives it a new vocabulary. Write me a dialogue between two people arguing it out beside my bed the second time I silence the alarm: one is a behavioral economist in the tradition of Ainslie and Laibson, for whom the crossing of hyperbolic discount curves is the whole story -- my valuation of the two options has simply, if temporarily, reversed, and there is no further mystery to explain. The other is a philosopher who, drawing on Davidson and Holton, argues that weakness of will is specifically a failure to hold to a resolution formed for just this kind of moment, and that a model built purely on shifting valuations can't distinguish that failure from sheer compulsion, since on such a model whichever curve happens to be higher simply wins. Have them work through my case point by point, each pressing the other on the weak spot in their own view -- the economist should have to say whether a preference I disavow even as I act on it still deserves the name, and the philosopher should have to say what work "resolution" is doing if it has no causal effect at the moment that matters.
