I cover AI and cognitive science for a science magazine, and I'm currently drafting a longer feature revisiting the "stochastic parrots" debate over whether large language models understand anything at all or merely manipulate linguistic form without ever touching meaning. While researching it, I went back to a thought experiment I'd only half-remembered from a computational linguistics course years ago: the "octopus test" that Emily Bender and Alexander Koller used in their well-known 2020 paper on meaning and form (the one everyone in the field just calls "the octopus paper"). Two fluent English speakers, marooned on separate islands, discover an old telegraph cable linking them and start exchanging messages over it. Unbeknownst to either, a hyper-intelligent octopus taps the line; having no access to anything the words refer to, it learns purely from the statistical shape of the exchange, and eventually cuts the cable and starts impersonating one of the two correspondents convincingly enough that the substitution goes unnoticed - until, so the story goes, the remaining human is suddenly confronted with a real physical emergency and begs for urgent practical advice using only whatever is at hand, at which point the octopus's replies come out fluent, confident, and useless, because it never had any purchase on what the words were actually about.

For a sidebar to the piece, I'd like to commission an original short story, something in the range of 1,000 to 1,800 words, that takes this scenario as a jumping-off point rather than a fixed destination. I've now read a dozen retellings of the parable itself, so a straightforward dramatization won't add much; what I'm after is a story that pushes on the spot where the thought experiment's tidy verdict starts to feel less secure, or, alternatively, sharpens it in a way the original version doesn't quite manage. You might tell it from inside the octopus, or the cable, rather than from the two humans; you might construct a version of the emergency that the octopus could genuinely talk its way through without cheating; or you might update the premise for something closer to a modern multimodal system that has at least some sensory contact with the world it describes. Follow whichever of these, or some other idea entirely, actually generates the most interesting story, rather than trying to cover all of them.

The one thing I'd ask you to hold onto is intellectual honesty about the underlying puzzle - the relationship between distributional form and grounded reference, and what kind of evidence could possibly settle whether something is tracking meaning rather than completing a pattern - rather than letting the piece drift into a familiar "machine wakes up and turns out to have a soul after all" arc. An ending that leaves the question genuinely open, or unresolved in an uncomfortable way, is entirely fine: I'm more interested in a story that takes the philosophical stakes seriously than one that resolves them neatly.
