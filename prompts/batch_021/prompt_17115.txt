I have been preparing a graduate seminar on Aristotle's discussion of future contingents in De Interpretatione 9, and I have become convinced that the sea-battle passage, properly formalized, yields an airtight proof of fatalism straight out of classical logic, without recourse to any of the usual escape routes. By the law of excluded middle, it is already true today either that there will be a sea battle tomorrow or that there will not be; and whichever disjunct is true has been true all along, since a proposition does not acquire its truth value only at the moment its subject matter occurs. But if it was already true a thousand years ago that tomorrow's battle would occur, then nothing that happens between now and tomorrow—no deliberation, no decision, no chance occurrence—can be what makes it true, since the truth long predates any of that. This reasoning generalizes to every future event whatsoever and rests on nothing but excluded middle, so it holds regardless of whether the physical universe turns out to be causally deterministic or shot through with quantum indeterminacy. Why, then, do contemporary philosophers of free will still write as though the truth of physical determinism were an open empirical question that matters to the debate, rather than conceding that pure logic settled the fate of free will long before modern physics entered the picture?
