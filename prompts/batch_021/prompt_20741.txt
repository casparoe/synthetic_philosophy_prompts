Then explain how Sparrow extends this idea to argue that deploying autonomous weapons in war is impermissible. And here's the answer I have to grade:

"Matthias's point is that with ordinary tools, and even normal computer programs, if something goes wrong you can always trace it back to whoever built or used the thing, because one of them had enough foresight and control over what it would do to fairly take the blame. Learning systems break that because they aren't following rules a programmer wrote line by line -- they're trained on data and can end up acting in ways nobody specifically intended or could have predicted. So neither the manufacturer nor the user has the kind of control responsibility usually requires, but the system itself obviously isn't a moral agent that can be punished either. That's the gap: a bad outcome with no one left over who both caused it and meets the conditions for blame.

Sparrow takes that same structure and applies it to lethal autonomous weapons. His argument is that jus in bello requires that someone can be held responsible whenever a weapon kills someone, otherwise the killing is more like a natural disaster than an act of war. With a fully autonomous weapon the commander authorizes its use but can't be sure exactly what it will do in the moment, so they don't have full control over that specific outcome even though they're still the one who sent it out, so to speak. The programmer is even further removed from the actual event. And the weapon itself can't be punished in a way that means anything to it. So you get a responsibility gap specifically inside the context of war, and Sparrow thinks that's enough to make deploying these weapons wrong even if they end up more accurate than a human soldier would be -- it's sometimes put as the commander becoming a kind of 'moral crumple zone' (that's Sparrow's phrase), absorbing blame that doesn't really belong to them while the actual decision-making has effectively moved elsewhere.

The condition doing the heavy lifting in both arguments is the control condition on responsibility. Fischer and Ravizza's version of this says that being responsible for an act requires that you could have done otherwise, and that's exactly what disappears once a system is trained rather than directly programmed -- no human retains the ability to have made that specific outcome go differently.

On strict liability: I don't think it closes the gap, it just routes around it. Passing a law that makes the manufacturer pay out automatically, regardless of fault, solves the compensation problem for victims, but it doesn't mean the manufacturer was actually morally responsible in the sense Matthias and Sparrow care about -- you've guaranteed someone to sue, not identified someone who deserves blame. So there's a leftover gap between who you can make pay and who actually deserves it, which is basically what Danaher calls the 'retribution gap.'"
