For the last couple of years I've been quietly obsessed with anthropic reasoning -- the Doomsday Argument, the presumptuous philosopher, and that whole cluster of puzzles -- ever since I stumbled on some old essays about it online. I have no formal background in philosophy, but I've read enough by now to follow the arguments reasonably well. The other night the topic came up over dinner with two friends of mine, Renata and Josh, and what I intended as a casual explanation turned into a genuine argument between the two of them that ran through the rest of the meal, one I couldn't referee even with all my reading.

(1) Renata opened with the basic Doomsday Argument, built on the self-sampling assumption: you should reason as though your birth rank among all humans who will ever live is a random draw from that entire population. Since we're something like the hundred-billionth human or so ever born, she said, that rank would be a much bigger coincidence if humanity goes on to produce trillions more descendants than if the eventual total stays in the same rough neighborhood as today's population -- so the mere fact that we exist this early is itself mild evidence for a shorter human future rather than a longer one. (2) Josh objected that this style of reasoning, taken seriously, licenses something absurd, and brought up Bostrom's "presumptuous philosopher": imagine two cosmological theories, equally well supported by all the physical evidence gathered so far, one implying a trillion observers exist in the universe and the other a billion, with physicists about to run an experiment to settle which is true. If merely existing as an observer counts as evidence for theories that predict more observers like you, a philosopher could announce the trillion-observer theory is true before the physicists even finish, purely because that's the kind of universe he'd expect to find himself in -- and that can't be right, so whatever reasoning gets you there, including Renata's, is suspect. (3) Renata replied that Josh had actually merged two different assumptions pulling in opposite directions: her argument only needs the self-sampling assumption, about ranking yourself among people who actually exist, whereas the presumptuous philosopher additionally needs the self-indication assumption, a separate and far more controversial claim that weights hypotheses upfront by how many observers they predict. In fact, she pointed out, adding the self-indication assumption is precisely what philosophers use to cancel the Doomsday Argument out, since it counteracts the very tendency to look atypically early in big-population worlds -- so Josh can't wield "the self-indication assumption leads somewhere absurd" against an argument that never invokes it. (4) Josh conceded the technical distinction but said it only relocated the problem rather than solving it: Renata's version avoids assigning probabilities over how many humans will ever exist only by quietly picking a reference class -- why "all humans," rather than "all primates," "all sentient beings," or "everyone who has seriously thought about the Doomsday Argument"? Change that choice and the argument's verdict changes with it, sometimes drastically, so she hasn't avoided an arbitrary, population-size-dependent judgment call at all -- she's just hidden it inside the choice of reference class instead of an explicit likelihood ratio, which struck him as no real improvement on what the presumptuous philosopher was doing.

Neither of them backed down, and I honestly don't know who was right at which step, or whether they're both missing something more basic.
