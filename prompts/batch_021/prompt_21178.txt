So I went down a bit of a rabbit hole this weekend after getting into an argument with a coworker about what makes a claim "real science" — I brought up Popper and falsifiability, which I sorta know from all the A/B-testing/hypothesis-testing talk we do at work. Turns out Popper himself apparently went back and forth on whether Darwin's theory of natural selection even counts as a real scientific theory. From what I read, sometime in the 70s he basically called it "almost tautological," more of a metaphysical research program than a testable theory (something about "survival of the fittest" being true by definition), and then later did a public walk-back of that and openly "recanted" at some lecture — I want to say it was at Darwin College but dont hold me to that, my memory of the exact details is fuzzy. Apparently creationists had a field day quoting the first part and conveniently ignoring the second.

This actually hits close to home for me because I use genetic algorithms / evolution-strategy type methods for hyperparameter search at work sometimes, and I've always had this nagging feeling that "the fittest configs survive to the next generation" is a bit circular — fitness is basically *defined* by what we keep around, so of course the fittest survive. That feels like basically the same tautology charge Popper leveled at biological natural selection before he changed his mind about it.

So, given Popper's original tautology argument AND his later recantation, what are the most important implications for how someone like me should think about whether claims like "the algorithm evolved an optimal solution" are genuinely falsifiable/meaningful scientific-ish claims, or just tautologies dressed up in fancy language? I'm not looking for a full philosophy-of-science lecture, just like 4-6 concrete, punchy takeaways I could actually apply next time someone in a meeting says our model "evolved" the best answer.
