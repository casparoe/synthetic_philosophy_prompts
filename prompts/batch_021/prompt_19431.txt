I've been reading about AI alignment stuff, and separately picked up an old econ textbook, and I keep noticing these two fields hit the exact same wall from totally different angles. Economists have this idea called revealed preference: you don't need to ask someone what they want, you can just work it out from what they actually choose, as long as those choices are consistent across situations. AI folks do basically the same move with inverse reinforcement learning: watch what an agent does and back out the reward driving it. But both hit trouble - revealed preference falls apart once real peoples choices turn out inconsistent (framing effects, preference reversals, etc), so theres often no single ordering left to recover, and with IRL, apparently even infinite clean behavior data still can't nail down one unique reward, since a rational agent who wants X and an irrational one who wants something totally different can look exactly the same from the outside. Is this the same underlying problem wearing two different disciplinary costumes, or is there a real difference that makes one more fixable than the other? And has either field actually solved it, or do people just quietly assume the problem away in practice?
