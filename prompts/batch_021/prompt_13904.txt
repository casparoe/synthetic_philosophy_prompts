I'm taking an upper-level seminar on philosophy of mind this term, and over the last couple of weeks we've gone through Putnam's Twin Earth thought experiment and the causal theory of reference in a fair amount of detail: the idea that "water" picks out H2O on Earth and the superficially identical but chemically distinct substance XYZ on Twin Earth, so that two thinkers who are internal duplicates of one another can still end up thinking about different things, because the content of a thought like WATER depends partly on the thinker's environment and not solely on what's "in the head." I feel like I have a solid grip on that core argument. What I'm stuck on is something my professor raised only in the last few minutes of one lecture, almost as an aside: she said this sort of content externalism generates a real tension with the intuitive idea that we have privileged, armchair knowledge of our own thoughts, and mentioned "the McKinsey paradox" before promising we'd come back to it properly later in the course.

Impatient, I tracked down McKinsey's original short paper along with a couple of secondary discussions of it, and I think I can follow the argument step by step: if the very concept I'm employing — WATER, rather than some Twin-Earthian look-alike concept — depends on facts about my environment, and I can also know just by introspecting that I am thinking, say, that water is wet, then it seems I can chain these two claims into a piece of a priori reasoning that delivers a substantive empirical conclusion — that water, and therefore H2O, exists in my environment — without conducting any empirical investigation whatsoever. That conclusion is obviously not something introspection alone should be able to deliver, but I don't yet have a real feel for why the argument is so hard to escape. My study group is split on this: half think it's a cheap trick that dissolves as soon as you look closely, and half think it's a serious problem that has occupied committed externalists for decades.

Could you talk me through the paradox in an intuitive, gut-level way rather than by re-presenting the formal derivation, which I already have from the paper itself? I'm especially interested in what's really doing the work in generating the trouble, and in why the two "obvious" escape routes — giving up a little bit of privileged access, or weakening the externalist premise just slightly — turn out to be less costless than they look at first glance.
