I have no formal training in philosophy, but I have spent a good deal of time over the past year thinking about causation, ever since I served on a jury in a civil case where two separate acts of negligence each looked sufficient, on their own, to have caused the plaintiff's injury. The instruction we were given was that the defendant's conduct had to be a "but for" cause of the harm, meaning the harm would not have occurred without it, and this simply did not fit the facts, since the harm would have occurred anyway through the other party's negligence alone. That experience sent me down a long path of independent reading, starting with Judea Pearl's book on causal inference and eventually leading me into more purely philosophical territory, where I kept running into two rival ways of analyzing causation: one descending from David Lewis, on which causation is a matter of counterfactual dependence between events across possible worlds, and one associated with James Woodward, on which causation is analyzed instead in terms of what would happen to one variable under a hypothetical intervention on another.

What I have not been able to work out on my own is exactly where these two approaches part ways once you get past the textbook cases. They seem to agree completely about ordinary examples, but to diverge sharply on cases of redundant causation. I gather there is a recognized difference between "early preemption," where a backup process is cut off at some intermediate point well before the effect occurs, and "late preemption," where the backup process runs right alongside the main one and is only forestalled at the very last instant, as when a backup assassin, primed to fire a fraction of a second after the first, never actually gets the chance because the first assassin's shot has already killed the victim. From what I can tell, Lewis's original account of causation as a chain of stepwise counterfactual dependence handles the early case reasonably well, since there is some intermediate event on which the effect depends and which fails to occur in the backup process, but the account struggles badly with the late case, where no such intermediate difference exists until the very moment of the effect itself. This is part of what pushed Lewis, and others after him, toward talk of "influence" and matters of degree rather than a flat, all-or-nothing dependence.

I would like to actually understand this rather than just collect the vocabulary. Could you walk me through why late preemption is so damaging to Lewis's original counterfactual analysis in a way that early preemption is not, what the later patches, his own and other people's, were actually trying to fix, and whether Woodward's interventionist framework, with its structural equations and its notion of "invariance," genuinely handles the very same case better, or whether it just relocates the same difficulty under a different vocabulary? I am less interested in a list of differences between the two theories than in a real, defended opinion about which of them is doing the better job on this particular kind of case.
