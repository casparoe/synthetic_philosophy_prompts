I spent thirty-four years as an experimental physicist building instruments for outer-planet missions, and I have spent much of my retirement on the reading I never had time for during my career, most recently working through Gödel's and Löb's theorems with a small study group of former colleagues. That is how I first came across the discussion, in the AI-safety literature, of what is sometimes called the Löbian obstacle to "tiling" agents: Löb's theorem shows that a consistent formal system can adopt the principle that whatever it can prove must be true only for statements it can already prove outright, since adopting it as a blanket policy would amount to the system proving its own consistency, which Gödel's second theorem forbids. That fact turns out to block a very natural-seeming engineering scheme, in which one reasoning system is meant to certify the trustworthiness of a successor system that reasons in exactly the same way.

By an odd coincidence, I still sit on the independent flight-software safety review board for the laboratory where I spent my career, and a case on this month's agenda seems to me to turn on precisely this issue, though none of the engineers are using that language. The mission is a probe intended to operate for several decades beyond the outer planets, well past the point where round-trip communication delays make real-time correction from Earth practical; its onboard "governor" module will only install a software patch -- generated on board, in response to conditions the original designers did not anticipate -- once an internal theorem prover certifies that the patch meets a fixed safety specification; the newly patched governor then inherits the job of certifying the next patch on the same terms. The engineering team's design document calls this an "unbroken chain of proof-verified trust" running the length of the mission. A logician the board brought in as a consultant objects that no such chain can be unbroken in the sense claimed: getting one governor to certify, without restriction, that anything an equally strong successor proves safe really is safe amounts to exactly the kind of self-consistency proof that Gödel's and Löb's theorems jointly forbid. The engineers' answer is that they never invoke that unrestricted principle -- the chain is built as a strictly descending hierarchy of successively weaker proof systems, so only a bounded, pre-counted number of patches (a few hundred, on their estimate) is ever certified this way, and the obstacle simply has no purchase on a finite descending chain. Some board members regard that as a complete answer; others worry that the mission's patch budget is optimistic for a multi-decade voyage, or that the descending hierarchy quietly limits how sophisticated a late-mission patch can be in ways the design document never states.

Would you draft the decision memo I will circulate to the rest of the board ahead of next week's vote? I want a clear recommendation -- approve the governor design as submitted, approve it contingent on named changes, or return it for redesign -- attached to a concrete timeframe, and I want the engineers' "bounded chain" defense taken seriously as the strongest thing that can be said for their design, rather than dismissed as a technicality that the mere existence of the Löbian obstacle lets us wave away.
