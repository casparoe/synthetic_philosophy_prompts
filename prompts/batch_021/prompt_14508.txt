I spent thirty-odd years doing population genetics fieldwork on small mammals before I retired, and lately I've been reading up on the old arguments over whether genes, organisms, or groups are the 'real' target of natural selection. Its not just armchair stuff for me anymore though: I sit on the scientific advisory panel for a small conservation trust, and we've got an actual proposal in front of us. A university lab wants our sign-off and some bridge funding to field-test a gene drive in the mice on one of our seabird islands, built off a natural mutation that already cheats the normal 50/50 odds of inheritance, to crash the mouse population before it finishes off the chicks. Half the panel calls this just smarter pest control. The other half says that deliberately harnessing a gene that spreads by hurting the organism carrying it is a different kind of bet than poison bait ever was, including the real chance that ordinary selection on the mice breeds resistance in a few generations and the scheme quietly unravels. Could you draft the short memo I need to circulate before the panel meets, recommending we approve, reject, or approve with conditions - and actually engage with that 'different in kind' worry instead of just brushing past it?
