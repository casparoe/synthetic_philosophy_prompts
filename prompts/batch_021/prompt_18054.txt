In my philosophy of religion seminar, we recently finished the unit on the kalam cosmological argument and moved on to Leibniz's argument from contingency, and a remark our professor made in passing has stayed with me since. She pointed out that Craig's kalam argument has a comparatively easy time of it: because its causal premise is restricted to things that begin to exist, it never has to explain why the uncaused first cause exists or acts as it does. The Leibnizian argument can't take that shortcut, since the whole inference rests on an unrestricted Principle of Sufficient Reason (PSR) on which every contingent fact -- including facts about how a necessary first cause exercises its causal power -- needs an explanation. This is close to the worry Peter van Inwagen pressed against PSR back in the early 1980s: if literally every contingent fact needs an explanation, and the explanation of the totality of contingent facts has to bottom out in a necessary being whose nature is the same in every possible world, then whatever that being does looks like it must also be the same in every possible world, so that nothing turns out to be genuinely contingent after all. Contemporary defenders of the Leibnizian argument, such as Alexander Pruss, typically try to block this "modal collapse" by inserting a libertarian free choice into the explanatory chain between God's necessary nature and the contingent world God creates, on the grounds that a free choice can explain an effect without necessitating it.

I'd like you to argue that this free-will move does not actually save the Leibnizian argument from modal collapse. Specifically, please press the dilemma that inserting a free choice is either explanatorily idle or self-defeating: on one horn, the choice is itself just one more contingent fact, which -- by the very same unrestricted PSR the argument needs at every other step -- requires a further explanation, so that the regress has merely been relocated rather than stopped; on the other horn, the choice is explained (without being necessitated) by God's necessary nature and reasons, in which case you should say what "explains without necessitating" really amounts to, and why it isn't just a euphemism for weakening "sufficient" reason into "merely partial" reason. Please engage with the strongest version of the free-will response you can construct rather than a strawman of it, and be explicit throughout about exactly which notion of "explanation" and of "sufficient reason" each step of your argument presupposes, since I suspect that much of the disagreement in the literature ultimately turns on that choice of notion rather than on anything else.
