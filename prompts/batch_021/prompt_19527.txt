For my seminar on Kant's moral philosophy, I need to write a short paper comparing Kant's own treatment of animals with Christine Korsgaard's Kantian case for direct duties toward them. Kant holds that we have no direct duties to animals, since only rational beings can be ends in themselves; the duties we appear to have "regarding" animals — his stock example is a man who has his old dog shot because it can no longer serve him, thereby wronging no one, since the dog cannot judge — are really indirect duties to humanity, owed because habitual cruelty dulls a sensitivity we need in how we treat other rational beings. In *Fellow Creatures*, Korsgaard argues that this very Kantian argument, spelled out consistently, should have led Kant to the opposite conclusion. Please reconstruct, as precisely as you can, exactly where she thinks Kant's inference from rational nature to unconditional worth goes wrong, and then judge whether the view she ends up with is a natural completion of Kant's own argument or a revision that quietly drops a premise Kant treated as non-negotiable. Defend your verdict rather than just laying out both sides.
