I sit on the six-member jury for a small literary nonprofit's annual fiction prize for emerging Black writers. The prize's charter, written by its founders in the 1970s, explicitly invokes Du Bois's argument in "Criteria of Negro Art" that art concerned with Black life carries an inescapable social function, and that the demand for "art for art's sake" has usually functioned, in practice, to protect a status quo that more engaged art would disturb; jurors are instructed to weigh a manuscript's "contribution to the cause" alongside its craft, the founders' own paraphrase of the essay's argument.

This year we are deadlocked 3-3 between two finalists. Manuscript A is a technically dazzling novella about a marriage dissolving during a house renovation; the characters happen to be Black, but nothing in the book concerns race, and early readers who saw it blind guessed the author was white. Manuscript B is a rougher but sincere linked-story collection about a family losing its home to eminent domain — unambiguously "about" the material the charter has in mind — though two jurors sympathetic to its politics still think several of its characters are mouthpieces rather than people, and that a couple of its central images have gone stale from overuse elsewhere. The three jurors backing Manuscript A argue that a prize which starts grading manuscripts on message over execution has stopped being a literary prize, and that Du Bois himself, who could be scathing about Renaissance-era writing he thought traded in stereotype to satisfy white readers' appetite for the primitive, would be the first to fault B on craft grounds alone. The other three think a charter built on Du Bois's premise cannot in good conscience crown a novella that erases the very stakes it exists to recognize, however well the sentences are made.

Please draft the memo I will circulate to the full jury, and eventually the foundation's board, recommending how to resolve the tie. It should reach an actual recommendation rather than splitting the difference for its own sake, engage the losing side's strongest argument on its own terms rather than a caricature of it, and say plainly whether Du Bois's actual position — as distinct from the slogan version quoted in our charter — really settles this case, or leaves room for reasonable jurors to land differently.
