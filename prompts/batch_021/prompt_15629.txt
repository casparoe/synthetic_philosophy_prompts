At a meeting of our philosophy department's curriculum committee last month, two colleagues fell into a disagreement I haven't been able to settle to my own satisfaction. At issue was whether our postdoctoral fellows -- who teach several of the affected courses but hold fixed two-year contracts -- should get a vote on a proposed redesign of the core curriculum, alongside the tenured and tenure-track faculty.

1. Ines argued that postdocs will have to teach whatever curriculum results and are affected by the decision as much as anyone in the room, so on any reasonable "all-affected-interests" standard, they belong in the electorate.
2. David objected that this proves too much: undergraduates are affected still more directly, since they're the ones who'll actually sit through the redesigned courses, and so, on the same logic, are the not-yet-hired faculty of the future. If affectedness is the criterion, half the university qualifies.
3. Ines replied that the right standard is subjection, not affectedness: postdocs, unlike students, are subject to the department's ongoing authority -- assigned courses, bound by the chair's rulings -- and it's this standing exposure to the department's power over them, not mere effect, that grounds a claim to a vote.
4. David answered that undergraduates are subject to the curriculum in just this sense too, since they must take whatever is on the books; and in any case, a departmental vote is a mechanism of self-governance for those who will remain to live with, and later revise, the decision -- which postdocs, by contract, will not.
5. Ines shot back that "will not remain" is exactly what's in dispute, not a neutral fact that settles it: an assistant professor might leave in two years too, we just don't know it yet, so building expected permanence into the franchise criterion quietly decides the question it claims only to describe.

Neither budged, and the matter was tabled. Please go through the exchange move by move, saying who has the better of the argument at each step rather than just who wins overall, and note anything you think both of them are missing about how a department -- unlike a nation-state, which is the setting this debate is usually conducted in -- ought to draw this boundary.
