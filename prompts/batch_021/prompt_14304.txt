Two students in my graduate seminar on philosophy of language got into the following exchange right after we finished discussing Kripke's *Wittgenstein on Rules and Private Language*, and since I led the session myself, I'd rather someone else referee it.

(1) Nora opened by arguing that the skeptical solution doesn't actually resolve the rule-following paradox, only relocates it: if no fact about an individual's finite past use can fix whether she meant plus or quus, then no fact about the community's finite collective history can fix this either, so appealing to communal agreement just spreads the same indeterminacy across more people rather than removing it.

(2) Theo replied that this misdescribes the maneuver: the skeptical solution isn't offering the community as a bigger, better truth-maker that supplies the missing fact, but replacing truth-conditions with assertability conditions. A solitary individual has no practice of being corrected against anything and so no room for the concept of a mistake, whereas the community is precisely what makes correction, and hence meaning-talk, possible at all -- a different job than the one dispositions were asked to do and found unable to perform.

(3) Nora answered that this only postpones the difficulty: for a newcomer's use of "+" to count as agreeing with the community, rather than merely tracking it so far, the community's own practice must already be determinate, and nothing Theo has said rules out their all imperceptibly diverging once the numbers get large enough that no one has actually checked -- which simply reruns Kripke's argument against the individual case on a bigger, but still finite, sample.

(4) Theo answered that this objection smuggles back in exactly the notion the skeptical solution discards: for Kripke's Wittgenstein there is no further fact against which the community's usage could count as a collective mistake, so the community's drifting apart tomorrow would not be a matter of getting anything wrong, any more than redefining the standard metre in Paris could be.

(5) Nora closed by suggesting the whole exchange might be moot, since Kripke concedes in his preface that this reconstruction may be "Wittgenstein" as filtered through his own preoccupations rather than an exegetically faithful argument, and Baker and Hacker deny outright that Wittgenstein thought there was any genuine skeptical paradox about meaning in need of a "solution" to begin with.
