I have been reading about money-pump arguments against agents whose preferences are intransitive or incomplete, and about the main strategies philosophers use to resist the conclusion that such agents are thereby irrational. For an independent project in my school's philosophy elective, I would like help evaluating how well these strategies actually hold up against one another. Please rank the following six responses to the money-pump argument from most to least successful at blocking the threatened exploitation without conceding something just as costly to the agent's overall rationality:

- Sophisticated choice: the agent uses backward induction to foresee where the sequence of trades is heading and simply declines to enter it.
- Resolute choice (McClennen): the agent commits to a plan at the very first choice node and sticks to it even when later tempted to deviate.
- Biting the bullet: conceding that the agent can be pumped for money, but insisting this only shows a practical vulnerability, not a genuine failure of rationality.
- Time-slice rationality (associated with Brian Hedden): the view that rational requirements only ever govern an agent at a single moment, so a pattern of choices spread out over time cannot by itself convict anyone of irrationality.
- Modeling indifference as a semiorder with a just-noticeable-difference threshold (Luce): what looks like a cycle is only ever a cycle of indifference, never of strict preference, so no exploitable cycle actually exists.
- Denying that rational preference must be complete in the first place, on the grounds that some pairs of options are merely "on a par" rather than better, worse, or equally good (Ruth Chang's notion of parity).
