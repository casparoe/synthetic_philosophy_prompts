For the last few months I've been part of a small, church-affiliated network that shelters people avoiding immigration enforcement, and more than once I've had to decide, in real time, how to answer an officer's direct question at someone's door. I was raised Catholic and still take the tradition's moral reasoning seriously, so I went back to Augustine, who is usually cited as the source of the Church's hardest line against lying, hoping he might leave me some room to maneuver. Below is a short piece I've drafted for a reading group at my parish. I'd like comments on two things in particular: whether I've represented Augustine's actual position fairly, and whether the argument in the last two paragraphs really holds up.

Augustine wrote two treatises specifically on lying, roughly twenty-five years apart: De Mendacio and Contra Mendacium. Neither budges. In De Mendacio he sorts lies into about eight kinds, graded by how much harm and benefit they cause and to whom, running from lies told about God and religious doctrine down to lies that hurt no one and are told only to save an innocent life or protect someone's chastity. Even that last, least serious kind he still calls sin. Contra Mendacium, written years later to a layman named Consentius who had proposed smuggling an agent into the Priscillianist heretics under a false identity in order to expose their secret rites, is if anything less forgiving: not one lie is permitted, Augustine insists, however good the cause and however small its content.

What Augustine does permit is silence, a true but incomplete answer, or simply refusing to respond. He thinks there is a real difference between asserting something false and withholding, by any legal means, the whole truth. That distinction is what first drew me back to him: it seemed to license exactly the evasive-but-technically-true answers I've caught myself giving at the door, while leaving his blanket prohibition on lying intact.

I no longer think the distinction earns its keep. What makes lying wrong, on Augustine's own account, is the will to deceive expressed through a mismatch between what is in one's heart and what one signifies to another - not the narrower fact that the signal took the form of an asserted falsehood rather than an implicature. If Augustine forbids me from saying "I haven't seen him" about someone hiding in my basement, it's hard to see why he should not equally forbid my saying, quite truthfully, "there's no one here I can send you to," in a tone and context engineered to produce exactly the same false belief in the officer's mind, with exactly the same intention behind it. The vehicle changes; the will to deceive, which is where Augustine locates the sin, does not. So either his permission to mislead by true statement isn't really available to someone in my position, or his account of where the wrongness of lying resides can't do the work he needs it to do in keeping assertion and implicature apart.

Augustine spent a great deal of care on this question, so I doubt the tension is one he simply missed. What's the strongest Augustinian reply available here, and does it actually work?
