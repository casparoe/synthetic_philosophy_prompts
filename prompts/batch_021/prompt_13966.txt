I've followed Robin Hanson's writing on futarchy since my early lurking days on Overcoming Bias and, later, LessWrong -- the kind of thing I otherwise only ever got to argue about in blog comment sections -- and about eighteen months ago I finally persuaded the board of the forty-person worker-owned software cooperative I belong to to pilot a modest version of 'vote on values, bet on beliefs' governance. Members vote quarterly on the weights of a composite welfare index built from trailing revenue per member, voluntary turnover, and client-satisfaction scores; for any major fork in strategy, we then run paired conditional markets, settled in an internal points currency rather than real money, on the index's likely value three years out under each option, and the board pre-commits to recommending whichever option the market rates higher. It worked cleanly for small things, like choosing a project-management tool and a parental-leave policy, and made converts even of members who'd found the whole idea faintly ridiculous at first.

The pilot is now facing its first serious test. A larger firm has offered to acquire us, converting everyone's patronage shares into cash and restricted stock in the acquirer and ending our one-member-one-vote structure; the charter still requires a supermajority of the membership to approve any such change, but the futarchy markets were supposed to settle what recommendation the board would bring into that vote. For weeks, the 'accept' market traded at a modest but clear premium over 'decline.' Three days before the board meeting where we're due to finalize that recommendation, a member doing routine due diligence noticed that three trading accounts opened the week the talks leaked -- registered to a contractor the co-op hadn't worked with in over a year and to two relatives of a senior engineer -- had placed large, suspiciously well-timed 'accept' bets as trading thinned toward the deadline, and that the engineer's retention bonus is contingent on the deal closing. Half the board wants to honor the market's verdict anyway, on the grounds that Hanson's own research on manipulation suggests attempts like this tend to invite corrective trading from better-informed participants and so are often self-defeating rather than harmful; the other half doubts that result was ever meant to travel to a market this thin, where informed participation has never numbered more than a dozen or so members on a good week. A quieter third faction has raised what I privately think is the sharper worry: that the whole vote-on-values/bet-on-beliefs split presupposes the welfare index was settled well enough in advance to serve as a neutral scoreboard, and that this decision is revealing how much that index was always going to smuggle in a judgment about payout versus mission -- one no index can actually settle.

I'm one of the five board members and need to circulate a memo before Friday's emergency meeting, short enough that five busy people will actually read the whole thing. Please draft it. It should reach one concrete recommendation -- ratify the market's 'accept' signal and forward it to the membership vote as planned, delay that recommendation pending a specific, time-bounded investigation into the suspicious accounts, or set the futarchy result aside for this decision and let the board form its recommendation the ordinary way -- and defend that choice. I want it to take the manipulation allegation seriously on its own terms, weigh honestly whether overriding the market now would cost the mechanism more credibility over the long run than any single bad call could, and not dodge the deeper objection about the welfare index by treating this as a purely procedural question about market integrity.
