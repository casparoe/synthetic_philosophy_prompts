I'm working on a novel that has a subplot riffing on Alan Turing — not a straight biopic, more of a "what if he'd somehow lived long enough to watch the field he basically invented turn into what it is now" kind of thing. While digging around for research I went back and actually reread his 1950 Mind paper properly instead of just skimming the famous bits, and I got kind of stuck on the section near the end where he takes on what he literally titles "the argument from extra-sensory perception" as an objection to the imitation game. He doesn't just wave it off — he says the statistical evidence for telepathy available at the time struck him as basically overwhelming, and he seriously suggests you'd need to run the test in a "telepathy-proof room" so a human contestant couldn't gain an edge by reading the interrogator's mind. Its such a bizarre, very-of-its-moment detail that I want to build a whole scene around it, but my writing group thinks it's too niche and keeps telling me to cut it.

Before I decide, I'd like to actually get the intellectual history right instead of just winging it. Turing died in '54, so he never lived to see what happened to that "overwhelming" evidence: the single most famous card-guessing result, the Soal-Goldney experiments, got exposed in the late 70s as almost certainly the product of doctored data, and then decades after that Daryl Bem published his precognition studies in a mainstream psychology journal, which a lot of people now credit (or blame) for helping kick off the whole replication crisis once other researchers started reanalyzing his numbers in a Bayesian framework and watched the effect basically dissolve. Given how seriously Turing took statistics and empirical evidence generally (the man broke codes for a living), do you think he'd have been the type to fully recant that section once all this came out, or is there something in how he actually reasoned about it in the first place that suggests he might have found a way to keep some sympathy for the idea regardless? I'd like a real, argued-out answer rather than a one-liner, since there's a decent chance a version of it ends up in a character's mouth.
