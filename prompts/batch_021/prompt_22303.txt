So I'm an econ PhD student (game theory / mechanism design person, not a historian of thought by any stretch) but this term I'm sitting in on a history-of-economic-thought seminar mostly because my advisor thinks it'll be "good for me," and this week we had to chew through the justice-in-exchange bit of Book V of the Nicomachean Ethics. Every textbook summary of this passage says some version of "Aristotle basically invented the theory of value / supply and demand," and I went in expecting something clean, but the actual argument is way more tangled than the secondary lit lets on. Here's the chunk we've got for Thursday's discussion section (Ross's translation, the one everyone seems to assign; it's from Book V ch. 5, Bekker numbers something like 1133a-1133b if that helps):

"Now proportionate return is secured by cross-conjunction. Let A be a builder, B a shoemaker, C a house, D a shoe. The builder, then, must get from the shoemaker the latter's work, and must himself give him in return his own. If, then, first there is proportionate equality of goods, and then reciprocal action takes place, the result we mention will be effected. If not, the bargain is not equal, and does not hold; for there is nothing to prevent the work of the one being better than that of the other; they must therefore be equated. (And this is true of the other arts also; for they would have been destroyed if what the patient suffered had not been just what the agent did, and of the same amount and kind.) For it is not two doctors that associate for exchange, but a doctor and a farmer, or in general people who are different and unequal; but these must be equated. The number of shoes exchanged for a house (or for a given amount of food) must therefore correspond to the ratio of builder to shoemaker. For if this be not so, there will be no exchange and no intercourse. And this proportion will not be effected unless the goods are somehow equal. All goods must therefore be measured by some one thing, as we said before. Now this unit is in truth demand, which holds all things together (for if men did not need one another's goods at all, or did not need them equally, there would be either no exchange or not the same exchange); but money has become by convention a sort of representative of demand; and this is why it has the name 'money' (nomisma) — because it exists not by nature but by law (nomos) and it is in our power to change it and make it useless."

What's tripping me up (and my one classmate who's a classicist by training couldn't fully talk me out of it either) is that Aristotle seems to be running two different equalizations together, and I can't tell if thats a real tension in his account or just me misreading 100 year old English. For most of the passage the thing getting "equated" sounds like it's the producers themselves — builder to shoemaker, doctor to farmer, "people who are different and unequal" — like some ratio of their relative worth, skill, or labor has to balance for the trade to be fair. But then right at the end he says the actual unit of measurement, the thing money merely stands in for, is demand (chreia) — which sounds much more like a subjective, want-based story, closer to what we'd now call utility, and not obviously the same quantity as "how good a builder is relative to a shoemaker."

So: in Aristotle's own argument, is the builder/shoemaker equalization just a restatement of the demand-based story (i.e. demand is what fixes the ratio, and the producer-talk is just shorthand for that), or is he actually running an objective, cost/labor-flavored notion of equalization alongside the demand-based one without noticing the seam? I also vaguely remember hearing there's a whole fight in the literature over whether Marx's famous reading of this exact passage in Capital vol. 1 is fair to Aristotle, or projects a labor theory of value onto him that isn't really there — if that's relevant to untangling this I'd love to know how. Basically I want an actual close reading of what work "equated," "demand," and "nomisma" are each doing in getting him from "goods are really incommensurable" to "but money measures them well enough for practical purposes" — not the one-line "Aristotle discovered supply and demand" gloss I keep getting from people who clearly haven't read past the first sentence.
