I am a PhD student in philosophy working on the ethics of war, and I'm drafting a dissertation chapter on Elizabeth Anscombe's 1956 pamphlet "Mr Truman's Degree," written against Oxford's decision to award Truman an honorary doctorate. I want to check my reconstruction of her argument before I build on it further: as I currently read her, the case ultimately turns on proportion — the bombs killed civilians on a scale so disproportionate to any plausible strategic gain that the action fails a straightforward cost-benefit test, and her repeated use of the word "murder" functions mainly as rhetorical emphasis on how badly that test is failed. The difficulty is that Anscombe was demonstrably not a pacifist and elsewhere accepted that large-scale killing in wartime can be justified by sufficiently weighty military ends, which leaves me unsure what makes her cost-benefit verdict on the bombing come out so differently from the cost-benefit verdicts she was prepared to accept elsewhere. Have I misidentified the structure of her argument, or does her position really just amount to a stricter accounting of costs and benefits than the one offered by Truman's defenders?
