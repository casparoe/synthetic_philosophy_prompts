I'm a PhD student in epistemology, and this term's seminar on peer disagreement has left me stuck on a reflexivity worry that our instructor mentioned only in passing and that I haven't seen fully resolved in the assigned readings. The basic thought is this: conciliationism -- the view, associated with writers like Adam Elga and David Christensen, that on discovering an epistemic peer disagrees with you about some claim you should move your credence substantially in their direction -- is itself a controversial thesis within epistemology. Plenty of careful, well-informed philosophers (Thomas Kelly and Jennifer Lackey, for instance) reject it in favor of some version of steadfastness or an intermediate "justificationist" position. If conciliationists are willing to apply their own principle consistently, shouldn't the sheer existence of this informed, persistent disagreement about conciliationism itself require them to lower their confidence in conciliationism, by conciliationism's own lights? And if the answer is yes, doesn't the view risk a kind of vertigo in which no one could ever be stably confident in the correct theory of disagreement -- including whatever theory tells us when it's rational to be stably confident in anything?

I recall that Elga addresses something like this worry in his "How to Disagree about How to Disagree," and that his way out turns on the thought that judgments about who counts as a genuine epistemic peer can never be made from some neutral, view-from-nowhere standpoint, so the equal-weight view was never committed to the kind of unrestricted self-application the objection assumes -- but I don't trust my memory of the details enough to know whether that move actually defuses the problem or just relocates it one level up. Could you reconstruct the strongest version of this self-undermining objection to conciliationism, lay out Elga's reply (correcting my gloss on it as needed) or whichever serious reply you think fares best, and then say whether you think the objection ultimately shows that conciliationism is unstable in a way that steadfastness or Lackey-style justificationism is not?
