I sit on the steering committee for a smallish regional SFF award — a scrappy, volunteer-run thing where maybe 150-200 members vote in a given year — and I need a memo drafted before our meeting this coming Saturday. Here's the situation: in the Best Novel category last year we had five finalists, three of which were fairly similar cozy mysteries that ended up splitting the sympathetic vote three ways, while a horror novella that maybe a third of the membership loved (and the rest ranked near dead last) won outright under our plain one-pick, most-votes-wins ballot. Nobody cheated exactly, but a lot of members feel the winner didn't reflect what people actually wanted, and there's now a loud push to switch to ranked-choice voting next year so "this can't happen again."

Here's my problem: our treasurer, who has an actual math background and loves to remind everyone of it, keeps shooting this down at meetings by invoking a theorem — I'm fairly sure it's the Gibbard-Satterthwaithe theorem, though he sometimes calls it "the Arrow thing" and I don't think he keeps the two straight himself (I'm not sure I do either, honestly, so don't trust my spelling above). His point, as far as I can follow it, is that once you've got three or more finalists, no voting rule can be made fully safe from someone benefiting by voting insincerely, so switching to ranked-choice is just theater that'll make people feel better without actually fixing anything. Half the commitee thinks he's being a contrarian for its own sake and wants to move on; I suspect he's got a real point buried in there somewhere, but I don't trust either of us to represent it fairly to the rest of the group.

Could you draft the memo I'll circulate before Saturday? I want it to actually land on one recommendation — keep plain plurality, switch to ranked-choice, switch to approval voting, whatever you think is most defensible — rather than a wishy-washy list of pros and cons, and it needs to be written for writers, not mathematicians, since that's who's in the room. But please take the treasurer's objection seriously on its own terms instead of waving it off: if the theorem really does mean no system can be made fully manipulation-proof, say plainly what that does and doesn't imply about whether we should switch anyway. (Side note: I'm shameless enough to be filing this whole saga away for a subplot in the novel I'm drafting about a backstabbing little writers' guild, so don't feel like you need to sand off the pettier details on my account.)
