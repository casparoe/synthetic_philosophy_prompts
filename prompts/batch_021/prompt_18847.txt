My parents are celebrating their fiftieth wedding anniversary at a family dinner in three weeks, and as their eldest child I have been asked to give the toast. Six weeks ago my father was diagnosed with early-stage Alzheimer's; in conversation he is still entirely himself, still finishes my mother's sentences the way he always has, but the diagnosis is now something the whole family carries into every gathering whether or not anyone mentions it, and this dinner will be the first big occasion since we found out. I am also halfway through a graduate seminar on the metaphysics of persistence, and the debate between endurantism and perdurantism -- whether a persisting thing is wholly present at every moment of its existence, or is instead a four-dimensional whole composed of distinct temporal parts unified by relations of continuity -- has stopped feeling like a seminar exercise. Our professor kept returning to Lewis's problem of temporary intrinsics, the puzzle of how one and the same thing can be bent at one time and straight at another, and I now find myself running that same puzzle in the background whenever I think about what these fifty years add up to, and about what it would mean, on either view, if my father continues to change.

I would like a toast of about four minutes, built on this distinction, that reads as a toast rather than as a philosophy lecture that happens to be delivered at a family dinner. It should let me say something specific about persistence and change, rather than falling back on the usual line that people change while the love stays the same. And it should not quietly help itself to whichever view -- an enduring substance carried whole through decline, or a four-dimensional life in which every stage remains undiminished by whatever happens to later stages -- happens to be more comforting, unless that preference is actually earned rather than assumed. My cousin, who is finishing a philosophy degree this year, will be at the table and will notice if the toast fudges the theory to get to the comforting part faster; everyone else has never heard either term and shouldn't need to in order to feel it land. If it turns out there is something true that can be said on either view, without having to declare a winner between them, that would be the best outcome; if not, I would rather the toast sit honestly with that tension than paper over it. Please write the toast.
