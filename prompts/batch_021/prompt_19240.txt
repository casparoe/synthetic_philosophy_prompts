> Van Inwagen's Special Composition Question (SCQ) asks: given some objects, the xs, under what conditions is there a further object y that the xs compose? This is different from what he calls the General Composition Question (GCQ), which asks for a non-mereological account of what composition itself is — i.e. what it is for some things to compose something, full stop, rather than when they do it. Van Inwagen thinks the GCQ is probably unanswerable in any illuminating way, but he's more optimistic that the SCQ has a true and informative answer.
>
> He sorts candidate answers into "moderate" answers, on which composition occurs for some arrangements of the xs but not others (e.g. answers appealing to contact, fastening, or the xs forming a cohesive whole), and two "extreme" answers that give the same verdict no matter how the xs are arranged: Universalism (defended by David Lewis), on which any xs whatsoever compose something, and Nihilism (defended by Cian Dorr and Ted Sider), on which composition never occurs and the only objects are mereological simples.
>
> Van Inwagen's argument from vagueness is supposed to rule out every moderate answer. The relations moderate answers appeal to — contact, fastening, and so on — admit of borderline cases: it can be indeterminate whether two things count as "fastened" rather than just touching. If whether the xs compose something turned on such a relation, it would sometimes be indeterminate whether a y exists that they compose, and hence indeterminate how many objects are in a given region. This is essentially Evans's proof against vague identity transposed into a new key: just as Evans argued that "a=b" can never be a genuinely indeterminate matter, on pain of violating Leibniz's Law, van Inwagen argues that whether the xs compose some y can never be genuinely indeterminate either, since that is ultimately a question about the identity of an object with a certain description. Since existence and identity can't be vague, no moderate answer, which makes composition depend on inherently vague relations, can be correct.
>
> That leaves the two extreme answers, plus van Inwagen's own proposal. He rejects Universalism because it commits us to absurd, gerrymandered objects (a fusion of your nose and the Eiffel Tower counts as a single thing on this view), and he rejects Nihilism because it can't accommodate the existence of ordinary composite objects, most importantly organisms like ourselves — and van Inwagen wants to hold onto the datum that we exist as material things with parts. His own answer, Organicism, says the xs compose something just in case their activity constitutes a life; the only composite objects are living organisms (plus, trivially, anything composing itself). This gives him a sharp, non-moderate-looking answer that's still less costly than the two extremes, because — unlike "fastening" or "being stuck together" — whether some activity constitutes a life isn't the kind of thing that admits of borderline cases, so the vagueness argument doesn't touch it.
