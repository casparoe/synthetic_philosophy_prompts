I'm a PhD student working on formal epistemology and decision theory, and I'm still trying to sort out a disagreement that derailed our decision theory reading group last week. We had been going through updateless decision theory (UDT) and its stock example, the "counterfactual mugging": an infallible predictor flips a fair coin. If it lands heads, the predictor approaches you and asks for $100, which you are entirely free to refuse. If it lands tails, the predictor gives you $10,000 with no strings attached in that branch -- but whether it does so at all depends on a prediction it made before flipping about what you would do if the coin had instead landed heads: it pays out on tails only to those it predicted would have paid on heads. In the scenario under discussion, the coin lands heads, and the predictor duly asks you for the money.

Person A defended the updateless verdict (pay), and Person B pushed back, across several rounds. (1) A argued that the policy "pay whenever asked" has far higher expected value ex ante than "refuse whenever asked," since with an infallible predictor your disposition is what fixes the payoff in both branches, so a rational agent should want to be, and therefore should be, a payer. (2) B answered that wanting, in advance, to have a certain disposition is not the same as its being rational to act on that disposition once you are actually in the heads branch, knowing the coin came up heads; at that point paying has a real cost and no possible causal benefit, since the tails branch and its $10,000 are simply no longer live possibilities. (3) A replied that this is exactly the reasoning that makes the "rational in the moment" agent self-undermining: an infallible predictor would foresee it, so such an agent is denied the $10,000 in the tails branch as well, meaning a decision rule that provably does worse in expectation cannot be what rationality actually recommends. (4) B countered that this trades on an equivocation between doing well ex ante, aggregated over branches that have not occurred, and doing well in your actual situation once the relevant uncertainty is already resolved -- there is no expectation left to optimize once the coin has landed, and insisting otherwise just assumes the updateless view rather than arguing for it. (5) A pressed further that the same reasoning would make it "rational," once actually facing the sirens, for Ulysses to break free of the mast, even though everyone agrees the wise move was to have had himself tied down in advance; accepting the earlier commitment as rational while refusing to follow through when the moment comes amounts to recommending self-defeating advice. (6) B closed by denying the analogy: the sirens work by temporarily corrupting Ulysses's preferences, so tying him down protects his stable, considered preferences from that corruption, whereas nothing in the counterfactual mugging corrupts anyone's preferences -- you still prefer keeping the $100 to not keeping it, and paying will not retroactively bring about anything.

Neither side budged, and I genuinely don't know how to score it. Could you go through the exchange move by move and say, for each of (1) through (6), who has the better of the argument at that point, rather than just declaring an overall winner? I would also like to know whether there is some further consideration -- one that neither speaker raised -- that would help settle whether the relevant notion of rationality here should be indexed to policies chosen in advance or to acts assessed in their actual context, and, in light of that, which broader position -- the updateless one or the more traditional causal one -- you find closer to correct.
