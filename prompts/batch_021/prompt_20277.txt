For a project in my personal-finance class, my group interviewed the director who oversees my high school's scholarship endowment about how she decides how much financial aid to pay out to current students versus save for future ones. She told us her formula discounts the value of aid promised to a future student by about 5% per year, split into two pieces: roughly 3 percentage points because society will likely be wealthier by then, so an identical dollar of aid does a bit less for an equally deserving but better-off future student than for an equally deserving student today; and a separate 2 percentage points she calls "pure impatience" — helping a student now simply counts for more than helping an equally deserving, equally well-off student fifty years from now, for no reason beyond the second student coming later. My group is fine with the first component, but a couple of us are uneasy about the second and can't quite say why. Once wealth differences are already priced in, is there a principled reason to object to discounting a future student's benefit just for arriving later, or are we just uncomfortable admitting that time itself might be a legitimate basis for treating equally deserving people differently?
