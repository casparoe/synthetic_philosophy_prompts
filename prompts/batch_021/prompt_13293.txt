I co-host a small philosophy podcast a few of us started as part of my high school's debate club, and next week's episode is with a guest speaker: a legal philosopher who has just published a book reviving Patrick Devlin's case for legal moralism and applying it to a live policy question. He argues that commercial surrogacy, currently legal in a number of U.S. states, should be a criminal offense — not because it harms anyone directly, but because tolerating it corrodes the shared morality that, on his account, is what makes a political community a community in the first place. I watched a recording of a talk he gave at another school last spring, and whenever an audience member pressed him on whether this "disintegration thesis" has any actual evidence behind it, he deflected by insisting it isn't meant as a sociological prediction at all, just a conceptual point about what a society's identity consists in — so pointing to societies that liberalized their morals and never collapsed apparently misses the point. We covered the original Hart-Devlin exchange in my "Law and Society" elective, so I know the basic shape of the argument, but I don't want our episode to turn into him giving that same talk again while we nod along.

Could you draft eight to ten interview questions for me? I'd like the first three to form a short sequence: start by granting him that opening move, then press progressively harder on it, and end with a question about what, if anything, he'd accept as evidence against his own view. For the rest, I want at least one question on why a community's feelings of intolerance, indignation, and disgust should be allowed to ground the criminal law at all, given how often such feelings have historically tracked nothing more than prejudice; one built on Hart's charge that Devlin conflates a society's actual ("positive") morality with a morality that is actually defensible ("critical"), asking him to justify why the law should defer to the former; and one built on Dworkin's objection that a lot of apparent moral consensus is really just prejudice, or a reaction to mistaken beliefs of fact, or people repeating what they've been told, dressed up as conviction. For each of these three, please add a short follow-up question keyed to the answer he's most likely to give, so that I'm not caught off guard if he simply falls back on his standard line.
