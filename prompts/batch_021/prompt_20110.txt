I've spent the past couple of weeks falling down a population-ethics rabbit hole, set off by rereading the "Mere Addition Paradox" chapters of Parfit's Reasons and Persons alongside a stack of old LessWrong and EA Forum threads arguing over which of the field's various "unacceptable conclusions" is the least bad one to swallow. My reading group has since split into two camps that I haven't been able to fully adjudicate on my own, so I'd like your help thinking it through more rigorously than either side has managed so far.

One camp simply accepts the Total View and bites the bullet on the Repugnant Conclusion: since the value of a population is just the sum of welfare across its members, a sufficiently large population of lives barely worth living can always be made to outweigh a much smaller population living in great flourishing, no matter how high the latter's per-person welfare is. The other camp prefers Critical-Level Utilitarianism, where a fixed positive constant is subtracted from every person's welfare before the totals are summed, so that a life only counts as a net-positive contribution once it clears that bar; they claim this blocks the Repugnant Conclusion. What nobody can agree on is whether the critical-level move is real progress or just a relocation of the same problem, since it is usually paired with something its proponents are less eager to advertise: the so-called Sadistic Conclusion. This says that, for any number of decent-but-sub-threshold lives one might add to a population, there is some smaller number of lives containing genuine suffering that the theory rates as the better addition instead.

Could you compare the two views in some depth: how each is formally defined, how each handles the step-by-step logic of the Mere Addition Paradox differently (in particular, which premise Critical-Level Utilitarianism actually gets to deny), and whether Critical-Level Utilitarianism escapes the Repugnant Conclusion for good or merely relocates the threshold at which a structurally similar conclusion reappears. Then, in the spirit of Arrhenius's impossibility results, I would like an actual verdict rather than a survey of objections: given that some bullet here seems unavoidable, is the Sadistic Conclusion really more defensible than the Repugnant Conclusion, and does Critical-Level Utilitarianism get us any closer to an acceptable population axiology, or is it just trading one failure mode for a differently shaped one? Please justify your answer rather than simply restating the standard objections to each side.
