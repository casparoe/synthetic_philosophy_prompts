In my Latin class we've been reading Cicero's De Senectute, and I'd like to apply its argument to a question it obviously never anticipated. Cato defends old age through a few connected moves: the affairs that matter most -- governing, advising, judging -- run on judgment and authority rather than physical strength, so they needn't decline along with the body; much of what gets blamed on old age is really the delayed cost of an indulgent youth, so an old man should use whatever strength remains moderately rather than chase a young man's vigor; losing the sharp edge of appetite is treated as a genuine gain rather than a loss, since it frees the mind for the calmer goods proper to that stage of life; and death itself shouldn't be feared or endlessly postponed, since a life, like a play, has a fitting point at which to end. Given all four of those moves, how do you think Cicero would judge today's biomedical longevity movement -- the push to use drugs, gene therapies, and strict regimens to halt or reverse aging so a person can stay biologically youthful indefinitely and keep deferring that 'final act'? Would he treat this as just a more capable version of the moderate self-care Cato already endorses, or as running against the deeper logic of his argument, which depends on each stage of life having its own proper good rather than one stage stretched out forever? Please reconstruct the argument he would actually make from his own premises, rather than just giving me a quick verdict.
