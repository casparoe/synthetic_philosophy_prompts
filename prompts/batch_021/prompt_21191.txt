I'm about a third of the way into a novel whose plot turns on a research team discovering a physical regularity — something with all the predictive robustness of a conservation law — that appears to have governed exactly one event in the universe's history, with no other instances before or since. A friend who trained in philosophy before switching to fiction read the draft and told me that the central scene, in which two characters argue over whether their discovery even deserves to be called a "law," doesn't really hold up philosophically as written, and that I should go read up on the dispute between Humeans and anti-Humeans about laws of nature before revising it. I've since worked through David Lewis's Humean "best-system" account, on which a law is simply a theorem of whichever true deductive systematization of the entire Humean mosaic of events best balances simplicity and strength, and D. M. Armstrong's anti-Humean rival, on which laws are relations of natural necessitation holding between universals — and I'd like to get the underlying dispute right before I trust myself to put it in a character's mouth.

Here is the specific difficulty I keep coming back to. Armstrong treats cases like my one-off regularity as a central advantage of his view over the Humean's: a necessitation relation between two universals, he insists, can obtain even if it is instantiated only once, or never, whereas a Humean regularity theorist seemingly needs an actual, repeated pattern in the mosaic of events before there is anything for a law to be. But this has always struck me as sitting badly with Armstrong's own official, broadly Aristotelian commitment that universals are immanent rather than Platonic — that a universal exists only in, and by virtue of, its instances. If a universal doesn't exist at all prior to being instantiated, it's not obvious that Armstrong is any better placed than Lewis to say what makes a once-instantiated (or barely-instantiated) regularity a genuine law rather than a cosmic accident. Could you reconstruct both sides of this dispute carefully, work out whether Armstrong's theory actually escapes the problem he presses against Humeanism once his own commitments about universals are taken seriously, and tell me which account — if either — leaves my characters on firmer ground when they insist that what they've found is a law and not a coincidence?
