My only real exposure to Bayesian reasoning comes from machine learning, where picking the "best" hypothesis simply means the one with the highest posterior probability once a prior penalizing needless complexity has been fixed. That background has left me puzzled by how philosophers of science present inference to the best explanation (IBE) as a genuinely distinct, non-deductive rule of inference, one supposed to do work that Bayesian conditionalization cannot do on its own. Peter Lipton, in his book on the subject, tries to preserve this independence by distinguishing the "likeliest" explanation from the "loveliest" one, then arguing that loveliness is our guide to likeliness. Please argue that this distinction does not secure any independent inferential role for IBE: that whatever makes an explanation lovely — simplicity, scope, unification, and so on — is only ever truth-conducive because it already encodes prior or likelihood information a Bayesian has access to anyway, so that a properly reconstructed IBE just is Bayesian inference with a built-in simplicity prior, rather than a distinct rule standing alongside it.
