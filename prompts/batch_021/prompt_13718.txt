So I've been on a bit of a Bayesian epistemology kick lately after re-reading a stack of old Overcoming Bias posts, and I fell down a rabbit hole into Clark Glymour's "problem of old evidence." The setup still kind of messes with my head: standard Bayesian confirmation theory says evidence E confirms hypothesis H (relative to background knowledge K) when P(H|E,K) is bigger than P(H|K). But if E is something you already knew for certain *before* H was even proposed, then P(E|K) is already 1, so conditionalizing on it again literally cannot move your credence in H even a little. And yet the textbook example is Mercury's perihelion precession (that ~43 arcsec/century wobble Newtonian gravity couldn't account for), which everybody, including Einstein himself, treated as a huge confirmation of general relativity back in 1915, even though the data had been sitting around since Le Verrier's calculations decades earlier. That really feels like a bug in naive Bayesian updating, not just some weird edge case.

I brought this up with an old friend from my philosophy-of-science reading group and we ended up arguing about it for like an hour. I've seen the "logical omniscience" fix floating around (something like Garber and Jeffrey's move, where the real new evidence isn't E itself but the discovery that H entails E, since nobody knew that deductive fact beforehand), and I've also come across newer "counterfactual"/synchronic approaches that try to construct a hypothetical prior for E without literally asking the scientist to forget things they already know. Then theres the Earman line from Bayes or Bust, which is basically that this whole mess shows Bayesian confirmation theory can't be a complete story about evidential support all by itself. My friend is convinced the logical-omniscience move basically dissolves the problem; I lean more towards Earman's pessimism about it.

Can you write me an essay that lays the problem out properly, actually weighs these solutions against each other on their merits rather than just listing them like a Wikipedia page, and then commits to an actual verdict instead of ending on "reasonable people disagree"? It's totally fine if the verdict is conditional (e.g. "solution X handles cases shaped like Mercury but not cases shaped like Y") as long as you argue for it rather than just asserting it.
