Several years ago I turned down a stable, well-paid position to take a far riskier one in a field I found more meaningful, and to this day I cannot decide, even in hindsight, whether that was the better choice, the worse choice, or simply a wash -- it feels like none of the three. Reading around the subject on my own, I discovered that philosophers who study practical reason have a genuine, technical disagreement about exactly this kind of situation. One camp, associated with Ruth Chang, holds that when neither option is better, worse, nor precisely equal, the two stand in a distinct fourth relation -- being "on a par" -- and that this relation is what makes such choices hard rather than merely difficult to calculate. Another camp, associated with John Broome, denies that any special fourth relation is needed: what looks like parity is really just vagueness in the ordinary "better than" relation, the same kind of indeterminacy that makes it unclear exactly when a pile of sand becomes a heap. I would like to see this argued out rather than summarized. Please write a dialogue between a philosopher defending parity as a genuine fourth value relation and one defending the vagueness account. Each should state their strongest case, directly challenge how the other would describe a choice like mine, and respond to the objection that if two options were truly equal in value, even a slight improvement to one of them should make it the outright winner -- yet a slight improvement to one side of a hard choice does not obviously make it the winner either. Neither side should get the last word unanswered.
