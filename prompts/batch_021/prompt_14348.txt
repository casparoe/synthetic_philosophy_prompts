I'm taking a seminar on decision theory this term, and we recently covered Hofstadter's notion of superrationality. The idea is that in a symmetric, one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma, agents who reason identically should cooperate: any line of reasoning that led one of them to defect would, by symmetry, lead the other to defect as well, leaving mutual cooperation and mutual defection as the only self-consistent outcomes, and cooperation is jointly better. My professor mentioned, almost as an aside, that this has a distinctly Kantian ring to it, since it turns on asking what would happen if everyone reasoned exactly as you do — which sounds close to the universalizability test behind the categorical imperative. I'm building my term paper around this comparison, and I want to argue for an actual position rather than just note the resemblance: would Kant have recognized superrational cooperation as a genuine instance of acting on the categorical imperative, or does his ethics give him principled grounds to reject the analogy? I'd like your reasoned verdict, and the argument behind it, rather than a simple list of similarities and differences.
