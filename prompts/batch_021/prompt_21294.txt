So I'm nearly done with a thriller where the antagonist, Marek, has had a neural implant since his old agency days that will override his motor cortex and force him to pull the trigger if he ever starts to hesitate—except he never hesitates, not once in the whole book, so the implant just sits there unused the entire time. My editor loves this as the philosophical hook of the novel and wants a short "author's note" at the back explaining where the idea comes from, since its not something I made up, it's an actual famous thought experiment from a 1969 paper by Harry Frankfurt about whether moral responsibility requires that you could've done otherwise. Below is my attempt at the note, and I'd love a read on whether I'm getting the philosophy right, because my editor is the type to forward it to her brother who has a PhD in this stuff, and I'd rather not get corrected in front of everyone.

"This novel's central image—a man who commits an act entirely of his own will, even though a device would have forced him to do it anyway had he hesitated—borrows from Harry Frankfurt's famous argument against the idea that being morally responsible requires that you could have done otherwise. The twist is that the controlling device never actually has to switch on, so from the outside the whole thing looks exactly like an ordinary free choice. Some philosophers have tried to defend the 'could have done otherwise' requirement anyway, by pointing out that Marek could still have shown some flicker of hesitation—a trembling hand, a half-second pause—even though he couldn't have stopped the shot itself, and that this flicker is really all the 'alternative possibility' the theory ever needed. A separate objection points out that for the implant to reliably catch every case of hesitation before it turns into a change of heart, the sign of hesitation would basically have to guarantee the outcome on its own, which quietly assumes determinism is true from the start, and so doesn't really prove anything to someone who already thinks free choices can't be determined."

Do those last two sentences actually function as two different objections, the way I've laid them out, or have I taken one point and made it sound like two separate ones? And more broadly, is the "flicker of hesitation" stuff even fair to include for a general reader, or is it going to look like I'm undermining my own premise? Also completely fine to just tell me to cut the whole note down to two sentences, I just don't want to put something in print that's confidently wrong.
