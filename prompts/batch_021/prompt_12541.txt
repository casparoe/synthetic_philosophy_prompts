I'm a retired geologist, and since hanging up my hammer a couple of years back I've been trying to fill the gap with philosophy, which I never had proper time for during my working life beyond one first-year course decades ago. Lately I've been down a bit of an effective-altruism-adjacent reading rabbit hole, and I ran into an idea called "moral trade" that's been rattling around my head all week. The basic idea, best as I can reconstruct it: imagine a woman who's a committed vegetarian on moral grounds (she thinks eating meat is wrong) but couldn't care less about her own carbon footprint, and a man who's fanatical about offsetting every flight (he thinks unoffset flying is wrong) but happily eats steak every night and sees nothing wrong with that. Neither thinks the other's core conviction is correct, but they can still strike a deal: he pays her to cut back on her flying, a real sacrifice for her but not one she believes is morally required, so cash squares it, and she pays him to cut back on his meat-eating, same deal in reverse. The upshot is a world with a bit less flying, which is what he wanted all along, and a bit less meat-eating, which is what she wanted all along -- both come out ahead by their own lights, and neither has budged an inch on what they actually think is right. Its basically comparative advantage, except applied to ethics instead of wine and cloth.

This got me thinking about Adam Smith of all people. He's the guy who explained how self-interested exchange between people who don't agree on much can still make everybody richer, but he also wrote a whole separate book grounding morality in sympathy and this "impartial spectator" business rather than in haggling or self-interest. I gather there's even a whole 19th-century German academic dispute usually called "the Adam Smith Problem," about whether his two books actually hang together at all. So here's what I keep getting stuck on: would Smith have looked at "moral trade" and just seen the invisible hand doing its usual trick, now applied to virtue instead of pins, or would the Moral Sentiments side of him be suspicious of it, given that he draws such a sharp line between justice, which he thinks can rightly be extorted by force like a debt, and beneficence, which by his own account can only ever be freely given and never bought? Is a "moral trade" deal the kind of thing his impartial spectator could ever sign off on, or does turning your convictions into bargaining chips corrupt the whole sympathy-based picture he built? I'd love your best reconstruction of his likely reasoning here, grounded in the actual content of his two books rather than just a one-line gut reaction.
