In discussions of higher-order evidence, there's a recognized tension between two ideas. The first is that rationality requires harmony between your first-order beliefs and your higher-order beliefs about how well-supported those beliefs are: you shouldn't end up believing P while also believing that your evidence doesn't actually support P, or that you have no business believing it. Call this the "enkratic principle"; violating it produces a state of epistemic akrasia that many treat as a cousin of Moore's paradox, a combination no fully rational person could stably occupy. The second idea is that once you've actually reasoned correctly at the first order, it can seem perfectly sensible to keep trusting that reasoning even after getting evidence that something has probably gone wrong with your faculties — you learn, say, that you were given a reasoning-impairing drug, or that most equally competent colleagues who double-checked your work disagree with you. Revising in light of such higher-order evidence can just as easily compound an original mistake as correct it, so why not hold your ground? Call this the "level-splitting" response. The two ideas pull hard in opposite directions, and it is genuinely unclear which one should give.

I tried explaining this tension to some non-philosopher friends recently, and it landed as dry technical hairsplitting rather than anything with real bite. Write a joke that actually turns on it — ideally built around a concrete comic setup (a card game, a bar bet, a courtroom, a chemistry exam, or whatever works best) whose punchline depends on genuinely tracking the difference between the akratic combination the enkratic principle rules out and the kind of self-trust the level-splitting response is meant to vindicate. It shouldn't work as a generic "philosophers overthink everything" joke: someone with no idea what higher-order evidence or epistemic akrasia is shouldn't find it funny, while someone who does should immediately recognize which position is being sent up and why.
