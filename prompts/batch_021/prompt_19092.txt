Ok so this thing has been bugging me for weeks and I finally got it into words, curious what you think.

I got into Bayesian stuff a while back from following election forecasting models, and somewhere along the way I ran into a problem that I guess is called "old evidence" (I didnt know the name till I googled around, but nobody online actually explains it in a way that satisfied me, they just state it and move on). Basic idea: Bayes' rule says a theory gets more credible when it correctly anticipates some evidence, and how much more credible depends on how surprising that evidence would've been if the theory were false. But if the evidence was already sitting in your background knowledge before the theory even existed, your probability for it is already 1, so plugging it back into the formula cant move anything, you just get out exactly what you put in. The textbook example is Mercury's orbit: astronomers had already clocked a weird extra wobble in its perihelion decades before Einstein, and then general relativity comes along and its math spits out that exact wobble, and everybody treated that as one of the biggest points in favor of GR there is. But by the "old evidence" logic, a fact that was already known for certain shouldn't be able to confirm anything at all. That seems obviously wrong, so something in the setup has to be off.

Here's the fix I keep landing on: the thing that's actually new isn't the wobble, its the fact that "GR implies that wobble." Before someone sits down and grinds through the equations, that entailment isnt something anyone has actually verified yet, so learning it counts as genuinely new info, and that's what the confirmation is tracking, not the old data point itself.

What I can't decide is whether this actually fixes the problem or just kicks it up a level, cause now you need some probability for a mathematical fact before you've even checked whether it's true, and I don't know what that would even mean. It also feels like it opens a door to cheating: if you built the theory specifically to hit a number you already knew, does "discovering" that it hits the number really deserve the same credit as an actual prediction? Is this a real fix people already thought of, and if it's already been shot down somewhere, what's the actual hole in it?
