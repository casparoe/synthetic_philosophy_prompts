I'm working on a novel with a section set in Paris in the mid-1920s, a few years after Henri Bergson's public clash with Einstein over the nature of time, in which an aging but still formidable Bergson is cornered at a dinner party by a young philosopher of the emerging analytic school and pressed to give his verdict on a metaphysical position the real Bergson never had occasion to consider: perdurantism, the view that a persisting object or person is really a four-dimensional aggregate of momentary temporal parts, "spread out" in time exactly as an object is spread out in space, so that strictly speaking nothing is ever wholly present at any single moment — as against endurantism, on which one and the same whole entity exists, entire, at every instant of its career. I want the scene to be philosophically serious rather than a rigged debate, so before writing it I'd like help thinking through, as rigorously as possible, what Bergson himself would most plausibly say.

My own hunch, for what it's worth, is that he would treat perdurantism as an unusually pure specimen of exactly the error his whole career was organized around diagnosing: the substitution of a spatialized, homogeneous, infinitely divisible "time" for the heterogeneous, indivisible flow he called duration, no genuine phase of which is separable from the whole without falsifying it. But I'd want that objection reconstructed properly, drawing on the actual apparatus of Time and Free Will, Matter and Memory, and Creative Evolution — the contrast between quantitative and qualitative multiplicity, the treatment of memory as the survival of the past within the present rather than its storage in discrete slices, and so on — rather than a generic "Bergson liked flow, four-dimensionalists don't" gesture. I'd also like the argument brought into contact with a concrete case that actually motivates perdurantism in the literature, such as Lewis's problem of temporary intrinsics (how one persisting thing can, without contradiction, be bent at one time and straight at another), so as to see whether Bergson's own resources let him solve that puzzle on endurantist terms, dissolve it as resting on a falsely frozen picture of instants to begin with, or actually leave him worse off than a defender of temporal parts.

Finally, since this means extrapolating a real thinker into a debate he never entered — and one whose later, most explicit defenses lean partly on just the relativistic picture of spacetime he had publicly contested in 1922 — I'd like you to flag which parts of the answer are secure extensions of positions he demonstrably held in his published work, and which are speculative bridges being built on his behalf.
