In my department's philosophy of language reading group, we've just finished the chapter on radical translation in Quine's *Word and Object*, and I want to check that I've drawn the right methodological lesson for my own fieldwork on an undocumented language. As I now read him, Quine's indeterminacy thesis is really just a special case of the general Duhem–Quine underdetermination of theory by evidence: exactly as rival physical theories can be empirically equivalent, rival analytical hypotheses about how to segment an utterance like "Gavagai" into referring parts — rabbit, undetached rabbit part, rabbit-stage — are compatible with all the same stimulus meanings and dispositions to assent or dissent, so the two cases are just the same underdetermination phenomenon showing up in a new domain. If that is right, indeterminacy of translation should not worry a field linguist any more than ordinary theoretical underdetermination worries a physicist: in both cases you adopt the simplest hypothesis consistent with the data and proceed, and there is no special skeptical threat hanging over the grammars and lexicons we eventually publish. Is that the correct way to domesticate Quine's thesis, or have I collapsed a distinction he was explicitly at pains to preserve?
