I'm a PhD student in cognitive science TAing a graduate seminar on naturalizing mental content this term, and I'd like a second opinion on how to grade one of the responses to a take-home question before I return it. Here is the question as posed:

Ruth Millikan's teleosemantics analyzes a representation's content in terms of the "Normal conditions" under which the mechanism that produces it historically performed its proper function. (a) Using the frog/fly/BB-pellet case, explain how this apparatus is supposed to solve the disjunction problem facing purely causal or covariational theories of content. (b) Critics have used the Swampman thought experiment to argue that Millikan's account cannot be right, since content seems to survive the total absence of any selection history. State the objection precisely, and briefly assess whether Millikan has an adequate response to it.

Here is the student's answer:

(a) On a purely causal theory, the frog's snap-mechanism is triggered by both flies and BB pellets, so if content just tracks whatever reliably causes a state, the mechanism represents the disjunctive property fly-or-BB-pellet, and there is no way to say the pellet-caused snaps are erroneous. Millikan avoids this by individuating content historically: what the mechanism represents is whatever it was selected to detect, i.e., flies, because detecting flies (not pellets) is what explains why snap-producing mechanisms of that type spread through the frog population. The relevant background conditions are what Millikan calls "Normal conditions" — the conditions that statistically prevail when the device functions and that explain the trait's having been selected for. Since flies, not pellets, are what the mechanism was selected to respond to, BB-pellet snaps are misfires rather than a disjunctive success, and misrepresentation becomes possible again.

(b) The Swampman case — which Millikan originally devised in Language, Thought, and Other Biological Categories as a stress test for her own account, before other authors turned it against her — describes a molecule-for-molecule duplicate of a person that comes into existence by cosmic accident and so has no selection history at all. On Millikan's view, no history means no proper functions, and hence no content; yet Swampman behaves exactly as if he understands English and recognizes his friends. Critics take this to show that historical etiology cannot really be what fixes content, since content seems to be present even when the relevant history is entirely absent. Millikan's response is basically to deny that our intuitions about such a bizarre, low-probability case are a reliable guide to the correct theory, and to note that Swampman would quickly start acquiring proper functions of his own through ordinary interaction with a linguistic community. I think that reply blunts the intuition pump reasonably well, though it does less to address the more general worry that etiology looks like the wrong kind of property to be metaphysically necessary for content.
