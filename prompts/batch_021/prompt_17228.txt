I've belonged to a small rationalist-adjacent discussion group for about four years now -- the sort of crowd that argues about base rates over potlucks and has strong opinions about calibration training. At our last meeting we worked through some old material on what's sometimes called the dogmatism paradox, generally credited to Saul Kripke and Gilbert Harman: roughly, if I know that some proposition P is true, I already know that any future evidence against P would have to be misleading, so it seems I'm entitled, right now, to discount whatever such evidence eventually turns up -- which makes a certain kind of dogmatism look rational rather than a vice. A few of us immediately thought of Yudkowsky's old point about "conservation of expected evidence," though that connection ended up complicating things more than settling them once two of the group's regulars, Mark and Sonia, got into it over Daryl Bem's 2011 "Feeling the Future" experiments, which used entirely standard methods from social psychology to report evidence for precognition.

(1) Mark said he'd never actually read Bem's paper and felt no need to: he already knows precognition is impossible given everything else we know about physics, so whatever produced those results was some mix of chance, sloppy method, or misconduct -- and the dogmatism paradox shows this is a rational place to stand, not a failure of curiosity. (2) Sonia objected that this is exactly the unfalsifiable move the paradox should make us suspicious of, not license: if enough confidence entitles you to wave away any future evidence sight unseen, nobody who's ever felt sure of anything would need to update again, however good the evidence got -- and Bem's experiments used the same peer-reviewed toolkit psychologists trust when results point somewhere less exotic. (3) Mark replied that Sonia's principle proves too much in the other direction: if strong prior justification never licenses discounting any future evidence, however weak or predictable, nobody knows anything about the world at all, since some conceivable counter-evidence always looms over almost every belief we hold. (4) Sonia refined her view by distinguishing between correctly predicting that low-quality evidence won't move you much, and pre-committing to disregard an entire category of well-conducted, statistically significant findings simply because of what they'd show if true; the field's actual response to Bem, she noted, was to run large preregistered replications, not to declare the results misleading by decree. (5) Mark shot back that Sonia doesn't extend the same courtesy to astrology or perpetual-motion claims -- she dismisses those in advance too, on background theory alone, which is the very move she's condemning him for, just applied more selectively. (6) Sonia's last word was that the disanalogy lies in the strength and specificity of the prior together with the surface quality of the evidence: some claims are foreclosed by principles so overdetermined that no serious research program attaches to them, whereas parapsychology has at least occasionally recruited competent researchers using orthodox methods -- so scrutiny should be proportioned to both factors, not fixed by a blanket policy decided in advance.

I'd like you to go through this move by move -- I've numbered all six above -- and say whether each one holds up, rather than just declaring an overall winner between Mark and Sonia. I'd also like to know what, if anything, you think both of them are missing about how the dogmatism paradox is actually supposed to be resolved, or about the respects in which it resists full resolution -- that's the part I keep getting stuck on myself.
