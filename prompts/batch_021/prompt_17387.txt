Now that I'm retired from a career in field ecology and have taken up philosophy in earnest, one debate has caught my attention precisely because of my old habit of hunting for genuine signal in noisy correlational data: the dispute between virtue ethics and its situationist critics. Situationists often invoke Mischel's finding that cross-situational consistency between personality-trait measures and actual behavior rarely exceeds a correlation of about 0.3 — the so-called 'personality coefficient' — as evidence that stable Aristotelian character traits are largely a fiction. But by the standards I'm used to applying to effect sizes in messy field data, a correlation of that magnitude is far from negligible, especially once one notices that the classic situational manipulations themselves — obedience to authority, hurried indifference to a stranger in distress, and so on — have, by some reanalyses, been shown to involve effect sizes of roughly comparable magnitude. I'd like you to write a dialogue between a philosopher pressing the situationist critique in something like the spirit of Doris or Harman, and a virtue ethicist responding in something like the spirit of Kamtekar or Sreenivasan. Make this effect-size question the central point of contention, alongside the deeper issue of just how much cross-situational consistency an Aristotelian conception of virtue actually presupposes. Please have each side engage directly and substantively with the other's strongest points, rather than talking past one another.
