I'm a philosophy PhD student working through Gödel's unpublished philosophical writings, and I want a careful assessment of the argument for personal immortality he set out in private correspondence rather than in any published work — distinct from, though related to, his separate and better-known modal ontological proof of God's existence.

Between July and October 1961, prompted by his mother Marianne's question about whether the two of them would ever meet again, Gödel wrote her four letters defending an affirmative answer by philosophical argument rather than mere consolation. The reasoning runs roughly as follows: the world is rationally organized and meaningful, a premise he grounds in the regularity that natural science both presupposes and continually confirms, treating it as an extension of the principle that everything has a cause. Yet an ordinary human life realizes only a small fraction of the vast field of development and relationships open to it, rather as if a builder laid a foundation and then walked away. Since a genuinely rational world could not tolerate this kind of waste, Gödel infers that we must persist — carrying forward the "raw material" of this life's experience — into a further existence in which that potential is finally realized, explicitly not through reincarnation into another body but through a state in which we remember, and for the first time truly understand, what we lived through here.

Write an essay assessing the inference on which this argument really turns: from the premise that the world is causally ordered, which is well supported by the success of the natural sciences, to the much stronger claim that it is teleologically ordered so as to guarantee that no one's rational potential goes permanently to waste. Is this best read as an equivocation between two different senses of "rational," or as a consistent extension, to axiological matters, of the same rationalist principle of sufficient reason that Gödel invokes elsewhere? Weigh both readings and defend a definite verdict on whether someone who accepts Gödel's premise about the order revealed by science, but not his wider anti-materialist and conceptual-realist commitments, has any real reason to accept his conclusion.
