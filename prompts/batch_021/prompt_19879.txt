A friend and I spent much of last night arguing about a claim she had come across online: that Gödel's incompleteness theorems already prove no machine, including a large language model, could ever match human mathematical insight. I objected that this conflates Gödel's own far more cautious position with the much stronger argument that Lucas, and later Penrose, built on top of it. In his 1951 Gibbs lecture to the American Mathematical Society, Gödel's actual conclusion was a disjunction: either the human mind outstrips the powers of every finite machine, or there exist mathematical problems that are absolutely undecidable — not merely relative to some formal system, but for the human mind itself. He maintained that while the disjunction itself was mathematically demonstrable, deciding between its two halves was a philosophical question the mathematics could not settle. My friend wasn't convinced this distinction matters much in practice. Setting aside what Lucas and Penrose went on to do with the theorem, what do you think Gödel himself, given that guarded stance and his own mathematical Platonism, would have made of today's large language models and the arguments now advanced in his name? Would his epistemic caution have kept him from endorsing the strong anti-mechanist conclusion, or do you think his deeper philosophical commitments would have pushed him toward it regardless?
