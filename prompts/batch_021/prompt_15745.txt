I'm in the fourth year of a philosophy PhD, and the dissertation chapter I've spent the last two years on is about Galen Strawson's Basic Argument against ultimate moral responsibility: the regress showing that because how you act simply flows from how you are, and because nobody can be the uncaused cause of their own character (nothing is truly causa sui), nobody can ever be truly, deeply deserving of praise or blame for anything — a conclusion Strawson insists holds whatever the truth about determinism turns out to be. Until a few weeks ago this was a clean, contained, seminar-room problem for me. It stopped being contained when my younger brother, Marcus, who has spent the last six years incarcerated, was approved to walk at the small commencement his facility holds every spring for the handful of men who complete an associate's degree through a partnership with a public university. He asked me, not our parents, to be the family member who says a few words, and I agreed before I'd thought through what I was agreeing to.

The ceremony itself is short, maybe twenty minutes for five graduates, each introduced by a brief address from a relative, but I keep discarding my own drafts, because every one of them defaults to exactly the sentence I no longer feel entitled to say: that Marcus chose this, that he made himself into someone different, that he deserves everything he is about to receive. That is precisely the strong, self-authorship-implying credit that the Basic Argument says nobody can actually earn, since he no more chose the temperament, the luck, and the circumstances that made this change possible than he chose the ones that landed him there in the first place. Strawson himself is adamant that accepting all this doesn't and can't stop anyone from actually feeling pride, gratitude, or resentment in ordinary life, and I have found that much easier to agree with in a seminar than to inhabit while standing at a lectern in a prison gymnasium trying to mean something. At the same time, I have no intention of delivering a bloodless, deflationary little speech that makes six years of real, hard-won change sound like nothing happened; his fellow graduates, their families, and the instructors will rightly have no patience for a philosophy lecture masquerading as a sister's tribute.

So I would like your help actually writing the address: roughly three minutes read aloud, somewhere around 400 to 450 words, warm and specific enough to sound like someone who genuinely loves her brother, but built so that nothing in it depends on the strong desert-claim the Basic Argument rules out. I'd rather it find other, honest things to praise — the effort as it actually felt from the inside, the people who showed up for him over six years, what he did with a stretch of good luck once he finally got some — than smuggle ultimate credit in through the back door with words like "deserve" or "earned." One last thing: my advisor, who supervised the Strawson chapter, is coming as my guest, and I would love exactly one moment in the speech, a single line or turn of phrase, that lands for everyone else in the room as an ordinary, heartfelt thing for a sister to say, but that she alone will recognize as a precise and considered nod to the argument itself.
