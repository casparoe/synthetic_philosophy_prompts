Over the past several months I've been working my way through translations of classical Chinese philosophy in my spare time, mostly because a colleague mentioned the "white horse" paradox to me in passing and I haven't been able to let it go since. I have no formal training in logic or philosophy and don't read Chinese, so everything I know about Gongsun Long and the School of Names comes from a handful of translated primary texts and whatever secondary commentary I've managed to track down on my own. What strikes me is that almost every summary I've come across treats the entire White Horse Dialogue as though it stands or falls on a single mistake: that Gongsun Long conflates the "is" of identity with the "is" of class-membership, so that "a white horse is not a horse" only comes out true because he quietly switches senses of "is" partway through. Below is my own attempt to pull the dialogue's individual moves apart, because I've come to think that diagnosis, while fair to some of what he says, doesn't fit all of it equally well.

As I read it, the dialogue actually runs together at least three distinct arguments. The first turns on naming shape versus naming color — "horse" names the shape, "white" names the color, and naming one is not naming the other — which really does look like a words-versus-things version of the very equivocation people point to, so I think the standard charge lands squarely here. The second is the "seeking a horse" passage, where a yellow or black horse satisfies someone asking for "a horse" but not someone asking for "a white horse." My suspicion is that this argument isn't equivocating at all: it's pointing to a genuine difference in extension between the two terms, and the usual objection — that a white horse is still a horse, so the two must be "the same" in whatever sense would make "is not" false — simply assumes what needs to be shown. The third move, about "horse" failing to fix any color while "white horse" does fix one, strikes me as a restatement of the second argument dressed up as a claim about determinacy rather than extension, so I'd fold it back in rather than count it separately. If that's right, the dialogue isn't one sophism recycled three times but two arguments of noticeably different quality, and most treatments I've read run right over that distinction. (There's also a further passage about combining "white" and "horse" into a single compound name that I suspect is doing something different again, but I don't have a firm enough grip on it to say anything useful about it.)

I'd genuinely appreciate your comments on this. Is my reconstruction of the "seeking a horse" argument actually defensible as I've stated it, or am I smuggling in the very assumption Gongsun Long needs and then calling it a "genuine" difference in extension? Is the claim that these three moves collapse into two already a small, settled point in how this text tends to get read — in which case I would just be re-deriving something already known — or does it actually cut against interpretations you're aware of? And is there a way to say what makes the second argument logically stronger than the first without simply importing modern set-theoretic vocabulary, talk of members and subsets, that Gongsun Long plainly did not have available to him?
