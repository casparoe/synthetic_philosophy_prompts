I'm nearing the end of a novel whose central figure, referred to throughout only as "Grandfather" (which is deliberate), spent four decades producing fake old-master drawings so accomplished that at least two still hang in private collections attributed to the masters he imitated, and who was never caught in his lifetime. The book's penultimate chapter is his wake, narrated by his adult granddaughter, the character through whose eyes we've seen most of the story, and I need the eulogy she delivers there to function as the emotional and intellectual keystone of the whole novel. My writing group agrees the chapter is nearly finished but that the eulogy itself still reads like an argument wearing a black tie rather than something a grieving person would actually say out loud, and I think they're right, which is why I'd like your help.

The mourners are a deliberately awkward mix: the family, who loved him and mostly never knew exactly what his work involved; an old associate from the trade who quietly helped him with the paper and ink over the years, foxing a sheet here, aging a watermark there; and a retired art-fraud detective who spent fifteen years failing to build a case against him and came anyway, out of something like professional respect. The eulogy has to survive being heard by all three at once, and that rules out the two easiest ways of writing it. It can't quietly imply that what he did doesn't really matter because no one could ever tell his work from the real thing — that would be false to the novel's own logic, in which something genuine is lost even when the deception is flawless, and the detective in the pews would recognize the evasion instantly. But it also can't turn into a confession dressed up as a eulogy, both because that isn't what eulogies are for and because the granddaughter genuinely believes, and has earned the right to argue, that her grandfather was a serious artist who simply was never permitted to sign his own name to anything.

What I need, in other words, is a real answer, worked out in the granddaughter's own words rather than merely gestured at, to the question of what was actually lost — not to the buyers, who mostly got the object they wanted, but to him — every single time a drawing that was unmistakably his triumph left his hands credited to somebody else. Could you draft that eulogy, roughly four or five minutes read aloud, in a voice that sounds like grief and pride declining to cancel each other out, rather than like philosophy?
