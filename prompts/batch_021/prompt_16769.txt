My sister Elena is getting married in six weeks, and she has asked me to give a toast at the reception. The complication is her fiancé, Daniel, who wrote his senior thesis on David Lewis's modal realism and has not really stopped talking about it since. He is only half-joking when he says the toast is allowed to be sentimental but not allowed to be false: on his view, "actual" is merely an indexical term, on a par with "here" and "now," rather than the name of some uniquely privileged world among all the possible ones, so there is no cosmic sense in which he and Elena beat the odds, were fated to meet, or are the only ones who could have made each other this happy. Among the concrete, causally isolated worlds that his view commits him to, there are counterparts of Daniel who married someone else entirely, or never met any counterpart of Elena at all, and those worlds are, on his account, every bit as real as this one; nothing about this one was picked out, luckily or otherwise, from among the rest.

I am taking a metaphysics seminar this term that just spent three weeks on On the Plurality of Worlds, so I understand the view well enough to know how much standard wedding-toast material it quietly rules out, and I have watched enough of Daniel's dinner parties end with him getting what he calls, borrowing Lewis's own phrase, "the incredulous stare" to know he is serious about this. About half the guests will be philosophy friends from his cohort who will notice if I fudge it, and the other half are relatives who have never heard of a possible world and should not have to. Could you write me a five-minute toast that feels genuinely warm rather than a lecture on modality, that avoids any claim about fate, destiny, or "the one" that Daniel would feel obliged to correct afterward, and that ideally puts the indexical point itself to some positive use—the idea that caring more about your own here and now than about equally real elsewheres is not a mistake—rather than just carefully tiptoeing around it?
