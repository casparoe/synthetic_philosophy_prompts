I've published four novels but had never sat on the other side of a literary prize until this year, when I agreed to join the five-person judging panel for a small, well-regarded award given annually to a debut novel -- I'll call it the Anthem Prize, since its real name doesn't matter here. We have not even received the longlist yet, and the panel has already split over how we ought to reach our verdict.

One of my fellow judges, a retired actuary who reads as widely as the rest of us but thinks in the language of probability, has circulated a note arguing that our usual method -- months of correspondence followed by a final afternoon of argument that ends in a vote -- is close to the worst possible design for actually finding the best book. He invokes the Condorcet Jury Theorem: if each judge is even modestly better than a coin flip at picking the better of two novels set side by side, a large enough panel voting independently and simultaneously, without conferring, will converge on the right answer with near certainty, whereas deliberation mostly just lets whoever argues most forcefully drag the rest of us toward their own taste. To make the case concrete, he calculated that if each of us is individually right about 60 percent of the time in such a comparison, our current panel of five would pick the better book roughly 68 percent of the time, while an independent panel of eleven would get it right about 75 percent of the time -- and on this basis he wants us to recommend to the trustees both that expansion and a switch to blind, independent scoring at every stage, from longlist to shortlist to winner. Two of the other four judges are already quietly persuaded. I am not, but when I try to explain why, my objection keeps collapsing into "I would miss the arguing," which is not obviously a principled reason to reject a method that might simply work better.

The trustees have asked whichever of us feel strongly about the panel's structure to put our reasoning in writing before their spring meeting, where next year's judging procedure will be decided. Could you draft that memo for me? I would like it to end in a concrete recommendation -- expand the panel and move to independent scoring, keep the deliberative model, or adopt some third option -- defended with reasons that take the actuary's argument seriously on its own terms rather than dismissing it out of hand. In particular, I would like the memo to clarify three things: what the Condorcet Jury Theorem actually requires of those who invoke it; where a panel like ours does or does not meet those requirements; and whether "picking the better novel" is even the kind of question the theorem was built to answer -- since I suspect that question is doing more work in this dispute than either side has noticed.
