Ok so this has been bugging me for a while, and a friend of mine who's doing a phil of mind PhD basically laughed at how I originally framed it, so now I want a more careful answer instead of my half-baked version. I've absorbed a ton of the rationalist-sphere predictive processing stuff over the years -- Scott's old SSC review of Andy Clark's *Surfing Uncertainty*, the various LW-style posts treating the brain as running hierarchical Bayesian inference where perception is just top-down predictions getting corrected by bottom-up prediction error, that whole picture. Separately I've been reading some actual philosophy of perception (Fodor's modularity of mind stuff, Pylyshyn) where the big point is that perception is supposed to be informationally encapsulated -- the classic example is the Müller-Lyer illusion still looking unequal even once you *know*, via measuring, that the lines are the same length, which is meant to show your beliefs cant reach in and edit what the perceptual module spits out.

My problem is these two pictures feel like they're in tension and I can't tell if that's a real tension or just me not understanding one of them properly. Predictive processing seems to say perceptual content just *is* whatever the generative model's current best hypothesis is, built out of priors that live in the same hierarchy as higher cognition, with no obvious principled place to draw a line between "expectation" and "perception" -- which sounds like it just hands victory to the cognitive-penetration-of-perception side (Churchland-ish, or Macpherson/Siegel more recently) over Fodor's impenetrability thesis almost by definition. But I get the sense the people who actually work on this (i.e. not me) don't treat it as that easy a slam dunk, and that there's some more careful move available -- either preserving an encapsulation-like distinction from inside a predictive processing architecture, or arguing that not every case of "priors shaping perception" counts as cognitive penetration in the sense philosophers actually care about (something about directness, or about which level of the hierarchy is doing the influencing).

Could you actually walk through this properly -- what's the strongest version of the argument that predictive processing really does collapse the perception/cognition line Fodor wanted, versus the strongest version of a reconstructed encapsulation/impenetrability thesis that survives inside a predictive-processing-style architecture? I'd rather get the real argumentative moves on both sides than a "well it's complicated" -- even if the honest bottom line ends up being that the jury's out.
