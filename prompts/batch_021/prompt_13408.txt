A friend and I argued for most of dinner last night about what money-pump arguments actually establish, and neither of us gave any ground. My friend defended what Edward McClennen calls "resolute choice": an agent who commits at the outset to a plan for an entire sequence of trades, and refuses to deviate later even when their preference at that moment favors it, can retain cyclic preferences and still never be pumped, so the argument shows at most that agents need a diachronic policy for choosing over time, not that their preferences must satisfy a synchronic constraint like transitivity. I objected that this concedes the point rather than escapes it: a resolute chooser must, at some step, decline a trade that their own momentary ranking favors, so the cyclic preferences stop explaining anything about what the agent actually does, and a preference that never manifests in choice isn't doing the work a preference is supposed to do. Neither of us could settle a further wrinkle: the same trades could instead be offered all at once, as a single bundle to accept or refuse, where resoluteness has no foothold since there is no future self left to bind, yet presumably any agent, however they rank the options involved, would still decline a bundle that simply returns them to their starting point minus a fee - does that matter, and if so, for which side? I'd like you to write a dialogue between a philosopher defending resolute choice as a genuine vindication of cyclic preferences against the money pump and one defending the traditional, transitivity-requiring reading of the argument, in which they work through both the dispute above and the bundle wrinkle using a concrete case: a cyclic preference over three goods, traded round and round with a small payment changing hands at each step. Each should press the other's strongest remaining objection rather than restate their opening position, and neither should be allowed to win simply because the other fails to notice a good reply.
