A friend and I spent an unreasonable amount of time last weekend arguing about "moral trade," an idea I believe traces back to Toby Ord's paper of the same name: the thought that two people who disagree sharply about ethics can still strike a deal that leaves each of them better off by their own lights, much as ordinary trade lets people with different endowments and tastes both gain from an exchange. The standard illustration has someone who is a committed vegetarian but fairly indifferent to the ethics of abortion trading with someone who is a committed opponent of abortion but fairly indifferent to diet: each does something they think barely matters in exchange for the other doing something they think matters enormously, and — on the assumption that both are honest about their own values and under no duress — both end up ahead by their own reckoning, even though neither has changed their mind about anything. My friend's position was that such a deal is basically unobjectionable whenever it is genuinely voluntary and leaves both parties better off ex ante; I could not shake the feeling that some structurally identical deals are obviously bribery, exploitation, or something like the corrupt commodification of a moral conviction, but I could not pin down what feature, beyond mutual benefit and voluntariness, separates the cases that seem clearly fine from the ones that do not.

Could you help me get clearer on this? Please construct a concrete, specific example of a moral trade that satisfies the formal conditions philosophers typically take to make such trades acceptable (it is voluntary, non-deceptive, harms no third party, and leaves both parties better off according to their own antecedent values) but that you think most people would nonetheless intuitively judge to be objectionable — for instance as a disguised bribe, an exploitative arrangement, or an illegitimate commodification of moral commitment. Then state, as precisely as you can, exactly what feature of your example distinguishes it from the vegetarian/pro-life case, and what more general principle that difference points to for telling legitimate moral trade apart from its illegitimate look-alikes.
