So I've been reading Spinoza's political writings for my diss chapter and theres something that keeps bugging me. In the TTP (ch. 16) he calls democracy "the most natural" form of state, and even in the unfinished Political Treatise -- he died partway through the chapter on democracy -- he'd already called it the most "absolute" regime, his technical term for the state where sovereignty stays most fully unified in the whole body of the people instead of getting parcelled out to a subset. But this is the same guy whose Ethics denies libertarian free will outright and says flat out in E4 that good and bad aren't real, mind-independent properties of anything, just relative labels for what's useful to us, given that literally everything follows with the exact same necessity regardless. So what's actually left for him to mean when he says one regime is normatively better than another? Is "democracy is best" secretly smuggling back in some notion of freedom or value he's not supposed to have, or is there a legit way to cash that claim out just through conatus and the right-equals-power move, without needing libertarian freedom or objective goodness anywhere in the mix? I don't want the Spinoza-was-basically-a-liberal cliffnotes version -- I want to know how the metaphysical machinery is actually supposed to be doing the normative work here.
