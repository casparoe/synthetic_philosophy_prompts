In the epistemology of higher-order evidence, the Enkratic Principle holds that a rational agent cannot believe P while also believing that her evidence does not support P -- a combination usually called epistemic akrasia. The principle comes under pressure from cases in which an agent works through a piece of reasoning and then receives reliable testimony that she has been given a drug that impairs logical reasoning, one that produces no felt change in how her thinking seems from the inside, so that evaluating the testimony itself requires trusting the very faculties it calls into question. Please argue that the rational response to such cases is genuinely akratic, and that each of the two standard alternatives is worse than akrasia itself: full deference to the higher-order evidence threatens a regress in which no belief is ever safe from retraction on the strength of further, equally underminable testimony, while outright resistance to it looks like unprincipled special pleading against exactly the kind of evidence the agent would otherwise treat as decisive.
