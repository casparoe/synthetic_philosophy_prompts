Been down a bit of a rabbit hole this week on Popper and evolution, partly inspired by re-reading some old David-Deutsch-pilled LessWrong posts about critical rationalism, and it's messing with my head a little. So apparently in the 70s Popper said natural selection was basically "almost tautological" and more of a metaphysical research programme than a real testable scientific theory, which is a wild thing for THE falsifiability guy to say about Darwinism of all things. Then a few years later, at some lecture (I think it was Darwin-themed but don't quote me), he walked most of that back and admitted natural selection does generate testable predictions after all, sorry Darwinists. I only know this secondhand from stuff I've read so I might be garbling the details a bit.

Anyway, here's what I keep chewing on. Michael Behe's whole "irreducible complexity" thing and the intelligent-design crowd around the Dover trial in the mid-2000s basically recycle young Poppers line - evolution isn't really falsifiable, it's just a story that always fits after the fact - to argue ID deserves equal time in biology class. Popper died in '94 so he never got to weigh in on any of that directly. Given that he'd already publicly recanted the tautology charge, and given his whole "evolutionary epistemology" thing where he treats the growth of scientific knowledge itself as a Darwinian process of conjecture and refutation, what do you reckon his actual, internally consistent verdict would've been if he'd somehow been dragged in to referee the Dover-trial-era ID vs evolution fight? Would he side with the reasoning his younger self used, side with his later recantation, or find some third way of drawing the falsifiability line that leaves neither camp happy? Genuinely curious what a considered Popperian answer looks like here, rather than the "gotcha" version both sides seem to quote at each other.
