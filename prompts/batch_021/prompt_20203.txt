This semester I am taking a seminar on the philosophical foundations of probability. We have just finished a unit on Joseph Bertrand's 1889 chord paradox—the geometrical paradox about "random" chords in a circle, not the three-box paradox that shares his name—and the trouble it causes for the Principle of Indifference. For my notes, I am trying to assess how successful the various proposed resolutions actually are, and I would appreciate your help ranking them.

Here are the resolutions I have in mind:
- Von Mises: a "random chord" is not well-defined without a specified generating experiment (a "collective," in his terminology), so the paradox reflects mere conceptual confusion rather than any real inconsistency in probability theory.
- Jaynes: given a fully specified physical randomizing procedure (e.g., straws tossed onto the circle from a distance), the resulting distribution must be invariant under the relevant transformation group (translations, rotations, and scalings); imposing this requirement singles out one of the three classical constructions as the correct one.
- Marinoff: the three classical constructions are not competing answers to a single question but correct answers to three different questions, so the paradox dissolves once each construction's implicit sample space is made explicit.
- Van Fraassen: his cube-factory case generalizes the underlying structure of the paradox to show that transformation-invariance requirements of Jaynes' sort cannot, in general, single out a uniquely privileged distribution, since inequivalent "natural" variables remain available.
- Shackel: treats Bertrand's paradox as one instance of a broader family of paradoxes of geometrical probability, arguing that no privileged parametrization of a continuous possibility space can be identified on purely formal grounds, so the Principle of Indifference cannot be salvaged wholesale for such cases.
- Keynes: restricts the Principle of Indifference to alternatives that cannot be further subdivided on the basis of relevant evidence, so as to rule out the very constructions that generate the paradox as illegitimate applications of the principle.

If you were to grade each of these resolutions on the same scale a professor might use for essays—A through F—based purely on how successfully each resolves the tension between the paradox and the Principle of Indifference, how would you assign the grades?
