A few months ago a friend of mine, who has an engineering background but no interest in philosophy, mentioned over dinner that a famous twentieth-century logician had supposedly "proved God exists using math." That sounded like nonsense to me, so I spent some lunch breaks over the following weeks looking into it, mostly by working through the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy's entry on ontological arguments, since I never studied philosophy formally and don't have anyone around me to ask about this sort of thing. I think I have a reasonably solid handle on the original, much older version of the argument, due to Anselm: he defines God as the greatest being that can possibly be conceived, points out that even someone who denies God's existence still has that concept in mind, and then argues that a being existing both in the mind and in reality is greater than one that exists in the mind alone — so if the greatest conceivable being existed only as an idea, we could conceive of something greater still, namely a version of it that also exists, which is a contradiction. I also know the standard objection to this, from Kant: that existence isn't a "real predicate" that makes a concept more perfect — a hundred real dollars aren't a single cent richer than a hundred merely imagined ones — so you can't get existence out of a definition just by building it in.

Where I got completely lost was the part about Gödel, who turned out to be the logician my friend meant. As far as I can tell, he rebuilds the argument using modal logic and a primitive notion of a property being "positive," and from a small set of axioms about positive properties — things like: a property or its negation is positive but never both; anything necessarily entailed by a positive property is also positive; being "God-like" (having every positive property) counts as positive; and necessary existence itself is a positive property — he derives, as a theorem, that a God-like being necessarily exists. What I can't work out on my own is whether this formal machinery is doing something philosophically different from what Anselm did, or whether it's the same basic move of defining existence into a concept, just carried out in symbols instead of prose. I also came across a mention that Gödel's axioms lead to something called "modal collapse" — supposedly the result that, if the axioms hold, there is no real distinction left between what's necessarily true and what merely happens to be true, because everything actually true would turn out to be necessarily true. That sounds like a serious cost, and I don't have the tools to see for myself why treating necessary existence as a "positive property" would produce that side effect, or whether some quieter version of the same problem is already hiding inside Anselm's original, unformalized argument.

So here is what I'd like your help with: please compare the two arguments directly rather than just describing each on its own. Does Gödel's use of modal logic and "positive properties" genuinely fix a weakness in Anselm's reasoning, or does it just relocate the same weakness somewhere harder to see? Does Kant's objection that existence is not a predicate still apply to Gödel's version, given that Gödel is technically working with "necessary existence" as a property rather than plain existence — or does that distinction actually let him get around Kant? And could you walk me through, in plain terms, why the axioms lead to modal collapse, and whether that problem should make us more suspicious of Gödel's argument than we already were of Anselm's, or whether it actually cuts the other way? I'd appreciate it if you kept the explanation at the level of what I've described above, without assuming I know formal logic notation.
