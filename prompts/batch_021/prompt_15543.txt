I'm a software engineer, and for the past few months I've been part of a small, informally-run reading group (a couple of retirees, one rabbinical student, and me) slowly working through Maimonides' Guide for the Perplexed. We recently reached Book III, where he sets out his rationale for the commandments, and I found his treatment of animal sacrifice genuinely striking: as I understand it, his argument is that God could not simply command the Israelites, fresh out of a culture saturated in sacrificial idol-worship, to switch overnight to prayer alone — that would be too abrupt a break with deeply ingrained habit — so sacrifice was retained but redirected exclusively to Him and hedged with restrictions, as a transitional accommodation to human psychology, with the real endpoint being a purer, sacrifice-free form of worship. As an engineer, it struck me as structurally similar to keeping a deprecated API alive behind a compatibility shim while you migrate everyone off it, rather than breaking all your callers at once.

The rabbinical student in our group then pointed out that this isn't the end of the story: Nahmanides, writing decades later, has a well-known rebuttal in his own Torah commentary, arguing that Maimonides' account can't be the whole truth, since sacrifice long predates any Israelite exposure to Egyptian idolatry — Abel and Noah both offer sacrifices that Scripture describes God as favorably receiving, right down to the "pleasing odor" of Noah's offering, which suggests sacrifice had intrinsic religious meaning from the very beginning rather than being merely a concession to a historically contingent bad habit. Apparently Nahmanides also has his own, more symbolic, account of what the ritual is supposed to represent for the person bringing the offering.

I'd love to see this argument properly dramatized rather than just summarized. Could you write a dialogue between Maimonides and Nahmanides — imagined as though they could respond to each other directly, even though they weren't really contemporaries — working through this disagreement? I'd like both of them represented at their strongest, genuinely engaging with each other's specific objections rather than delivering pre-packaged position statements, and I'd like the back-and-forth to surface the deeper methodological question underneath the textual dispute: whether it's legitimate, within a framework that treats the commandments as divinely given, to explain any of them as a temporary concession to a historically contingent human weakness rather than as an expression of timeless value — and if so, what would stop that same move from being used to explain away any commandment one happens to find inconvenient.
