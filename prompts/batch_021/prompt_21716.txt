Question: "Discuss the development of George Eliot's personal religious views and explain how this development is reflected in the treatment of faith and doubt in her fiction. Refer to at least one specific biographical episode and at least one specific novel or character."

Her answer: "George Eliot was raised in a strict Evangelical household, but around 1842 she stopped attending church with her father after being influenced by Charles Hennell's skeptical study of Christianity's historical origins, in an episode her biographers have nicknamed the 'Holy War' between her and her father. She went on to translate two major works of German biblical criticism into English, David Strauss's The Life of Jesus and Ludwig Feuerbach's The Essence of Christianity, both published under her now-famous pen name George Eliot. Feuerbach's argument that God is really just humanity's own best qualities projected outward onto an imaginary being seems to have stuck with her permanently, and by the 1870s she had essentially adopted Auguste Comte's Religion of Humanity as her personal creed, including its calendar of secular saints, as a replacement for the Christianity she could no longer literally believe in. Even so, she never lost her respect for sincere religious feeling, which is why Silas Marner treats Dolly Winthrop's simple churchgoing faith with so much warmth instead of mocking it, and why Dinah Morris in Adam Bede is one of the most sympathetic characters in the book despite being a Methodist preacher. For Eliot, what mattered in the end was not doctrine but duty and human sympathy."
