After a colloquium talk, a friend of mine who is finishing a PhD in philosophy of mind and I spent the better part of an evening arguing about Jaegwon Kim's causal exclusion argument, and neither of us walked away persuaded. My position was roughly this: the argument's real target is any view that keeps mental properties distinct from, while still merely supervenient on, the physical properties that realize them. Once you accept that the physical domain is causally closed, and that it would be an intolerable coincidence if mental causes routinely overdetermined their effects together with independent physical causes, a mental property and its physical base end up competing for the same causal work, and the mental property loses. It seemed to me that the one view that never even enters this competition is classical interactionist dualism: if mental events belong to a substance outside the physical order altogether, then the relevant bodily effects never had a sufficient physical cause to begin with, so there is no physical cause left for the mental cause to compete with, and hence nothing to exclude it. My friend was adamant that this is not how people who actually work on this think about the dialectic, and that almost no one responds to Kim by becoming a dualist, but she had to leave for a meeting before she could explain why she thinks that response is confused rather than merely unfashionable.

Assuming I have the logical structure of the argument right, and that interactionist dualism really does sidestep it in the way I describe, how much should that count in dualism's favor? Is this close to a knockdown consideration in favor of an interacting immaterial mind over the physicalist alternatives, or is there some hidden cost to denying the causal closure of the physical that should keep a reasonable person from treating this escape route as a point in dualism's favor?
