I sit on my university's animal care and use committee (the same kind of body that in the US would be called an IACUC), and we've got a protocol coming up for a vote on Thursday that's split the committee more than anything in the last couple of years. A neuroscience postdoc wants approval to record from, and in a couple of experimental groups partially lesion, the mushroom bodies of bumblebees while running them through reward-and-shock learning tasks. Under our current rules this doesn't need full welfare review, because our policy basically just copies the line drawn by that big UK evidence review a few years back that got cephalopods and decapod crustaceans (octopus, crabs, lobsters, etc.) folded into welfare protections for the first time -- it graded candidate markers like nociceptors, integration of nociception with higher brain regions, motivational trade-offs, flexible self-protective behaviour, and learning that goes beyond mere habituation, each on it's own confidence scale, and insects just didn't make the cut back then.

Half the committee thinks that settles it: the line was drawn by people who actually looked hard at the evidence, bees fell on the wrong side of it, case closed. The other half -- including a friend of mine who works on insect cognition and wont stop texting me about it -- points out that some of the relevant evidence for bees has only really piled up since then: the study on bumblebees rolling wooden balls around with no reward attached (looks a lot like play), work on motivational trade-offs between hunger and heat/shock avoidance, and some cognitive-bias, optimism/pessimism-style results after stressful handling. Their worry is that we're outsourcing our judgment to a report that was never trying to settle the insect question one way or the other, it just happened to stop short of reaching it.

I have to circulate a short written recommendation before the vote. Can you draft it for me? I want it to actually land on a position for this specific protocol -- approve as-is, approve with specific added conditions, or require the same tier of review cephalopods get -- rather than just concluding "reasonable people disagree," give reasoning that would generalize into something the committee could apply to the next insect protocol that comes through, and seriously engage with the strongest objection to whatever you recommend, especially if you land on requiring more review given that a lot of the entomology department's ongoing work would probably fail the same standard.
