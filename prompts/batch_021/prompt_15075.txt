I'm a phil of mind PhD student doing a clinical ethics practicum, so I've got a non-voting seat on my teaching hospital's ethics committee this term. Thursday's case: a patient with locked-in syndrome (brainstem stroke, fully cognitively intact) communicates entirely through an external eye-gaze speech device she's used daily for three years -- by now she barely composes sentences on it consciously, she just looks and it says what she means, and it's also how she consents to her own treatment decisions. Hospital finance wants to reclassify the device's software support contract as routine 'durable medical equipment' upkeep, which can be downgraded for budget reasons with 30 days' notice and no ethics review -- same bucket as wheelchair repairs. Our charter says anything that could 'fundamentally affect a patient's decision-making capacity or means of expression' needs committee review first, but doesn't say who decides which bucket a case falls into. One attending, whose weirdly well-read in Clark and Chalmers, argues the device meets basically every criterion they give for something counting as literally part of a person's cognitive system -- so this isn't equipment triage, it's closer to proposing to partially silence her mind. Others think that proves way too much (glasses and insulin pumps would qualify too) and that hospital policy can't hinge on unresolved philosophy of mind. Can you draft the short opinion memo I'll circulate before the meeting? It needs a concrete recommendation with a timeline, and it has to actually engage the 'it's just equipment' side instead of waving it away.
