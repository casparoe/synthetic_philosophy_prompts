I'm a PhD student in philosophy working on normative ethics and decision-making under moral uncertainty, and this term I've been co-running a reading group on Toby Ord's proposal for "moral trade." The core idea borrows the economist's logic of gains from trade: even when two people flatly disagree about which acts are wrong, each can come out ahead by their own lights by swapping concessions, rather than settling for a compromise that splits the difference. To fix ideas, consider two people who disagree about the relative importance of factory farming and trophy hunting: one cares intensely about the former and is indifferent to the latter, while the other cares intensely about the latter and is indifferent to the former. If one goes vegetarian and the other forgoes trophy hunting, each conceding something that costs them little by their own values, both can judge, entirely by their own standards, that the resulting world is better than the status quo — a genuine Pareto improvement secured through trade rather than through any convergence on a shared moral view.

At our last meeting, one of the other students raised an objection I haven't been able to shake, and that nothing on our syllabus addresses head-on. A great many first-order ethical disagreements are precisely about whether an act's moral status depends on the reasons or intentions behind it, rather than only on the act-type or its effects on the world: think of Kantian strictures on acting from duty rather than merely in conformity with it, or virtue-ethical accounts of what an action expresses about the agent's character. If one or both trading partners hold views of this kind, then what a trade actually secures for me is your abstaining-for-a-consideration, not your abstaining from genuine conviction or a virtuous disposition — and on exactly the theories that would make your abstention matter so much to me in the first place, that difference in motive looks like it could be doing all the work, rather than being an incidental detail that Ord's framework can safely set aside.

I'd like you to argue for the position that moral trade, as standardly presented, cannot in general deliver the Pareto improvement "by each trading partner's own lights" that its proponents advertise, because the proposal is implicitly built for agent-neutral, consequence-tracking moral views and breaks down once motive- or reason-sensitive views are allowed to be parties to the trade. Please present the strongest form of the objection, then work through the best replies available to a defender of moral trade — for instance, that most of what even demanding theories value is still the occurrence or non-occurrence of the act rather than the trading partner's psychological state, or that a party can privately retain non-instrumental motivating reasons even while openly entering into an explicit bargain — before giving your own considered verdict on whether the objection ultimately succeeds.
