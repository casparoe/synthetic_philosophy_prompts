Among the recurring topics in the rationalist blogosphere are akrasia and the various precommitment devices people build to fight it: Beeminder-style commitment contracts, apps that lock your phone, promises made to accountability partners, and so on. The theoretical backdrop for all of this, as I understand it, is the money-pump argument applied to time preference: if your ranking of "reward now" versus "reward later" flips purely because time has elapsed, and not because you've learned anything new, then in principle a sufficiently patient counterparty could get you to pay, again and again, for the privilege of switching which reward you're currently committed to, extracting a fee from you every time while you never end up any better off than when you started. Hyperbolic discounting is the standard textbook example of a discount function that produces exactly this kind of time-inconsistent ranking, which is presumably why it gets treated as the culprit behind akrasia in the first place.

What I can't work out is why this doesn't settle the normative question entirely. If time-inconsistent preferences really do amount to a formal money pump, it seems like the only way to be diachronically rational is to discount the future exponentially, since that is the one discount function that never reverses its own rankings and therefore can never be exploited by the pump to begin with. And yet I keep noticing that the people who write most confidently about hyperbolic discounting -- treating it as basically settled fact about how humans (and even some animals) value the future -- don't go on to conclude that human preferences are simply and universally irrational and need to be replaced wholesale with exponential ones. Instead they talk about "managing" or "outmaneuvering" the hyperbolic self with commitment devices, as though the underlying time-inconsistent preferences were a fixed constraint to design around rather than a bug to be fixed at the level of the discount function itself. So which is it: is there some way to be immune to the money pump while keeping time-inconsistent preferences fully intact, or is everyone who talks this way quietly failing to take the exploitability argument to its logical conclusion?
