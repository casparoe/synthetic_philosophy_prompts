"Explain the Frege-Geach problem as a challenge for expressivist (non-cognitivist) theories of moral language. Illustrate with an example, then briefly explain how Blackburn's quasi-realism tries to respond to it."

And here's the answer I got:

"Expressivism says that when I say 'Stealing is wrong,' I'm not describing a fact about stealing but expressing my disapproval of it — roughly, 'Boo, stealing!' Geach's problem for this view is that ordinary moral language gets embedded in more complex sentences all the time, and expressivism has trouble handling that. Take this argument:

1. If stealing is wrong, then getting your little brother to steal is wrong.
2. Stealing is wrong.
3. So, getting your little brother to steal is wrong.

This looks like ordinary modus ponens. But on the expressivist story, 'stealing is wrong' expresses disapproval of stealing when it's asserted by itself, as in premise 2. In premise 1, though, it's just sitting inside the antecedent of a conditional — unasserted — so someone can accept premise 1 without disapproving of stealing at all. Since the expressivist has no way to say the two occurrences express the same attitude, 'wrong' ends up equivocal between the premises, and so the argument is actually invalid, in the same way an argument that slides between two meanings of a word like 'bank' is invalid.

Blackburn's answer to this, which he calls norm-expressivism, is to give a systematic account of when combinations of attitudes are consistent or inconsistent with each other, the same way ordinary beliefs can be consistent or inconsistent with each other. Accepting premise 1 commits you to disapproving of getting your little brother to steal whenever you disapprove of stealing itself, so if you also disapprove of stealing (premise 2), you're stuck disapproving of getting your little brother to steal too, on pain of inconsistency. That's what makes the argument valid again — not because of some shared proposition running through both premises, but because of a consistency relation between the attitudes involved."
