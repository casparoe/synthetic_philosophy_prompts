Since retiring from a long career in the experimental sciences, I have been reading my way through epistemology, and two puzzles I keep encountering side by side are the lottery paradox and the preface paradox. Structurally, they appear to share exactly the same form: in each case one is individually justified in believing every member of a set of propositions, while also being justified in believing that the conjunction of that entire set is false (some ticket will win; some claim in the book is mistaken). Yet, so far as I can tell from my reading, the two cases are treated quite differently in the literature: the preface case is generally accepted as involving a perfectly rational author, whereas the lottery case is treated as a genuine crisis in the relationship between probability and outright belief, one that seems to demand some special rule of acceptance. This recalls nothing so much as the multiple-comparisons problem I spent decades working around, where correcting one's significance threshold tells you how to control the rate of error across a whole set of results, without telling you why disbelieving any single result should feel so much stranger than accepting that a paper, taken as a whole, probably contains a mistake somewhere. I would like to understand what principled difference philosophers take there to be between the two paradoxes, if indeed there is one, and whether that difference still holds up once it is restated in these more statistical terms.
