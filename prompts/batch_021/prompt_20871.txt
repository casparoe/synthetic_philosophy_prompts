In my graduate seminar on jurisprudence this term, we are spending a session on the Hart–Fuller exchange, and I want to push my students past the point at which most treatments of it stop. Nearly every summary I encounter reduces the debate to the grudge‑informer case and the slogan that Hart keeps law and morality conceptually separate while Fuller does not, and it seems to me that this framing obscures more than it reveals. What actually interests me is the mismatch between the two writers’ respective concessions to natural‑law thinking: Hart’s “minimum content of natural law,” grounded in a handful of truisms about the human condition — roughly, our vulnerability to violence, our approximate equality of strength, our limited altruism, the scarcity of what we need, and our limited understanding and strength of will — versus Fuller’s eight principles of legality, or “inner morality of law,” illustrated through the parable of the hapless lawgiver Rex, who fails in turn at generality, promulgation, clarity, non‑contradiction, and so on.

I would like you to compare these two theses on their own terms, rather than folding them back into the “separation of law and morals” slogan. First, reconstruct the actual structure of each argument: what kind of premises does each writer rely on — empirical or anthropological facts about human beings, in Hart’s case, versus something closer to the functional or purposive conditions of rule‑governed action, in Fuller’s — and precisely what conclusion is each entitled to draw from them? Second, tell me whether Fuller’s principles, unlike Hart’s minimum content, genuinely license something like the classical natural‑law thesis that a sufficiently defective enactment fails to be law at all, or whether that is a common misreading of Fuller that he would have resisted. Third, consider a legal order that satisfies Hart’s minimum content comfortably — it forbids violence, secures a rough form of property, and enforces agreements — while flagrantly violating most of Fuller’s eight principles through retroactive, secret, self‑contradictory, and inconsistently enforced decrees. Would Hart and Fuller actually disagree about whether this order counts as a legal system, or would they, on reflection, agree about the facts of the case while merely using “law” in two different senses? I am ultimately trying to work out, for my own teaching notes, whether the two theses are genuine rivals, quietly compatible, or simply orthogonal to each other, so please argue for a definite answer rather than just setting out considerations on both sides.
