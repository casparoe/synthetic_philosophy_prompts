In my economics class, we covered the one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma, where mutual defection is the unique Nash equilibrium, and that led me to read on my own about "program equilibrium," a framework in which players submit programs that can inspect each other's source code rather than simply choosing actions directly. One well-known construction in this literature, often called FairBot, achieves mutual cooperation in the one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma: it cooperates with an opponent program if and only if it can find a formal proof that the opponent cooperates with it, and when two copies of it are matched against one another, a proof using Löb's theorem (a result from provability logic) shows that both actually cooperate. This is often presented as a genuine solution to the problem of securing cooperation among purely self-interested agents, succeeding exactly where ordinary game theory says it cannot be done.

Please argue against that framing. Make the case that the Löbian trick does not really solve the cooperation problem but merely relocates it — for instance, to the prior question of how agents with genuinely conflicting interests could ever arrive at common knowledge that they are running matching, trustworthy proof systems in the first place. Before arguing it down, engage seriously with the strongest version of the view that this really is a solution.
