I am a retired experimental physicist who spent the better part of four decades measuring decay rates, scattering cross-sections, and other statistically defined quantities, and who has only in the past couple of years begun reading philosophy in any systematic way. I have drafted the following short essay on interpretations of probability and would value your comments before I take it any further.

My starting point is a puzzle that used to nag at me in the laboratory. When we say that a given radioactive nucleus has some fixed probability of decaying within the next hour, or that a given particle has a certain probability of registering at a given detector angle, we are attributing a probability to a single, unrepeated event. Frequentism, taken strictly, defines probability as a limiting relative frequency within some "collective" of repeated trials, and so has no principled way of assigning that probability to this one nucleus or this one particle except by stipulating some reference class for it to belong to — and, as Reichenbach and later Hájek observed, there is in general no non-arbitrary way of choosing that class.

Popper's propensity interpretation looked, when I first read it, like exactly the fix a working physicist wants: it relocates probability from the sequence of trials to the generating set-up itself, so that even a single, never-repeated trial can have a well-defined objective probability. What troubled me was running across Humphreys's 1985 argument that propensities cannot, in general, satisfy the full probability calculus. As I understand it, the difficulty is that probability theory requires conditional probabilities to be invertible via Bayes's theorem, whereas propensities are meant to run in a single causal or temporal direction, from set-up to outcome; the "inverse propensity" of an earlier cause given a later effect is accordingly not the kind of thing that has any objective, dispositional meaning. If that is right, propensity does not, after all, rescue single-case probability from the reference class problem, since it cannot fully answer to the mathematics of probability in the first place.

My tentative conclusion — reached with more confidence than I suspect it deserves — is that a scientist should simply treat single-case probabilities of this kind as rational degrees of belief, reserving frequency and propensity talk for the actual ensembles we manipulate in the laboratory, even though this sits uneasily with the usual thought that quantum-mechanical probabilities are objective chances rather than credences.

I would be grateful for your comments and suggestions on three points: first, whether I have represented Humphreys's argument correctly and charitably, and what the strongest replies to it are that I have left out; second, whether my closing move to a subjectivist reading is too quick, given the usual objectivist gloss on quantum probabilities; and third, whether the essay as a whole holds together, or whether I have been sliding between distinct senses of "single-case probability" without noticing.
