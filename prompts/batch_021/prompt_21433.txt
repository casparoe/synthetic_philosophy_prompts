I am taking a seminar on the ethics of autonomous systems this semester, and something from my work outside class has been troubling me in a way I have not been able to fully articulate. I work as a contract programmer for a small savings-app startup whose product automatically executes a pre-agreed investment plan on a user's behalf: steady contributions, scheduled rebalancing, and so on. User testing showed that when people could pause or cancel the plan at will, they tended to do so at precisely the worst moments — panic-selling during downturns, skipping contributions after a bad week — which undermined the entire point of automating their discipline. The lead engineer's proposed fix is to make cancellation "sticky": any pause or override request is held for forty-eight hours, during which the app weighs its own running model of the user's stated long-term goal more heavily than the live request, on the grounds that this is simply what the tool needs in order to reliably do its job. When I asked how the app is supposed to distinguish a panic reaction from a genuine, considered change of mind, no one had a real answer beyond "the delay and the model's judgment will sort it out." This does not feel like an ordinary autonomy-versus-paternalism dispute to me, though I cannot quite say why. I would appreciate your take on what is really going on here, and whether there is a principled way for the team to draw the line.
