For my school's philosophy club, I've been putting together a presentation on panpsychism as a response to the hard problem of consciousness, and most of what I've read treats the combination problem as the view's most serious weakness: if fundamental particles have simple, isolated experiences, how could countless micro-experiences ever add up to one unified subject, like me? Cosmopsychism is often presented as a way around this, since it starts from a single cosmic subject and treats individual minds as somehow derived from it, so there is no longer any "summing" of many subjects into one. But cosmopsychists still owe us an account of how one undivided cosmic experience gives rise to distinct, bounded subjects like you or me, a challenge that has come to be called the decombination problem. I'd like you to argue that the decombination problem is not actually any easier than the subject-summing problem it was meant to replace, so that cosmopsychism's supposed advantage over constitutive (bottom-up) panpsychism is illusory. Please also engage with the strongest response a cosmopsychist could give against your argument.
