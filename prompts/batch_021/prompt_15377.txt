I spent something like 35 years doing fieldwork on corvids before I retired, and lately I've fallen down a philosophy of science rabbit hole — specifically Hempel's raven paradox, which amuses me to no end given my actual professional history with the birds in question (for what it's worth, not every raven I ever banded was perfectly black, but that's a seperate argument for another day). I started a little podcast out of my garage a few months back, mostly so old colleagues who are as bored in retirement as I am have something to listen to, and next month's guest is a young philosopher whose whole dissertation defended the probabilistic dissolution of the paradox — the line that says yes, technically, spotting a red herring confirms "all ravens are black" (since it's an instance of the logically equivalent claim "all non-black things are non-ravens"), but the degree of confirmation is so close to zero, once you account for how vastly outnumbered ravens are by non-black things, that our intuitions and the logic actually end up agreeing, more or less. Every time I've pushed back on this over email he retreats to basically the same line — "confirmation comes in degrees, and once you actually do the math the degree here is negligible, so there's no real tension" — and I'd like interview questions that don't let him rest on that so easily.

Could you draft me eight to ten questions for the actual interview, building so that his favorite move gets harder to fall back on as we go? Somewhere near the start I want a question that just makes him lay out, for listeners who've never heard of any of this, exactly how Nicod's criterion plus the equivalence condition combine to generate the paradox in the first place — I don't want to assume everyone already gets why a red herring is supposed to be evidence about ravens. In the middle I want a question that really presses on whether "vanishingly small but technically positive" is doing any genuine philosophical work, or whether it's just a polished way of not answering why our intuitions were so far off. And I definately want one built around I. J. Good's old counterexample, where, given the right background assumptions about how ravens and non-black things are distributed, actually finding a black raven can lower rather than raise your confidence in "all ravens are black" — which seems to me like it threatens his whole strategy, since apparently you can't even trust the math to get the sign right, let alone the size. For each question, throw in a follow-up tuned to whatever answer he's most likely to give, the way a good interviewer already has the comeback loaded before asking the first version.
