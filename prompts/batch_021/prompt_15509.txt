Here is the question as it was posed, followed by the student's answer in full:

Question: In "The Paradoxes of Time Travel," David Lewis considers Tim, who builds a time machine and travels to the past hoping to kill his own grandfather before Tim's father is ever conceived. Explain (a) Lewis's distinction between a time traveler's personal time and external time, (b) why Lewis thinks Tim can never actually succeed in killing Grandfather even though nothing intrinsic to Tim rules it out, and (c) how this bears on the question of whether a time traveler can change the past as opposed to merely affecting it. Close with a brief verdict: does Lewis's discussion succeed in showing that backward time travel of this sort is a logically and metaphysically coherent possibility?

Student's answer: Lewis's example concerns a time traveler named Tim, who builds a time machine specifically in order to travel back and kill his own grandfather before Tim's father is conceived, thereby preventing his own existence. Lewis distinguishes between external time -- the ordinary time order of events in the world, the one an outside observer or a calendar would track -- and personal time, which is the time order internal to the traveler, tracked by his aging, his memories, and the causal order of the stages of his life. For an ordinary person the two coincide, but a time traveler's personal time can run in a different direction, or at a different rate, from external time; Tim can be, say, thirty years old in personal time while arriving at a point decades before his own birth in external time.

Lewis's central move is to distinguish two senses of the verb "can." Holding fixed only facts intrinsic to Tim -- his marksmanship, the loaded rifle, the clear shot, the absence of anyone stopping him -- Tim can kill Grandfather. But holding fixed a wider set of facts, including the fact that Grandfather did in fact survive that day and went on to father Tim's parent, Tim cannot kill Grandfather: were he to try, something would always intervene, not because of some special time-travel force field, but simply because the facts about the rest of the world guarantee it. Lewis reconciles the two verdicts by treating them as claims relative to different sets of held-fixed facts, and, to make the reconciliation fully consistent, he takes the further step of holding that Tim's failed attempt is what forks the single timeline he departed from into two branches, one in which Grandfather survives and one in which he doesn't, so that the "can" and "cannot" claims end up being true of different branches rather than flatly contradicting one another.

Because no branch is ever overwritten or erased, Tim never changes the past, though he is certainly among its causes -- he affects it without changing it. On this basis, Lewis's discussion succeeds, and does more than establish mere logical coherence: it gives good reason to think backward time travel is physically likely, since nothing about the actual large-scale structure of spacetime rules out the closed causal loops the story requires.
