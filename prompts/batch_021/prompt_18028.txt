Three weeks ago I told my brother, in front of the rest of the family, that he had no standing to lecture my teenage niece about her screen time when he is on his phone at practically every family dinner we've had this year. He didn't deny that his own habit is a problem; he just said that fact makes it his business and not mine to raise hers, and the remark has stayed with me longer than I expected. Trying to work out whether he had a point or was simply deflecting, I've been reading around the philosophical literature on standing to blame -- there's a well-known essay by G. A. Cohen asking who is really in a position to condemn terrorism given the record of their own governments, and a newer set of exchanges about whether it's hypocrisy itself, as opposed to plain inconsistency or insincerity, that actually does the disqualifying work -- but I'm no closer to a settled view of my own.

Against that backdrop, I ended up far more invested than I expected when two members of the board of the co-op building I live in -- Nora and Jared -- got into an extended disagreement in the hallway after last week's meeting, over a case that had nothing to do with my family but turned on exactly the same question. Our board president had used part of the meeting to reprimand a resident by name for repeatedly leaving his car overnight in the visitor spots, calling it "inconsiderate and exactly the kind of rule-breaking that makes people resent living here" -- and the president is herself seen doing the same thing often enough that half the building has noticed. (1) Jared said the president had no standing to say any of that: she is doing precisely what she is condemning, and someone who won't hold herself to a rule forfeits the right to enforce it on someone else. (2) Nora replied that this confuses two separate questions -- the president's hypocrisy doesn't make her wrong that the parking is inconsiderate, and dismissing the criticism because of who raised it is just a tu quoque that lets the actual behavior off the hook. (3) Jared conceded the content might still be true, but said that was beside the point: the issue was never whether the parking is bad, but whether the president, specifically, gets to be the one standing up and saying so, and those are different questions. (4) Nora granted the distinction but pushed back further: if merely falling short of a rule stripped you of the standing to invoke it, almost nobody could ever criticize anybody, since everyone fails to live up to some standard they genuinely hold; what should matter is whether the president actually believes the rule is worth following and regards herself as bound by it, not whether she has ever personally broken it. (5) Jared answered that there is a real difference between an occasional lapse by someone who is honestly trying and a pattern the whole building can point to; at some point, habitually breaking a rule without any apparent effort at compliance stops looking like a lapse and starts looking like quietly exempting yourself from it, which is exactly the kind of double standard that should undercut anyone's standing to enforce that rule on someone else.

Neither of them was willing to concede the point, and I've gone back and forth on who has the better of it ever since.
