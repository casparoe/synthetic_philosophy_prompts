And here is my friend's answer:

"Consequentialism says you should always do whatever produces the most good overall, weighing everyone's welfare equally. The demandingness objection points out that if you take this seriously, you're basically required to give away almost everything beyond what you need for a bare minimum of welfare, since your $30 at the movies could instead save a life through an effective charity, and there's no natural stopping point until the marginal cost to you outweighs the benefit to others. Singer's drowning-child case is the classic illustration of why we're supposed to feel this pull even though it demands more than we usually think morality can ask of an ordinary person.

Two responses: first, agent-centered prerogatives, proposed by both Scheffler and Kagan, hold that agents should be permitted to give extra weight to their own projects and interests, so you aren't always required to sacrifice your own good for a slightly greater good elsewhere. Second, satisficing consequentialism, defended by writers like Murphy, says you only have to do your fair share of promoting the good rather than the theoretical maximum — if other people aren't doing their part, that's not your job to cover for them, so you just have to reach a 'good enough' level, which is a much lower bar than maximizing and takes the pressure off any one person. The main difference between the two is that the prerogative view protects your own interests specifically, while satisficing caps the total amount of good any one person has to produce.

One cost of these responses is that they can look ad hoc: once agents are allowed to stop short of the maximum, it's hard to say in a principled way exactly where the new, lower bar should sit, and critics worry this just smuggles back in whatever level of sacrifice already felt comfortable."
