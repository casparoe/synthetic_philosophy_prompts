This term, in my seminar on formal epistemology and rational agency, we've just finished a unit on program equilibrium. The original result, due to Tennenholtz, is that if the players in a game submit programs — rather than bare strategies — that can inspect one another's source code before acting, mutual cooperation can become a genuine equilibrium even in a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma. We then covered a later refinement built on provability logic, in which agents such as FairBot and PrudentBot cooperate only if they can prove that the other program cooperates against them, and in which mutual cooperation between two FairBots turns out to follow, rather surprisingly, from Löb's theorem, with no repeated play, reputation, or outside enforcer required.

For the final assignment, instead of a paper, we've been asked to write a short story that makes the philosophical stakes of this material vivid for a reader who knows no logic. Could you write one for me, around 1,200–1,600 words? Let it involve two parties — not necessarily AI systems; nations, firms, or something stranger would work as well — facing a single, irreversible choice to cooperate or defect, who can extend any trust at all only by exchanging verifiable decision procedures. I'd like the plot itself, rather than any appended commentary, to make clear why reasoning of the form "I'll cooperate if I can prove you will" threatens an infinite regress, how a Löbian trick of this kind escapes it, and whether PrudentBot's refusal to be exploited by unconditional cooperators shows that transparency alone is still not sufficient for trust.
