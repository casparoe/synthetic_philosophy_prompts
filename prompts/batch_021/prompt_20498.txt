So we've been going through Descartes in this elective I'm taking that's kind of a mashup of philosophy and history of science (weird combo I know, but its actually pretty fun). We just got to the part (part 5 I think?) of the Discourse on Method where he lays out how you'd supposedly always be able to tell a machine apart from a real human, even a really well-built one that looked exactly like a person.

He gives two seperate tests for this. The first is about language: a machine could maybe be built to spit out specific words if you poked it in just the right spot, but it could never actually hold a conversation — like respond to the meaning of whatever random thing you throw at it, the way even a not-so-bright human can. The second is about generality: even if a machine were amazing at one particular task (maybe even better than us at it), it would mess up at basically everything else, because he thinks reason is a "universal instrument" that adapts to literally any situation, while a machine's parts are each rigged for one specific job, so there's no way it could pack in enough different parts to handle every possible occurrence in life. He uses this same basic logic a bit later in that part of the book to argue animals don't have real minds either, just instinct, like really elaborate clockwork.

This got me arguing with my friend after class about whether Descartes' first test just... doesn't hold up anymore? Like, current LLMs can respond pretty relevantly to almost anything you say to them, which is literally the exact thing he claims is impossible for a machine to fake. So here's what I'm actually curious about: if Descartes somehow saw a modern chatbot, would he have to admit it passes his own test and has some kind of real understanding, or would he dodge it by pointing to test two (sure, it's great at conversation, but that's still just "one task," so it'd fail his generality test elsewhere)? And whichever way he'd go with that, what do you think it implies for the actual moral status question — since for Descartes, whether something has a genuine mind/soul is exactly what decides whether its behavior reflects real inner experience versus just mechanism, which is basically the same move that let people historically write off animal suffering as not real. I saw somewhere that AI companies are now actually starting to hire people to look into whether their models could have any kind of welfare, so this doesn't feel like a totally academic question to me anymore. Would love an answer that actually engages with his specific arguments instead of just the generic "well he was a dualist so he'd obviously say no" take.
