Question: "Explain Thomson's lamp and the conclusion Thomson drew from it. Then explain how Paul Benacerraf's reply to Thomson complicates that conclusion, addressing in particular how the infinite sequence involved in the lamp case compares to the infinite sequence of sub-tasks involved in Zeno's paradox of the runner."

Student's answer: "Thomson's lamp starts off and is then switched on at 1/2 minute, off at 3/4 minute, on at 7/8 minute, and so on, with the nth switching occurring at 1 − 1/2^n minutes, so that infinitely many switchings occur within a single minute — what Thomson called a 'super-task.' He argued that at the one-minute mark the lamp cannot be on, since every stretch during which it is on is brought to an end by a later switching-off, and cannot be off, by the same reasoning run in reverse; yet by excluded middle it must be one or the other. Thomson took this to show that completing a super-task of this kind is not a coherent possibility, and used the lamp to cast general doubt on whether infinitely many tasks could ever be completed in a finite stretch of time.

Benacerraf's reply points out that Zeno's runner faces a structurally similar situation: to cross a room, he must first cross half of it, then half of what remains, and so on, completing infinitely many sub-runs whose durations — 1/2, 1/4, 1/8, ... minute — sum to a finite total time. We do not conclude from this that the runner never crosses the room; we conclude that he does, because the sequence of times involved converges. Benacerraf extends this reasoning to the lamp: because the switching times 1/2, 3/4, 7/8, ... likewise converge to one minute, the lamp's state converges too, settling at whichever value the later switchings are heading toward — which, since each switch-on is immediately followed by a switch-off, is 'off.' On this reading, Thomson's mistake was to think that no answer was available, when the mathematics of the situation, properly understood, supplies one: the lamp is off at t = 1, and the paradox is defused in essentially the same way Zeno's is."
