I'm writing a historical novel about Émilie du Châtelet and I've hit a scene I can't crack myself. It's her daughter's wedding at Cirey, and she's been talked into giving the toast -- some actual advice to the bride on how to be happy in a marriage. Problem is the room is split: half are her usual crowd (Voltaire, a couple of the mathematicians who've actually read her essay on happiness) and half are the groom's very correct, very Catholic relatives, who'd be scandalized if she said what she actually thinks. I dont want her chickening out into generic 18th-century wedding-toast piety about virtue and duty, but I also don't want her being shocking just for the sake of it -- I want the toast to smuggle in two or three of her real ideas from the Discourse on Happiness (the stuff about illusions, passions, gambling, study -- whichever of those you think is actually load-bearing) in a way that works for the people in the room who know that text without alienating the ones who don't. Can you write it -- something she could plausibly stand up and say out loud, maybe 400 words, that would make a Voltaire in the corner smirk without making the mother-in-law faint?
