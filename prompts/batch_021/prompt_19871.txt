I've served for the past couple of years as the required "community member" on my hospital's Compassionate Use and Allocation Committee - the group that decides how a handful of manufacturer-donated doses of a not-yet-fully-approved cell therapy for a rare inherited condition get distributed each quarter, since we always have more medically eligible patients than doses. I have no training in medicine, economics, or philosophy - I run a small equipment-rental business the rest of the week - but I've spent a lot of my own time this past year reading about the ethics of rationing so that I'm not just the token layperson nodding along, and one disagreement on the committee has stuck with me because I think both sides are onto something real and I honestly can't work out who's right.

Our policy ranks patients on a clinical-priority formula, and when two land in an exact tie - which happens most cycles, since the formula is fairly coarse - the remaining dose currently goes to whoever wins a random drawing. One committee member, a physician who did a fellowship in health economics, wants to drop the drawing in favor of a fixed tiebreaker, such as longer time on the registry. His argument, which he traces to the economist John Harsanyi, runs roughly like this: any defensible way of adding up people's welfare under uncertainty has to reduce to maximizing a weighted sum of expected benefit, and judged by that standard, a fixed rule and an honest lottery come out identical, since nobody can predict in advance, before patients are even diagnosed, whose registry date will end up being the longer one any more than anyone can predict a coin toss. Since the two procedures are equivalent by the only standard that should matter, he argues, we should simply adopt whichever one is administratively simpler and easier to explain to families, rather than keep a drawing that only serves to make the process feel fairer. A few other members are unpersuaded, but they keep losing the argument on his terms: pressed on what the drawing accomplishes that the fixed rule doesn't, given that for the two specific families sitting across from us tonight one already has the longer wait and the other doesn't, the best they can offer is that a coin flip "feels fairer," which he doesn't accept as a reason by itself.

Could you write the short memo I'd circulate before our next meeting? I'd like it to come down clearly on one option - keep the drawing, replace it, or adopt something else entirely - with a concrete implementation timeline, and I'd especially like it to engage with the Harsanyi-based argument on its own terms rather than just reasserting that randomness is intrinsically fair, since that move is exactly what hasn't worked on our committee so far.
