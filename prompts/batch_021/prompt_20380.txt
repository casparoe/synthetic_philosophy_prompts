A single meeting of the three-member personnel committee I sit on has drawn me unexpectedly deep into the theory of judgment aggregation. Under our bylaws, a candidate earns tenure just in case their record is judged meritorious in both scholarship and teaching. On the file we reviewed last month, one member found both criteria met and voted to grant tenure; the second found scholarship met but teaching not met, and voted to deny it; the third found teaching met but scholarship not met, and also voted to deny it. Tallied criterion by criterion, however, a majority of the committee affirms that scholarship is met and a majority affirms that teaching is met, so a premise-based procedure yields "grant," even though only one member, taken individually, favors that result. The resulting dispute over which vote represented the committee's real judgment nearly ended the meeting in acrimony, and I have since learned that we had walked directly into a textbook instance of the discursive dilemma. I now have to draft handbook language settling, for future cases, whether such committees should aggregate premise-by-premise or should instead simply tally each member's own all-things-considered verdict. Please construct the strongest possible case for enshrining the conclusion-based (outcome) procedure rather than the premise-based one, engaging directly with the sharpest objection to it: that outcome voting can ratify a verdict for which no majority of the committee shares a single justifying reason.
