A friend and I spent the better part of an evening arguing about whether two perfectly honest, perfectly rational people can ever agree to disagree about a straightforward empirical question. The disagreement started over something fairly mundane — we were debating how likely a mutual acquaintance's new venture was to succeed — and when I suggested that two reasonable people can look at the same murky situation and land on different estimates, my friend, who has a background in game theory, replied that this is exactly the kind of case Aumann's agreement theorem rules out, at least among agents who count as rational in the relevant sense. I followed the rough shape of Aumann's result well enough: if two Bayesians' posterior probabilities for some event are common knowledge between them, those posteriors must coincide. What I couldn't get a satisfying grip on, and what my friend kept invoking without ever quite unpacking, was the role played by the "common prior assumption" in getting that result off the ground, and whether the assumption is itself something rationality demands of an agent or merely a modeling convenience that quietly does the real work.

I'd like your help getting clear on this. Could you explain, in some depth, what the common prior assumption actually says, how it enters into the derivation of the impossibility-of-agreeing-to-disagree result, and what it would even mean for two agents' priors to differ "irrationally" as opposed to just differently? I'm especially interested in how the assumption relates to the Harsanyi doctrine — the idea that any apparent difference in priors must ultimately be a difference in information about some deeper, shared state of the world — and whether that doctrine amounts to a substantive philosophical commitment or is instead something that Bayesian rationality already forces on us. If there's a serious case for the opposite view — that Bayesian rationality, on its own, is silent about where one's prior comes from, and therefore never actually requires two rational agents to share one — I'd like to understand that case too, since it was more or less the crux my friend and I kept circling back to without resolving.
