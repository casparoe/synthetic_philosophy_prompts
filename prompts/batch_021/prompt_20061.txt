Family Thanksgiving got a little heated this year and I'm still turning it over, so let me get a second opinion. My uncle R. basically checked out on his wife and two kids for a few years back in the 90s -- bad gambling habit, drank a lot, borrowed money from pretty much everyone and never payed it back, missed most of his daughter's childhood. He's been sober for close to sixteen years now, has a totally different temperment, is actually a pretty involved grandfather these days -- by all accounts a genuinely better and kinder person. At dinner he tried once again to explain to my mom (his sister) why she should finally let it go, and the line he used was "that wasn't me, I don't even recognize that guy anymore, I was basically a different person back then." My mom was furious about it -- she said that's a cop-out, that he doesn't get to just declare himself a new person and walk away from what he did -- and honestly I went back and forth all night about who I agreed with more.

I happen to be teaching our "self and personal identity" unit this term with a comparative bent -- Locke and Parfit alongside some classical Buddhist material on the self -- so naturally that's the direction my brain went, though I realize I don't actually have a clean answer here, just competing intuitions. Is there something philosophically serious behind R.'s "I was basically a different person" move, or is it just a rhetorical dodge that happens to borrow the language of some fairly respectable theories? And if we do take it seriously -- if enough psychological change over time really can loosen the grip of "he did this, full stop, end of story" -- doesn't that same reasoning also mean he can't take much personal credit for having "become" sober and kind, since that transformation was, on his own logic, mostly the doing of a string of not-quite-him selves rather than of him specifically? Curious how much weight you think this move can actually bear, and whether it cuts as cleanly in R.'s favor as he seems to think it does.
