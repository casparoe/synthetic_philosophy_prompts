I'm reporting on the long-running debate over whether insurers should be permitted to see the results of predictive genetic tests, and I think I've come across the detail that settles it. I.J. Good's 1967 theorem on the value of information establishes, as a matter of Bayesian decision theory, that a rational agent's expected utility can never be lowered by learning a relevant, cost-free piece of evidence before acting on it — the expected value of information, in the jargon, is always non-negative. If that's a genuine mathematical result, then any rule barring insurers or applicants from using genetic information would seem to make the resulting contracts strictly less accurate, and therefore less efficient, whatever the equity case for such rules might be. Yet plenty of serious economists and bioethicists argue that restricting this information can leave everyone, including future policyholders, better off. Have I missed some wrinkle in how Good's theorem applies to a case like this, or are these economists really just setting aside a settled piece of decision theory for political reasons?
