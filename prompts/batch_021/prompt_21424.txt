Since retiring from a career in experimental physics, I have been auditing a philosophy of mind reading group at the university, and last month we spent a session on Alan Turing's 1950 essay "Computing Machinery and Intelligence." Of the nine "contrary views" Turing canvasses in that essay, the one that has stuck with me is the last: the argument from extrasensory perception, where critics apparently worried that human thought draws on telepathic or clairvoyant powers that no computer could ever be built to share. My understanding is that Turing handles this the way any good empiricist should handle a parapsychological claim: he walks through the statistical case that had been built up by researchers like J. B. Rhine, finds it wanting, and concludes that the objection dissolves once the alleged phenomenon is shown not to hold up, leaving the machine free to compete with the human contestant on equal footing.

When I raised this at the reading group, a couple of the philosophers present seemed to think I had the section backwards, treating it instead as a minor scandal in the paper — something historians of the Turing test tend to hurry past rather than hold up as an example of his rigor. Neither of them explained exactly what I had gotten wrong before we ran out of time. Given how careful and quantitatively literate Turing was everywhere else in this essay, I would be grateful for a clear account of why his treatment of the ESP objection specifically has this reputation among people who study the paper closely, and where exactly my reading of it as a straightforward statistical debunking of telepathy goes wrong.
