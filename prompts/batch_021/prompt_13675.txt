I'm a PhD student in cognitive science -- my own dissertation work is on decision-making under uncertainty, not this exact stuff, but our lab runs a friday reading group that's spent the whole semester on predictive processing (a bunch of Friston's papers, most of Hohwy's book), so I can follow it well enough. After last week's session two people who stuck around, Sam and Priya, got into a genuine argument over drinks and I want an outside read on it, because I was too tired to referee and I honestly keep flip-flopping on who had the better of it.

(1) Sam opened by saying the free energy principle is basically unfalsifiable: any system that keeps existing at a nonequilibrium steady state will, just by persisting, occupy a narrow repertoire of states, so you can always define a distribution over those states where "typical" equals "low surprise" -- meaning the whole apparatus just redescribes "living things don't dissolve into their surroundings" in the language of variational Bayes and calls it a theory of the brain. (2) Priya said that's a fair hit on the free energy principle in its most general, "FEP-proper" form, but its not what's doing the actual scientific work -- the process theories built on top, like predictive coding's specific claims about cortical microcircuits (superficial pyramidal cells carrying prediction errors forward, deep layers carrying predictions back, precision implemented as synaptic gain), generate falsifiable predictions, and some of them, like mismatch negativity and repetition suppression, have actually held up. (3) Sam granted that, but brought up the dark room problem: if minimizing prediction error is really the brain's fundamental imperative, the cheapest way to do that is to find a dark, silent, unchanging room and stay there, yet real organisms do the opposite -- they explore, play, chase novelty and even hardship -- so the theory's headline prediction seems to fail against the most obvious facts about behavior. (4) Priya answered that this was solved, not patched on after the fact: active inference agents minimize *expected* free energy over policies, which decomposes into a pragmatic term (approach preferred outcomes) and an epistemic term (resolve uncertainty, i.e. seek information), and separately, your own generative model already contains priors about yourself as a foraging, social creature, so a dark room massively violates those self-expectations instead of satisfying them. (5) Sam shot back that this is exactly what makes the theory unfalsifiable where it actually counts: now any behavior can be redescribed after the fact as either pragmatic or epistemic value-seeking, or blamed on having the "wrong" priors, so a framework flexible enough to explain both novelty-seeking and its opposite (creatures of pure habit) hasn't been rescued from the dark room, it's just bought its way out by giving up predictive bite.

Neither of them budged and I walked home still chewing on it. Could you go through moves (1) through (5) and say who's actually got the better argument at each one, not just an overall winner? And is there something more basic going on here that you think both of them are talking past?
