I'm drafting a piece on how the great nineteenth-century antislavery coalition splintered over what was, at bottom, a surprisingly narrow question of textual interpretation, and Frederick Douglass's own change of mind sits at the center of it. For roughly a decade after he began speaking for the Massachusetts Anti-Slavery Society, Douglass held William Lloyd Garrison's position that the Constitution was fundamentally a slaveholders' document, to be denounced and refused participation in rather than invoked on behalf of enslaved people — Garrison's own phrase for it, "a covenant with death, and an agreement with hell," became something close to a Garrisonian article of faith.

By 1851, though, Douglass had broken publicly with Garrison, persuaded in part by the constitutional arguments of writers like Lysander Spooner. His new position was that the document had to be read according to its actual language and stated purposes rather than the private wishes of individual framers, and that on a plain reading it never once uses the words "slave" or "slavery" — not in the three-fifths clause, not in the fugitive-slave clause, not in the clause temporarily shielding the international slave trade from congressional interference. Read that way, Douglass argued, the Constitution was best understood as an antislavery document that proslavery interests had simply hijacked in practice. It's hard not to notice how closely this anticipates arguments we still have under the heading of textualism versus original intent, which is part of why I don't want to treat it as mere biography.

A constitutional historian I interviewed for the piece thinks Douglass's later reading was closer to wishful advocacy dressed up as exegesis; another was just as certain it was the more rigorous of the two positions. Rather than adjudicate between my sources, I'd like to see how the argument actually holds up on its own terms. Write an essay that weighs the Garrisonian reading against the mature Douglass reading of the pre-Reconstruction Constitution, focused specifically on how the disputed compromise clauses should be interpreted given that none of them names slavery outright. Engage seriously with the strongest version of each side, take up at least one real objection to whichever position you end up favoring, and close with an actual verdict rather than a both-sides shrug — a qualified conclusion (something like "defensible for clause X but not clause Y") is perfectly fine, so long as you argue for it rather than just gesture at the complexity.
