My father-in-law has always been a bit of a JFK guy but ever since he retired he's gotten WAY more intense about it, and it's turned into a whole thing at every family dinner now. I decided that instead of just rolling my eyes and arguing surface-level facts with him, i'd try to actually understand the epistemology of why conspiracy theories are (supposedly) bad reasoning, so i went down a bit of a rabbit hole myself reading philosophy papers, of all things. I found this idea, I think from Brian Keeley's paper on this, that the hallmark of an "unwarranted" conspiracy theory is that it's self-sealing: any piece of counter-evidence (an official denial, a document release that doesn't show what was predicted, an expert saying there's nothing there) just gets absorbed as more proof of the cover-up, so the theory can never actually be falsified by anything.

That felt like a real insight until it occured to me in the shower that this is basically also just how "legitimate" science handles anomalies sometimes. Uranus's orbit didn't match Newtonian predictions, and astronomers didn't scrap Newton, they hypothesized an unseen extra planet was tugging on it — and they were right, that's how Neptune got discovered. But then the same guy (Le Verrier) tried the exact same move on Mercury's orbit, predicted another invisible planet called Vulcan to explain ITS weird precession, and that one turned out to be completely wrong (no planet, it was general relativity all along). So the "explain away the anomaly with an extra unfalsifiable-ish assumption" move produced one triumphant success and one total dud, using literally the same method on the same guy's part. I mentioned this to my sister-in-law who's ABD in philosophy and she said something about Lakatos and "progressive vs degenerating" research programs and then had to run, so that didn't fully close the loop for me.

So here's my actual question: is there a principled, non-question-begging difference between the auxiliary-hypothesis-saving move scientists make (Neptune, or even the failed Vulcan attempt) and the cover-up-hypothesis move conspiracy theorists make, or is Keeley's "self-sealing" criterion actually on shakier ground than it looks once you notice that real science leans on the same Duhem-Quine trick of protecting a theory from refutation? I'd like an actual answer to this, not just a comforting gesture at "well, one of them is usually true and one usually isn't" — that's the conclusion I already know people want to reach, not the mechanism that's supposed to get you there.
