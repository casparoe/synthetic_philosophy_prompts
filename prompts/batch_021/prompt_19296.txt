My partner and I recently began IVF after I learned, somewhat unexpectedly through unrelated genetic testing, that I carry the mutation for Huntington's disease that already affected my grandmother and is now affecting my father. Because our clinic offers preimplantation genetic testing, each embryo from our first cycle was biopsied and analyzed before we decided which one to transfer. Of the six that survived to testing, three carried the mutation and three did not, and our genetic counselor walked us through the results using phrases like "giving your child the best possible start."

That phrase has bothered me ever since I repeated it to a friend, who pointed out something that seems obviously true once stated plainly: whichever embryo we transfer will become one particular person, so choosing an embryo without the mutation over one with it does not make any single future child healthier. It decides which of several different possible children gets to exist at all - there was never a scenario in which "our child" both existed and had the mutation removed, only distinct possible children among whom we are choosing. I still think it is obviously right to transfer an unaffected embryo, but I am no longer sure I can defend that preference by saying we would be sparing "the child" a devastating illness, since no particular child is thereby spared anything. I am also not sure this is quite the same situation as a couple simply choosing when to conceive, since our embryos already exist as distinct entities before we ever choose among them, which seems relevantly different from picking a moment in time and letting whichever sperm and egg happen to meet.

I have a follow-up appointment with the counselor in a couple of weeks, and I would like to walk in with a clearer head rather than leaning on the comforting phrases everyone around me keeps reaching for. Is there a genuine philosophical difficulty here about what, if anything, we owe to the child who is eventually born as opposed to the embryos we do not transfer, or am I overthinking a distinction that should not actually change how we decide?
