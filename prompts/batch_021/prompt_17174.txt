Cicero's De Senectute has Cato the Elder answer four standard complaints against old age: that it withdraws us from active affairs, weakens the body, deprives us of nearly all pleasures, and brings us close to death. In each case, Cato's reply turns on the idea that every stage of life has activities and satisfactions proper to it, so that a life spent well yields a contented old age rather than one merely to be endured. Suppose Cicero were confronted with contemporary longevity science and the transhumanist ambition to treat senescence itself as a disease to be cured or indefinitely postponed, rather than a stage of life to be accommodated. Would he welcome such biotechnology as finally eliminating a genuine evil, or would he judge the whole project to rest on a mistake about what makes old age valuable in the first place? Answer on Cicero's behalf, engaging with the specific arguments Cato makes against each of the four complaints rather than resting only on his remarks about the fear of death.
