For a couple of years now, a small weekday-morning study group at my synagogue has been working through Maimonides' legal code, the Mishneh Torah, a chapter or two a day, in what people call a "Rambam Yomi" cycle. I'm a software engineer with no background in Jewish philosophy or rabbinics, and I only started showing up because a coworker more or less guilted me into it, but somewhere around the laws of Shabbat I got hooked, and now we're finishing Sefer Avodah, the volume covering the Temple service and its sacrifices. It describes, in loving and exhaustive detail, exactly how an animal is to be slaughtered, its blood caught and dashed against the altar, its parts salted and burned, down to where the officiating priest is meant to stand -- the kind of detail that makes the whole institution feel permanent rather than provisional.

That is the detail that has been nagging at me while I try to figure out what to say. In this same law code, Maimonides is clear that the sacrificial service is expected to resume once the Temple is rebuilt. But in the Guide for the Perplexed, writing in a far more skeptical tone, he argues that God commanded animal sacrifice in the first place only as a concession to the idolatrous habits of the ancient world -- something the Israelites could not have been weaned off all at once -- and that had they already been capable of it, He would have commanded prayer alone from the outset. Nachmanides, reaching the same verses in his own Torah commentary, all but accuses Maimonides of hollowing out the meaning of a large part of the Torah in order to make it philosophically respectable. I don't think this has a tidy resolution, and I'd be suspicious of a speech that pretended otherwise.

Our rabbi has asked me to give a few minutes of remarks at the siyum, the small celebration marking the end of a cycle of study, and I'd like you to write that speech for me. The room will be about thirty people: the rabbi and four or five study-group regulars who will recognize this exact tension the moment I raise it, and everyone else -- including my parents, who are driving up for it -- who have never heard of the Guide for the Perplexed and are mainly there for the bagels afterward. The speech needs to actually sit with the problem rather than ignore it or use it to score a cute point about how none of what we studied really mattered, and it has to work for both halves of the room at once. Somewhere around five or six minutes, please, warm rather than academic, and it should land as genuine respect for what we just spent two years learning rather than as a critique of it.
