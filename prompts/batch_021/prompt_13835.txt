My friend and I spent a long dinner arguing about Taurek's "Should the Numbers Count?" -- his claim that no one bears anything like the loss of five lives, since each of the five loses only what any single person can lose, and his suggestion that a fair coin toss can be as defensible as automatically saving the greater number. She thinks this is a seminar-room curiosity that would collapse the moment real lives were at stake; I'm no longer sure, because the scarce-medications subcommittee I sit on is about to write standing policy for almost exactly this problem.

Our regional antivenom supply is dispensed in lots that cannot be freely divided between patients: a single lot can either fully treat one patient with severe, multi-system envenomation, or adequately treat four or five patients presenting the same week with moderate envenomation, but never both. A bad snake season has made this collision a near-monthly occurrence, and case-by-case decisions are no longer defensible. One faction wants a fixed rule: give the lot to whichever group is larger, adjusted for each side's odds of survival. Another, making exactly the argument my friend dismissed at dinner, wants a weighted lottery giving the single severe patient a genuine chance rather than a foregone conclusion, since that patient's claim isn't diminished by how many other separate people happen to need it that week.

Please draft the short memo I'll bring to the subcommittee: recommend one concrete standing policy, spell out how it applies the next time a lot must be split this way, and take the losing side's strongest argument seriously rather than dismissing it the way my friend dismissed mine.
