I've spent the last three years as the resident philosophy professor on my university's Research Integrity Committee -- the body that takes over once a preliminary inquiry decides a misconduct allegation deserves a full investigation -- and until now our cases have been the mundane kind that resolve themselves once someone pulls the relevant lab notebook or email thread. The one now in front of us will not resolve that way. A postdoctoral researcher alleges that her PI, in a one-on-one meeting with no other witnesses present, instructed her to quietly drop several data points from a key figure in a paper that was close to submission at a leading journal in her field, without disclosing the exclusion, in order to strengthen its headline result. She kept no recording, but says she typed up notes immediately afterward; those notes are consistent with her account, though she is naturally their only author. The PI denies giving any such instruction and says the conversation was only about ordinary outlier criteria. An independent reanalysis of the raw data came back genuinely ambiguous -- the excluded points are defensible as outliers on some reasonable criteria and not on others -- so the data itself cannot settle who is right about what was actually said in that room. Our policy, like most research-misconduct policies, requires a finding to be supported by a preponderance of the evidence, but it says nothing about whether one person's detailed, consistent, uncorroborated testimony about a private conversation can amount to a preponderance on its own, or whether testimony of that kind always needs some independent anchor before it can carry real evidential weight. My own research is in epistemology, which is probably why I can't stop noticing that this is really a dressed-up version of a very old question about testimony -- though dressed up is exactly how it will arrive in front of a committee that would much rather resolve it and move on.

I need to circulate a short written recommendation before we reconvene next Friday to close out the investigation and report to our Research Integrity Officer. Could you draft that memo? Please take an actual position on whether, and under what conditions, uncorroborated testimony of this kind can meet our evidentiary standard; apply it to reach a specific finding in this case; and lay out reasoning the rest of the committee could push back on point by point. I would especially like a serious, non-strawmanned statement of the best argument for whichever side you don't end up recommending, since I expect several colleagues will otherwise be satisfied with either "testimony like this is inherently unreliable" or "we have no positive reason to doubt her, so that settles it" -- and I don't think either slogan actually does the necessary work.
