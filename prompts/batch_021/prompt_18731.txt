I'm a retired physicist (condensed matter, if it matters) who's gotten pretty into philosophy since I stopped working, mostly through an informal group of old colleagues who meet every couple of weeks to argue about whatever book we're currently working through over coffee. Lately it's been philosophy of mind, and two people in the group -- Diane, who did a PhD in mathematical logic decades ago before switching careers, and Frank, who spent his career writing compilers -- got into a long back-and-forth about the Lucas-Penrose argument, the one where Godel's incompleteness theorem is supposed to show minds can't just be formal systems / computers. (I gather Penrose revived the idea from a guy named Lucas who wrote about it back in the 60s.) I wrote the exchange down afterward because I honestly couldn't tell who was winning, and nobody else in the group could referee it either.

Here's roughly how it went. (1) Diane opened with the standard move: for any consistent formal system F strong enough to contain arithmetic, Godel gives you a sentence G(F) that's true if F is consistent but that F itself can't prove; a human shown F can step outside it and see that G(F) is true, so we can't just be implementing one fixed F, since otherwise there'd be an arithmetical truth we could never recognize as such no matter what, and that seems wrong given that we just did, for whichever F you hand us. (2) Frank objected that this "seeing G(F) is true" move isn't free: it only holds conditional on F actually being consistent, and Godel's *second* theorem says a consistent system can't prove its own consistency -- so unless Diane has some special access to her own total consistency that a machine running on the same "source code" couldn't equally have, she hasn't out-proved anything, she's just helped herself to an unearned premise. (3) Diane replied that she doesnt need to certify her own consistency wholesale, only to look at whatever specific candidate F a mechanist actually hands her, inspect its axioms from the outside, and judge that particular finite list of axioms consistent -- which isn't the same as F proving its own consistency internally, so the second theorem doesn't touch it. (4) Frank came back that this just relocates the problem, since the "inspect-and-judge" faculty has to itself belong to whatever F is supposed to capture her total mathematical competence, on pain of leaving out precisely the step doing the work -- and, seperately, that we don't even know real human mathematicians are consistent, since they hold contradictory beliefs and get argued out of "obvious" claims all the time, and Godel's theorem simply says nothing about inconsistent systems. (5) Diane's comeback was that the inconsistency point cuts against Frank, not her: if actual people are too messy to be modeled by any single tidy formal system, that's a problem for the mechanist's own premise, not for her argument, which was always about idealized mathematical competence rather than anyone's particular Tuesday-afternoon reasoning.

Nobody budged and we more or less ran out of coffee. Could you go through the exchange move by move and say who had the better of it at each step, rather than just picking an overall winner? And is there some further consideration -- something neither of them raised -- that actually matters for whether the Lucas-Penrose argument survives all this?
