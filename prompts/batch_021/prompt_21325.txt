I recently moved from a philosophy PhD into a technical governance role at an AI lab, where I've been asked to help draft the specification for our next model's shutdown protocol, and preparing for that has forced me to confront how unsettled the underlying theory still is. One camp in the safety literature holds that a fully rational agent with complete preferences over outcomes can still be made safely corrigible: either by engineering its utility function so that it is indifferent to whether it gets shut down (the classic "utility indifference" construction), or by keeping it sufficiently uncertain about our true objective that deferring to a shutdown command remains instrumentally rational, as in the off-switch game. A second camp, drawing on more recent work on the shutdown problem (Elliott Thornley's, in particular), argues that any agent with genuinely complete preferences will generically retain some incentive to resist shutdown, so real corrigibility requires giving the agent incomplete preferences instead, along with whatever decision-theoretic complications follow from abandoning completeness. Write a dialogue between two safety researchers, one from each camp. Have them take each other's strongest arguments seriously rather than caricaturing them, and press on the specific weak points of the opposing approach -- for instance, whether indifference terms stay stable under self-modification or capability gain, and whether incomplete preferences can be protected from exploitation without quietly reintroducing completeness. Let the dialogue end with the two of them agreeing on exactly where their substantive disagreement lies, even though neither changes their mind.
