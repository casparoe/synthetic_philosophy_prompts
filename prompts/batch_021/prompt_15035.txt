I spent my working life as an experimental condensed-matter physicist, and since retiring I have been trying to fill in the gaps in my philosophical education, partly through a small reading group that recently spent two sessions on the economics of belief and information. What stopped me in my tracks there was Aumann's "agreeing to disagree" result and its descendant, the Milgrom–Stokey no-trade theorem: if two risk-averse traders share a common prior, start out at an allocation that is already Pareto-optimal relative to that prior, and it is common knowledge between them that any later offer to trade can only be motivated by private information, then no trade should occur at all, because the very willingness of a rational counterparty to trade reveals exactly the adverse information that made the offer look attractive, so the deal unravels before anyone signs anything. Economists have apparently taken to calling this the "Groucho Marx theorem," after the line about never wanting to belong to a club that would accept him as a member, and it struck me as very much like a no-go theorem out of physics, in the spirit of Bell's theorem: a short list of innocuous-looking assumptions yielding a conclusion flatly at odds with everyday experience. Aumann's original result, I gather, has also been invoked by philosophers of disagreement as formal backing for something like the "equal-weight view," so the puzzle is not confined to stock exchanges; it bears on when, if ever, it is rational to keep disagreeing with someone once you know their opinion.

What I cannot judge on my own, never having had formal training in game theory, is how the standard proposed escapes from the theorem really compare with one another as accounts of why real asset markets are nonetheless enormously liquid. From my reading so far, the main candidates seem to be: (i) heterogeneous, non-common priors, rather than a shared prior plus private information; (ii) liquidity or hedging trades driven by needs exogenous to any belief about asset value; (iii) noise or "irrational" traders whose activity lets informed trades hide inside random-looking order flow; (iv) overconfidence, or some other departure from ideal Bayesian rationality, on the part of at least one side; and (v) merely approximate, rather than infinitely iterated, common knowledge of everyone's rationality.
