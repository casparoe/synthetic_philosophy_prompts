I spent thirty-five years working in condensed-matter physics before retiring, and over the past year I have been reading my way into philosophy of mind, where I keep encountering panpsychism and, in particular, the combination problem -- the worry that no amount of summing simple micro-level experiences can yield the single, richly structured experience each of us seems to have (a worry usually traced back to James's observation that a hundred feelings, however combined, never add up to a hundred-and-first). I have drafted some notes toward an essay on this and would value your comments before I develop them further.

My central idea is to use phase transitions as a model. In a ferromagnet or a superfluid, the collective order parameter -- long-range phase coherence, quantized vortices, the whole low-temperature structure -- is not present in, and cannot be read off from, any single constituent particle; it appears only for the aggregate, and it has organizational features that no individual particle possesses. I want to argue that this at least offers a proof of concept for how a rich, spatially structured macro-experience could emerge from a swarm of simple, qualitatively impoverished micro-experiences, without those micro-experiences already containing that structure in miniature -- speaking to what I gather philosophers call the palette problem and the structural-mismatch worry, and perhaps even to the question of why a determinate boundary picks out one subject rather than many overlapping candidates.

What I cannot tell, from my reading so far, is whether this does real philosophical work or merely redescribes the problem in the vocabulary of statistical mechanics: physicists are happy to use words like "coherence" and "order" for purely structural, third-person facts, which is exactly what critics say leaves the first-person, subject-involving part of the combination problem untouched. I gather a similar charge has been leveled against Goff's phenomenal bonding and Seager's combinatorial infusion -- that they name the trick rather than perform it. Does my phase-transition version fall to the same objection, or does it add something those proposals lack? I would also welcome your sense of where the argument is weakest.
