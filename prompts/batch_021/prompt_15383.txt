I serve on the governance committee of our national philosophical association, and we've reached an impasse; I need to circulate a clear recommendation before the matter comes to a vote at the spring business meeting. Our constitution currently limits voting on bylaws amendments, resolutions issued in the association's name, and the election of officers to 'members holding a permanent or tenure-track appointment.' A few years ago, after the association issued a public statement on a wave of contract non-renewals without consulting anyone actually holding a contingent appointment, a caucus of contingent faculty, graduate student members, and unaffiliated independent scholars organized, and they have since petitioned for full voting rights. They argue that they are affected by the association's placement service, its interventions in academic-freedom cases, and its statements on department closures at least as directly as anyone holding a permanent line, and often more urgently, since their livelihoods are more exposed. A bloc of long-serving permanent members opposes full enfranchisement — not, to their credit, by denying that these groups are affected, but by pointing out that graduate student members alone would roughly double the electorate overnight. That, they worry, would let a population that typically turns over within five or six years help decide matters like reserve funds, multi-year contracts with conference venues and publishers, and the association's long-run relationship with the field's flagship journals — questions whose consequences will mostly be borne by the much smaller number of people still active in the association twenty years from now.

My own specialty is nowhere near political philosophy, but even I can see that this is a live instance of the 'boundary problem' one meets in democratic theory: any body that decides who gets to help decide is already, by construction, standing on one side of the very line it is drawing, so there is no obviously non-circular place from which to settle the question. Naming the puzzle does not dissolve it, and the committee needs more from me than that. Please draft the memo I will circulate to the full committee: take a clear position on how voting membership should be redefined, or propose a concrete procedure and timeline for resolving the question — a referendum, a phased extension keyed to some marker of continued involvement, tiered voting weights, or something else entirely — and give the 'swamping' worry its strongest reading rather than dismissing it as mere entrenchment by incumbents.
