Faye said that sentence is actually incoherent, not humble: I clearly believe every single factual claim I put in the book is accurate, or why print it, but the disclaimer has me confidently believing at least one of those claims is false. That's not two attitudes sitting comfortably side by side, she said -- it's believing the first claim, the second, all the way through the book, while also confidently believing they can't all be true, and that's a flat contradiction if belief is closed under conjunction at all. Ben said that's not fair to authors -- that she'd basically just reinvented something philosophers call the preface paradox, and that nobody serious thinks belief has to be closed under conjunction like that, we're finite and error-prone, and being confident in a thousand individual claims while expecting the odd one to be wrong is just what ordinary epistemic humility looks like, no logical crime involved.

Faye wouldnt drop it though -- she said fine, but then Ben has to say what he's actually willing to give up, because he can't keep both premises and just deny that the contradiction shows up. Either the "belief" in each claim was never full belief to begin with (more like 95% confidence, in which case authors should be hedging claim by claim instead of dumping it all into one disclaimer), or he's biting the bullet on rejecting conjunction and should own that as a real cost instead of acting like it's free. She backed this up by pointing at the lottery paradox: it's rational to believe ticket #1 won't win, and #2 won't, and so on for however many tickets there are, while it's also rational to believe some ticket will win -- same shape of problem, she said, and everyone already agrees that one's paradoxical, so why should this be any different.
