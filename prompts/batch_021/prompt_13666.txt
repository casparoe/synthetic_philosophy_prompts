My grandmother, who helped raise me back home in South Africa and always spoke of Ubuntu, moved into a care home a few months ago as her dementia advanced, and she no longer recognizes any of us or seems to carry any memory from one visit to the next. This came to a head recently when my aunts Nomsa and Zanele argued about it after a visit. Nomsa said that, in the tradition she was raised in, a person is a person through other people, so as long as the family keeps recognizing, greeting, and caring for her as one of us, she remains as much a person as she ever was, even though the 'inside' has gone quiet. Zanele objected that this is comforting but cannot be the whole truth: either there is still someone in there having experiences or there is not, and no amount of loving treatment on our part can settle which it is, any more than handling a photograph tenderly makes it conscious. Nomsa replied that demanding a hidden inner fact already smuggles in the individualist picture Ubuntu rejects, since most of what we count as someone's personhood -- her name, her roles, her history, what she is owed and owes -- has always been sustained by other people; dementia just makes that visible instead of hiding it. Zanele granted the point but said that 'personhood' in that sense is then merely a social status, one that leaves completely untouched the question she actually cares about: whether her own mother can still suffer or feel joy. Could you go through their exchange and say who has the better of it at each step, and point out anything important that both of them are missing?
