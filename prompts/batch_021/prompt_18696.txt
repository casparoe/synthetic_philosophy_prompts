Here's what they handed in:

"The Lucas-Penrose argument says that Gödel's incompleteness theorem shows no Turing machine can fully model human mathematical reasoning. For any consistent formal system F strong enough to encode arithmetic, there's a Gödel sentence G that is true but that F cannot prove. Since a human looking at F from outside can recognize that G is true, while F itself cannot, Lucas and Penrose conclude that human mathematical understanding outruns any single fixed algorithm.

The standard computability-based objection is that this move is too fast: we can only be confident G is true if we're confident F is consistent, and Gödel's second theorem says that F can't prove its own completeness, so there's no way to settle that question from inside F. Since the human hasn't independently established F's consistency either, they're not obviously in a better epistemic position than the machine -- they've just assumed it.

Bringing in computational complexity doesn't add a new objection here, it just restates this one using different vocabulary. Saying a human can do something no Turing machine running for any amount of time could do is a stronger version of saying the human can solve problems no Turing machine could solve quickly, i.e. that the human operates outside NP entirely. So P vs NP is really just the computability worry wearing a disguise, not a seperate line of argument.

That said, I don't think either version tells us much about whether the mind literally is a computer: even a proof that P = NP wouldn't show that any specific brain runs in polynomial time, since P vs NP is a statement about worst-case complexity classes, not about what any actual system does."
