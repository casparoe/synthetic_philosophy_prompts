Within the decision-theory literature that grew out of Newcomb's problem, a further and stranger proposal has emerged under the label "acausal trade": the claim that a rational agent can benefit from coordinating with another agent with whom it shares no causal contact whatsoever -- a civilization beyond our cosmic horizon, a distant branch of the quantum multiverse, or a hypothetical future superintelligence -- purely because sufficiently similar decision procedures will produce correlated outputs even when no causal channel connects them. The idea draws on functional and updateless decision theory (developed partly to handle cases like "counterfactual mugging," where what you should do now depends on what you would have committed to doing in a situation that, as it happens, didn't arise) and on Hofstadter's older notion of "superrationality." It has been pushed to striking practical conclusions by some thinkers in the AI-safety world, who argue that a future AI we build should be designed to care at least a little about value systems other than our own, on the theory that civilizations elsewhere reasoning the same way will do likewise -- so that, in expectation, everyone benefits despite no signal ever passing between the parties.

I've seen this move dismissed as a kind of decision-theoretic sleight of hand: "trading" with a partner you can never observe, verify, or hold to account looks a lot like unilaterally guessing at a partner convenient to whatever conclusion you already favor. I've also seen it defended as simply what falls out of taking seriously the idea that rationality concerns the output of your decision procedure rather than the causal consequences of any one action. I'd like to see this disagreement played out between two people who each find the other's position uncomfortable to dismiss outright. Please write a dialogue between the Trader, who argues that acausal trade is something a rational agent or civilization must factor into its present choices, and the Skeptic, who doubts that it is either coherent or practically significant. Have them press each other on the specific sticking points: what exactly distinguishes correlation-without-causation from wishful thinking about a convenient partner; what, if anything, could count as evidence that a correlation is strong enough to act on; and whether notions like "defection" or "enforcement" are doing any real work once causal interaction is off the table entirely. Neither character should be a strawman the other can easily dispatch, and each should respond to the other's actual arguments rather than talk past them. Let the dialogue close with each side stating, as precisely as it can, what would have to be true for it to change its mind.
