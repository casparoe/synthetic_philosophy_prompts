So I went down a rabbit hole this week that started with re-reading Yudkowsky's old "Cartoon Guide to Löb's Theorem" post and some of the MIRI stuff on the "Löbian obstacle" for self-modifying agents, which sent me back to basics on Gödel for the first time since undergrad. Now I've got a thought stuck in my head that my study group (we run an informal reading group on math logic over Discord) couldn't quite talk me out of either. Isn't the whole first incompleteness theorem basically just the Liar's paradox wearing a trenchcoat? The Gödel sentence G says "G is not provable in F," which sure looks like exactly the same self-referential move as "this sentence is false" -- your diagonalizing the system against itself either way. I even dug up the bit where Gödel says, right in the original 1931 paper, that the resemblance to the liar antinomy basically "leaps to the eye."

So if even Gödel admits it's the same underlying trick, why do we get two totally different verdicts out of it? The Liar makes any naive theory of truth explode into outright contradiction, but somehow the identical move applied to provability just calmly shows the system is "incomplete" instead of blowing up the same way. Every explanation I've read just asserts that incompleteness and inconsistency are seperate failure modes, without really explaining why running the same self-referential construction doesn't collapse both cases into contradiction. Is there an actual principled, technical reason the two self-referential sentences land in such different places, or is "it's incomplete, not inconsistent" more of a story logicians tell each other so they don't have to admit they're running the identical paradox and just refusing to call it one?
