For my "Computability & Logic" class (it's cross-listed between the CS and Philosophy departments, which is why I'm taking it as a philosophy major) we just finished a unit on the Church-Turing thesis. My professor kept emphasizing how strong the evidence for it is, since totally independent approaches like Turing machines, Church's lambda calculus, and Godel/Kleene's recursive functions all ended up defining the exact same class of computable functions, and nobody has ever found a counterexample despite people trying for decades. She basically presented it as one of the most secure ideas in all of computer science. Then this weekend a friend of mine who's really into quantum computing sent me an article about Google's Sycamore chip and "quantum supremacy," and said something like "yeah but this is kind of moot now, quantum computers already broke the Church-Turing thesis since they can solve problems no classical Turing machine ever could." We ended up arguing about it for like an hour and didn't really get anywhere.

So now I've got two things I'm stuck on and would love a clearer picture on. First, is my friend right that stuff like Shor's algorithm or these supremacy experiments actually violate the Church-Turing thesis by computing things that are impossible for a classical Turing machine, or is there something else going on that were both missing? And second, if the thesis is really as well-supported as my professor made it sound, with all these independent formalisms converging on it, why do people still call it a "thesis" and treat it as something that could in principle be overturned, instead of just a proven theorem in the same way the equivalence of Turing machines and lambda calculus is a proven theorem? It feels weird to me that something could simultaneously be a total slam-dunk and also the kind of claim a new technology might overturn.
