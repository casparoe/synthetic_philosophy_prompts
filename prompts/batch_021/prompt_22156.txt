So this whole thing started because our team does this informal "paper club" every other Friday, and a couple weeks back someone dug up an old Douglas Hofstadter piece about "superrationality" -- his claim that if you and an exact copy of yourself each play a one-shot prisoner's dilemma in seperate rooms, the truly rational move is to cooperate, not defect, even though defection is the textbook dominant strategy. Somebody joked that this is basically what happens when you spin up two instances of the same LLM with temperature 0 and have them play against each other, and that joke somehow turned into a full-blown argument between two people on my team, Nate and Farah, that spilled out of the meeting and into Slack for like two days. I got cc'd as the unofficial tiebreaker and I honestly don't have the background to referee this, so I'm hoping you can.

Here's roughly how it went. Nate: if you and your copy are running the identical decision procedure, then whatever you decide, the copy decides too, so the only real outcomes are (cooperate, cooperate) or (defect, defect) -- and since the first beats the second for both of you, cooperating is what a rational copy of you actually does. He said calling defection "dominant" secretly assumes you can hold the copy's move fixed while changing your own, and that's not a coherent thing to imagine given the setup. Farah: your move still doesn't cause the copy's move, it only correlates with it, and rational choice should be about what your action brings about, not what it's evidence of -- she compared it to Newcomb's problem, where reasoning from correlation instead of causation makes it feel like you're choosing for two people when you only ever get to choose for one. Nate: that's exactly backwards, because two-boxers are the ones who reliably end up poorer in Newcomb's problem, and here the "I defect, copy cooperates" counterfactual isn't just causally blocked, it's logically impossible if you take the setup seriously, so refusing to budge on that is a choice to follow causal decision theory, not some neutral fact about what rationality requires. Farah: fine, but Newcomb's problem stipulates a perfectly reliable predictor, and real copies -- two people, or even two instances of a model, given floating point nondeterminism -- are never perfectly correlated, so superrationality only holds in a limit case that doesn't actually occur, and once correlation drops even slightly below perfect, plain expected value can favor defecting again depending on the payoffs. Nate's last word was that this is true of every idealization in math or engineering and isn't a reason to throw the idea out, and that people have since built entire decision theories (he name-dropped "functional decision theory" without really being able to explain it) trying to handle exactly the partial-correlation case Farah is pointing at.
