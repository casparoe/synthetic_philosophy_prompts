Okay so this might be a weirdly specific question but I've been stuck on it for like three days now. In my school's philosophy elective we just got through Part 1 of Spinoza's Ethics (the whole geometry-proof-style thing about substance and God), and for extra credit our teacher handed out a few pages of the Stanford Encyclopedia's entry on "grounding," which mentions Spinoza's substance/mode stuff as one of the historical ancestors of what metaphysicians today call metaphysical grounding. Me and my friend got kind of hyped about that connection, but when we went back and actually reread the primary text we're not sure it holds up the way the handout made it sound, and when I asked my teacher about it she just said "good question, sit with it" instead of actually answering lol.

Here's the specific bit, from the Definitions and the very first Proposition of Part I:

"By substance, I understand that which is in itself, and is conceived through itself: in other words, that of which a conception can be formed independently of any other conception. ... By mode, I understand the modifications of substance, or that which exists in, and is conceived through, something other than itself. ... PROPOSITION I: Substance is by nature prior to its modifications. Proof: This is clear from Definitions iii. and v."

What's bugging me is that "conceived through" sounds like a claim about concepts or definitions — like you literally can't define "mode" without dragging in "substance" — which seems more like a semantic or logical fact than a metaphysical one. But grounding, at least going off the bits of Fine and Schaffer the handout quoted, is supposed to be this heavier "in virtue of" relation about what actually explains what in reality, not just which definitions nest inside which other definitions. So is Spinoza's one-line proof of Prop 1 basically smuggling in the assumption that conceptual dependence and metaphysical/explanatory dependence are the same thing? Or is there something else going on later in the Ethics that would actually license treating them as the same, so its not as sloppy a move as it looks from Part 1 alone? I don't want the "yeah Spinoza kind of invented grounding" answer that's all over the internet, I want to know whether the actual inference goes through.
