I spent my whole career as an analytical chemist assuming that when reaction A reliably produces product B, there's some real necessary connection doing the work, not just a coincidence that keeps happening. Since retiring I've fallen into philosophy pretty hard, and my little reading group just finished the section of Al-Ghazali's "Incoherence of the Philosophers" where he goes after Avicenna and co. on causation -- the discussion with the cotton and fire example (which amused me, since burning cotton is literally just chemistry to me). His move seems to be: we never actually observe fire necessitating the burning, we just observe the two events together, and God could perfectly well create the contact without creating the burning, which is how he explains away miracles like Abraham surviving the furnace. Someone in the group said "oh thats basically Hume" and everyone nodded and moved on, but that feels too quick to me -- Hume ends up agnostic about the deep metaphysics of causation and just chalks it up to habit of mind, whereas al-Ghazali seems to have a very definite metaphysical story (occasionalism, God recreating the world moment to moment) that Hume never signs up for.

Could you actually unpack what's doing the same work versus different work in the two arguments -- like, do they even agree on what we're allowed to infer from "all we ever observe is constant conjunction," or does al-Ghazali need some extra premise that Hume doesn't? And while you're at it, I keep seeing Malebranches name dropped right next to al-Ghazali's as a fellow "occasionalist," but I have a hunch he got there from a totally different problem (something about Descartes' mind-body split rather than fire and cotton). Am I right that they're solving different puzzles but landing on structurally similar answers, or is there more of a real lineage there than that? I'd like something more precise than the "sort of the same idea, basically" gloss I keep running into.
