I'm the elected chair of a five-person fantasy-writing collective that has published its novels for over a decade under a single joint pen name, Wren Ashby, and that settles disputes over cover credit and royalty splits by a vote of the full membership. Our bylaws, drafted long before anyone anticipated this dispute, define a co-author of a given title as anyone who "substantially participates in the collective's creative process for that title"—language that has never needed sharpening before, since in that time everyone who showed up to help plot a book also ended up drafting some of it.

That stopped being true with our fourth Hollow Court novel. At the retreat where we plot each installment, one member, Ines, proposed the book's central mystery and spent three intensive days working out its architecture with the rest of us, chapter by chapter; four days after the retreat ended, she withdrew entirely because of a family medical emergency and never wrote, revised, or commented on a page again. The remaining four of us wrote the whole book from those retreat notes, departing from them substantially as the draft demanded. Ines is now asking to be listed as co-author and paid an equal share, on the grounds that the collective has always treated "the creative process" as something that happens in the room as much as on the page. Two of the four of us who actually wrote the book are ready to agree, partly out of sympathy and partly because it matches how we've handled lopsided contributions in the past; a third is resisting on principle, arguing that joint authorship has to involve seeing a plan through, not just shaping it and then stepping away, and that stretching the definition now will empty it of meaning the next time someone wants credit for an afternoon's brainstorming.

I'm genuinely undecided myself, which is partly why I'd rather have a carefully reasoned outside opinion than just call a vote; I've also floated the dilemma with novelist friends outside the collective, and their sympathies split along exactly the same lines. Our publisher needs the final credit line in three weeks, and however we rule will function as precedent the next time this happens. Could you draft the memo I'll circulate to the full membership: a clear recommendation on Ines's credit and royalty share, a timeline for settling it before the deadline, and reasoning solid enough that whichever side turns out not to prevail would recognize it as a fair account of what we actually owe each other, rather than just a tally of votes?
