A friend and I got into a long argument last night about anti-realism and truth, and it turned entirely on Fitch's paradox of knowability. She was defending the knowability principle -- that if something is true, it must at least be possible to know it -- as a near-truism that any reasonable theory of truth should accept. I countered that this, together with the modest assumption that at least one truth is never actually known, lets you prove that every truth is actually known by someone, which seems absurd. She tried to save the principle by restricting it to "ordinary" propositions, excluding ones that build in a reference to their own unknownness, like "p is true and unknown" -- but I said that restriction looks like a rule invented on the spot purely to block the proof. Neither of us could reconstruct the derivation carefully enough to settle who had the better of it. Could you walk through, step by step, exactly how the contradiction follows from those two assumptions -- ideally in a way I could redo myself -- and then assess whether her restriction is a principled move or, as I suspect, just an ad hoc patch?
