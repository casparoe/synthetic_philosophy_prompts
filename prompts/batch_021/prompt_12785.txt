I am a novelist working on my first whodunit—a country-house mystery set in the classic Golden Age mode—and I have run into a disagreement with my writing group that no amount of re-reading craft guides has resolved. The solution depends on a reader combining three things that are each, individually, stated plainly somewhere in the book: an offhand remark in chapter two about which train a guest claims to have caught, a throwaway line in chapter five about the drawing-room clock running fast, and a detail about the victim's evening habits, revealed in chapter eight, that becomes suspicious only once it is set beside those first two facts—a connection nothing in the text invites before the detective makes it in the final chapter. Half of my beta readers call this textbook fair play, since nothing is invented or withheld at the reveal and every fact needed for the solution is there in black and white before the explanation begins. The other half feel ambushed: they insist that although the facts were technically present, no reasonable person could have assembled them into the solution on a first read, and that this amounts to the same sin as a detective pulling a decisive clue out of nowhere in the last chapter.

My first instinct was that this is simply a matter of taste, or of how carefully any given reader happens to read, but the longer I sit with it, the more I think both camps are gesturing at something real that I do not have the vocabulary for. There does seem to be a genuine difference between a fact being present in a text and a fact being something a reader can fairly be expected to have worked out from it, and I cannot articulate what should govern the size of that gap: how many separate details a reader can reasonably be asked to hold in mind and combine unprompted, whether it matters that the connection depends on specialized background knowledge (train schedules, medical trivia) rather than everyday inference, or whether the number of steps between a fact being stated and a mystery being solved matters in its own right. My own tentative rule of thumb—that a solution feels fair when the missing piece is a new way of looking at facts the reader already has, and unfair when it is a wholly new fact sprung on them at the end—turns out not to help here, since all three of my clues are plain facts rather than interpretations, and my readers still cannot agree on whether the puzzle plays fair.

I would rather work this out properly than simply defer to genre convention, particularly with my agent waiting on the finished manuscript and "that is simply how mysteries work" not being an answer I find satisfying. Is there a principled way of distinguishing information a reader has merely been given from information a reader can be held responsible for having derived, and if so, what determines where that line falls? Or is "fair play" in detective fiction ultimately just an unprincipled truce between writers and readers that happens to work well enough in practice, with no real principle underneath it at all?
