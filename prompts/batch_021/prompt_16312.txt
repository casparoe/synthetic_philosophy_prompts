Now that I'm retired from a career in experimental physics I've been reading my way through philosophy, and I keep noticing that 'cause' meant something very specific in my old world: some physical quantity actually getting transferred from one event to another, energy, momentum, that kind of thing. So I got a kick out of finding out theres an actual philosophical theory of causation built on more or less that idea, Phil Dowe's "conserved quantity" theory. But apparently it runs into real trouble with so-called causation by omission - cases like saying a drought caused a crop failure, or that I caused my neighbor's ficus to die by forgetting to water it while she was away. On Dowe's picture that seems hard to make sense of, since nothing physical gets transferred from something that never happened. Lewis's counterfactual theory doesn't have that problem: if I'd watered it, it wouldn't have died, so my not watering it caused its death, simple as that. Except then it seems like literally everyone else who also didn't water the ficus (you, me, the mailman) comes out a cause of its death too, which seems nuts. Could you compare how these two theories actually cope with omissions, and say whether either one has a genuinely principled way of drawing the line, or whether they're both just papering over the same crack in different spots?
