I host a small podcast on the history of philosophy, and I just spent most of a dinner arguing with a friend about al-Ghazali's treatment of causation in the Tahafut al-Falasifa. My friend's position was that the fire-and-cotton discussion is essentially Hume's constant-conjunction argument in theological dress: strip away the talk of God's "custom" ('ada) and you're left with the same admission that we only ever observe two events occurring together, never a necessitating connection between them. I argued that this erases a real difference -- Hume ends in a naturalistic story about habit, whereas Ghazali ends in occasionalism proper, with God as the sole true agent behind every event, a far stronger claim that also creates real trouble for his own Ash'ari account of human responsibility via kasb ("acquisition"), which I don't think he ever makes fully intelligible. Neither of us gave ground.

Next week I'm interviewing a historian of classical Islamic theology for the show who also defends, as a live position, the strong occasionalism she reads Ghazali as actually having held -- not the more modest, deflationary reading that recent scholarship (Frank Griffel's, among others) has pushed toward, but the view that no created thing has any causal efficacy whatsoever. Please draft eight to ten interview questions that go beyond having her restate the position. I want at least one question, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give, on: (1) whether her strong reading really buys any distance from Hume, given that both views seem to rest on the same negative claim -- that we never perceive necessitation, only concomitance; (2) how kasb can explain moral responsibility without quietly granting humans exactly the kind of real causal power occasionalism denies to everything else; and (3) Ibn Rushd's objection in the Tahafut al-Tahafut that denying real natures leaves Ghazali unable to explain why the "customary" patterns are so reliable in the first place, undercutting both natural science and his own theological arguments from the order of creation.
