A friend of mine and I spent most of a long dinner arguing about Alfred North Whitehead's philosophy of education and where it would land him on today's debates about personalized AI tutoring. My friend teaches high school and has been experimenting with an adaptive learning platform that lets each student move through a subject's "mastery modules" at their own pace, with an AI system that adjusts problem difficulty in real time and won't advance a student to new material until the current unit is mastered. She's convinced that Whitehead, of all people, would have loved this: doesn't his idea of the "rhythm of education," with its cycling stages of Romance, Precision, and Generalization unfolding on each individual mind's own developmental clock, sound almost like a description of adaptive learning software avant la lettre? I pushed back: I think there's real tension between what these platforms actually optimize for (rapid, measurable mastery of discrete, testable skills) and what Whitehead meant by those stages, and I suspect he'd have seen something like this as a highly sophisticated way of manufacturing what he called "inert ideas" — and would have worried about what happens to the Romance stage, and to the whole notion of education as a student's own self-development, once a large share of a child's intellectual encounters are mediated by an optimization algorithm rather than by a teacher's judgment about when someone is actually ready to move from one stage to the next.

Neither of us could really settle it, so I'd like your help adjudicating. Drawing as specifically as you can on Whitehead's actual arguments in his educational essays — not just the general reputation of "rhythm of education" and "inert ideas" as buzzwords — could you reconstruct how he would most plausibly have reacted to today's AI-driven adaptive and personalized learning platforms? I'm especially interested in whether the mastery-based, self-paced structure of these systems genuinely honors his rhythm-of-education framework or subtly violates it; what he would likely have made of a teacher's role being partly delegated to software; and whether his remarks about the interplay of freedom and discipline, or about the dangers of fragmenting a curriculum into small, separately testable units, bear directly on the question. If you think my friend's reading has more going for it than I gave it credit for, feel free to make that case instead.
