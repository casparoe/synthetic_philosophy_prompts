Last night a friend and I had a long, occasionally heated argument about Pascal's Wager that began after a seminar on philosophy of religion and carried on well past midnight over dinner. Her central point, which by the end had largely convinced me, was that the Wager only ever looks compelling because Pascal is allowed to stay vague about the probability of God's existence. Once you are honest and plug in something like a realistic, modest estimate for that probability, she argued, the expected value of believing stops looking obviously superior to the expected value of not believing: a small probability multiplied against even an enormous reward for salvation should still come out as a comparatively modest number, one that then has to be weighed against the ordinary costs of organizing your life around religious devotion. On her view, this is really the whole story, and everything else philosophers argue about in connection with the Wager is secondary to this basic piece of arithmetic.

I have since caught myself repeating her argument to other people, but I would like to sanity-check it before I keep doing so. If this probability-based worry is really so decisive, why do philosophers of religion keep producing serious papers defending, reformulating, or attacking Pascal's Wager, rather than treating it as something a first pass at the expected-value arithmetic already puts to rest? Is there something about how the Wager is supposed to be structured that her argument overlooks, and if this particular objection is not the one doing the real work, what is?
