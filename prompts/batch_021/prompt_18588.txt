In a graduate seminar on philosophy of language, we recently spent a session on the rule-following paradox that Kripke extracts from Wittgenstein: the argument that nothing about my past behavior, my dispositions, or even the complete history of my mental states settles whether, in adding numbers, I ever meant addition (call it plus) rather than some deviant function like quus, defined so that x quus y equals x plus y for any pair of numbers smaller than, say, 57, and equals 5 otherwise. Afterward, a classmate who works mostly on Quine claimed that this is essentially Quine's indeterminacy of translation -- the "gavagai" problem -- restated with a different example, while another classmate insisted that the resemblance is superficial and that the two arguments are not really aimed at the same target at all. Our professor seemed sympathetic to both classmates but ran out of time to settle the dispute before the session ended, and the question has been nagging at a few of us since.

I would like help adjudicating this properly, rather than a generic side-by-side summary of each position. Please spell out precisely what Kripke's meaning-skeptical paradox and Quine's indeterminacy thesis share -- both, on the face of it, deny that any finite body of past behavior or dispositional fact can fix a unique semantic value for a term going forward -- and then identify where they genuinely diverge: for instance, whether Kripke's puzzle is fundamentally about an individual meaning something by a word in her own idiolect, whereas Quine's is about the underdetermination of a translation manual between two languages with no antecedent common ground; whether the two arguments stand in the same relation to dispositionalism, given that Kripke explicitly argues against a dispositional analysis while Quine's behaviorism seems to presuppose something like it; and whether Kripke's "skeptical solution," which relocates meaning in communal assent and warranted assertability, has any genuine counterpart on Quine's side, or whether Quine is simply willing to let determinacy of meaning go without offering anything analogous. I would also value your own considered view on whether the classmate who assimilated Kripke's puzzle to Quine's was substantially correct, or whether that assimilation obscures something distinctive and important in Kripke's argument.
