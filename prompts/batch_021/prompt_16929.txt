I'm working on a piece about the mathematics of collective choice, and I'd like to pin down precisely a contrast that popular explainers usually only gesture at. Arrow's impossibility theorem shows that no rule for aggregating individuals' ordinal preference rankings into a social ranking can satisfy unrestricted domain, independence of irrelevant alternatives, the Pareto principle, and non-dictatorship all at once. Harsanyi's far less publicized aggregation theorem, working instead with preferences over lotteries, appears to derive a full utilitarian social welfare function—a weighted sum of individual utilities—from similarly modest-looking axioms. Given that both frameworks start from individual preferences plus some Pareto-style condition, what exactly changes between the two setups that turns an impossibility result into something closer to a uniqueness result? I've also noticed that both proofs lean on an axiom called "independence," though I suspect Arrow's version and the von Neumann–Morgenstern version used by Harsanyi aren't doing remotely the same job, and I'd like to know whether Harsanyi's weights only amount to an "equal-weight" utilitarian sum once interpersonal comparability is smuggled in separately, or whether the theorem delivers that conclusion on its own.
