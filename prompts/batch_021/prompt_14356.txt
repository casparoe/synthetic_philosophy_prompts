I spent something like thirty-five years doing fieldwork on lungless salamanders (Plethodon, mostly, in the southern Appalachians) before I retired a couple years back, and one thing that always nagged at me was how messy species boundaries get once you actually look closely at them in the field -- populations everybody calls "the same species" that won't interbreed even where they overlap, and populations called "different species" that hybridize just fine. Since retiring I've had time to finally sit in on a philosophy of biology reading group at the university near me, and we just spent two sessions on Aristotle's biological works (bits of Parts of Animals and Generation of Animals) and his idea of eidos -- species/form as a fixed, eternal essence, the thing that makes a thing the kind of thing it is, arrived at through genus and differentia.

That's what's got me stuck. Aristotle seems to think there's a real joint in nature at "species" -- a true essence the classifier is trying to get right, not impose. But so much of what actually trips up field biologists doesn't look like that at all: ring species, like the classic Ensatina salamander case out in California, where a chain of interbreeding populations loops around a valley and the two ends, when they finally meet back up, can't interbreed at all -- so which populations share the "essence" and which don't? Or bacteria, which trade genes across what we call species lines so promiscuously that the whole idea of a species as a reproductively-bounded essence barely gets off the ground. Even the people who study this stuff for a living can't agree on which of the dozen-odd "species concepts" on offer is the right one, or whether there's a right one at all.

So here's my actual question: if you gave Aristotle a real crash course on the ring species problem and on bacterial gene transfer specifically, do you think his essentialism about biological kinds survives in some real form, maybe by leaning on his own matter/form distinction, or does it just fall apart? I'd rather you dig into what he actually says about genus, differentia, and essence and reason it out from there, instead of giving me the generic "he was a great empiricist, he'd just update his views" answer -- I already got that from a guy in my reading group and it didnt satisfy me one bit.
