Over the past two years, my younger brother has drifted into a closed world of conspiratorial belief, and I've come to realize that no evidence or argument I offer ever actually reaches him — not because he's unintelligent, but because we no longer seem to share the premises from which an argument could even get started. Recently, a friend trying to help me make sense of this pointed me toward Wittgenstein's notion of "hinge propositions" in On Certainty, and in particular toward a sequence near the end of the book where he imagines a community that consults an oracle rather than a physicist, and asks whether calling their practice "wrong" is anything more than our own language-game squaring off against theirs. He then writes:

"Where two principles really do meet which cannot be reconciled with one another, then each man declares the other a fool and a heretic." (§611)

"I said I would 'combat' the other man — but wouldn't I give him reasons? Certainly; but how far do they go? At the end of reasons comes persuasion. (Think what happens when missionaries convert natives.)" (§612)

I can follow the individual sentences, but I don't think I understand the claim. Is Wittgenstein saying that giving reasons and merely persuading someone are different in kind, or is it a difference of degree — reasoning that simply runs out of shared premises and shades into something else? And does the language of "combat" commit him to saying that no one, including him, can judge either side right or wrong from outside, or is there a narrower point here that stops short of that kind of relativism? I want to understand what he's actually claiming, not just borrow the passage as a mood.
