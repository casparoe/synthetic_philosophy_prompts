Had a long argument with my friend Priya last night about the Lucas-Penrose thing -- the argument that Gödel's incompleteness theorems show human minds can't be Turing machines. She's doing a logic-heavy MA and was defending a Penrose-style view, I was arguing the computationalist side, and honestly the first twenty minutes were just us talking past each other with the pop-sci versions of the argument ("there's always a truth the machine can't prove but we can just see is true!" vs "but you can't even know your own reasoning is consistent!"). It only got interesting once she walked me through the more careful version from Shadows of the Mind, where Penrose posits a formal system F that's supposed to capture everything we can soundly know via mathematical reasoning, and argues that if such an F existed we could reflect on it and come to know its own Godel sentence G(F) is true, which F itself can't prove -- so no such F can exist after all. I came back with the old Benacerraf/Putnam line that even if I am "equivalent to" some formal system, I'm never in a position to know which one, so I can't run the diagonal trick on "myself" with the certainty the argument needs, plus the point that by Gödel's second theorem a consistent system can't even prove its own consistency, so where's my supposed knowledge of my own soundness meant to come from in the first place? We went back and forth on this for ages and honestly by the end neither of us had really moved the other, we just ran out of steam.

Could you write the dialogue our conversation should've been -- between a careful, technically literate Penrosean and an equally careful mechanist, each giving their strongest move and actually responding to what the other just said rather than restating their opening pitch? I want the Penrosean to have to answer the "which machine are you" objection and the consistency-vs-soundness worry head-on, not just gesture at "well F is different, it was built to handle that" -- and I want the mechanist to have to actually grapple with whether Penrose's F really does escape those objections or just relocates them, since he clearly designed the Shadows argument with them in mind. Give me a handful of real exchanges, neither side strawmanned, and if it's possible for them to get there, have them end by pinning down the actual crux of the disagreement rather than fatigue-quitting the way we did.
