Next month I am giving the opening remarks at a small two-day workshop my lab is co-hosting under the working title "Testing for Hidden Minds." The invitees split roughly evenly between machine-learning researchers who are firmly convinced that the entire question of machine intelligence dissolves into benchmarks, and philosophers of mind, including two parapsychologists invited quite deliberately: one of our co-organizers has spent a decade running carefully pre-registered ganzfeld-style experiments on anomalous cognition, the sort of work Scott Alexander once used as a stress test for whether ordinary statistical inference can be trusted at all. I want to open with a fact I did not quite believe until I checked the original text myself: the same 1950 Mind paper that gave us the imitation game also contains Turing's own extended, largely non-dismissive treatment of the ESP objection to his test. He took the card-guessing statistics seriously enough to worry that a telepathic human contestant might make the game unfair to the machine, and half-seriously proposed running it inside what he called a telepathy-proof room.

Could you draft those opening remarks for me? They should run to about five minutes read aloud. I want them to use Turing's digression on ESP to make a genuine point about what a purely behavioral test can and cannot settle about a hidden property — whether that property is intelligence or psi — in a way that leaves the machine-learning side of the room a little less smug about benchmarks and the parapsychologists a little more candid about what pre-registration cannot rule out either. What the remarks must not do is read as quiet mockery of the psi researchers on one side, or as a wink toward taking ESP seriously on the other; and above all, they should not oversell what Turing himself believed. I would much rather the speech under-claim than have one of the several attendees who have actually read the paper catch me misusing the history afterward.
