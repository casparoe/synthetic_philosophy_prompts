I'm a backend engineer, and a few colleagues and I run an informal lunchtime reading group on AI safety topics. This week we worked through the Yudkowsky and Herreshoff paper on "tiling agents," which introduces what they call the Löbian obstacle: because of Löb's theorem, a formal system satisfying the usual provability conditions can prove a statement of the form "if I can prove P, then P is true" for a given sentence P only if it can already prove P outright. Applied to a self-modifying agent, this seems to mean that an AI reasoning in some formal system cannot, in general, certify in advance that a successor reasoning in that same system will only ever reach true or safe conclusions: building that certification in as a general schema collapses into the agent proving every sentence whatsoever, which just means the system was inconsistent to begin with. One person in the group also pointed out that this feels like the same territory as Gödel's Second Incompleteness Theorem, which says a consistent system can never prove its own consistency.

Here is where I would like a sanity check before I repeat this argument to my team. Since no consistent formal system can certify the general soundness of a copy of its own reasoning, doesn't the Löbian obstacle amount to a proof that recursively self-improving AI, of the sort where each version formally verifies that the next one is safe before handing off control, is a logical impossibility rather than merely a hard engineering problem? And if that is right, doesn't it follow that alignment research relying on formally verifying successor agents is provably a dead end, leaving empirical testing as the only option left? I would appreciate knowing whether that conclusion genuinely follows from what Löb's theorem establishes, or whether we are stretching a narrow technical result well past what it can actually support.
