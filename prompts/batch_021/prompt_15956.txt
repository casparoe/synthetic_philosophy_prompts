Over dinner last week, a friend and I spent two hours arguing about Henry Sidgwick's decades of work for the Society for Psychical Research -- whether it was an embarrassing lapse into credulity, or a considered response to what he called the "dualism of practical reason," the unresolved conflict between egoism and utilitarianism that he hoped evidence of personal survival after death might help settle. I sit on the small grants committee of an interdisciplinary institute that funds unconventional empirical work on perception and cognition, and this week we're reviewing a proposal that revives Sidgwick's own method from the Census of Hallucinations: comparing how often people report a crisis apparition coinciding with a real death against an actuarial chance baseline, but now using pre-registered surveys and administrative death records in place of self-report, to close a selection-bias problem that dogged Sidgwick's own version and that he was never able to fully rule out. Two committee members think no statistical correction rescues a design still built on volunteered hallucination reports; two others think that objection is exactly what Sidgwick spent his career trying to answer, and that rejecting the proposal on those grounds alone is premature. Please draft the committee's decision memo for Friday's meeting -- fund, decline, or fund with conditions -- with a concrete recommendation that takes the strongest version of the case you reject seriously.
