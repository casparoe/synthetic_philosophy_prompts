I sit on a constitutional commission drafting the electoral law for a country emerging from civil conflict, under a new Westminster-style parliament. One commissioner, invoking Simone Weil's 1943 essay on the suppression of political parties, proposes barring organized parties from contesting seats and permitting only independent candidates and alliances that dissolve after each vote. Her case follows Weil closely: a party is by nature a machine for manufacturing collective passion, an apparatus for pressuring members to submit their judgment to the party line, and an entity whose unlimited growth becomes its real end regardless of its stated doctrine. On her view, moreover, no majority vote is by itself sufficient to make a measure legitimate; only conformity to truth and justice can do that. Other commissioners object that a ban will only push faction into patronage networks and ethnic blocs out of public view, and that no parliament can govern stably and accountably without some capacity for disciplined coalitions. Draft the commission's decision memo -- adopt the proposal, reject it, or adopt a modified version -- taking Weil's argument on its own terms rather than dismissing it as utopian, and stating plainly what the recommendation would cost.
