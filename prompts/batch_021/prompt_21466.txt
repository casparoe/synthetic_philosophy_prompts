Ok this has been bugging me for weeks and I can't find anyone who talks about it directly. The classic argument for why your preferences have to be transitive is the money-pump thing: if you like A over B, B over C, and C over A, a bookie can trade you around the loop forever and skim a bit of cash off you every lap, since at each single step you're happily taking a trade you actually prefer. Then theres this separate argument, the dutch book one, for why your beliefs have to obey the probability axioms - if they dont, a bookie can build a set of bets that guarantees you lose money no matter what happens. People talk about these two like they're basically the same move, "be coherent or get exploited," but the money-pump one only works if the bookie walks you through an actual sequence of trades over time - so why couldn't you just notice halfway round the loop what's going on and refuse to keep playing, even though your gut preferences underneath are still cyclic? You'd get to keep the weird preferences AND the money, just by being stubborn about it. The dutch book, on the other hand, can apparently be done as one pile of bets offered all at once, so there's no "catch on partway through" escape hatch available. Is that timing difference an actual weak spot in the transitivity argument that the probabilism one doesn't have, or is there some sneakier version of the money pump that nails a stubborn/forward-looking agent too? Compare the two arguments properly for me and tell me which one ends up looking shakier.
