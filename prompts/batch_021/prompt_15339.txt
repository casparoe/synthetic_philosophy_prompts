And here's what one of my students (who otherwise does pretty solid work) turned in:

"Inverse reinforcement learning tries to recover an agent's reward function from observations of its behavior, the reverse of standard RL, where we compute an optimal policy given a reward. The problem is ill-posed because many different reward functions are compatible with the same behavior; in the extreme case, a constant reward function like R=0 makes every policy trivially optimal, since all policies get the same value. Ng & Russell (2000) address this by requiring the reward to make the demonstrated policy better than every alternative action by as large a margin as possible, which rules out degenerate solutions like the all-zero reward.

Later work relaxes the assumption that the demonstrator is perfectly rational. Maximum Entropy IRL (Ziebart et al., 2008) treats the human as Boltzmann-rational: instead of always taking the single best action, the human samples trajectories with probability proportional to the exponential of their reward, so mildly suboptimal behavior isn't automatically treated as evidence for a different reward. The method works by maximizing the entropy of the recovered reward function subject to matching the feature expectations of the demonstrated trajectories, which yields a unique answer without smuggling in extra assumptions.

Armstrong & Mindermann push this further: once the demonstrator can be irrational, behavior underdetermines both the reward AND the "planner" (the rationality model mapping reward to behavior), since you can always pair a weird reward with a compensating weird planner and reproduce the exact same behavior. Their key result, though, is that a simplicity prior over these (reward, planner) pairs comes to the rescue: because pathological planners that invert or scramble the reward are more complex to specify than the "normal," mostly-rational human planner, an Occam's-razor-style prior ends up favoring the intended pair after all, which is why a lot of modern value-learning proposals build in an explicit simplicity penalty on the inferred reward."
