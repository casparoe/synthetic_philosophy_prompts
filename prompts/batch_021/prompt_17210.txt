I don't have any formal background in philosophy, but over the past year I've been working through Wittgenstein's On Certainty on my own, after a frustrating argument with a family member over a belief of his that I consider obviously false made me realize that no amount of evidence either of us produced was ever going to change the other's mind. Someone in an online discussion pointed me toward the idea of hinge propositions as a way of making sense of that kind of standoff, and once I actually sat down with the text itself instead of just reading summaries of it, I ran into a tension that the explainers I've found online tend to skip past rather than address directly.

In the well-known stretch around remarks 341 to 343, Wittgenstein compares certain propositions to the hinges of a door: our doubts and investigations only make sense, he says, because some things are held fast and exempt from doubt, in the way a door can only turn because its own hinges stay put. That makes hinges sound close to fixed, almost like the logical scaffolding of thought rather than beliefs we happen to hold. But earlier, around remarks 96 to 99, he offers a very different picture, comparing the propositions that make up our worldview to a riverbed: some claims are hardened and channel the more ordinary, fluid claims that run over them, yet he explicitly says this arrangement changes over time, so that hardened propositions can turn fluid again and fluid ones can harden, meaning the bed itself shifts and not merely the water on top of it. That sounds far less like a fixed axis and much more like whatever a given community happens to have stopped questioning at a given moment, with no principled line separating a hinge from an ordinary belief that has simply gone unchallenged for a long time.

What I can't work out is whether these two images are really compatible, and this is where the more recent hinge epistemology material I've come across seems to split into two camps. Writers following Crispin Wright's notion of entitlement seem to want to preserve some positive epistemic status for hinges: we are rationally entitled to accept them without evidence, which still treats them as a kind of belief that can be assessed, just not in the ordinary evidential way. Others, following a line I've seen credited to Duncan Pritchard, want to say that hinge commitments are not really beliefs at all, but something closer to a way of acting that gets shown rather than held, which would put them outside the reach of epistemic evaluation altogether rather than granting them a special, evidence-free kind of warrant. I'd like help understanding which of these two approaches, if either, actually makes sense of the riverbed passages: the entitlement reading seems to have an easier time explaining how a hinge could shift, since it's still a belief that could in principle lose its entitlement, whereas the view that hinge commitments aren't beliefs at all seems to struggle to explain what it would even mean for something that isn't a belief to harden or turn fluid again. Or is this simply a real, unresolved seam in Wittgenstein's own notes that neither reading fully closes?
