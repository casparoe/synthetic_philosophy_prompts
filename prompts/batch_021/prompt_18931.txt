I'm a PhD student in clinical psychology, and I'd like an outside perspective on a review comment I haven't been able to settle in my own mind. Our trial tested whether a brief mindfulness intervention reduces anxiety. The pooled effect came out null, but an exploratory analysis turned up a significant effect within the subgroup of participants with high baseline rumination, and our revised manuscript now foregrounds that subgroup result. One reviewer wants to reject the paper outright, pointing out that this is the second time our lab has rescued this same intervention from a null finding by carving out a subgroup after the fact -- a few years ago it was session length, not baseline severity, that supposedly did the moderating -- and arguing that a hypothesis defended this way can never really lose, since there's almost always some subgroup where the numbers look better. My advisor is unbothered: she notes that subgroup analyses like this are routine in the field, and that baseline rumination was at least listed as a secondary moderator in our pre-registration, even though we didn't foreground it there and only started emphasizing it once the main analysis came back null. I can see the pull of both positions. Independent of just wanting to defend the paper: does the reviewer have a real point, and if so, what would need to be different about a subgroup finding like ours for it to count as a legitimate refinement rather than a maneuver that never really puts the theory at risk?
