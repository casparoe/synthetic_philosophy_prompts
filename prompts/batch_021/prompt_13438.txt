During four decades as a field biologist, I applied the biological species concept every day without ever pausing to ask what, metaphysically, made it respectable; a recent, rather late-flowering interest in philosophy has left me considerably less settled on the matter than I once was. I would like you to write a dialogue between two equally sharp philosophers of biology who disagree about the ontological status of species. One should argue, in the spirit of Michael Ghiselin and David Hull, that species are historical individuals rather than natural kinds, so that an organism relates to its species as a part relates to a whole, not as a member relates to a class. The other should argue, in the spirit of Richard Boyd's homeostatic property cluster theory, that species remain natural kinds after all, since the clusters of properties that define a kind, and the mechanisms sustaining them, are themselves free to change over time. Within the dialogue, press both positions against a genuinely hard case: an anciently asexual lineage, such as the bdelloid rotifers, where no interbreeding exists to hold a population together, and where morphologically defined "species" have turned out to conceal far more cryptic genetic divergence than expected. Neither character should simply restate their own view; each should respond directly to the specific difficulty the other has just raised, and the conversation need not end in agreement.
