I spent thirty-some years as an atmospheric chemist, so I've had a front-row seat to organized doubt-mongering -- watched more or less the same playbook get run on the ozone hole, then acid rain, then climate change, always with a shiny new set of "experts" and glossy pamplets. Now that I'm retired I've fallen down a philosophy rabbit hole instead, and lately I've been chewing on Hannah Arendt's two essays on lying in politics -- "Truth and Politics" and the Pentagon Papers one ("Lying in Politics," I think it's called). What's really stuck with me is the distinction she draws between the old-fashioned political liar, who still lives in the same world of facts as everyone else and just hides or denies a particular one, and the more modern thing she's worried about: organized, image-based manipulation that tries to swap out whole chunks of reality for a more palatable alternative picture -- to the point where, and this is the part that really got me, even the manipulators themselves can start loosing their grip on what's actually true (I want to say she has a specific term for this, something like "defactualization," but don't hold me to the exact word).

Here's why I care beyond the history-of-science angle: my brother-in-law has gone pretty far down a conspiratorial hole on YouTube over the last couple of years, and I keep turning over, philosophically, what's actually happening to him and whether that tells me anything about how to deal with it. So could you describe what you take to be the most important implications of Arendt's distinction for a case like his -- both for diagnosing what kind of phenomenon algorithm-fed conspiracy content actually is (an industrial-strength version of the old-fashioned lie, or the qualitatively different modern kind she's describing) and for whether her framework has anything useful to say about whether patiently correcting facts with someone in that position is likely to work at all, or whether it points toward some entirely different strategy?
