Two weeks ago, a mammogram I flagged for recall was read as normal by a colleague whose standing is, on paper, identical to mine: same fellowship, comparable years in practice, similar audited recall and cancer-detection rates. Per our department's protocol, the discordant pair of reads went to a third, arbitrating radiologist, who sided with her; the biopsy I insisted on anyway came back benign. I've kept turning the case over since then, less because I was wrong than because of how I reasoned in the interval between learning of her disagreement and learning the arbiter's verdict, and I'd like a second read, so to speak, on the philosophy I've been using to think it through. Below is a short piece I've drafted for our department's journal club; I'd be grateful for comments on whether I'm representing the relevant positions fairly and whether the conclusion actually follows from them.

The case seems to me a fairly clean instance of the problem of peer disagreement in epistemology. Adam Elga's "equal weight view" holds that on learning a genuine epistemic peer disagrees, you should treat their assessment as carrying the same evidential weight as your own, which in a simple two-person case means moving most of the way to the midpoint rather than merely nudging your prior confidence. Applied to my case, that would have meant collapsing most of the distance between my roughly 85 percent confidence in "recall" and her apparently similar confidence in "normal," rather than staying close to where I started.

What actually stopped me was something closer to David Christensen's independence requirement: you're not entitled to discount a peer's verdict using reasoning that just restates the reasoning generating the dispute. My instinct was to think, "she probably didn't weight the asymmetry with last year's film as heavily as I did" - but that thought comes from the very first-order look at the images that produced our disagreement, so by Christensen's lights it isn't independent evidence of my greater reliability; it's my side of the argument, relabeled as a reason to trust myself.

Thomas Kelly's total-evidence view seems to leave room for exactly that move, though, provided the asymmetry is real rather than self-serving: if I genuinely noticed something she missed, that is first-order evidence in my favor, and no amount of equal weighting between persons should override an actual evidential asymmetry between two readings of the same film. The trouble is that I cannot find a principled way, from the inside, to tell my case apart from the self-serving one Christensen is worried about.

Radiology doesn't obviously give me a clean way out, either. My colleague's audited numbers and mine make us peers on average, but "peer on average" doesn't settle who was more reliable on one unusually subtle film, which is exactly what the disagreement was about - so the audit data doesn't escape the circularity Christensen describes, it just relocates it one level up. I'm left thinking the benign biopsy result is almost beside the point: even knowing now that she was right, I still can't tell whether staying near 85 percent for those ninety minutes was reasoned confidence or the unearned self-exemption the equal weight view exists to rule out. I'd welcome pushback on any of this, especially on whether I've represented Elga, Christensen, and Kelly correctly, and on whether "peer on average but not obviously on this case" points to a genuine gap in the independence requirement, or whether it's just the sort of permission slip conciliationism predicts I'd go looking for.
