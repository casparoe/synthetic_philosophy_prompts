I am a software engineer who sits on the Model Governance Board at the logistics company where I work. We run an internal forecasting tool whose probability estimates feed a small internal prediction market, where staff whose forecasts beat the tool's earn real quarterly bonus money.

A routine audit surfaced a genuine problem: on a recent batch of vendor-delay questions, the tool priced "at least one of Vendor A or Vendor B ships late" lower than it had already priced "Vendor A ships late" on its own — even though "Vendor A ships late" can't be true unless "at least one of Vendor A or Vendor B ships late" is too. One sharp forecaster noticed and has been quietly placing paired bets against the tool that lock in a profit no matter which outcome occurs. The data science lead wants to ship a "coherence patch" that mathematically adjusts the tool's answers so they are mutually consistent before they ever reach the market. Two board members object, for opposite reasons: one argues the patch would simply dress up a tool that is genuinely bad at forecasting in tidier-looking numbers; the other argues that this kind of numerical inconsistency is perfectly compatible with being well-calibrated and worth listening to, so treating it as the thing to fix conflates a market-exploitability problem with a forecasting-quality problem.

I need to circulate a short memo before Monday's meeting recommending one of: (a) ship the patch and reopen the market, (b) suspend the tool from the market pending a deeper investigation of its forecasting quality, or (c) leave the tool as it is and close the exploit through changes to the market's rules instead. Please draft that memo. It should give a clear recommendation, and it should seriously engage whichever of the two objections its recommendation does not fully satisfy, rather than waving it away.
