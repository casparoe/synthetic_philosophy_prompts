I spent thirty-four years as an experimental particle physicist before retiring, and it is only in retirement, largely at the prompting of a former graduate student who now teaches philosophy herself, that I have started reading philosophy with any real seriousness. My old department has asked me to give this year's commencement address to a graduating class of about sixty physics majors, and I would very much like your help composing it around a piece of intellectual history that is, for me, still a little raw.

As a young physicist I held, mostly unreflectively, to the comforting thought that the indeterminacy built into quantum mechanics leaves just enough room in nature for something like libertarian free will: since the world is not fixed all the way down at the microphysical level, it seemed there must be space left over for us to be genuinely undetermined authors of our own choices. A year spent reading Kane's own careful attempts to rescue libertarian freedom by way of exactly this kind of indeterminacy, together with the skeptical replies to him from Mele and van Inwagen, has disabused me of this rather thoroughly: introducing sheer randomness at the moment of decision does not obviously hand me any more control over that decision than determinism did, and arguably hands me less, since an undetermined choice starts to look less like the exercise of agency and more like a decay event that happens to occur inside my skull rather than in one of my old detectors. What has genuinely cheered me, reading Hume, Frankfurt, Strawson, and Dennett in that same year, is discovering that the compatibilists never thought determinism was the threat to freedom and moral responsibility I had always assumed it was.

I would like this exact arc -- the seduction and the failure of the "quantum rescue" of free will, followed by the compatibilist alternative that renders the rescue unnecessary -- to be the real spine of the address, not a throwaway aside, while the whole thing still reads as a warm, genuine commencement speech of about six or seven minutes rather than a philosophy lecture. The room will hold physics faculty and a few sharp graduating seniors who will catch every reference, but also parents, siblings, and university administrators who have never once used the words "compatibilism" or "incompatibilism," plus one philosopher from across the quad whom I invited myself and would rather not embarrass myself in front of. If you can find one honest, physics-flavored joke -- about Schrödinger's cat, Bell's theorem, or radioactive decay -- that makes the failure of the quantum rescue vivid for the specialists without losing everyone else, I would love to have it; failing that, I would rather go without a joke than have a strained one. Could you draft this speech for me?
