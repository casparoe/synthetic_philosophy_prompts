I'm working on a piece about the limits of self-knowledge (yes, another one, I know) and I got sucked into Timothy Williamson's "anti-luminosity" argument this week -- he's the same guy known for the epistemicist line on vagueness, if that rings a bell. The bit that keeps rattling around in my head is his margin-for-error setup: something like, you can't notice the difference between feeling cold at 9:03am and 9:04am on a slowly warming morning, even though by 11am you're clearly not cold anymore. He uses that to argue the KK principle fails -- knowing something doesn't guarantee you know that you know it.

Here's where I think I might be going wrong, and I'd love someone to set me straight. Doesnt that basically amount to a proof that knowledge in general is bunk? Like, if there's always some borderline case where I genuinely can't tell whether I know P or just really believe P super hard, doesn't that mean "knowledge" never had solid ground under it to begin with -- that KK failing is just a more technical, respectable-sounding version of the old skeptical line that you can never really be sure of anything? I ran this by a philosopher friend at a dinner party and she said I had "the direction of the argument backwards," but two glasses of wine in, she couldn't quite reconstruct why. Is there something structural I'm missing about what the failure of luminosity actually shows, or does Williamson's argument quietly smuggle in the same skeptical conclusion through the back door?
