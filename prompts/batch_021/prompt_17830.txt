I'm preparing a graduate seminar on legal moralism and would like to examine the strength of a specific objection to Hart's position in Law, Liberty, and Morality. There, Hart tries to defeat Devlin's disintegration thesis by insisting that a society need only share a critical morality of harm-avoidance to remain cohesive, not the particular positive morality that Devlin wants the criminal law to enforce. Yet Hart himself concedes substantial room for legal paternalism — for instance, in denying that consent should excuse serious bodily harm — and for restricting public indecency on grounds that look closer to protecting onlookers' sensibilities than to preventing harm as such. Please construct the strongest argument you can that these concessions leave Hart without any principled basis for rejecting Devlin's core claim, so that Hart's considered position collapses, against his own self-understanding, into a qualified legal moralism.
