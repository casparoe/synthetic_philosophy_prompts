The question was:

Aristotle distinguishes the akratic person, who acts against her own better judgment while that judgment remains intact, from the self-indulgent person, who has no competing judgment to overcome because she has come to believe that indulgence is simply right. In "How Is Weakness of the Will Possible?", Donald Davidson tries to reconstruct this phenomenon in a way that is compatible with the view that intentional action is always done for a reason the agent takes to be good. Some behavioral economists, meanwhile, model akratic-looking behavior with hyperbolic rather than exponential discount functions, on the grounds that these generate "preference reversals" as a reward moves from the distant to the near future.

(a) Explain Davidson's account of how weakness of will is possible, distinguishing the conditional ("prima facie") judgment from the unconditional, all-things-considered judgment.
(b) Explain what a preference reversal under hyperbolic discounting is, and why it looks, from the outside, like akrasia.
(c) Are Davidson and the discounting theorists describing the same phenomenon in two different vocabularies, or are they answering different questions? Defend your answer.

Here is the answer that was submitted:

(a) Aristotle's incontinent person never stops believing that she should resist; what gives way is her self-control, not her judgment, whereas the self-indulgent person has revised her values so that indulgence no longer conflicts with anything she believes. Davidson takes up this puzzle and wants to explain how someone can act against an evaluative judgment without ceasing to be a rational, purposive agent. His key move is to distinguish a conditional, or prima facie, judgment — "x is better than y in light of considerations C" — from an unconditional, all-things-considered judgment — "x is better than y, period." The akratic agent acts on a conditional judgment favoring the worse action without ever weighing it against every relevant consideration at the moment of choice. Because the all-things-considered judgment is never actually brought to bear when she acts, the two judgments never really compete head-on, so Davidson in effect vindicates Socrates: nobody acts against a best judgment that is genuinely present to her at the time of acting, and what looks like weakness of will is really a case of the wider judgment simply not being operative.

(b) Exponential discounting applies a constant rate to every future reward, so the ranking of two rewards should never flip just because time has passed. A hyperbolic discount curve falls much faster over short delays than over long ones, so a smaller-sooner reward can beat a larger-later one when both are close at hand, even though the larger-later reward wins when the same gap is pushed further into the future — someone might prefer ten dollars today to eleven dollars tomorrow, yet prefer eleven dollars in thirty-one days to ten dollars in thirty. When day thirty arrives, the near-term preference reasserts itself and she takes the ten dollars, contradicting the plan her more distant self had settled on. From the outside, this looks exactly like weakness of will, because the agent appears to abandon a plan she herself endorsed. It is essentially the same reversal that Kahneman and Tversky modeled in prospect theory, where the value assigned to an outcome depends on the perspective from which it is evaluated rather than on the outcome itself.

(c) I think this is one underlying fact described in two vocabularies. Both Davidson and the discounting theorists are describing a situation in which a person's local, in-the-moment ranking of her options fails to match the ranking she would both endorse and predict from a distance; Davidson supplies the philosophical anatomy of that mismatch, and the discount function supplies its mathematical shape.
