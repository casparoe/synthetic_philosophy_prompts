One heuristic I have picked up from years of reading rationalist blogs is Chesterton's fence: do not tear down an inherited rule until you understand why it was put up. Maimonides' treatment of animal sacrifice is a striking variant on this, because understanding why the fence went up seems to argue for tearing it down — yet he never draws that conclusion.

In the Guide for the Perplexed (III:32, elaborated in III:46), Maimonides denies that sacrifice was ever valued for its own sake: the Israelites emerged from a milieu of idolatrous, star-worshipping ("Sabian") cultures in which sacrifice was the only conceivable form of worship, so God tolerated the practice — now redirected toward Himself and purged of idolatry — as a psychologically necessary way-station toward the sacrifice-free worship of prayer and contemplation that Maimonides clearly prefers. Yet the Mishneh Torah devotes an enormous, minutely detailed body of law to this very institution, treats its restoration in a rebuilt Temple as an unqualified halakhic ideal, and elsewhere lists the Torah's permanence among Maimonides' thirteen principles of faith.

If the rationale for sacrifice was time-bound, and had, on Maimonides' own account, already done its pedagogical work, why does he legislate its future reinstatement so uncompromisingly rather than treating it as dispensable? Please compare these two faces of Maimonides on sacrifice, and defend a view on whether they are genuinely reconcilable — whether the tension is best resolved by reading the Guide and the Mishneh Torah as pitched at different audiences with differing degrees of candor, or whether the historicist rationale can be held together with permanent legal validity without positing any such esoteric split.
