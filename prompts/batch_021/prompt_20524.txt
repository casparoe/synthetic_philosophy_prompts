So this is a genuinely weird committee assignment and I want to get the framing right before I actually have to present something on Wednesday. I'm a CS PhD student (I work on RL/alignment stuff for my dissertation) and somehow I got roped onto my university hospital system's ethics advisory committee for emerging care technologies, apparently as the resident "AI person." A vendor is pitching a pilot for the memory care unit: a system that runs inverse reinforcement learning over each resident's logged behavior -- meal choices, which activities they engage with, how they react to different staff and routines, some wearable data -- to infer individual care preferences for residents whose dementia has progressed past the point where verbal self-report is reliable. The pitch is that this beats the status quo, which right now is either one-size-fits-all protocols or family members guessing, often off a directive written a decade before diagnosis, or off their own preferences for how mom should be cared for.

The committee is split roughly in half, and honestly so am I. One side thinks this is obviously good for autonomy, since it grounds care in the resident's own current behavior instead of a stale document or a relative's memory. My worry (well, two worries, and I don't think they're the same worry, so I want the memo to actually keep them seperate) is different. First there's a technical point: IRL can't uniquely recover a reward function from behavior without assuming something like approximate rationality on the agent's part, and that's exactly the assumption most in doubt for people whose behavior might reflect confusion, habituation, or symptoms of the disease rather than preference at all -- so you get a confident-looking number out of a genuinely underdetermined inference. Second, there's an older fight that predates any of this technology: whether the care of a person with dementia should track their current, cognitively changed state, or the prior competent self's stated wishes -- I keep thinking of the Dworkin vs. Dresser/Jaworska debate on precedent autonomy and the "Margo" case from my bioethics seminar years ago. My real worry is that the system doesn't resolve that debate at all, it just launders it, since "infer preference from behavior" quietly picks a side without ever arguing for it.

Could you draft the memo I'd actually bring to the full committee? I need a real recommendation -- approve, reject, or approve with specific conditions -- not a pro/con list, and it should spell out what an approval-with-conditions would concretely require (what kinds of decisions the inferred preferences should never drive on their own, what would even count as validating the outputs, how prior directives and family input factor in when they conflict with the inferred result, a hard date to revisit the pilot). But please genuinely engage the autonomy side's best argument before landing on a recommendation -- they have a real point about the status quo being just as unprincipled, and I don't want to strawman them when I present this Wednesday.
