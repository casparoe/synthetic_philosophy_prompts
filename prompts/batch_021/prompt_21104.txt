I'm a science-and-ideas journalist and the alumni representative on my university's committee reviewing honorary degrees given to controversial historical figures. The hardest case is a doctorate the university awarded Harry Truman in the 1950s — the same degree that prompted G.E.M. Anscombe's pamphlet "Mr. Truman's Degree." Anscombe argued that ordering the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki was murder: Truman intentionally killed vast numbers of civilians as his means of forcing surrender, rather than merely foreseeing their deaths as a side effect of hitting military targets — a distinction she thought no wartime outcome could launder away. A fellow committee member, a historian of the Pacific War, counters that the bombings almost certainly saved lives overall by making an invasion unnecessary, and that revisiting a seventy-year-old degree now is cheap moralizing at a dead man's expense. Draft the memo I'll circulate before next week's meeting: recommend a specific course of action — rescind, retain, or something in between — with a timeframe, and take the lives-saved argument seriously rather than caricature it.
