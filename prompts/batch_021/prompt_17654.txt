I spent my career as a physicist and have only taken up philosophy seriously since retiring, largely through a small discussion group of fellow retirees. At last week's meeting, two of the regulars, Renata and Julian, fell into a disagreement about Hobbes that the group never quite settled, and I would value an outside verdict on it.

The occasion was Hobbes's conviction -- formed, on his own account, after a late-life discovery of Euclid -- that civil philosophy could attain the same demonstrative certainty as geometry, provided one began from sufficiently clear definitions. Renata pointed out that Hobbes spent the following decades insisting, against the Oxford mathematician John Wallis's repeated and decisive refutations, that he had squared the circle, and never once conceded the point, even as it cost him standing among the mathematicians of his day. She argued that this bears directly on how much certainty we should grant the deductions of Leviathan, since they rest on that very same self-assessed rigor. Julian called this a non sequitur: competence in Euclidean geometry and validity in an argument about covenant and sovereignty are separate matters, each to be judged on its own terms. Renata replied that the relevant link isn't subject matter but disposition: an inability to register one's own refutation, which would corrupt a thinker's reasoning anywhere, and arguably matters more in politics, where -- unlike in geometry -- no external procedure existed to catch the error. Julian answered that this cuts the other way: geometry had exactly such a procedure, and Hobbes still would not yield, which shows something about his temperament under refutation specifically, rather than about whether disagreement over political premises -- contested on their substance rather than checked against any formal proof procedure -- reflects unreliable reasoning at all.

Could you go through their four moves in turn, saying who has the better of each specific point, and then close with your sense of what both of them are missing?
