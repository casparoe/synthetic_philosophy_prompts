I'm a backend engineer and for the last few months I've been building agentic LLM pipelines at work — nothing fancy, just three model calls wired together with roles: a Planner, a Coder, and a Reviewer, passing a shared scratchpad back and forth until the Reviewer approves the diff or we blow through the token budget. My girlfriend's doing a philosophy MA and used the phrase "joint commitment" at dinner a few weeks back in a way that snagged in my brain, and it turns out theres a whole literature on what actually makes two or more agents count as doing something together, versus each just doing their own thing in a way that happens to line up (Bratman, Gilbert, Searle, that crowd). I burned a weekend reading around it and tried to apply the theories to my own pipeline, since it's the only "multi-agent system" I actually understand from the inside.

Here's roughly where I landed, more notes than essay at this point. Bratman's conditions for shared cooperative activity are basically: mutual responsiveness (each party's next move is conditioned on the other's), commitment to the joint activity itself and not just your own slice of it, commitment to mutual support, sub-plans that mesh instead of conflict, and common knowledge of all that. My pipeline seems to tick every one of those boxes almost trivially — Reviewer's critiques get folded back in, Planner re-plans around Coder's blockers, none of it works unless the sub-plans mesh. But then Gilbert's stuff makes me think I'm missing something, since her whole point is that shared intention needs an actual "joint commitment" that generates standing between the parties, like a right to rebuke the other for not pulling their weight, and a commitment that can't just be unwound by one side deciding it's over. My pipeline clearly flunks that one: if I delete the Reviewers system prompt mid-run, literally nothing registers that an obligation got violated, the other two agents don't even blink.

So my tentative, probably naive conclusion is that I've built something that's shaped like Bratman-style joint action sitting on top of exactly zero Gilbert-style joint commitment, and I can't tell if that means (a) Bratman's conditions are too easy to satisfy and don't really capture "acting together" in any interesting sense, (b) my example's just a degenerate test case, or (c) I'm butchering one or both theories, since I've got no formal training here beyond some wikipedia and skimming two papers. Could you pick apart the reasoning above — specifically where I'm actually misreading Bratman or Gilbert versus where the disanalogy might be philosophically real — and maybe say whether Searle's "we-intentions" idea cuts through this more cleanly than either of them does? Also totally fine if you just tell me this is a well worn dead end people settled decades ago and I should go read the obvious thing before opening my mouth about it at work again.
