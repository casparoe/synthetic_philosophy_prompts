Two friends of mine, both software engineers, got into an argument at a meetup that I still can't stop thinking about. The maintainers of a well-known open-source project walked away after a licensing dispute, and much of the community forked the project under a new name. Years later, every original line of code had been replaced and the fork was governed by a different foundation, though it had kept the original project's stated mission and most of its longtime contributors. (1) Iris said the fork simply is the real continuation of the original project -- same people, same purpose, just new code, "like a river that's still the same river even though the water flowing through it is never the same twice." (2) Marcus said that's an image, not an argument: once none of the original code, name, or governance survives, whether it's "really" the same project has no factual answer -- it's simply a choice about how to use the word "project," and two people could choose oppositely without either being wrong. (3) Iris said this can't be pure semantics, since companies fight expensive trademark and licensing suits over exactly this kind of question, and judges don't think they're merely refereeing a word choice. (4) Marcus replied that courts enforce conventions and contracts, not metaphysical facts, and that a dispute closable simply by everyone agreeing on a definition is thereby shown to have had nothing further at stake. (5) Iris said that if it were purely definitional, then agreeing on every non-verbal fact -- same people, same goals, zero shared code -- should have dissolved their disagreement, but it didn't. Neither will budge, and they've now made me tiebreaker, which I'm not qualified to be. Could you go through the exchange move by move, say who has the better of each specific point, and flag anything important both of them are missing -- including whether their disagreement is really about a further fact, or merely about how to use a word? End your reply with a final verdict on its own line, in exactly this format: \answer{substantive} or \answer{verbal}.
