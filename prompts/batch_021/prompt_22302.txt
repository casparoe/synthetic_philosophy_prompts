So I've been re-reading the Nicomachean Ethics this week after yet another rationalist-sphere post rehashing the situationist critique (Doris, Harman, the usual Darley-and-Batson "seminarians stepping over the guy in the doorway" study), and there's a bit in Book II.4 that I can't get to sit right in my head:

"Again, the case of the arts and that of the virtues are not similar; for the products of the arts have their goodness in themselves, so that it is enough that they should have a certain character, but if the acts that are in accordance with the virtues have themselves a certain character it does not follow that they are done justly or temperately. The agent also must be in a certain condition when he does them; in the first place he must have knowledge, secondly he must choose the acts, and choose them for their own sakes, and thirdly his action must proceed from a firm and unchangeable character."

(Ross translation, if it matters.)

Part of me reads that third condition and thinks Aristotle is basically handing the situationists their whole argument for free — if a "firm and unchangeable character" is definitionally required before an act counts as virtuous, and the empirical record on cross-situational consistency is as bad as Doris says, then basically nobody clears the bar, ever, full stop. But then I remember he spends a good chunk of the rest of the treatise on continence/incontinence and plain vice as categories distinct from virtue, which makes me suspect "firm and unchangeable" was never meant to describe normal peoples behavior to begin with, only the vanishingly rare phronimos. So which is it: is that third condition doing real empirical work the situationists could actually land a hit on, or is it more of an idealization that's true by definition, which just means the whole debate talks past itself? Someone who's actually chewed on this, please walk me through it, I keep flip flopping.
