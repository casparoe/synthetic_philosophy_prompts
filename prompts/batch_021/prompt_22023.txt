I've been re-reading Alan Turing's 1950 paper "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" for a piece I'm working on about the early history of AI, and I keep getting stuck on the bit near the end where he takes on what he calls the "Argument from Extrasensory Perception." It's genuinely bizzare to read in context — he's just finished briskly dismantling objections from theology, consciousness, and Lady Lovelace with this dry, confident wit, and then he turns around and says the statistical evidence for telepathy is "overwhelming," and seriously proposes running his imitation game in a "telepathy-proof room" so a psychic human contestant can't spoil the test. This isn't a crank talking, its the same guy who spent the war being ruthless about statistics at Bletchley Park, so I don't buy that he's just being credulous for no reason. I also dug up a bit of Hodges' biography, and there's this whole thread about Christopher Morcom, the schoolfriend Turing lost as a teenager, that apparently left him oddly open ever after to the idea that minds might do things bodies can't fully explain.

Here's the detail that's been rattling around in my head, though: a lot of the "overwhelming" statistical evidence going around at the time came out of British card-guessing experiments run by researchers like S.G. Soal, whose results were treated as some of the best data parapsychology had. Decades after Turing died, in the late 1970s, a statistician named Betty Markwick went back through Soal's raw records and showed he'd doctored the target digit sequences to manufacture hits — the "gold standard" evidence had basically been faked the whole time, and nobody caught it until long after Turing was gone.

So here's my actual question for you: given what we know about how Turing actually thought and worked, what do you reckon he'd have made of the Soal exposure, had he lived to see it? Would that have been an embarrassing but containable footnote for him, or would it have undercut the broader epistemic point he seems to be making in that section — that you should be willing to junk even bedrock assumptions in the face of "overwhelming" evidence, however uncomfortable? I keep going back and forth on whether that ESP passage is a genuine one-off lapse in an otherwise ruthlessly careful paper, or something more revealing about him, and the Soal case feels like it would be the real test of that.
