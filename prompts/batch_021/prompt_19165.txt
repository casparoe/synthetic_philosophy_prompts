I'm a novelist a couple of years into a long, multigenerational book about a hill-farming family in mid-Wales, and in the course of researching it I've spent many evenings talking to actual sheep farmers about the unglamorous realities of the trade: lambing, markets, debt, and the working animals who make the whole enterprise possible. One of those conversations has, rather to my dismay, stopped being research and turned into a live argument inside my own family. My father-in-law, who has kept a smallholding for over forty years, has decided to have his old sheepdog, Nel, put down now that arthritis has ended her working days, even though the vet says she could live on quite comfortably for years yet as a pet. When my wife objected, he didn't get defensive; he laid out his reasoning almost placidly. He owes Nel nothing, he says, beyond having treated her decently and consistently while she was working for him, because she has no idea what is happening to her and never did — there is no sense in which things can go well or badly for her, from her own side of it. Keeping a dog who can no longer earn her keep, on his view, would only be indulging his own sentiment at the expense of a clear head, and a farmer who lets sentiment cloud his judgment about one animal soon makes worse decisions generally, and gradually becomes a less trustworthy person all round.

My wife was sure there was something wrong with this and said so, but when he asked her to actually locate the flaw, all she could manage was "that's not the point" — which even she found unsatisfying afterward. I'd like to use a version of this argument in the novel itself (I have a character who I think would make exactly this case, and I don't want him to win merely because nobody in the room is quick enough on their feet), so I would rather understand the dispute properly than referee it from the gut. Is my father-in-law's position actually self-consistent, or does it come apart somewhere on its own terms? And if my wife's instinct deserves better than "that's not the point," what is the strongest way of cashing it out — ideally one that doesn't just fall back on insisting that Nel's feelings must count for something?
