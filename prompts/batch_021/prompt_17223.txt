My work with knowledge graphs occasionally runs into paraconsistent logics — formal systems built to tolerate contradictions in the data without the whole thing collapsing into triviality. That side interest pulled me into the Jain doctrine of anekāntavāda and its logical apparatus, syādvāda, specifically the sevenfold predication scheme (saptabhaṅgī) that qualifies every claim about a thing with syāt, "in some respect."

What I can't pin down on my own is whether syādvāda amounts to a genuine form of dialetheism — the view that some contradictions are literally true — or whether it's something logically tamer: an elaborate way of indexing "is" and "is not" to different respects (substance, place, time, mode) so that no real contradiction is ever asserted in the same respect at the same time. The "avaktavya" (inexpressible) predications look like the hard case for that tamer reading, since they involve asserting existence and non-existence of the same thing at once rather than one after the other. Please compare syādvāda directly with dialetheism, stake out where it actually falls among plain logical consistency, paraconsistency, and full dialetheism, and justify your answer with specific reference to the avaktavya predications.
