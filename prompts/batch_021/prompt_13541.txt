I don't have any formal training in mathematics or philosophy, but over the past year I've spent a lot of my free time reading about epistemic game theory, and one result has really stuck with me: the game theorist Robert Aumann's proof that two Bayesian agents who share the same prior probabilities cannot "agree to disagree" once their opinions on some question become common knowledge between them, in the sense that each knows the other's opinion, knows that the other knows theirs, and so on without end — in that situation, their opinions must actually coincide. This strikes me as a remarkable result about what rationality demands of us. Yet in fields like economics, nutrition science, and public policy, I constantly see credentialed experts debate the very same questions for years, each one clearly aware of the other side's arguments and conclusions, without ever converging. Given Aumann's result, doesn't this kind of persistent, fully-informed expert disagreement mean that at least one party in every such dispute must be reasoning irrationally, and shouldn't it make me generally distrustful of "expert consensus" in any field where informed disagreement is this widespread and durable?
