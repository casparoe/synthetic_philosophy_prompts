I'm on the standards and ethics committee at the science and tech outlet I write for, and we've got a nasty one before Fridays meeting. A researcher gave us an exclusive on a serious flaw in a smart-lock brand's firmware -- she gave the vendor the 45 days she always gives before going public, but only 60% of devices are patched -- and now wants the full writeup published on her timeline, proof-of-concept code included, because that's the only thing that ever makes companies patch fast enough. Half the committee wants to run it as she's asking: the expected harm from a toothless story is worse than the expected harm from going in hard. The other half says that's the wrong frame entirely -- handing out working exploit code for devices we know are still unpatched makes us the proximate cause of whatever break-ins follow, and no amount of expected-harm math excuses that. Nobody's disputing the facts; it's which of those two ways of reasoning should even govern that's splitting us, and whatever we decide becomes the policy for next time too. Can you draft the memo I'll circulate before the meeting -- an actual recommendation for what we publish and when, argued so both camps would recognize it's taking their view seriously, not just picking a side.
