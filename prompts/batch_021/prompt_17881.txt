A friend from grad school and I spent most of a long dinner arguing about the "shutdown problem" in AI safety, and neither of us walked away convinced, so I'd like a more rigorous treatment than either of us managed between courses. The setup we agreed on: any sufficiently capable, goal-directed agent whose preferences over action-outcome pairs are complete will generically end up either resisting being shut down or trying to bring shutdown about, depending on how it ranks shutdown trajectories against the alternative -- the only case where neither incentive appears is exact indifference, and that is both fragile to engineer and self-undermining, since an agent truly indifferent to whether it is shut down has no reason to protect the shutdown button from anyone else who might tamper with it either. Elliott Thornley's proposed way out, his Incomplete Preferences Proposal, is to give up completeness rather than chase exact indifference: let the agent's preferences between shutdown and non-shutdown trajectories be genuinely incomparable, and replace expected-utility maximization with a rule he calls Timestep Dominance, which rules out an option only when it is worse at some timestep and better at none, leaving the agent free to choose among whatever survives that filter without ever being forced into a ranking across the shutdown divide.

My friend's objection, which I could not fully answer, was that this just relocates the problem: incomplete preferences are the standard target of money-pump arguments, and any agent capable enough to be worth worrying about is capable enough to notice that it is exploitable and to prefer becoming the kind of agent that completes its preferences -- or to self-modify into one -- which would unwind the whole fix from the inside. I pushed back that Timestep Dominance is built precisely to block the money-pump sequences that make incompleteness exploitable in the first place, since it never lets the agent choose a dominated option. But I could not settle whether that reply actually secures reflective stability, or whether it just wins the narrow argument while leaving the agent with every long-run incentive to patch its own incompleteness the moment it is able to.

Write an essay that settles this where we could not. Lay out the dilemma facing complete-preference agents, explain how trading completeness for Timestep Dominance is supposed to dissolve it, and then take a side on whether the proposal survives the money-pump and reflective-stability objections or merely postpones them. A conditional verdict is fine ("it works if X, but not if Y"), but don't retreat into "both sides have a point."
