Coherence arguments for expected utility maximization move from a narrow, structural premise to a surprisingly substantive conclusion. The premise is that an agent whose preferences are incomplete, intransitive, or otherwise fail to satisfy the axioms of expected utility theory can be "money-pumped": offered a sequence of trades that it will accept one by one, each seemingly reasonable on its own terms, but that leave it strictly worse off, or simply poorer with nothing to show for it, by the end. The conclusion is that any agent rational enough to notice and avoid this kind of exploitation must have preferences representable by a single utility function that it maximizes in expectation. This inference has done real work in recent debates about advanced artificial agents: if coherence really is forced on anything smart enough to avoid being pumped, then sufficiently capable AI systems should be expected to converge on goal-directed, expected-utility-maximizing behavior, with whatever downstream risks — relentless resource acquisition, resistance to being shut down or corrected, and so on — are supposed to follow from that.

I'd like a dialogue between a coherence theorist, who defends this inference, and a defender of rational incompleteness, who denies it. Both should be equally sharp, and neither should be a strawman for the other's benefit: real back-and-forth is the point, not a lecture with an interlocutor attached. The incompleteness theorist should press two distinct objections, and the coherence theorist should have genuine answers to both; there should be at least one further round of pushing and replying after that first exchange, rather than the dialogue settling for a single volley on each side. First, that an agent can dodge every money pump by committing in advance never to accept a trade that would return it, at a cost, to some option it had already paid to avoid — so-called resolute choice — while keeping its preferences genuinely incomplete or intransitive throughout. Second, and more corrosively, that "coherence" in the sense these arguments actually establish is nearly empty. Once an agent is allowed a sufficiently rich space of candidate utility functions to be coherent with respect to — functions defined over entire histories, say, rather than over simple outcomes — almost any pattern of choices can be redescribed after the fact as expected utility maximization. Showing that an agent is "coherent" in this thin sense, then, may tell us nothing about whether it is goal-directed in whatever sense actually grounds the worry about advanced AI.

Set the exchange wherever feels natural — two colleagues arguing at a whiteboard while specifying an agent's preference structure, two philosophers cornered after a talk, or something else entirely — but keep the setting incidental; the argument should be doing the work, not the scenery.
