I'm a PhD student in philosophy of physics working on a dissertation chapter about idealized infinite processes (things like spacetime singularities and Zeno machines), and Thomson's lamp keeps coming up as an object lesson that I can't quite settle in my own mind. In James Thomson's original 1954 setup, a lamp is switched on at t = 0, off half a minute later, on again a quarter-minute after that, and so on, so that infinitely many switchings occur within a single minute; Thomson argued that the lamp cannot coherently be on or off at the one-minute mark, since every "on" in the sequence is followed by an "off" and every "off" by an "on," so that no determinate final state is compatible with the history of switchings, and he took this to show that the very idea of completing such a supertask is self-contradictory, not merely difficult to physically realize. Paul Benacerraf's 1962 reply, "Tasks, Super-Tasks, and the Modern Eleatics," argues that this conflates two different things: the switching rule, as stated, only fixes the lamp's state at each moment strictly before t = 1, so all that follows is that the state at t = 1 is left logically undetermined by the description of the process, not that it is contradictory; on his diagnosis, one could simply stipulate a state for the lamp at t = 1 — on, off, or even something else — without generating any inconsistency, so the "paradox" dissolves into an artifact of illegitimately treating a limit point as though it had to inherit a determinate value from the terms approaching it.

A friend in my reading group insists that Benacerraf basically ends the discussion, and that Thomson's argument, once this confusion is cleared up, has no remaining force at all; I'm not so sure, since it seems to me Thomson might still be pointing at something real about why we should distrust the intelligibility of "completing" an infinite series of discrete, alternating operations, even if his own diagnosis of a strict contradiction was too strong. Could you write an essay that reconstructs the strongest versions of both the Thomson and the Benacerraf positions, addresses whatever you take to be the best objection to the side you end up favoring, and then commits to an actual verdict on the underlying question — does Thomson's lamp, or some repaired version of the argument, show that this kind of supertask is impossible in some interesting sense, or does Benacerraf's diagnosis fully dissolve the difficulty? Please don't hedge indefinitely between the two readings; if your considered view is that the truth lies somewhere in between, say precisely what narrower claim you think survives and why.
