I've been on a total Schelling kick lately (re-reading The Strategy of Conflict and Arms and Influence) at the same time as falling down a rabbit hole on "program equilibrium," and I can't stop seeing connections between them, so I want a smarter take than mine and my housemate's.

Quick context in case it's not fresh: program equilibrium is the move (I think it's originally from a Tennenholtz paper around 2004, building on even older "metagame" stuff by John Howard back in the 70s) where instead of players picking actions in a game, they submit programs that get to read each other's actual source code before outputting a move. Once you allow that, there's a folk theorem saying you can sustain more or less any individually-rational payoff as equilibrium, including full cooperation in a one-shot prisoners dilemma, via programs like "cooperate iff you can prove you're the kind of program that cooperates with me." The MIRI/LessWrong crowd pushed this further with the Lob's-theorem "FairBot" stuff to dodge the infinite-regress problem (I think this got nicknamed "modal combat" at some point), and more recently there's a whole "open-source game theory" literature (Oesterheld, Conitzer, and others, mostly post-2016) extending it into things like robust program equilibrium and "safe Pareto improvements," partly motivated by worrying about how AI agents that can inspect each other's code or policies might avoid conflict.

My housemate's take is that this is just Schelling's whole theory of commitment -- burning boats, throwing the steering wheel out the window in a game of chicken, the doomsday machine -- reformulated in computer-science clothing, and that Schelling basically called this decades before anyone wrote a line of it. I think that's too fast, because Schelling's whole point, especially in the arms-control material, was that credibility is the scarce, expensive, risky resource: you need some real device that binds you and it might fail or get faked, whereas program equilibrium just assumes perfect transparency exists and asks what follows, which feels like skipping past the hard part rather than resolving it. So: if Schelling had lived to actually sit down with this literature, what do you think he'd say? Would he treat the program-game folk theorem as basically his commitment theory vindicated and formalized, or would he have a specific, sharp objection to the transparency assumption itself? And on the arms-control angle -- given how much he worried about verification problems in real disarmament proposals -- do you think he'd be optimistic or pretty skeptical about people now suggesting this kind of source-code (or cryptographic-commitment) transparency as a way to get AI systems to avoid conflict, the way a doomsday machine or a hotline was supposed to?
