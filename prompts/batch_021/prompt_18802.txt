I'm a 4th-year econ PhD student, and every other week a few of us run an informal philosophy-of-economics reading group over cheap pizza in the department lounge (started mostly because a few of us were tired of pure math-econ seminars). Last session was nominally about revealed preference theory but basically turned into a slugging match between two of the senior students, Leo and Marta, over what "utility" even means in modern economics, and it never really got resolved, so I want a neutral outside read on it.

(1) Leo's opening claim was that Samuelson basically settled this back in 1938: once you have revealed preference theory, "utility" isn't a psychological quantity at all, it's just an ordinal number representing whatever consistent ranking is implied by someone's choices, and that's exactly why the concept has been so durable -- it doesn't rest on anything unobservable like pleasure or desire. (2) Marta answered that this can't be the whole story, because economists constantly use "utility"/revealed preference as a stand-in for welfare -- consumer surplus, willingness-to-pay, cost-benefit analysis for policy -- which requires exactly the normative content Leo just said wasn't needed. Worse, she said, if "revealing a preference for X" just means "being observed choosing X," then nobody can ever choose something that's bad for them by definition, which makes addiction, akrasia, and manipulated choices conceptually impossible instead of the very real phenomena they obviously are. (3) Leo's comeback was that this is a fair hit on applied welfare economics but doesn't actually touch revealed preference theory itself, which he said was only ever meant as positive theory (predicting choice), not a theory of the good -- if you want welfare, go use something like Kahneman's "experienced utility" or idealized preferences, that's a seperate project. (4) Marta said that's a bigger concession than Leo seemed to realize, since basically all of applied welfare economics conflates the two, so if he's right, huge amounts of real cost-benefit policy work are quietly equivocating on "utility." She also pointed out that revealed preference theory needs actual choices to satisfy the consistency axioms (WARP and friends) to even generate a well-defined ordering in the first place, and that the classic preference-reversal experiments -- people ranking gambles one way when choosing between them and another way when pricing them -- suggest real choices often don't. (5) Leo's response was that economics treats that kind of inconsistency as noise around a stable underlying ordering, the same way any science tolerates measurement error, and that Marta's own preferred alternative, people's self-reports about their experience, is just as manipulable by framing and even less comparable across people, so nobody actually escapes relying on some proxy or other.

Can you go through this move by move and say, for each of (1) through (5), who's got the better argument (or if its a wash), rather than just handing down an overall winner -- and then say what you think both of them are missing about what "utility" is actually doing for economists here?
