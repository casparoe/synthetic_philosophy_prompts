I'm drafting a magazine piece on why the most confident, well-informed people so often turn out to be the least movable by counter-evidence: the partisan who dismisses an inconvenient poll as skewed, the scientist attached to a paradigm who waves away an anomaly as measurement error, the specialist who won't revisit a diagnosis. I want to frame the piece around a decades-old puzzle in epistemology, the dogmatism paradox, and I'd value your comments on the section below before I go any further with it.

The argument was first outlined by Gilbert Harman and later sharpened, and named, by Saul Kripke, in work dating to the 1970s. It runs in three moves. First, suppose I know that some claim, h, is true. Second, precisely because h is true, any future evidence that seems to count against it must be misleading evidence, since evidence against a truth is, by definition, evidence pointing the wrong way; so knowing h already puts me in a position to know that any apparent counter-evidence to it is misleading. Third, if I already know that a piece of evidence is misleading, I don't need to be moved by it once it turns up — I'm entitled to have discounted it in advance. String these three moves together and you get a conclusion nobody wants: knowing something entitles you to disregard whatever future evidence might come along to challenge it. Knowledge, rather than making us more answerable to evidence, turns out to license us to stop being answerable to it at all.

Nobody treats this as a genuine discovery; it's called a paradox precisely because the conclusion is intolerable, so something upstream has to give, and the interesting question is what. My own sense, from reading around the topic, is that the cleanest way out is to deny the KK principle — the thesis that knowing something requires also knowing that you know it. Give that up, and knowing h no longer certifies, in advance, that some specific, not-yet-encountered item of apparent counter-evidence belongs in the misleading pile; the entitlement to wave it away unseen evaporates with it. On that diagnosis, real-world dogmatists are trading on a tacit, false assumption that their knowledge comes with a guarantee attached.

If that diagnosis holds up, the piece can end on a properly unsettling note: dogmatism isn't a vice bolted onto knowledge from the outside, the way overconfidence or motivated reasoning are — it's what knowledge looks like when it's wrongly credited with a self-certifying power it was never shown to have.

I'd welcome your comments on three things: whether I've reconstructed the argument's premises accurately, whether resting the resolution on the KK principle actually holds up or whether I've reached for the wrong culprit, and whether the closing move — recasting dogmatism as knowledge-plus-a-false-assumption rather than an external bias — is a fair gloss on what philosophers take the paradox to show.
