Here's the actual question: "In the Theodicy, Leibniz claims that God created 'the best of all possible worlds.' Explain (a) what work the notion of a 'possible world' is doing in his argument, (b) why Leibniz thinks the existence of evil is compatible with this claim, and (c) in what sense, if any, God's choice of this world is necessary, and whether his position is ultimately any different from Spinoza's necessitarianism."

And here's one student's answer, copied exactly as written:

"Leibniz's picture is that before creating anything, God's understanding contained infinitely many complete, self-consistent 'possible worlds' — every way a universe could have played out down to the last detail. Being perfectly wise and good, God compared them all and actualized the one containing the greatest amount of perfection (roughly, the richest variety of phenomena governed by the simplest laws). Crucially, not all possible individuals are compossible: a world with a Judas who doesn't betray Christ, or a Caesar who doesn't cross the Rubicon, is simply a different world from ours, so God couldn't cherry-pick the good bits from every possible world and leave out the bad ones. That's why the actual world contains evil at all — removing any part of it, even one sin, would turn it into a different, less perfect whole.

Leibniz sorts evil into three kinds: moral evil (sin), physical evil (pain, and natural evils like earthquakes and disease), and metaphysical evil, which he basically identifies with these same physical shortcomings — decay, weakness, disease — built into finite bodies. God doesn't will moral evil, he only permits it as an unavoidable side effect of choosing the best possible whole.

Because the principle of sufficient reason means God always has a reason to pick the best, and there's only one best world, God's choice, even though Leibniz calls it 'free,' is really necessary in the same sense that a conclusion is necessary given its premises — so despite all the talk of God 'choosing' among alternatives, the outcome is no less fixed than it is on Spinoza's picture, where the world just flows from God's nature without any choice at all."
