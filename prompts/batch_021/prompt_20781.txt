I cover artificial intelligence and cognitive science for a science magazine, and for the past year I've held a seat on the external ethics advisory board of a mid-sized video-recommendation platform — the sort of board a company convenes partly so it can say that independent eyes are examining decisions like the one now before us. The personalization team wants to retire the platform's explicit preference controls, the topic toggles and "show me less of this" buttons users set for themselves, in favor of a system that infers each viewer's real interests purely from behavior: watch time, rewatch rate, what gets clicked when an alternative is offered. Internally this is described as an inverse-reinforcement-learning approach — rather than trust what people say they want, infer the reward function they appear to be maximizing, on the old economists' premise that revealed preferences are harder to fake than survey answers. Wherever it has been tested, engagement rises, and almost no one switches back to manual controls.

Two board members trained in decision theory remain opposed, and not over the engagement numbers. Their objection is that behavior alone cannot settle what a person wants: any finite viewing history is compatible with indefinitely many reward functions, some considerably bleaker than "loves nature documentaries," and no amount of extra data breaks the tie without some prior assumption about how rational the viewer is being. Worse, the interface itself has spent years shaping the behavior now being read off as evidence of authentic desire, so compulsive viewing a person regrets by morning becomes nearly impossible to distinguish, on this method, from considered preference. The engineers' reply is that this cuts both ways: self-reports are not innocent of manipulation either, and treating them as the neutral default is itself a substantive, quietly paternalistic choice, not the safe option it sounds like.

I need to circulate a short written opinion before the board's vote next month and would like your help with a first draft. Please write the memo itself, not a summary of the debate: recommend one concrete option — approve the change, block it, or approve it only under stated conditions — and defend that choice on its merits, including a fair accounting of the side it rejects. Write for the full board, most of whom have no machine-learning background, so the philosophical stakes have to be argued in plain terms rather than asserted with jargon.
