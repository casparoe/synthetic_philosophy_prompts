My mother is in home hospice, and for the past few months I have been managing her medication schedule between visits from her palliative care team. Last week, at two in the morning, I asked an AI assistant for guidance on titrating her morphine dose within a range her own doctors had already authorized; it refused outright and told me only to call her physician -- true enough, but no help at that hour. The exchange left me turning over a harder question than I expected: when, if ever, is it legitimate for an AI system to override a competent adult's own judgment about their situation 'for their own good'? Write an essay on this controversy that reaches and defends a specific verdict rather than settling for 'it depends.' It should engage seriously with the Millian liberal case against paternalism and the exceptions Mill himself allowed, the standard distinction between 'soft' paternalism (correcting for choices that seem uninformed or compromised) and 'hard' paternalism (overriding even fully competent ones), and the argument that an AI's structural inability to verify a user's context or expertise might justify a degree of caution no human professional would need -- weighed against the counter-argument that such caution is self-defeating if it simply pushes users toward less careful sources of the same information.
