Spent 35 years as a field biologist before I retired, and lately I've been chewing through some philosophy of biology stuff just for fun. There's this long running argument about what we actually mean when we say a trait's 'function' is such and such. One side (goes back to a guy named Wright, then Neander sharpened it up) says the heart's function is to pump blood because that's exactly what got ancestral hearts favored by selection and kept around, so the function is basically written into the trait's history. The other side (Cummins) says skip the history, just look at what the part contributes to some capacity of whatever bigger system your interested in right now — so relative to circulation the heart's job is pumping blood, but relative to a doctor listening with a stethoscope its job could just as well be making thumping noises. Same organ, totally different function, depending which system you pick.

Anyway it got me thinking about Aristotle, who spent half his life cutting open animals and talking about what organs are "for," centuries before anyone had natural selection or this systems-relative idea to reach for. If you dropped him into the middle of this argument today, would he of sided with the historical camp, the systemic camp, neither, or would he say we're all mangling what a final cause actually is? Curious what you think.
