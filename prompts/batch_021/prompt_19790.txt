I'm a PhD student in philosophy of biology, and a dispute broke out in my reading group last month that I haven't been able to settle in my own head.

We'd just covered selected-effects versus causal-role theories of biological function, and two people in the group, Owen and Nadia, had the following exchange.

(1) Owen defended the etiological line: sweating's function is thermoregulation, because ancestral hominins with an efficient sweat response survived and reproduced at higher rates than those without one.

(2) Nadia objected that this can't be the whole story. Suppose a new gene variant changed sweat composition so that it also flushed out a harmful toxin, helping its bearer immediately, before selection had acted on the variant across even a single generation. That toxin-flushing effect is doing something for the organism already, so tying function that tightly to a selection history rules out brand-new traits having any function at all. She said we need Cummins' causal-role notion instead: a trait's function, relative to some system, is just whatever it currently contributes to that system's capacities.

(3) Owen replied that this flexibility is bought too cheaply. Pick a generous enough system and sweating's "function" could just as well be signaling something about fertility through odor cues, or anything else some system happens to depend on: causal-role theory never tells you which containing system is the right one. Worse, it can't make sense of malfunction: someone with hyperhidrosis, whose glands sweat uncontrollably, isn't obviously malfunctioning on a view where function just is current causal contribution, since their glands are doing exactly what they causally do.

(4) Nadia answered that both problems are fixable without smuggling history back in. Restrict the relevant systems to ones bearing on survival and reproduction, which rules out arbitrary ones, and define malfunction as a statistical shortfall relative to how conspecifics' glands typically perform, rather than relative to any selected past.

(5) Owen said the statistical move doesn't avoid history, it just defers it one step: thermoregulation counts as sweating's normal, relevant effect -- rather than the odor cues or the toxin-flushing -- only because thermoregulation is what sweating was shaped by selection to do.

Neither of them budged, and both seemed onto something real. Could you adjudicate moves (1) through (5) individually, rather than just declaring an overall winner?
