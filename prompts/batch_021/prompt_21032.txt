I recently agreed to help run my local public library's "Douglass Day" event this coming February 14th -- the birthday Frederick Douglass chose for himself, since he was never told his actual one -- and as part of the day's programming I've been asked to record a short interview afterward for our library's small community podcast. The historian who agreed to be the guest wants to try something a little unusual: rather than summarizing Douglass's career the way a historian normally would, she's going to answer in character, as Douglass himself, defending the specific position he arrived at after 1851, when he broke publicly and painfully with William Lloyd Garrison over how to read the U.S. Constitution. Getting ready for this made me realize how little I actually understood beyond "he changed his mind" -- I hadn't appreciated how carefully argued, and how contested at the time, his shift was: from treating the Constitution as a pro-slavery "covenant with death" that no self-respecting abolitionist should vote under, to insisting that the very same, unamended text, properly construed, had been an anti-slavery document all along.

Could you draft eight to ten interview questions, addressed to Douglass in the second person, that actually engage the substance of that argument rather than letting him simply restate "I used to think X, now I think Y"? I'd like at least one question that puts to him the charge Garrison and Wendell Phillips made at the time -- that the turn was less a discovery than a capitulation to Gerrit Smith's money and to Lysander Spooner's legal theorizing; one that presses on the interpretive move at the center of his case, namely leaning on the fact that the words "slave" and "slavery" appear nowhere in the document, when clauses like the fugitive-slave clause and the three-fifths clause plainly presuppose the institution; one on why he thinks the text's plain, publicly enacted meaning should override the demonstrable private intentions of the men who drafted it; and a harder, closing question about whether such a generous reading of the framers doesn't simply repeat, in reverse, the very fault he accused the Garrisonians of -- settling on the conclusion first and then finding the interpretation to fit it. For each question, please add a brief follow-up keyed to the answer he is most likely to give, so the exchange doesn't stop at the first well-rehearsed reply.
