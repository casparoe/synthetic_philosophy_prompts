So in my Philosophy of Language seminar we spent the last two weeks on the sorites paradox and now we've moved onto "higher-order vagueness," and I feel like I get epistemicism and supervaluationism decently well on their own, but I'm getting tangled up on how they actually stack up against each other on this specific point. Williamson's epistemicist pitch is that a predicate like "heap" has a totally sharp cutoff (n grains is a heap, n-1 isn't) and we just can't know where it is, because knowing it would require a margin for error that our belief-forming practices don't have, given how the word's meaning gets fixed by patterns of use with no sharp edges built in. That's supposed to explain why borderline cases feel fuzzy even though there IS a fact of the matter underneath. Supervaluationists instead say "heap" has a bunch of admissible ways of being made precise, a sentence is true iff its true on all of them and false iff false on all of them, and borderline sentences are just neither -- which apparently lets them keep classical logic (excluded middle etc) around while still allowing truth-value gaps.

My professor keeps hammering on the point that "admissible precisification" is itself a vague notion, so which sharpenings count as admissible has fuzzy boundaries too, and it feels like this just regenerates the exact same sorites problem one level up. A couple of us argued about this after class and couldn't agree on how bad it actually is for the supervaluationist. I vaguely (ha) remember from the readings that Williamson thinks his margin-for-error story actually predicts higher-order vagueness for free, rather than it being an embarrassment the way it supposedly is for the other side. Could you walk me through the actual technical machinery here -- how exactly does the iterated margin-for-error thing generate higher-order vagueness for the epistemicist without that move just being ad hoc, and do supervaluationists have a real, non-question-begging response to the "admissibility is vague too" regress (fuzzy admissibility, supervaluations over supervaluations, something else), or do they basically just have to bite the bullet? I want to know if one theory is genuinely more consistent on this specific point or if it's a wash where both sides are just picking their poison in different spots.
