As part of the fieldwork for my PhD in migration studies, I have spent the last year working as an interpreter during asylum interviews, which has given me a close view of how caseworkers judge applicants' credibility. I've noticed a pattern that troubles me: applicants who recount traumatic events calmly and consistently are often treated as coached or dishonest, while those who become visibly distressed, or whose accounts shift slightly between retellings, are read as more "genuine." Yet the psychological literature on trauma and memory suggests that neither calmness nor consistency is a reliable sign of fabrication, and that norms for displaying distress vary considerably across the cultures represented in these interviews. When I mentioned this to a caseworker friend, she said it is simply unavoidable due diligence: credibility has to be judged somehow, and interviewers cannot take every account at face value. I take her point, but I suspect something more specific, and more troubling, than an imperfect heuristic is going on. Could you help me work out exactly what, if anything, is wrong with this practice, and whether "we have to judge somehow" actually answers the worry?
