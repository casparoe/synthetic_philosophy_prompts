A couple of people in my online probability book club got into a long back-and-forth about the two-envelope paradox -- one envelope has twice as much money as the other, you pick one, and theres a tempting argument that you should always switch -- and neither of them clearly won, so I want a neutral referee. Here's roughly how it went:

(1) Sam opened his envelope and found $20. He argued the other envelope has either $10 or $40, and with no info favoring either, 50/50 is right, making switching worth $25 in expectation -- so switch.
(2) Noor said that can't be right, since the same argument re-run right after switching would tell you to switch back, then switch again, forever, which is absurd -- so the reasoning is broken somewhere, regardless of the actual numbers.
(3) Sam agreed but pushed back on where exactly: treating unknown alternatives as 50/50 when you truly have zero other information is just the principle of indifference, not some rookie mistake.
(4) Noor said the 50/50 move quietly assumes something impossible: that whatever number you happen to see, you're equally likely to be holding the small envelope or the big one. That can only hold for every possible number if the amounts were drawn from a perfectly flat distribution over all positive numbers, and no such probability distribution actually exists.
(5) Sam conceded that, but said he'd gone and built an actual, fully legitimate distribution to stress-test her claim: the smaller envelope holds $1, $2, $4, $8, $16... with each amount exactly 2/3 as likely as the last (which sums to 1, nothing improper about it). He worked out the posterior probabilities and says that under this distribution, switching wins by a fixed margin no matter which amount you actually see -- so his original "always switch" conclusion basically survives after all, just for a different reason than he first gave.
(6) Noor wasn't buying it. She said that has to be a mistake somewhere on his end, because a bona fide, everyday-legitimate probability distribution shouldn't be able to reproduce the exact same absurd infinite-loop situation from (2).

So, who's actually right at each of these six moves? Is Sam's distribution in (5) legit, or is there something hidden wrong with it -- and if it does check out, does that rescue his opening argument in (1), or was (1) still bad reasoning that just happened to land on a conclusion that holds in this one special case? Curious whether you think there's a bigger lesson about this whole setup that neither Sam nor Noor is quite landing on.
