I'm a PhD student in economics, and fieldwork for a side project has left me with a puzzle about incentive design that I can't fully resolve. I already know the standard cautionary tales about rewarding easy-to-measure proxies, such as teachers teaching to the test or call-center agents racing through calls, but this case feels different, and I'd like to understand why before I write it up. A conservation NGO I've been shadowing divides its reserve into patrol sectors and pays each ranger team a monthly bonus based on the number of snares they find and dismantle, because years of internal audits showed that sectors with high snare counts were reliably the same sectors where camera-trap surveys later recorded the steepest declines in poaching. This year, though, independent data (camera-trap surveys and wildlife counts) show that one sector now has the least poaching in the entire reserve, yet that very sector's snare count has collapsed to almost nothing, taking its bonus down with it, while a sector still overrun with poachers keeps posting large snare counts and correspondingly large bonuses. Management is reading the newly successful sector's falling numbers as a sign that its rangers have grown complacent, and is discussing cutting their funding. I suspect that inference is confused, but not for the usual teaching-to-the-test reason, since the original link between snare counts and success was never spurious or manipulated. What's actually gone wrong here, and how should the NGO measure ranger performance instead?
