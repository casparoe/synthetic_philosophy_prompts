So I'm ABD in political theory and I've been going down a rabbit hole for my quals list on democratic legitimacy that I can't shake. It's this thing called "the boundary problem" -- I guess Whelan is usually credited with naming it back in the early 80s, the basic idea being that democratic procedures can't tell you who should be included in the demos that runs the procedure in the first place, because settling that question would itself require a vote, and you need to already know who's eligible to vote in order to hold that vote. Circular all the way down. Since then theres this whole literature trying to patch it -- Goodin's "all affected interests" piece, which he sort of admits blows up into near-global enfranchisement once you start counting possible/indirect effects; the "all subjected to coercion" alternative that Abizadeh runs with in his border-coercion stuff, where he ends up arguing the demos is in principle unbounded and states don't have a unilateral right to just draw their own boundaries; Nasström's paper arguing both of those secretly smuggle in the very "people" they're supposed to be justifying; and then Miller pushing back with a more nationality/community based account of who counts as a legitimate member. My advisor keeps saying none of this is just an academic exercise, and honestly this week I finally believe her.

Here's why: my family has had a little place on a lake up north since my grandfather's time, and it's "seasonal" -- we're there maybe four months a year and can't vote at town meeting because you have to be a domiciled year-round resident to be on the checklist. There's a huge fight going on right now because seasonal owners are something like 60% of the tax base, and a warrant article just passed (that we had zero vote on) capping short-term rentals in a way that's gonna hammer property values for exactly the people who can't vote on it but also can't hold office or run for select board. A few of the old-timers are furious that "summer people" even want a say, other year-rounders think it's obviously unfair given how much tax revenue comes from us, and somebody's now floating a compromise of a nonvoting advisory seat. Got pretty heated at the last two meetings apparently, my uncle said someone nearly came to blows over it.

Anyway -- rather than ask you to referee the local politics of it (which I don't think you can do responsibly without knowing a lot more than I could tell you), what I'd love is for you to lay out the most important implications of the boundary-problem literature for thinking through a case like this. Like, if I bring the Whelan/Goodin/Abizadeh/Nasström/Miller debate to bear on "should seasonal, non-domiciled property owners get a vote (or some kind of vote) at town meeting," what actually falls out of it? Where does the case fit cleanly onto the existing principles (affected-interests vs. subjection-to-coercion vs. ascriptive/national membership) and where does it kind of break them, e.g. because seasonal owners are neither transient outsiders nor full insiders in the way most of the literature's stock examples (immigrants, future generations, foreigners affected by war) seem to assume? I keep going back and forth on whether this whole literature was ever really built for "partial membership" cases like this one.
