I'm a software engineer (mostly backend stuff), so this is way outside my lane, but I went down a rabbit hole last week after my partner, who's doing a psych postdoc, mentioned this thing called "cognitive penetration" of perception. The basic debate, as I understood it: can what you believe or expect actually change what you *see*, at the level of the raw perceptual experience itself, or does perception stay "walled off" from your higher-level beliefs, with all the bias creeping in later at the judgment stage? The case for "walled off" is stuff like the Müller-Lyer illusion, wich apparently doesn't go away even once you've measured the lines yourself and know they're equal. The case against is stuff like radiologists seeing tumors trainees miss, or that old study where Dartmouth and Princeton football fans watched the exact same game film afterward and each side came away having genuinely seen the other team commit way more fouls. What's been stuck in my head since is the epistemology part: if bias can really get all the way down into perception, then the biased "seeing" wouldn't feel like a judgment you could catch yourself making, it would just feel like seeing, and it would even hand you what feels like fresh, independent evidence for the exact bias that produced it. That's a trap with no exit door, since you cant fact-check your eyes with your eyes.

Build toward one incident in the box late in the match, where he has to jog over to the pitchside monitor and review the replay himself before pointing one way or the other. I don't want the story, or the replay, to ever hand the reader a clean answer about whether he saw real contact or whether his eyes just gave him exactly the contact he needed to see -- the slow-motion angle should stay every bit as genuinely borderline as the live look was. End right on the decision, no postgame moral about bias tacked on. I want to be left in the same hole he's in, not told what to think about it.
