I spent the better part of an evening arguing with a friend who is halfway through a philosophy PhD on the ethics of war, and we never quite reached agreement about the moral equality of combatants thesis. She defended something close to Michael Walzer's traditionalist picture: jus in bello is independent of jus ad bellum -- the so-called independence thesis -- so that a soldier fighting on the unjust side of a war who nonetheless respects the constraints of discrimination and proportionality has done nothing wrong as a combatant, and deserves the same moral and legal standing -- POW protections, immunity from prosecution for the mere fact of having fought -- as a soldier on the just side. I kept pushing the revisionist line associated with Jeff McMahan: that this "equality" is really a legal fiction that has been mistaken for a moral truth, since a combatant who lacks a just cause also lacks the substantive right to kill, so his killings remain a form of wrongful harming even when he satisfies every in bello constraint -- though he may often be excused rather than blamed, given how little ordinary soldiers can know about the justice of the war they are fighting.

Where we got stuck was on three questions that seem to run through the literature more generally: whether the practical costs of abandoning the equality thesis -- weaker incentives for unjust combatants to observe the laws of war, harder war termination, a legal order that can no longer stay neutral about who is in the right -- should count against it as a moral view and not merely as a useful convention; whether the distinction between being justified and merely excused really saves revisionism from the charge that it turns most soldiers in most wars in history into wrongdoers; and whether the traditionalist picture is quietly relying on an act/agent-neutral way of thinking about killing that we would reject in any ordinary case of self-defense. I'd like you to write an essay that lays out the strongest version of both the traditionalist and the revisionist positions on the moral equality of combatants, engages seriously with these three sticking points, and then actually commits to a conclusion instead of leaving things in equipoise. A qualified verdict, such as "morally unequal, but legally equal for good reason," is a perfectly acceptable answer, so long as you argue for it rather than simply asserting it.
