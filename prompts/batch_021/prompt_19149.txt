I've spent the last few months reporting on how big philanthropic funders (the effective-altruism-adjacent ones especially) decide where money goes, and I keep running into this objection that apparently gives even committed consequentialists heartburn: "cluelessness." The rough idea, as it's been explained to me by a couple of people I interviewed, is that once you try to actually trace out the full consequences of an action -- not just the next few obvious effects but everything downstream, indefinitely -- you hit effects you have no real way to estimate, and the annoying part is that some of these seem to point in opposite directions rather than just being random noise that cancels out (e.g. a program that saves lives also shifts population growth, resource use, who gets born and what they do with their life, etc). Apparently there's a genuine split even among people who all buy into consequentialism about how serious this is: some think it's basically dressed-up ordinary uncertainty that expected value reasoning was always built to handle, others think it shows the whole project of picking actions by their consequences quietly falls apart once you actually take it seriously.

Could you write me a dialogue between two consequentialists hashing this out -- one who thinks cluelessness is real but ultimately tameable, the other who thinks it's fatal to the idea that we can rationally act on consequentialist grounds at all? Set it at a foundation's grant committee meeting, the night before they have to choose between a charity with well-established, measurable short-term benefits (think global health, bednets, cash transfers) and a much more speculative long-shot intervention (think some far-future / existential-risk project), and have them get stuck specifically on the cluelessness question rather than the more familiar "how do you compare a sure thing to a gamble" framing. I want the "fatal" side to actually spell out why some unforseen effects are the kind that wash out symmetrically across whatever you choose, and why they think this case isn't that kind. And I want the "tameable" side to have a real comeback, not just a restatement of "expected value is still your best guide" -- like, is there something self-undermining about claiming total cluelessness, given that even sizing up how clueless you are seems to require the same kind of confident judgment that's supposedly off the table? Basically, skip the cartoon version of this debate where the punchline is just "nobody can predict the future" -- I want them going after the sharpest form of the objection, each one actually engaging with the other's specific move rather than repeating themselves.
