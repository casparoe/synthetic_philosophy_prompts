A physicist I trained under is retiring after four decades in the field. For as long as anyone can remember, she has given each of her graduating doctoral students the same gift: a copy of Émilie du Châtelet's Discours sur le bonheur (Discourse on Happiness), inscribed with a line about study being the one passion that fortune cannot touch. I have been asked to give the toast at her retirement dinner. Roughly half the room are her former students, who received that same book and will notice immediately if I soften or sentimentalize what du Châtelet actually argued -- about the necessity of illusions, the unreliability of glory, or what becomes of a life organized around a passion that is now ending. The other half are relatives and old friends with no particular interest in eighteenth-century French philosophy. Write the toast: it has to move the second group without insulting the first, it has to face honestly what du Châtelet's essay says about losing a defining passion rather than settling for generic reassurance, and it still has to work as a toast rather than a seminar.
