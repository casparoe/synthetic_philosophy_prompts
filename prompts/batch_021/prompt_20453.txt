I'm several years into a novel that follows David Hilbert through his last isolated months in Göttingen in 1943, an old man largely cut off from a mathematical community that Nazi purges had scattered a decade before. For background I've been reading Constance Reid's biography of him alongside the English translation of his 1900 Paris address, and I've now reached the chapter I was most looking forward to writing and am most stuck on: a feverish, half-hallucinated sequence near the end in which Hilbert is somehow shown how his own first problem was finally settled. Cantor's continuum hypothesis, which he placed at the head of that 1900 list, turned out to be independent of the Zermelo–Fraenkel axioms — Gödel had already shown, in 1940, that it cannot be disproved from them, and Paul Cohen showed in 1963, via his newly invented method of forcing, that it cannot be proved from them either.

What I can't yet work out is what this news should actually do to my character, beyond generic astonishment. In the introduction to that same 1900 address, Hilbert argued that every properly posed mathematical problem admits of a definite resolution, and, pointedly, that a rigorous proof of a solution's impossibility counts as such a resolution in its own right, on a par with the classical proof that the diagonal of a square is incommensurable with its side, or Lindemann's later proof that the circle cannot be squared; the passage ends with his famous claim that in mathematics there is no ignorabimus. By the 1920s that early optimism had hardened into the far more specific project historians call Hilbert's program, aimed at a finitary consistency proof that would secure what he elsewhere called Cantor's paradise of infinite sets against the intuitionists — a project Gödel's incompleteness theorems had already thrown into serious doubt well before this second, more particular blow arrived. So: would Hilbert, by his own 1900 standard, be entitled to receive Cohen's result as a genuine solution to his first problem, an unsolvability proof rightfully joining the impossibility proofs he so admired, or does it violate something that "no ignorabimus" actually meant — that every problem has a determinate answer awaiting discovery, not merely a demonstration that none follows from a given set of axioms? And should his reaction differ at all from the one Gödel himself held, that the continuum hypothesis has a definite truth value that better axioms might someday uncover? I would rather have your reasoning worked through carefully than a tidy verdict; it is the texture of the thinking I need for the page, not a moral to end the scene on.
