here's the answer (worth 20 pts):

"Kripke's objection is that on Lewis's counterpart theory, 'Humphrey could have won the election' isn't really about Humphrey at all -- it's made true by some other guy in another possible world who merely resembles him, and Humphrey himself presumably couldn't care less what happens to that guy. So counterpart theory seems to change the subject: it gives us a truth about a stranger instead of a truth about Humphrey himself.

Lewis's reply is that this confuses the analysis with what ordinary speakers care about. The counterpart relation just specifies truth conditions for the modal sentence; it isn't claiming that Humphrey should be emotionally invested in his counterpart's electoral fortunes. And since counterparts have to be qualitative duplicates of each other for the relation to hold in the first place, Humphrey's counterpart is, for all descriptive purposes, just as much 'Humphrey' as anything in another world could be, so Kripke's complaint that it's really about 'someone else' loses most of its force.

A transworld identity theorist like Plantinga can seemingly dodge this whole problem by saying it's literally, numerically the same Humphrey who wins in the other world, not a counterpart of him. Since for Plantinga possible worlds are concrete alternate universes just like ours, only causally cut off from one another, Humphrey can be a full-blooded resident of more than one of them, which is exactly what Lewis's picture rules out. So Plantinga trades Lewis's problem for a cleaner-looking one, at the cost of needing some further story about how a single thing ends up spread across separate concrete worlds."
