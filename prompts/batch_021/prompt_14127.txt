In some reading on multi-agent decision theory, I came across Moshe Tennenholtz's notion of "program equilibrium," in which the players of a game do not submit actions or mixed strategies but rather programs that can read and simulate one another's source code before returning a move. The standard illustration is the one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma: if each player submits a program that cooperates just in case the opponent's program is textually identical to its own (and defects otherwise), mutual cooperation becomes a genuine Nash equilibrium of the resulting "meta-game" over programs, since any unilateral deviation from that program is immediately punished by mutual defection. This struck me as formally related to, but not obviously the same as, Douglas Hofstadter's older and much more informal idea of "superrationality," on which perfectly rational agents facing a perfectly symmetric dilemma should reason that, since any argument for defecting is equally available to their counterpart, the only self-consistent joint outcome is one in which both players make the same choice — and since mutual cooperation dominates mutual defection, superrational agents cooperate without needing to inspect one another's reasoning directly at all.

I'd like you to compare these two proposals in some depth, rather than simply noting that both recommend cooperation in the Prisoner's Dilemma. In particular: does program equilibrium amount to a formalization of Hofstadter's superrationality — a game-theoretic mechanism that finally cashes out what superrational agents were implicitly doing all along — or is it a genuinely different solution to the cooperation problem, one whose force comes from mutual code transparency and self-referential tricks like the identical-program test, rather than from the symmetry-based reasoning Hofstadter had in mind? Please justify your answer by pointing to specific features the two accounts do or do not share, and say which of the two, if either, strikes you as the more successful reply to the classical charge that cooperating in a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma is simply irrational.
