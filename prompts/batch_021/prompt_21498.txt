Question 4 (15 pts): A sculptor takes a lump of clay, Lump, and molds it over an hour into a statue, Athena, without adding or removing any clay. A year later someone flattens Athena into a pancake. Lump survives this (same parcel of clay, just squashed flat) but Athena doesn't - there's no statue left.
(a) [6 pts] Using Leibniz's Law, explain why Athena and Lump can't be one and the same object.
(b) [9 pts] Lynne Rudder Baker's "constitution view" says Lump constitutes Athena but Lump ≠ Athena, which is supposed to let us avoid saying two objects occupy the same place at the same time. Explain how this is meant to work, and say whether you think it succeeds.

Student's answer:
(a) Athena and Lump can't be the same object because they don't share all their properties. Lump can survive being flattened, Athena can't - once its flattened there's no statue, even though all the clay is still sitting right there. So Lump has the property "can survive flattening" and Athena doesn't, and by Leibniz's Law (if x = y then x and y share every property) they can't be identical. You can run the same move with time instead of shape: Lump existed as a shapeless ball before the sculptor started, Athena didn't exist yet, so again there's a property (existing at that time) they don't share, and so they aren't identical.
(b) Baker's move is that Lump and Athena are numerically distinct but aren't competing for the same spot the way two ordinary statues would be, because Athena is constituted by Lump rather than merely co-located with it. Constitution is a real, asymmetric relation - Lump constitutes Athena, not the other way round - and it's what lets Athena "borrow" properties like mass and color from Lump while having properties of its own, like being valuable as art, that Lump lacks. This is what dissolves the puzzle: Baker's constitution relation is looser than identity, and since Lump and Athena end up sharing so much - shape, weight, location, causal history from the moment the sculpting finished - she's basically allowing two things to share literally all of their properties without being identical, i.e. rejecting Leibniz's Law just for constituted objects. I think this basically works as a way of dodging the "two objects, one place" worry, though it does commit her to a pretty deflationary view of what an object even is.
