I'm a linguistics PhD student in a philosophy-of-language reading group. Two of the philosophers in it, Renata and Dmitri, have spent the past week arguing about how to interpret a voicemail from a mutual friend.

Our friend Talia left one voicemail the night before losing cell service for the rest of a solo backpacking trip: "Don't call the sublet company about the leak — I already told them it's fine, just leave the deposit under the mat like we agreed." Two facts sit awkwardly with this: Talia found her sublet through a friend-of-a-friend, with no company involved, and none of us has heard of any deposit-under-the-mat plan. She won't be reachable for weeks, so we can't just ask her.

Renata says the principle of charity requires finding a reading on which Talia's beliefs and words come out true and reasonable together, so we should conclude there's a sublet company and an agreement we simply weren't told about, rather than assume she's confused about her own arrangement. Dmitri objects that this misapplies Davidson: charity licenses attributing mostly true, rational beliefs, but any specific error still needs independent motivation from something we already know — inventing a company and an agreement from nothing isn't charity, it's rigging the interpretation so that nothing could count against it.

Renata answers that Dmitri's own reading — Talia confused about who she's actually subletting from, misremembering a plan that was never made — attributes a stranger error than a single unknown company would, and that charity should just favor whichever hypothesis is the smaller departure from rationality. Dmitri replies that "smaller departure" can't be settled by counting entities: mistaking an informal arrangement for "the company" is a mundane, well-attested kind of slip, while Renata's reading requires an entire undocumented history that nothing else about the trip supports — and rewarding that kind of unmotivated economy is exactly what Davidson's constraint was meant to rule out.

Go through the exchange move by move, say who has the better of each step, and close with a verdict on whether either of them is actually applying Davidson's principle correctly.
