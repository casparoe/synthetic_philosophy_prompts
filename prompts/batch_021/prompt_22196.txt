I'm assembling a small collection of short fiction in which each story is built around a single philosophical puzzle, and I've stalled badly on the entry for verbal disputes in metaontology: the idea, developed by Eli Hirsch and given its sharpest contemporary statement by David Chalmers, that many disagreements which look like disputes about what exists — are there mereological sums, is a statue identical to the clay that constitutes it — may really be disagreements about how to use words like "object" or "same," with no further fact left over once each side's terms are properly disambiguated. I've spent the better part of a year reading around the topic (Hirsch on quantifier variance, Chalmers's paper on the subject, and then Ted Sider pushing back with the claim that at most one of two rival vocabularies could actually carve reality at its joints), and I've become fascinated by the material without finding a way to dramatize it that isn't just two characters taking turns lecturing each other.

Here is the situation I keep returning to. Two communities descend from a single people divided by a civil war a few generations back; the treaty that ended the fighting fixed their shared border at a river, with a small island shrine in mid-channel held in trust for pilgrims from both sides. A severe flood some years ago sent the river into a new channel a few miles off — gradually enough by some accounts and suddenly enough by others that even the old legal distinction between accretion and avulsion doesn't cleanly settle which rule should apply — and the shrine now sits on dry ground annexed to one side's bank. One community's customary law identifies the border with wherever the water presently runs; the other identifies it with the original bed, holding that what now flows a few miles east is, properly speaking, a different river that has simply inherited the old name. Both sides agree on every fact about what the water has done and when. I want a young translator attached to the negotiating delegations to work out, over the course of the talks, that the dispute over whether it is "the same river" can be dissolved — that once each side's term is properly disambiguated, no residual disagreement about the river survives translation — and then to discover that dissolving it does not obviously settle who should have the shrine, since the real quarrel may have been about entitlement all along, dressed only in metaphysical clothing.

Could you write this story in full, somewhere in the 1,500-to-2,500-word range? I'd like the philosophy to be earned rather than announced: the reader should be able to reconstruct the Hirsch/Chalmers-style argument from watching what the translator actually does with the two sides' language, not from being told about it. And I'd rather you left standing, rather than resolved, the harder question of whether Sider is right that something real was at stake underneath the verbal one all along — that's exactly the point where my own draft keeps collapsing, and I'd like to see whether it can be left open without the story itself feeling unfinished.
