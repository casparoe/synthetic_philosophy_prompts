I'm a PhD student in history and philosophy of science, and every other week a couple of physics grad students crash our reading group, which is usually great but this time it derailed a bit. We'd just read the Postscript to The Structure of Scientific Revolutions for a session on incommensurability, and afterwards over beers my friend Owen (physics) and my cohort-mate Farah (philosophy) got into an argument that never really got resolved, and honestly both of them made points I couldn't knock down.

(1) Owen opened by saying the whole incommensurability thing is overhyped: special relativity reduces to Newtonian mechanics at low velocities, its literally the first limit you take in a mechanics course, so calling Newton and Einstein "different paradigms" that can't be compared is philosophers dramatizing what's really just cumulative refinement. (2) Farah said that's exactly the example Kuhn uses against him: Newtonian mass is a fixed, conserved property of a body, relativistic mass depends on velocity and converts into energy, so the two "masses" aren't the same concept measured to different precision, they're different concepts, and showing that one "reduces to" the other numerically already borrows Einstein's framework to redescribe Newton's — you don't get to use the successor theory to prove there was no real break. (3) Owen conceded the mass point but said that just shows incommensurability is a narrow claim about how a handful of terms shift meaning, not the radical "whole paradigms are incomparable" thesis people take Kuhn to be selling, and that Kuhn spent the rest of his career insisting he wasn't a relativist. (4) Farah agreed Kuhn denied being a relativist, but said look at what he offered instead: five values — accuracy, consistency, scope, simplicity, fruitfulness — that different scientists can legitimately weigh differently and still count as rational, which isn't a refutation of relativism so much as relativism wearing a lab coat, since it lets two people looking at identical evidence land on opposite paradigms with neither one being wrong. (5) Owen shot back that value-pluralism isn't "anything goes": the values still rule plenty of things out (astrology flunks accuracy, endlessly patched epicycles flunk simplicity and fruitfulness against Kepler), and demanding a mechanical algorithm before you'll call theory choice "rational" is a standard no account of confirmation meets, Bayesian or Popperian included. (6) Farah's comeback was that this doesn't rescue Kuhn, it just relocates the problem: admitting that equally rational people can permissibly land on different paradigms given the same evidence is precisely the "soft" relativism he spent decades denying he held.

We had to break it up for someone's defense practice talk before anyone budged. Could you go through moves (1) through (6) and say who's got the better of it at each step, rather than just declaring an overall winner? A score from -2 (clearly off base) to +2 (clearly right) for each move, with a sentence or two of justification, would be perfect. And if there's something substantive both of them are missing — including stuff Kuhn himself never fully squared away — I'd genuinely like to hear it, since this is basically the terrain my prospectus is supposed to cover and right now all I've got is beer-fueled vibes instead of an argument.
