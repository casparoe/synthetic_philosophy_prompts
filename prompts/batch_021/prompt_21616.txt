I'm part of a small meetup group that grew out of the local rationalist/EA scene, and I've been reading LessWrong on and off since the Sequences were still being actively posted. At our most recent 'giving season' discussion, our friend Mateo tried to convince the group to redirect most of their marginal charitable giving away from a proven global-health charity -- the kind that tops most of the standard evaluators' lists -- and toward a small, speculative research outfit that studies how humanity could keep growing food if a catastrophe (a supervolcano, a nuclear winter, that sort of thing) blocked out the sun for a few years. Our friend Yuki pushed back hard, and the two of them went several rounds without either backing down. Nobody at the table could agree afterward on who had actually gotten the better of it, so I'm hoping you can adjudicate.

(1) Mateo's argument was straightforward expected-value reasoning: even if the annual probability of the relevant catastrophe is very low, the number of lives at stake if it happens is enormous, and multiplying the two out gives a far higher expected value than the safe, well-evidenced charity, so more of the marginal dollar should follow the speculative option. (2) Yuki objected that this is structurally just Pascal's Mugging with a grant proposal standing in for the man on the street corner: any argument of the form 'tiny probability times huge number beats your sure thing' should make us suspicious of the argument rather than persuaded by its conclusion, whatever the specific numbers turn out to be. (3) Mateo replied that you can't adopt a blanket policy of distrusting small-probability, high-stakes reasoning, since that would also rule out taking nuclear war or engineered pandemics seriously -- both low annual probability, both civilization-scale in their stakes -- and everyone at the table already agreed those deserve real resources. (4) Yuki refined her point: the issue isn't smallness of probability as such, it's that in mugging-type cases both the probability and the payoff are numbers supplied by whoever is making the pitch, with no outside anchor -- no track record, no reference class, nothing that could ever have come back and falsified the estimate -- whereas nuclear risk at least has some historical and expert-elicited grounding. (5) Mateo shot back that this criterion fits every important new risk before anyone had built a track record for it -- the first person to worry seriously about nuclear winter, or about a genuinely novel pathogen, looked exactly like this too -- so Yuki's standard quietly guarantees that we only ever fund yesterday's problem. (6) Yuki then invoked what she called a 'leverage penalty': rather than refusing to multiply, you should also lower your estimate that you personally happen to be one of the tiny number of people positioned to affect that many lives, roughly in proportion to how many lives are on the table, since if the claim were true a great many people could make the identical claim about themselves -- but she immediately admitted that this same penalty seems to fall just as hard on someone who really is a rare, well-placed epidemiologist staring at a real emerging outbreak, so it can't actually be distinguishing genuine long-shot leverage from fake long-shot leverage. Mateo took this as a concession that the penalty is just a heuristic rigged to output whichever answer you wanted going in, and the two of them left it there.

Could you go through this move by move rather than just declaring an overall winner?
