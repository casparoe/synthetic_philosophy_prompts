I'm reporting a feature on the statistical side of the replication crisis, and I keep running into an unsettling claim from the reform camp: that in very large studies — the biobank-scale genetic association studies and large multi-lab replication projects that are now common — a result can clear the p < .05 threshold while a Bayesian analysis of the identical data assigns the null hypothesis a high posterior probability, sometimes overwhelmingly so. I gather this is a known phenomenon called the Jeffreys–Lindley paradox, and I can follow the algebra that produces it, but every write-up I've found just presents the formula rather than building intuition for it. Could you explain, without leaning on notation, why it is specifically the growth of sample size that drives the two approaches apart rather than bringing them into agreement, and why statisticians tend to locate the "problem" in the diffuseness of the prior under the alternative hypothesis rather than in the p-value itself? I need to be able to walk a general readership through the gist without hand-waving.
