A friend of mine works at a company where the manager, notorious for turning in reports late, recently spent an entire meeting scolding a junior colleague over a missed deadline. When my friend described the scene afterward, half the group's reaction was that the manager had "no right" to say anything, and that reaction has been nagging at me ever since: it doesn't seem obviously right, but it doesn't seem obviously wrong either. There's a real difference between saying "your accusation is false" and saying "your accusation might well be true, but you specifically don't get to be the one making it," and the complaint about hypocrisy is clearly aiming at the second sort of thing. What I can't work out on my own is what would actually have to be true for someone to lose that kind of standing to blame -- I've seen the phrase used online, though I'm not sure whether it's an established term or just something people made up -- as opposed to the manager merely being a graceless or irritating messenger who still happens to be right.

Could you write me a dialogue between two coworkers who were both in that meeting and end up arguing about it afterward, taking opposite sides on the underlying question? One should hold that a hypocrite genuinely loses, or at least seriously weakens, their standing to blame someone else for a fault they share, and should try to pin down exactly what grounds that loss: is it about fairness between people meant to be held to the same rule, about implicitly exempting yourself while enforcing the rule on others, about the way one's own conduct undercuts the message, or something else again? The other should deny this, holding that having standing to blame depends on facts such as whether the wrongdoing actually occurred and whether the blamer has some legitimate relationship to it, rather than on the blamer's own track record, and should resist the idea that "you're one to talk" is ever more than a rhetorical way of dodging the original accusation. I would like the two of them to genuinely engage with each other's strongest moves rather than simply restate their own view -- for instance, I would like to see how the second coworker handles a case where the hypocrite explicitly claims to be an exception to the rule, as opposed to a case where they are simply weak-willed and would readily admit as much if pressed, and how the first coworker answers the worry that their view lets people off the hook whenever their critic also happens to be flawed, which, on reflection, is true of almost everybody to some degree. The conversation does not need to end in agreement, so long as both positions have been pushed as far as they can go.
