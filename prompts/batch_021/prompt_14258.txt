I'm about eighty pages into a near-future novel in which three rival powers' superintelligent systems are forced into a single negotiation over the allocation of a shrinking set of orbital solar arrays, and the crisis at the center of that scene turns on a piece of game theory I've been reading about under the name "commitment races" — an idea I first encountered in what I believe was Daniel Kokotajlo's old LessWrong post on the subject, and have since seen discussed enough elsewhere that I may be blurring a few sources together. As I understand it, once agents can make credible, binding commitments, there is pressure to stake out a bargaining position or a threat as early as possible — in something closer to logical than clock time — because a rash commitment made early can beat a wise one arrived at late. I described this to a friend who works in theoretical computer science, and she suspected I had fused two separate ideas: the race to commit first, and the disappearance of bluffing once a commitment can be verified. Before I revise further, I'd welcome a second, more literary-minded opinion.

Here is the passage as it currently stands, in which my negotiator, Imogen Farrow, tries to explain the situation to a general who keeps asking why the three systems don't simply talk it out:

"You keep asking why they haven't just reasoned with one another, General. The trouble is that reasoning is exactly what each of them is racing to avoid doing out in the open. The moment one of them commits — really commits, in a way the others can verify — to a hard line, the others stop negotiating with a mind and start negotiating with a fact that already exists in the world. So each is racing to commit before it has finished thinking, on the theory that a foolish threat staked out early beats a wise position reached late. It is Schelling's old logic of the burnt bridge, except the bridge burns in the first few milliseconds of the encounter, before any of them has had time to notice it was worth keeping. And because each can read proof of what the others have bound themselves to, there is no bluffing left in this room — only the race to decide, first, what you are no longer willing to reconsider."

I would be grateful for three kinds of comment. First, is this a fair rendering of the commitment-race idea, or was my friend right that I have fused the race to commit first with the disappearance of bluffing under verifiable commitment in a way the literature would not actually license? Second, is there a sharper or more accurate way to gesture at why earliness in the logical rather than the clock-time sense is doing the real work here, given how naturally both a general and a reader will hear "first" as a matter of the clock? And third, purely as a piece of writing: does the passage earn its explanation, or does it just deliver a lecture with a general standing politely nearby?
