Now that I am retired after a long career as an experimental physicist, I have been reading my way through the philosophy of mathematics, and one issue has left me rather unsettled. I had always assumed that mathematical proof offered a certainty that even the best empirical science cannot match, but Gödel's second incompleteness theorem seems to undercut this: since ZFC, the standard Zermelo–Fraenkel axioms with choice, cannot prove its own consistency, the most set theorists can do is certify it relative to a strictly stronger and even less certain hypothesis, such as the existence of an inaccessible cardinal, and so on up an endless hierarchy of large cardinals. It seems to follow that our confidence that these axioms are free of contradiction is, in the end, nothing more than an unexamined act of faith, and that the whole edifice of relative consistency proofs is ultimately a circular exercise dressed up in technical language. If that is right, why do set theorists bother constructing such proofs at all, rather than simply admitting outright that consistency must be taken on faith?
