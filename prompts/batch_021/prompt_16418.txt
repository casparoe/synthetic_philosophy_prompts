Random rabbit hole from this week: I was talking to a philosopher friend after an AI-and-personal-identity panel, and she got me sidetracked on something called the "special composition question," which is apparently THE central puzzle in mereology (the study of parts and wholes) — basically, given some objects, under what conditions do they compose some further, bigger object? Like, the atoms in my keyboard — do they really add up to one single further thing, "the keyboard," or is that just a convenient fiction and really there's just a bunch of particles arranged keyboard-wise? She said the two extreme answers on offer are universalism (literally any objects whatsoever compose a further thing, no matter how scattered or random — so there's technically a single object made of my left earbud and Big Ben) and nihilism (composition never happens, full stop — no chairs, cars, or planets, only simples arranged in various ways). And then van Inwagen, who I gather coined the question in the first place, has this much weirder middle position where the only composite objects that actually exist are living organisms, meaning you and your cat are real unified things but the chair you're sat on, strictly speaking, isn't.

I can't stop thinking about how disorienting it'd be to actually perceive the world according to one of these views, and it reminded me a bit of the kind of premise Greg Egan or Ted Chiang would run with. So could you write me a short story — a page or so, it doesnt need to be long — that takes one of these three positions (universalism, nihilism, or van Inwagen's organisms-only view) and actually dramatizes it, the way something like Permutation City dramatizes a weird theory of personal identity? I don't want a generic "character realizes reality is an illusion" story — I want the specific metaphysical flavor of whichever position you pick to genuinely drive the character's situation and dilemma, not just be a backdrop. Pick whichever of the three seems like it'd make for the richest story.
