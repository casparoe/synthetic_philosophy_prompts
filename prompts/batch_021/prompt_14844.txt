so in my game theory class we just finished the unit on games with incomplete information and we spent a while on Aumann's "agreeing to disagree" result. I get why the math works -- if two people share a common prior and their posteriors on some event end up being common knowledge, those posteriors have to be identical, otherwise you get a contradiction from each side updating on the fact that the other's posterior is what it is. fine. but what's bugging me is the status of the common prior assumption itself, which the whole theorem (and honestly a ton of the info econ we've covered, like the no-trade results) leans on so heavily.

here's my issue: from a plain subjectivist bayesian standpoint (savage, de finetti, that whole tradition) your prior is basically just whatever coherent distribution you happen to start with -- theres no further rationality constraint on where it comes from, two perfectly rational people can have totally different priors over the same state space and neither one is making a mistake. but CPA bolts on this extra thing where everyone's beliefs have to bottom out in literally the same prior, and any disagreement has to come entirely from differences in private information. our professor described this as basically harmless bookkeeping (like it's just saying "assume everyone started equally ignorant"), but then two classes later was talking about the Harsanyi doctrine and no-trade theorems as if CPA were actually doing serious rationality work -- like real persistent disagreement between people who've both seen each other's evidence is basically irrational/impossible. those two framings feel like they're in tension. like either CPA is a contentless modeling convention (in which case idk why we'd get such a strong "you can't really disagree" conclusion out of it) or it's a substantive claim about what rational priors have to look like, in which case it needs its own justification, since nothing in bayesian coherence alone forces your prior to match anyone else's.

it also seems related to the fact that actual markets are full of speculative trading that the no-trade theorem says basically shouldn't happen if everyone's rational and started from the same prior -- so people just kind of shrug and say "well, priors must differ in practice," but if differing priors is always available as an explanation, doesn't that make the original "no rational disagreement" result kind of unfalsifiable? I feel like I'm missing the standard move economists/philosophers make to resolve this, or maybe it's genuinely still a live sore point in the literature. would love an actual breakdown of what's going on here rather than just "CPA is a simplifying assumption," since that's the part I don't buy.
