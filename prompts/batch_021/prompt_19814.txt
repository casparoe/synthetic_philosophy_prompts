I spent my career in statistical mechanics, and in retirement I have been reading my way through the philosophical literature on probability and rational choice. One small puzzle keeps nagging at me. In 1738, Daniel Bernoulli resolved the St. Petersburg paradox by arguing that a rational agent should evaluate a single gamble by the expected logarithm of resulting wealth, rather than by its expected monetary value, on the grounds that money carries diminishing marginal utility. Two centuries later, the Bell Labs information theorist John Kelly arrived at the very same logarithmic rule for bet-sizing, but on entirely different grounds: not diminishing marginal utility, but maximization of the almost-sure long-run growth rate of wealth under repeated play. Today's advocates of "geometric rationality" want to elevate Kelly's growth-rate argument - together with the ensemble-versus-time-average distinction I recognize from my own field - into a general rival to expected-utility theory itself; critics in the tradition of Paul Samuelson maintain that this move illicitly treats one particular utility function as though it were uniquely rational, and mistakes a fact about long sequences of bets for a constraint on any single decision.

If Bernoulli were briefed on this entire dispute, whose side do you think he would take, and why? Ground your answer in what his 1738 argument actually presupposes and requires, rather than in the mere coincidence that both arguments recommend the same formula.
