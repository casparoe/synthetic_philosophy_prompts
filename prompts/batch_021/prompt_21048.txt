My grandmother died in early spring, and a few weeks later my aunt — who has never struck any of us as credulous — arranged for the family to sit with a medium who claimed to be relaying messages from her. I went along mostly out of politeness, but came away unsettled by how much comfort it gave people I love, and I've spent the months since trying to think the matter through properly rather than simply dismissing it. That reading led me, by a route I didn't expect, back to Henry Sidgwick, whom I had previously encountered only in a university ethics course as the fastidious Cambridge utilitarian responsible for The Methods of Ethics and its unresolved "dualism of practical reason." It turns out he was a founder and the first president of the Society for Psychical Research when it was established in 1882, and stayed closely involved in testing mediums and investigating apparitions and telepathy right up until his death in 1900, all without, by his own account, ever finding evidence he considered adequate.

What I cannot square is how this fits with the reputation he has in every ethics survey as the most scrupulous, self-critical moral philosopher of his generation. My working assumption has been that the psychical research was really a separate chapter of his life — a private, rather sentimental hope about survival after death that ran alongside his professional philosophy but had no real bearing on the actual arguments of the Methods, and that two decades of essentially null results are best read as a poignant but philosophically inconsequential footnote to an otherwise rigorous career. Before I try to explain any of this to my aunt, I would like to know whether that is a fair way to understand Sidgwick's psychical research, or whether I am missing something about how it actually connects to his ethics.
