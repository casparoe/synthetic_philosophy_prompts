I'm working on a longread about anthropic reasoning and fell down a rabbit hole this week rereading the doomsday argument in Bostrom's *Anthropic Bias*. Everyone already knows the pop version (you're basically a random sample of all humans who'll ever live, so your birth rank being what it is should shift your beliefs toward doom coming sooner rather than later), but what I hadn't clocked until now is how the fight between the self-sampling assumption and the self-indication assumption basically eats that whole argument alive. If you weight hypotheses by how many observers they predict (SIA), the doomsday shift cancels out completely and the argument just dies -- but then you're stuck defending Bostrom's "presumptuous philosopher," who can apparently settle open questions in cosmology from his armchair just by noting that theories predicting more observers are automatically more probable, which cant be right either. Meanwhile the SSA side has its own open wound, the reference class problem, where nobody can say with a straight face who actually counts as "us" for sampling purposes. I've been annoying my partner with this over dinner all week lol.

Could you write me a dialogue that does justice to this fight? Two people run into each other at the hotel bar after a conference talk on observation selection effects -- one of them is fully committed to the doomsday argument via the self-sampling assumption and thinks the presumptuous philosopher is a price worth paying to avoid something worse; the other has adopted the self-indication assumption specifically because it kills the doomsday conclusion, and thinks the reference class problem shows SSA was ad hoc from the start. I want them actually going after each other's strongest version of the position -- pushing on the presumptuous philosopher, the reference class, maybe how each side handles a case like Bostrom's "Adam and Eve" scenarios -- rather than just restating their own axioms past one another. Neither should be a strawman, and neither needs to win; I'd rather they both leave a little less sure of their own footing than they arrived.
