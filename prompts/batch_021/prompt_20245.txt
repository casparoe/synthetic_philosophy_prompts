In my school's game theory club, we have been experimenting with the centipede game: two players alternate between "passing," which grows a shared pot, and "taking," which ends the game immediately and keeps the larger share for the taker, over a fixed and publicly known number of rounds. Our textbook presents the backward-induction solution as following necessarily from common knowledge of rationality: each player reasons from the final round backward, concludes that the other would always take if given the chance, and therefore takes at the very first opportunity instead. When our club actually played the game for modest cash stakes about fifteen times, almost no one took on the first move, and most pairs passed for several rounds before someone finally stopped. Since the textbook treats "take immediately" as the one outcome that must follow whenever both players are rational and each knows the other is rational, doesn't this simply show that ordinary people are less rational than economic theory assumes, and that the backward-induction outcome should be treated as the fixed benchmark against which every real departure from it counts as an error?
