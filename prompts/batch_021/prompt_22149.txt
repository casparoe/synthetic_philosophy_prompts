I've spent the last few weekends going down a rabbit hole on panpsychism, partly triggered by a conversation with a friend who's finishing her PhD in philosophy of mind, and partly because the view keeps coming up tangentially in things I read about integrated information theory and machine consciousness, which is closer to my day job — I work on distributed systems and, more recently, some ML infrastructure. The part of the debate that grabbed me is panpsychism's central internal difficulty, usually called the combination problem: if fundamental physical entities like electrons or quarks have simple micro-experiences, what explains how enough of them, wired up the right way, ever add up to one unified subject having a single integrated experience — mine, right now — rather than just a crowd of separate micro-experiences sitting next to one another?

I sketched out some notes trying to see whether an analogy from my own field actually dissolves this "subject-summing" problem, as I gather it's usually called, rather than just restating it in new vocabulary, and I'd like your honest comments on whether the move earns its keep philosophically. The core of it: in distributed systems you routinely get robust global behavior — an elected leader in Raft or Paxos, a flock's shared heading in a boids simulation — out of many components that each only see local state and follow local rules; no component holds, or needs to hold, the global fact, and nobody thinks the "flock's heading" is some further mysterious entity hovering over the birds that needs its own separate explanation. It's just a legitimate higher-level description of how the parts are organized. My tentative thought was that the same move dissolves the subject-combination problem: my unified experience right now isn't a further thing the micro-experiences have to add up to or generate, over and above themselves; it's just the right higher-level description of a great many micro-experiences organized in a specific way (maybe whatever the neural-synchrony story turns out to be is the relevant "protocol"). I ran a version of this by my friend and she said something like "that's an old move, and it doesn't actually work," but didn't have time to explain why. I also gather that Goff's "phenomenal bonding" proposal is meant to close the same gap in a very different, non-deflationary way, and that even he isn't fully happy with it, which makes me suspect my flock analogy is quietly smuggling in something illegitimate rather than solving anything.

Could you go through this point by point: (1) whether my distributed-systems analogy is doing genuine philosophical work here or just relocating the problem under a new name; (2) exactly where it breaks down, if it does, relative to the way the subject-combination, quality-combination, and structure-combination versions of the problem are usually distinguished; and (3) whether this is a recognizable move in the existing literature, and if so, what it's typically called and what the standard reply to it is. I'd rather you point out the holes in my reasoning directly than be diplomatic about it.
