My mother, a mathematical physicist who spent most of her forty-year career working on random matrix theory, died three weeks ago after a short illness, and her department is holding a memorial service next week at which I've been asked to give the eulogy -- partly because I'm the one who writes for a living (I've profiled her work twice for the magazine I cover science for), and partly, I suspect, because everyone else in the family is more afraid than I am of getting the physics wrong in front of her colleagues. She was the person who, at any dinner party, could be relied on to bring up her favorite fact in all of mathematics: that when number theorists finally computed enormous numbers of zeros of the Riemann zeta function, their spacing turned out to follow, statistically, almost exactly the law that Eugene Wigner had proposed decades earlier for the energy levels of heavy atomic nuclei -- a connection nobody has ever fully proven but which has been confirmed numerically to a degree she found almost embarrassing. She considered it the purest illustration going of what Wigner meant, in his 1960 essay, by the "unreasonable effectiveness" of mathematics, and she brought it up more, not less, as she got older.

The trouble is that she held extremely specific and strongly defended opinions about what this fact does and doesn't show, and she was known to correct other people's version of it, sometimes from the audience at their own talks. She thought it simply careless to treat the coincidence as evidence for mathematical Platonism, or for any deeper cosmic order, and she'd said so plainly enough times -- including in an email to me after almost exactly this happened at a colleague's retirement dinner -- that I know she would not want her own memorial to be the place it finally gets said about her, of all people. But she was just as dismissive of colleagues who waved the whole thing away as "just universality": showing that unrelated systems fall into a handful of the same statistical classes, she'd say, only moves the question up one level, since nobody has a good account of why nature and the primes should both be so stingy with their mathematics to begin with. Her stock answer to any journalist, including me, who reached for the word "miracle" was that unreasonable was Wigner's word, not hers, and that even he had meant it more as a worry than a compliment. Complicating things further: Warren, her closest friend since graduate school and a committed Platonist who has spent thirty years citing her own results as his best evidence without ever quite winning her forgiveness for it, will be sitting in the front row.

The rest of the room splits cleanly in two: colleagues and former students -- physicists and mathematicians who don't even agree amongst themselves on how the story should be told -- who will catch immediately if I flatten or fudge any of this; and family and old friends who have never heard of the Riemann zeta function and are there to grieve my mother, not to sit through a physics lecture in her honor. Could you draft the eulogy for me? I'm aiming for around seven minutes, so somewhere near nine hundred words. It needs to work first and foremost as a eulogy -- warm and specific, about her rather than about her ideas -- while still getting her actual position right: resisting both the mystical reading and the tidy deflationary one, rather than reaching for whichever makes the better closing line. If there's a line in there that makes her old students and Warren quietly smile without anyone else feeling excluded, that's the assignment done properly.
