I am currently taking an aesthetics seminar that has spent the last few weeks on the paradox of fiction: the puzzle of how we can be moved to genuine fear, grief, or pity by characters and events we know perfectly well do not exist, as in Radford's well-known example of feeling afraid for Anna Karenina. We went through the usual range of responses to it -- the naive "willing suspension of disbelief" view, Walton's make-believe theory, on which what we undergo are only "quasi-emotions" generated within a game of pretense, and rival "thought theories," on which merely entertaining a proposition, without believing it, is already enough to produce a genuine emotion. Since then I keep noticing versions of this dispute break out whenever people argue about whether they were "really" frightened or "really" sad about something fictional, and a fairly detailed version of it played out between two friends of mine this past weekend.

After a horror movie, my friend Nadia said (1) that she had genuinely been afraid: her pulse was racing, she jumped twice in her seat, and she had trouble falling asleep afterward, so by any reasonable standard this counted as real fear. Colin replied (2) that real fear is a response to a believed danger, that Nadia knew the entire time she was sitting safely in a theater with popcorn, and that what she had was just fear's bodily symptoms firing without the belief that is supposed to produce them -- which counts as real fear no more than a photograph of a fire counts as something hot. Nadia answered (3) that this proves too much, since a rubber spider dangled in front of someone with arachnophobia produces the very same involuntary terror even after the person has been shown, up close, that it is fake, and nobody hesitates to call that real fear. Colin came back (4) that the cases are not parallel: with the spider, some fast perceptual system is still registering "spider" before slower, better-informed belief can catch up, so a genuine if short-lived and irrational belief is doing the causal work, whereas nothing in the theater seat momentarily represented the killer as actually present. Nadia pointed out (5) that this fast-system story might cover a single jump scare, but not the hour of dread that built steadily through the film's second half, nor the three nights afterward when she kept the hallway light on -- there was no split-second misperception sustaining any of that, she knew the whole time it was fiction, and yet the state matched fear in every respect she cared about. Colin's closing move (6) was that an emotion is individuated by what it represents as true, not merely by its downstream effects on mood and behavior, and that collapsing this distinction would also force us to say that the relief someone feels on learning that a rumor of a friend's death was false is just a relabeling of the grief that preceded it -- when the two states obviously feel, and function, quite differently.
