Been down a rabbit hole this week reporting on these new "multi-agent" AI setups -- you spin up a handful of seperate LLM instances, give each one a role (planner, coder, critic, whatever) and have them pass messages back and forth until the task gets done "together." My editor keeps asking whether that's real teamwork or just a marketing gloss on a bunch of models doing their own thing in sequence, so I called a cognitive scientist for a sanity check and she pointed me at this whole "collective intentionality" literature in philosophy of action that I had no idea existed. From what I can piece together there's a real fight between people like Michael Bratman, who thinks shared/joint intention just IS a structure of individual intentions (his "I intend that we J" business) held together by mutual responsiveness, meshing sub-plans, and common knowledge -- no extra ingredient required -- versus people like Margaret Gilbert, Raimo Tuomela, and John Searle, who in different ways think that cant be the whole story, because there's some distinctively plural or "we-mode" element that doesn't reduce to individual intentions plus beliefs about each other (Gilbert's favorite example is that two people just agreeing to walk together generates obligations -- like noticing if your companion falls behind -- that seem to come out of nowhere on the individualist picture; Tuomela frames the same worry as "we-mode" vs. "I-mode" reasoning; Searle just says we-intentions are primitive and that's that).

Anyway, I'd love an essay that actually settles this for me. Lay out the strongest version of the reductive, Bratman-style picture, then the strongest version of the "you need something sui generis" camp, and run both through a few of the classic hard cases people throw at each other -- a rioting mob, an orchestra mid-performance, an assembly line where the foreman just barks orders and nobody's sub-plans are being negotiated in any meaningful sense. Then actually land somewhere instead of concluding "both sides have a point" -- a qualified verdict like "reductive accounts hold up fine for small, symmetric, cooperative cases but break down once you add hierarchy or hostility" totally counts, as long as you argue for it rather than just asserting it.
