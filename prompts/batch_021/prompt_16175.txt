My older sister is finishing her PhD in philosophy this spring, and in the same season she is marrying her partner of six years, Marcus, who has never taken a philosophy course in his life. I am a junior in high school, and most of what I know about philosophy so far comes from reading the drafts she leaves around the house. As her only sibling, though, I have been asked to give the toast at the wedding reception, and I would like it to actually engage with the problem her dissertation addresses rather than merely mention it in passing for an easy laugh.

Her dissertation concerns the "wrong kind of reasons" problem for fitting-attitude accounts of value: the family of theories, descending from Scanlon's "buck-passing" account of goodness, which hold that calling something good really just means it has properties that give us reason to want, admire, or pursue it. The difficulty these theories face is that a pro-attitude like admiration or desire can apparently be given a reason by circumstances that have nothing to do with whether its object is actually good, the standard illustration being a demon who will torture you unless you come to genuinely value a saucer of mud (I believe the paper that introduces this case literally has "demon" in its title, though I could not tell you which journal it appeared in). Philosophers have spent a great deal of effort trying to specify exactly what separates such "wrong kind" reasons from the "right kind" ones that are supposed to track an object's real merits, and I would like that distinction to serve as the spine of the toast: something that moves from a joke about whether my sister married Marcus for the right kind of reasons, or was essentially threatened with the philosophical equivalent of a saucer of mud, toward a sincere claim about what a right-kind reason for loving someone would actually look like. The complication is that roughly a third of the guests belong to her department and will notice immediately if I get the distinction wrong or use it as mere decoration, while everyone else, including Marcus's entire family, has never encountered the phrase "fitting attitude" and needs the joke to still function as a joke.

Could you write this toast for me? I am picturing a toast of reception length, roughly two to three minutes, containing at least one line that would make the philosophers in the room laugh out loud while working perfectly well for someone with no background in metaethics. My sister has also told me, half in jest, that she will never forgive me if the philosophy turns out to be wrong, so I would rather get that part right than earn an easy laugh, and in either case I want the whole thing to read as a wedding toast rather than a philosophy lecture that happens to end with a champagne glass.
