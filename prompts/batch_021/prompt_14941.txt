ok so a side character in the historical novel im drafting works at Otto Neurath's picture-statistics outfit in Vienna in the late 20s, and I fell down a research hole on Isotype and ended up writing a page of notes that turned into more of a mini essay about the thing that actually interests me philosophically in it. can you read it and tell me: (1) whether im getting the actual facts right -- I THINK the rule was you show more of something by repeating the same little icon over and over instead of blowing up one icon bigger, because a bigger icon reads as way more than it should, and I think Marie Reidemeister's job title in the workshop was literally "transformer" -- but im going off memory here and could easily have both wrong; (2) whether my big claim, that Isotype keeps failing at exactly the stuff novels are made of, is an actual observation about the system's limits or just a flattering analogy I've talked myself into; and (3) whether im being unfair to Neurath, since I have this nagging feeling he already knew pictures couldn't do everything and said so himself, in which case my character's whole disillusionment arc is built on a strawman.

here's the rough bit:

Isotype was never meant to replace language, just sit next to it -- Neurath's own pitch, near as I can recall, was that a little repeated icon of a coal wagon reads the same whether you speak German or Polish, so pictures could get across a border words couldn't. but the method only works by flattening a fact into something countable and repeatable: X little identical men equal X million people, and no single man is allowed to be bigger, sadder, or more tired than the others -- that's the whole point of "type" in Isotype. and that's exactly the move a novel won't make. a novel wants the one tired man, wants to say he wasn't tired yesterday and isn't sure himself if he's tired or just performing tired for his wife. Isotype has no icon for "not," no icon for "used to," no icon for "he claims." my character starts out thrilled by the discipline of the wordless chart and ends the book smuggling all the disallowed stuff, tense, doubt, irony, back in through the captions.

tell me where this is thin, and don't be nice about it.
