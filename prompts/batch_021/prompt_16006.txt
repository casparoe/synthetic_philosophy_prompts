I'm a novelist and the current chair of the five-member jury for the Fenwick Prize, a modest but locally well-regarded award our regional writers' guild gives each year to a member whose work or conduct has shown, in the founding language nobody has bothered to update, "moral courage in the practice of letters." Past winners have mostly been reporters-turned-novelists who took real professional risks to expose something true. This year's presumptive winner is a memoirist, Adeline Marsh, who came forward last spring to admit that she had substantially exaggerated the hardship narrative in her application for the guild's need-based fellowship three years earlier, and who voluntarily repaid the stipend in full and asked to be named publicly as a cautionary example; it cost her a book contract and a good deal of standing in the local literary scene, and two jurors were moved almost to tears when we first discussed her nomination. Ten days ago, though, one of my fellow jurors learned that the guild's board had quietly commissioned an audit of old fellowship files that same month, and that Marsh's own writing group already knew about it — so her confession may have pre-empted an exposure she saw coming, rather than sprung from unprompted conscience, though she insists — and may sincerely believe — that she had been meaning to come forward for over a year regardless. The jury is now split three ways: two members think the timing is irrelevant since the act itself was still costly and true, two think a prize for moral courage cannot go to something done to get ahead of exposure, no matter how costly it turned out to be, and one wants to award it together with a public statement acknowledging the ambiguity. Underneath the practical question, I think, is whether a costly, truthful act like this one keeps whatever moral worth it had once you learn it was also strategically convenient — and I'll admit part of my interest here is personal, since a version of that exact dilemma has stalled a subplot in my own novel-in-progress for months; fiction, it turns out, lets a writer dodge the question in ways an actual jury cannot.

As chair, I have to circulate a short written decision memo before we vote at our next meeting, three weeks from now and ahead of the spring gala where the winner is announced. Please draft it. It should recommend one concrete outcome rather than a menu of options, and state plainly what would have to be true for the jury to revisit that recommendation later. Since this is the second time in three years the prize has run into exactly this snag, I'd also like a short, usable standing principle the jury could invoke next time instead of relitigating the whole question from scratch. Whatever position the memo takes, it should engage honestly with the strongest version of the position it rejects — two of my fellow jurors hold their view for serious reasons, not sentimentality, and a memo that flattens their view into a caricature will not survive the meeting.
