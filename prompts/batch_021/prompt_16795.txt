I'm the vice president of my high school's environmental club. Each spring we award a small conservation grant, raised through our plant sale, to one local field project, and the officers vote on the winner this Tuesday. This year's two finalists are a nesting-protection program for a shorebird everyone agrees is genuinely endangered, and a proposal to protect a small population of tiger beetles found on a single stretch of dune near our town. These beetles have a slightly different courtship display and show modest but measurable genetic divergence from the common tiger beetle species found across the rest of the state, yet they've never been formally described as their own species, and the biologists on our review panel are split on whether this amounts to a distinct lineage in the making or just normal variation within one species. Half the club thinks funding the beetle project would be premature and unscientific, since 'it isn't even a real species'; the other half thinks withholding support for that reason misses what actually makes a population worth protecting. It falls to me to write the recommendation memo the club will vote on -- please draft it. It should recommend a specific allocation of the grant (a split between the two projects is allowed, but justify the proportions), and it should take the 'not a real species' objection seriously rather than brushing past it.
