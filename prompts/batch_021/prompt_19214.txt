I've spent years reading rationalist blogs and forums, and one argument I keep running into whenever virtue ethics comes up is the situationist critique that Gilbert Harman and John Doris developed out of the social-psychology literature of the 1960s and 70s. The evidentiary core is always the same trio of studies: Milgram's obedience experiments, Zimbardo's Stanford Prison Experiment, and the Darley-Batson "Good Samaritan" study, where seminary students in a hurry stepped right past someone slumped in a doorway regardless of whether they'd just been asked to prepare a talk on the parable of the Good Samaritan. As I understand Doris's argument, these studies show that ordinary moral behavior is overwhelmingly explained by small situational variables — time pressure, perceived authority, assigned social role — rather than by anything like a stable, cross-situationally consistent character trait such as honesty or compassion. That seems to me to knock out the load-bearing wall of Aristotelian virtue ethics, which is built entirely around the idea that a person can cultivate a robust disposition that reliably produces the right action across different circumstances.

I raised this with a friend from my reading group who's done more formal philosophy than I have, and he brought up phronesis, the idea that practical wisdom is precisely what lets a virtuous person respond differently to different situations, so variable behavior across Milgram-style setups is exactly what virtue ethics would predict rather than evidence against it. That struck me as a bit too convenient, though: if the theory can retroactively "predict" literally any pattern of behavior a person exhibits by attributing it to context-sensitive practical wisdom, doesn't that make virtue ethics unfalsifiable rather than vindicated? So I'd like a second opinion: given that decades of social psychology seem to have empirically demonstrated that temporally stable, cross-situationally consistent character traits basically don't exist, isn't the intellectually honest conclusion that virtue ethics is a piece of pre-scientific folk psychology that hasn't survived contact with the data, rather than a live option for anyone who wants a psychologically realistic ethical theory?
