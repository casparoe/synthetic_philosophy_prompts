I'm assembling a collection of stories that use the mechanics of authorship itself — drafts, revision, the strange authority a novelist has over people who don't exist yet — to get at old theological puzzles from an angle other than the usual essay-and-footnote treatment. One piece has been resisting me for months. A philosopher friend brought up Molinist "middle knowledge" almost as an aside at dinner, and I haven't been able to put it down since: Molina's claim that God's knowledge unfolds in three logical moments — natural knowledge of every bare possibility, middle knowledge of exactly what every possible free creature would do in every possible circumstance (true logically prior to any decision to create anything at all, and not something even God gets to stipulate), and only afterward free knowledge of the one world actually brought about. Providence, on this account, isn't puppetry; it's God choosing which circumstances to actualize while already knowing, through that middle layer, how they would be freely met.

I'd like a short story, somewhere in the 1,000–2,000 word range, that uses a novelist and one of her characters to dramatize this precise structure, rather than the looser "author as god" conceit that shows up constantly in metafiction, where the author simply knows or controls everything that happens. The pressure point I want the story to work its way into, organically, is the objection that has dogged this view since the Jesuit–Dominican fights over grace and freedom in Molina's own lifetime: if the character doesn't exist yet and the scene has never been written, what could possibly make it true that they would choose one way rather than another there — and do they get any real say in that truth, short of the novelist simply making it up as she goes, which would collapse the whole three-tier scheme back into ordinary invention? I don't want the story to resolve this; I don't think it can be. I would like it to earn its ending by sitting inside that discomfort rather than shrugging toward "free will is a mystery." A minor character being quietly rewritten for a new edition might be one door into it, though you should feel free to invent your own premise, setting, and names, so long as the mechanics stay honest to the three-tier structure and resist sliding into simple fatalism, or a last-minute twist in which the novelist turns out to have been in control all along.
