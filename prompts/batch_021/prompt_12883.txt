I'm taking a graduate seminar on philosophy of mind, and we've just covered David Chalmers's proposal that, alongside the "hard problem" of consciousness, there is a "meta-problem": the task of explaining, in cognitive or computational terms, why we judge phenomenal consciousness to resist physical explanation in the first place. Illusionists, following Keith Frankish, tend to treat a full solution to the meta-problem as thereby dissolving the hard problem, since our sense that something further needs explaining is itself just one more judgment that cognitive science can, in principle, explain. Phenomenal realists resist this move: they hold that a complete account of why we make such judgments would still leave open the further question of why those judgments are accompanied by actual experience, rather than occurring "in the dark" with nothing it is like to make them. For my next seminar response, I'd like to submit a short dialogue instead of a memo. Could you write an exchange between an illusionist and a phenomenal realist who both grant that the meta-problem admits of a complete cognitive solution, and who then try to work out whether anything is left over for the hard problem once that solution is in hand — and, if they still disagree, whether their remaining dispute is substantive or merely verbal? Please make sure each side presses the other on specific points rather than simply restating their opening position.
