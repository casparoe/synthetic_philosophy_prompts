I've been reporting a longer piece on whether large language models have any bearing on old nativism-vs-empiricism debates in cognitive science, and that somehow turned into a full-on argument at dinner last week between two friends of mine -- Lena, who does computational cognitive modeling, and Omar, who's ABD in philosophy and has spent way too long down the Fodor rabbit hole. I'd brought up one of Fodor's classic arguments for why thought has to run on an innate, language-like system of representation in the first place: that you can't actually learn a new concept, because learning one means testing a hypothesis about what falls under it, and stating that hypothesis already requires having the concept's components -- so even something like the concept CARBURETOR would technically have to be innate. Lena and Omar just went at it for like 40 minutes and I couldn't referee it, so I'm hoping you can.

Lena's first move was that the CARBURETOR conclusion is basically a reductio: nobody seriously thinks infants carry around a dormant concept for a mid-century auto part just waiting to be switched on, so one of the premises has to be false, and the obvious suspect is the assumption that possessing a concept requires having a definition of it -- most concepts don't have neat necessary-and-sufficient conditions anyway, so the whole hypothesis-testing picture never even gets off the ground. Omar's reply was that this doesn't actually let you off the hook, because it's the exact move Fodor makes himself in his 1998 book "Concepts" -- he drops definitions and goes full atomist about lexical concepts, but he still won't call acquiring a new one "learning," he calls it "triggering" instead, so the unlearned residue doesn't disappear, it just relocates. Lena came back that "triggering" isn't some ad hoc dodge -- its modeled on ordinary biological processes, like puberty being triggered on a hormonal schedule, which nobody thinks of as "learned" -- so theres nothing suspect about some concepts just switching on given the right input. Omar's counter was that the analogy backfires: puberty's trigger is a fixed, species-wide timetable indifferent to what you've actually experienced, whereas whatever triggers CARBURETOR obviously has to be sensitive to the specific, historically recent fact of encountering carburetors (or talk of them) -- and a trigger that's tuned that precisely to the content of your experience is suspiciously hard to distinguish from learning under another name. That's about where we had to actually order food.
