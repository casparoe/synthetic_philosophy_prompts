The White Horse Dialogue (白馬論), a chapter of the Gongsun Longzi attributed to Gongsun Long (fl. third century BCE) of the School of Names, stages a debate between two unnamed speakers over the thesis 白馬非馬, "a white horse is not a horse." Its defender offers several arguments: that "horse" names a shape and "white" names a color, so the compound term cannot simply reduce to the shape-term alone; and that a request for "a horse" can be met by an animal of any color, whereas a request for "a white horse" cannot be met by a yellow or black one, so the two expressions pick out different ranges of things. A. C. Graham traces much of the dialogue's apparent force to an unmarked ambiguity in 非 (fēi, "is not"), which can express either non-identity or exclusion from a class, with the two speakers seemingly trading on different readings without noticing it. The School of Names' output, including this argument, was long dismissed by rivals and later commentators as clever but ultimately empty wordplay. Chad Hansen instead reconstructs the argument as a serious mereological claim: on his hypothesis that classical Chinese general terms function semantically like mass nouns (as "water" does) rather than count nouns, what "horse" picks out is only a proper part of the larger whole picked out by "white horse," and a part is never identical to a whole that properly contains it. Graham later endorsed this part-whole analysis without committing to the mass-noun hypothesis itself, while Manyul Im rejects both readings, treating the dialogue instead as a reductio that exposes the inadequacy of the era's rival theories of naming rather than as any positive claim about horses.
