Over the past year I've been teaching myself set theory from library books and lecture notes I found online, mostly because a documentary mentioned Cantor's proof that there are strictly "more" real numbers than whole numbers, and the idea wouldn't leave me alone. That eventually led me to the continuum hypothesis (CH), the claim that there's no set whose size sits strictly between the two, and I was genuinely startled by how strange its status turned out to be. Gödel showed in the 1940s that it can't be disproved from the standard axioms of set theory (ZFC), and Cohen showed in the 1960s, using a technique called forcing, that it can't be proved from them either. So both "yes" and "no" turn out to be perfectly consistent with the axioms mathematicians normally use, and from what I can tell, even most large cardinal axioms, which I understand are the most natural candidates for new axioms, fail to settle it one way or the other.

What I haven't been able to work out for myself is what all this is supposed to mean philosophically. I've read that Gödel was convinced CH had to be true or false in some absolute sense, and hoped mathematicians would eventually discover new, self-evidently true axioms that settled the question, a hope people like Hugh Woodin have spent much of their careers chasing. But I've also come across Joel David Hamkins's "set-theoretic multiverse" view, which seems to treat the question the way mathematicians now treat Euclid's parallel postulate after the discovery of non-Euclidean geometry: not something with one correct answer, but something that holds in some perfectly legitimate universes of sets and fails in others, with no further fact about which universe is the "real" one. And then there is Freiling's "dart-throwing" argument, which is supposed to make CH's falsity intuitively compelling even though it is not a derivation from any agreed-on axioms. Having no formal training in mathematics, logic, or philosophy, I've mostly just been oscillating between these positions without any way to adjudicate between them.

Please describe the most important implications of the continuum hypothesis's independence from ZFC for the broader question of whether statements like it have objective, mind-independent truth values, i.e., for the dispute between mathematical realism and its rivals. I would especially like to understand what the disagreement between the "keep searching for new axioms" camp and the "multiverse" camp really turns on philosophically, and whether an informal intuition pump like Freiling's argument should be given any evidential weight at all, given that it cannot be derived from the axioms either way.
