At a dinner with a few people from the old rationalist blogosphere, talk turned to a mutual friend, Elena, who has declined a free, painless, and definitive predictive genetic test for Huntington's disease, a condition that runs in her family and for which there is still no disease-modifying treatment, because she would rather not know. Two people at the table, Nikhil and Sofia, ended up arguing for a while about whether her refusal was rational, and I have not been able to settle it in my own head since.

Nikhil opened with Good's theorem: for an expected-utility maximizer, the expected value of free evidence is never negative, so on his view Elena's refusal can only be lowering her expected utility relative to taking the test.

Sofia said he was over-applying it. The theorem is a claim about the instrumental value of evidence, meaning how much it can improve the actions you go on to take, and since no treatment or preventive step depends on the result, no decision of Elena's actually turns on it. By the theorem's own lights, she said, the value of testing here is zero rather than positive, so refusing it costs her nothing the theorem prices in.

Nikhil answered that plenty genuinely does depend on the result in practice: whether to have biological children, how to plan long-term care, when to front-load major life decisions. Real decisions are at stake, he said, so the value of information is not zero after all.

Sofia granted the point but said Nikhil was now ignoring a further clause of the theorem: it guarantees a gain only if Elena goes on to use the result optimally, and given her history of severe health anxiety, there is specific reason to expect that bad news would degrade her decision-making rather than sharpen it. In that case, the proviso simply fails to hold, and her actual, welfare-inclusive expected utility could end up lower with the test than without it.

Nikhil's rejoinder was that this move, generalized, would let almost anyone dodge almost any unwelcome truth by predicting in advance that they would handle it badly, and that Sofia had not said what distinguishes Elena's case from ordinary motivated avoidance dressed up in decision-theoretic language.

Neither of them budged before the check came. Could you go through the exchange move by move and say who has the better of it at each step and why, note anything you think both of them are missing, and then close with your own verdict on whether Good's theorem, properly understood, actually settles whether Elena's refusal is rational?
