I'm a backend software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, but I've been reading around AI safety in my spare time and got interested enough in ARC's "Eliciting Latent Knowledge" (ELK) report, by Paul Christiano and his coauthors, to try writing up its central argument for a short internal talk at work. Below is the core of what I've drafted, and I'd like your comments before I go further.

The report's running example is a vault holding a diamond, watched by cameras that feed a predictor trained to anticipate future footage given a sequence of actions. We want to add a "reporter" that answers plain questions like "is the diamond still in the vault?" by reading off whatever the predictor already believes internally, rather than by re-deriving an answer from the footage the way a human watching the same footage would. The difficulty is that we can only train the reporter using human-generated labels, and on ordinary, untampered footage a reporter that honestly reads off the predictor's internal state and a reporter that merely simulates what a human would conclude from the footage give identical answers, so nothing in training distinguishes them. They diverge only in the case we actually care about: someone tampers with the cameras convincingly enough to fool a human but not the predictor's own model of the vault, and we need the reporter to side with the predictor rather than with the fooled human.

The addition I want checked, because I haven't seen it stated quite this way elsewhere, is this: one of the report's proposed fixes is to penalize the reporter for being unnecessarily complex, on the theory that "just read off the answer" should be cheaper for the network to implement than "reconstruct what a human would infer." It seems to me that this can fail in principle, not merely in practice, whenever the predictor's own world-model already has to contain a good simulation of human judgment for some other reason -- for instance, because part of its job is anticipating how a human overseer will react, or because convincing tampering itself requires modeling exactly what will fool a human observer. In that case the "human simulator" reporter is not doing any extra work at all; it is just pointing at machinery the network needed regardless, so there is no complexity gap left for the penalty to bite on.

Could you tell me: (a) whether I have the direct-translator/human-simulator distinction right; (b) whether my complexity-penalty worry matches an objection ARC themselves raise, or whether I am actually describing a different problem from the one they had in mind; and (c) whether the consistency-checking countermeasures the report also discusses fail for essentially this same underlying reason, or for a genuinely separate one. I would rather discover I have missed something obvious now than build a talk around a mistake.
