I spent thirty-odd years as a paleontologist, and the thing that always bugged me was how we chop up one continuous, gradually-changing fossil lineage into separate named "chronospecies" -- there's never a non-arbitrary place to draw the line, just a slow slide from one form into another. Since retiring I've been reading philosophy and realized this is basically the sorites paradox. Now I'm kind of hooked on the fight between epistemicists (Williamson's crowd -- there IS a sharp cutoff, we just cant know where it is, because of margin-for-error limits on knowledge) and supervaluationists (no sharp cutoff, a sentence is only true if its true on every admissible way of making the term precise, otherwise it's a truth-value gap). Could you write a short dialogue where one of each hashes it out over my chronospecies case? Don't let either one get strawmanned -- make the epistemicist actually answer "how can an unknowable boundary do any explanatory work," and make the supervaluationist actually answer the higher-order vagueness problem (isn't "admissible" itself vague?).
