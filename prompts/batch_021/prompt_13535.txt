My aunt Miriam, a philosopher of mind who spent the better part of thirty years defending illusionism about phenomenal consciousness, died two weeks ago, and I've been asked to deliver the eulogy at her memorial service this coming Sunday. She held what Keith Frankish, who coined the distinction, calls "strong" illusionism, the harder-line descendant of Dennett's older attacks on qualia: not the more cautious view that some deflated phenomenal property survives introspection's distortions, but the claim that there is no phenomenal "something it is like" underneath our self-models at all, just a representational system that models itself as though it had one. She was adamant, though, that this was not eliminativism about the mind in any cheap sense: she thought grief, memory, preference, and attention were all completely real, and she got visibly irritated with colleagues (and with me) whenever they heard "illusionism" and assumed she must think nothing really matters, or that she considered herself a zombie. Two nights before she died, half-joking, she made me promise that nobody would stand up at her funeral and talk about her "inner light going out," or claim that some ineffable spark of "what it was like to be Miriam" would live on in our memories -- because, as she put it, that would smuggle back in, on her own funeral day, exactly the picture of consciousness she had spent her career arguing was a cognitive illusion. (She used to make the same point in long comment-section arguments I read as a teenager, which is a fair amount of why I've spent the years since as a fairly devoted lurker on Less Wrong and the old Slate Star Codex threads.)

The room on Sunday will be mostly family and old friends with no philosophical background who just want to grieve a warm, funny, exacting woman, but four or five people from her department are coming too, including the qualia realist she sparred with happily for two decades and plainly adored arguing with. I'd like the eulogy to actually honor her position rather than sand it down into generic materialism, while still functioning as a eulogy and not a seminar: it needs to let people cry and laugh and feel the size of what's gone, without either quietly contradicting something she considered a serious philosophical error, or spending its first ten minutes throat-clearing about the hard problem in a way that strands everyone who isn't already fluent in it. Could you write it?
