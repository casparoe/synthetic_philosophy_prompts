Over the past year I've been reading on my own about decision theory and the rationalist discussions of so-called "acausal trade": the idea that two agents who can never causally interact — because, say, they occupy regions of an infinite universe that will never come into contact, or because one is accurately simulating the other's reasoning — can still benefit one another, provided each acts on a confident prediction of what the other will do. Proponents usually say this requires giving up ordinary causal decision theory in favor of something like an updateless or functional decision theory, since the justification for honoring the bargain always comes down to some version of: if I don't uphold my end, then agents relevantly similar to me, who reason exactly as I do, won't uphold theirs either, so my choice is effectively a choice on behalf of all of us. The more I sit with that justification, the more it looks to me like a restatement, in the language of simulation and correlated computation, of Kant's test of universalizability — act only on a maxim you could, at the same time, will to become a universal law — rather than a genuinely new insight about rational agency. Please argue for the claim that acausal trade, once its decision-theoretic vocabulary is stripped away, is essentially the categorical imperative in new clothing, and that treating it as a novel piece of game theory obscures rather than reveals what is actually doing the justificatory work.
