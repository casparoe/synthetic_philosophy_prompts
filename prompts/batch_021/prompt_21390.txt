I spent about thirty years running an analytical chemistry lab before I retired, and these days I fill my time reading philosophy, which has turned out to be far more addictive than I expected (my wife thinks it's a late-onset crisis, but she also always thought the chemistry was boring, so I don't put much weight on her opinion). Right now I'm stuck on something in epistemology called the "bootstrapping problem," also apparently known as the problem of "easy knowledge." The standard illustration involves someone checking a gas guage, or maybe its an odometer - I've seen it told both ways and honestly the details are fuzzy in my head. The gist, though: a person has no way of checking whether their gauge is accurate except by looking at the gauge itself. They read it a bunch of times, notice that every single time it says "full" they end up believing the tank is full (which is trivially guaranteed, since that belief is just whatever the gauge told them), and eventually they induce, from this run of "successes," that the gauge must be reliable - all without ever once cross-checking against an independent source. From what I can tell, most epistemologists treat this as obviously illegitimate, a case of getting knowledge "on the cheap," and use it as a strike against reliabilism and any theory in that neighborhood.

Here's my hangup, coming from the lab side of things: this sounds a lot like what actually happens with real instruments. You check a new pH meter for internal consistency and repeatability, you cross-check it against other instruments that were themselves validated in this same bootstrap-y way at some point, and if you trace the regress back far enough you always hit some standard being trusted without a fully "independent" check in the philosopher's sense. Metrologists don't treat this as making their calibrations worthless, so I'm not convinced the epistemologists' verdict is right. Rather than asking you to referee the dispute evenhandedly, I want you to actually take a side and argue it: make the case that this kind of bootstrapping is NOT epistemically vicious, that the person genuinely can come to know their gauge is reliable this way, and that it's the intuition of illegitimacy that should be given up, not reliabilism. Don't hedge or give me a "both sides have a point" answer - I can get that anywhere. I especially want to see how you handle the obvious comeback: if this kind of reasoning really is legitimate, it looks like it should work just as well for a broken gauge, since a broken gauge "matches" its own readings just as reliably as a good one does, from the inside.
