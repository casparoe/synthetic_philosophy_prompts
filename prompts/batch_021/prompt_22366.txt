I've spent a long time reading Less Wrong and the various rationalist-diaspora blogs, and Frege's puzzle about identity statements is one of those bits of philosophy that comes up constantly there — almost always in its familiar 1892 form (Hesperus and Phosphorus, the fact that "the morning star is the evening star" is informative while "the morning star is the morning star" is trivial), usually as a stepping stone into discussions of belief individuation or Yudkowsky's intension/extension distinction from the "Human's Guide to Words" sequence. Wanting something less pre-chewed, I went back to where Frege actually first poses the puzzle, which is not the famous 1892 essay but section 8 of his much earlier Begriffsschrift (1879) — written thirteen years before he had the sense/reference (Sinn/Bedeutung) machinery available, when he was still working with a single undivided notion he called "content" (Inhalt). Published translations of this section vary quite a bit in wording, so what follows is my own careful rendering, cross-checked against the German:

"Identity of content is unlike conditionality and negation in one respect: it concerns names, not contents. Ordinarily a sign does nothing more than stand for its content, so that any combination of signs expresses only a relation among their contents; but once two signs are joined by the sign for identity of content, each one abruptly refers to itself rather than to its content, since the whole point of the assertion is that the two names share a content. Adopting a sign for identity of content therefore forces every sign to serve two masters: sometimes meaning its content, sometimes meaning itself.

It might seem that this is merely a fact about notation, with no bearing on the thought itself — that a single sign would have done just as well, and that we never really needed a sign for identity of content at all. The following example from geometry is meant to show that this impression is mistaken. Let A be a fixed point on the circumference of a circle, and let a line rotate about A. For each position of the line, let B be the second point at which it meets the circle (that is, the point of intersection other than A); as the line's position varies continuously, B likewise moves continuously around the circumference, so the name 'B' designates nothing determinate until some position of the line has been fixed. We can now ask: which point is B when the line stands perpendicular to the diameter drawn through A? The answer is: the point A itself. So the name 'B,' under this condition, turns out to have exactly the same content as the name 'A' — and yet we could not simply have used one name throughout, since it is exactly this coincidence that stood in need of proof. The point is thus given to us in two distinct ways: immediately, through intuition, as the point A; and by construction, as the point B associated with the line's being perpendicular to the diameter drawn through A."

What I can't quite pin down is what Frege is committing himself to at this early stage, and how charitably to read it against what he says about it later. When he describes the point in the example as being 'given' in two different ways, that already sounds close to the later notion of sense — as though the Begriffsschrift view were simply a rough draft of the 1892 theory, missing only the vocabulary. But the surrounding text explicitly says that identity of content is a relation on names rather than on contents, and that a sign flanked by the identity sign 'stands for itself' rather than for its usual content — which sounds like exactly the view Frege attributes to his earlier self and criticizes in the opening paragraph of "On Sense and Reference," namely that identity statements are covertly about signs rather than about the things signified. So which is it: is the 1892 self-criticism basically accurate, or is the Begriffsschrift view more subtle than Frege's own later summary of it suggests? And is there an actual use-mention slide in saying that a sign 'stands for itself' once it sits beside the identity sign — is Frege quietly trading on an ambiguity between a name and a name of that name, in a way that would make the whole apparatus incoherent, or is there a reading of 'stands for itself' on which this charge doesn't stick? I'd like to work through the logical structure of the passage itself rather than falling back on the standard morning-star-and-evening-star gloss.
