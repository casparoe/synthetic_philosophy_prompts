I don't have any formal background in philosophy, but over the past year I've worked my way slowly through Descartes' Meditations on First Philosophy and, more recently, Wittgenstein's On Certainty, mainly because a friend and I kept arguing about whether it even makes sense to try to doubt everything at once. What's been nagging at me is that Wittgenstein seems to be doing more than just offering a different answer to Descartes' skeptical problem — he seems to be denying that the problem is even well-posed. His argument, as I understand it, is that any act of doubting, including the deliberate, methodical doubt of the First Meditation, already leans on a background of things that cannot be treated as doubtful at that moment: things like the fact that the earth has been around for a long time, or that one's own body doesn't just vanish and reappear, which function less like beliefs held on evidence than like the hinges the rest of our judging turns on. If that's right, Descartes' plan to suspend belief in absolutely everything at once and rebuild knowledge from a single indubitable starting point starts to look like trying to take a door off by unscrewing its own hinges while it's still swinging on them.

So here is what I would like you to do. Imagine, even though it's plainly impossible, that Descartes somehow got hold of a copy of On Certainty — Wittgenstein was writing some three hundred years after him, after all — and had to answer this challenge himself, in his own terms, rather than being refuted secondhand by a modern commentator. I'd like you to work through his likely response as specifically as you can, drawing on what he actually does in the Meditations (the cogito, the wax argument, the later appeal to a non-deceiving God to re-license the senses), rather than on his popular reputation as "the guy who doubted everything." Does he have a genuine way of resisting the charge that hinge-type certainties make his method self-undermining, or would he be forced either to treat those certainties as just more unexamined childhood assumptions to be swept aside, or to quietly narrow what he means by "doubt" until the project loses its point? Please don't leave this as an even-handed survey — end with a definite verdict as to whether Wittgenstein's picture of hinge propositions genuinely defeats Cartesian doubt, or whether Descartes' position can be reconstructed so that his method survives the challenge.
