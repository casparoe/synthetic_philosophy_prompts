Over the past few months I have been co-curating a mid-career retrospective at the university art museum where I work, and an internal disagreement has left me unexpectedly absorbed in a problem I had previously only encountered in the abstract. For a gallery of works on loan that none of us on staff had seen in person before they arrived, I approved wall text that repeated, almost verbatim, a visiting scholar's published claims about the compositional balance and emotional restraint of two paintings -- claims none of us had independently verified by looking hard at the works ourselves. A colleague called this a kind of curatorial malpractice, arguing that we had no business asserting aesthetic properties we had not perceived firsthand, whatever our source. That argument sent me down a rabbit hole into the philosophical literature on aesthetic testimony: the debate over whether one can acquire justified aesthetic belief, or even aesthetic knowledge, purely by taking someone else's word for it, the way we routinely do for historical or scientific claims.

As it happens, the museum's public programs office has just confirmed a visiting philosopher for next month's fellowship lecture, and I have been asked to moderate the Q&A. She is well known for defending pessimism about aesthetic testimony: roughly, the view that because aesthetic judgment is governed by something like an acquaintance principle, testimony can at best hand you a true belief borrowed from someone else's eyes, never a first-personal aesthetic verdict of your own -- and that this makes aesthetic testimony importantly disanalogous to the testimony we accept without a second thought in history or science. I would like to press her on this rather than let her simply restate the position for a room of donors and undergraduates. Could you draft eight to ten interview questions for me? I want at least one question that forces her to say precisely why aesthetic judgment deserves this stricter standard; one that tests whether the acquaintance principle can be formulated without simply presupposing what is at issue against the optimist; one on whether there is a principled line between relaying a critic's bare evaluative verdict and relaying more specific descriptive observations -- that a piece is restrained, or garish, or off-balance -- which a viewer might legitimately use to inform a judgment of their own; and one that uses the parallel debate over moral testimony as a foil, since pessimists there run a structurally similar argument. For each question, please add a short follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give, so I am not caught flat-footed by the standard reply -- and close with a question that puts her on the spot about what, concretely, a museum should do about wall text and loaned works if she is right, since simply refusing to say anything until visitors have looked hard enough themselves seems both impractical and not obviously the lesson she wants drawn either.
