So this happened at my writers' group last week and I still haven't shaken it off. We workshopped a novel-in-progress by a guy named Kwame -- grim, well-written book about a Black family in a dying Rust Belt town: addiction, a shooting, nobody redeemed by the last page. Afterward, two others in the group, Ada and Sam, got into it over drinks and somehow ended up litigating W.E.B. Du Bois at eachother.

Ada said no matter how good the prose is, the book does exactly what Du Bois warned about in "Criteria of Negro Art" -- his line that all art is propaganda and ever must be -- because a portrait this hopeless just becomes more ammo for readers who already assume the worst, whatever Kwame meant by it. Sam said that's a bad reading: Du Bois wasn't against ugliness, he was mad that Black writers got held to a purity test nobody applied to the minstrel and pulp junk already flooding the culture, and that demanding uplift is its own propaganda. Ada said fine, but Du Bois also tied beauty to truth AND justice, and a book that's all despair with no sense that injustice is being done to these people, rather than it just being their nature, fails the justice half even if every sentence in it is true. Sam said that sense is right there, just structural -- the housing, the plant closures, the cop who's never even named -- and that's a stricter ask than the one Ada started with. Ada came back with: fine, but then her real problem is Kwame's agent already pitching it as "unflinching," and that packaging decides what propaganda the book does no matter what he meant.

Neither backed down, and I've got a manuscript of my own with basically this same problem, so I'd love an outside read. Can you go through this move by move and say who's got the better argument at each step, and what -- if anything -- they're both getting wrong about Du Bois's actual argument?
