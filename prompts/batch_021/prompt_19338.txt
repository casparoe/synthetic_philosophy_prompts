A friend and I spent the better part of an evening arguing about whether rationality requires transitive preferences. She made the standard money-pump argument: anyone who ranks A over B, B over C, and C over A can be led through a cycle of trades—each one a genuine improvement when considered in isolation—that returns them to their starting bundle once they have paid a small fee at every step, so a cyclic ranking leaves one exploitable in a way a transitive one does not. I countered that this only indicts a naive, link-by-link way of choosing, not the cyclic ranking itself: a planner who evaluates whole sequences of trades rather than one trade at a time can see the cycle coming and simply decline to complete the loop, ending up ahead (or at least no worse off) while still ranking the underlying goods A over B, B over C, and C over A. Her rejoinder was that this "solution" merely relocates the irrationality, since at some point the planner must decline a trade they would, by their own current lights, most prefer to make—which is no better than having intransitive preferences to begin with. I'd like to make the case for my side more rigorously than I managed in the moment. Please argue that the vulnerability money pumps exploit is a fact about this naive, one-trade-at-a-time choice disposition rather than about the mere possession of a cyclic preference ranking, and that a rational planner can retain thoroughly cyclic preferences over the underlying goods while never actually being exploitable—so that the money-pump argument, properly understood, motivates only a diachronic constraint on how one's choices across a sequence of trades should cohere over time, rather than a synchronic constraint requiring transitive preferences at an instant.
