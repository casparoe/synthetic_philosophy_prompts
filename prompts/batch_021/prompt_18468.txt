Last night, a friend and I had a long argument about how best to interpret ordinary de re modal claims about individuals, such as "I could have studied medicine instead of philosophy." She defended David Lewis's counterpart theory, on which such a claim is made true by a suitably similar counterpart doing so in another possible world, while I held out for the more standard transworld-identity view, on which I myself, one and the same individual, exist and act differently there. Kripke's Humphrey objection came up almost immediately, but we agreed that it has become too familiar to settle anything between us. Where we actually got stuck was Chisholm's Paradox: a sequence of possible worlds connected by only minute qualitative changes, such that identity seems preserved between each neighboring pair, even though the two endpoints look like essentially different individuals. Construct a new scenario in that same spirit, distinct from Chisholm's own case, and use it to show concretely why transworld identity, but not counterpart theory, is pushed toward paradox, given the differing demands each view places on the relation that links an individual across worlds.
