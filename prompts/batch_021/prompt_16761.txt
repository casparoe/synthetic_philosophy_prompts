"The reference class problem arises because frequentist theories define the probability of a single event as the relative frequency of similar events in some reference class, but any individual event belongs to indefinitely many reference classes, and these classes typically give different frequencies. For example, suppose we want to know the probability that Smith, a 45-year-old male smoker with a family history of heart disease, has a heart attack sometime in the next ten years. We could look at the frequency among 45-year-olds generally, among male smokers, among people with a family history of heart disease, among 45-year-old male smokers with a family history, and so on -- and nothing in frequentism itself tells us which class is the right one, since Smith belongs to all of them equally.

Reichenbach, who first posed the problem in these terms, tried to fix it by saying we should always pick the narrowest reference class for which we have reliable statistics -- so here, the class of 45-year-old male smokers with a family history, rather than any broader class. This solves the problem: so long as we always narrow as far as the data will allow, there's a unique class to appeal to and the arbitrariness goes away.

The deeper fix is just to drop frequentism for Bayesianism. On a subjectivist view probability just is degree of belief, so there's no reference class to pick out in the first place -- I can assign Smith a 30% credence without that number being anybody's frequency. This is basically the same move Bertrand's paradox pushes us toward: once 'the' probability turns out to depend on an arbitrary choice of class or parametrization, the moral is to stop treating probabilities as objective frequencies waiting to be discovered and just treat them as rational degrees of belief instead."
