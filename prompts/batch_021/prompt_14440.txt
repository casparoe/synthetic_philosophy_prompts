I spent about thirty-five years as an environmental toxicologist doing exposure risk assessments for a state environmental agency — the kind of job where you build a model, set an "acceptable" threshold, and then sit through public comment hearings where people who live next to the facility tell you things your model never captured. Since retiring I've finally had time to read philosophy properly instead of just skimming it over bad coffee at conferences, and I'm working through Dewey's *The Public and Its Problems* for the first time. There's a stretch where he's pushing back on Walter Lippmann's idea that complicated policy questions should basically get handed over to trained specialists and bureaus, and he writes:

"A class of experts is inevitably so removed from common interests as to become a class with private interests and private knowledge, which in social matters is not knowledge at all."

That line stopped me cold, and I've been chewing on it for a week now (my wife is tired of hearing about it). It doesn't read like the modest claim I'd have expected — something like "experts should consult affected people too," which fine, I lived that, no argument from me. It sounds like Dewey is saying expert knowledge that's detatched from the people bearing the consequences literally isn't knowledge, at least about social matters. Coming from my side of the table, where I spent decades trusting a designed study over somebody's say-so, I want to know if there's a real arguement buried here — something tied to his whole view that inquiry starts from a felt difficulty in an actual situation rather than a problem already framed by theory — for why political and social inquiry would have to work differently in kind from something like toxicology. Or is this just a rhetorical overreach that doesn't actually cash out into anything as precise as what I'm used to. I'd like to understand exactly what's being claimed before I decide whether it holds up.
