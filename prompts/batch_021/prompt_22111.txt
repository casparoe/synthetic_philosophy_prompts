In my AP Art History class, we were recently assigned to write "critical appreciations" of a handful of paintings that almost none of us have ever actually seen in person — a Rothko color-field piece and a small Vermeer domestic interior, among others — basing our judgments entirely on high-resolution reproductions, museum wall text, and quotations from well-known critics. Several of us complained that this felt like a hollow exercise, since we hadn't really engaged with the works ourselves, but our teacher pushed back with an analogy: we routinely accept a chemist's word that a compound is toxic, or a doctor's word that a mole looks benign, without personally verifying either claim, so why should trusting a critic's word that a painting is beautiful or moving be any different?

That question sent me down a rabbit hole into what turns out to be a real, ongoing debate in aesthetics. Philosophers working on this, building on ideas from Frank Sibley and Mary Mothersill and given a sharper formulation by Robert Hopkins, sometimes call it the acquaintance principle: the claim that a person cannot form a warranted, knowledge-level aesthetic judgment about a particular work purely on someone else's say-so, however reliable that person is, because such a judgment has to be grounded in one's own direct perceptual encounter with the work. Those who accept some version of this are usually labeled "pessimists" about aesthetic testimony, while "optimists" deny that aesthetic verdicts are epistemically special in this way and hold that testimony can transmit genuine aesthetic knowledge just as it transmits knowledge about toxicity or health.

I'd like you to argue for the optimist side of this debate. Specifically, please make the strongest case you can that aesthetic testimony can, in principle, ground genuine warranted aesthetic belief in essentially the same way ordinary testimony does elsewhere, with no deep asymmetry between "this painting is beautiful" and "this mole is benign." Rather than just asserting in general terms that testimony is usually reliable, I'd like you to engage seriously with the best motivation for the acquaintance principle — the thought that verdicts like "beautiful" or "moving," unlike "toxic" or "benign," are somehow constitutively bound up with the perceiver's own experience of the object, so that a report of someone else's experience could never quite substitute for having the experience yourself — and explain concretely where you think that thought goes wrong.
