Two people in my formal epistemology seminar have a running disagreement that, this week, turned into an argument about the disagreement itself. Back in week three, both privately estimated the probability that a specific long-standing open conjecture in mathematics will be resolved within ten years, then swapped numbers without sharing their reasoning: Priya said 15%, Dev said 60%. Neither has moved since, even though each fully trusts the other's competence and honesty.

(1) Dev says that's exactly the problem: two rational people who share a prior and have common knowledge of each other's posterior can't stably disagree like this, since each announced number is itself evidence and repeating the exchange should force convergence. Since it hasn't, somebody is being irrational.
(2) Priya says this smuggles in an assumption -- that the two of them share a prior -- which nothing guarantees; differing priors, she says, are just a fact about people, not a defect.
(3) Dev answers that a "prior" which survives three identical years of the same courses, professors, and reading lists isn't tracking the conjecture at all -- it's tracking something about them, which is exactly what a prior is supposed to screen off before any evidence arrives.
(4) Priya replies that Dev's standard would make almost any disagreement not settled by shared evidence irrational, which seems far too strong to accept.

They've asked me to referee, and I'm out of my depth.
