Two people in my philosophy of mind reading group disagreed after our session on Integrated Information Theory (IIT). (1) The first argued that IIT cannot be a serious theory of consciousness, since it implies that a simple feedback system -- even something as mundane as a thermostat -- has nonzero integrated information (Φ) and therefore, by the theory's own criterion, some sliver of experience; as nobody seriously believes thermostats are sentient, a theory committed to this should be rejected. (2) The second replied that biting this bullet is not ad hoc: IIT treats consciousness as graded rather than all-or-nothing, and the same framework correctly predicts a real asymmetry that neuron-counting theories miss -- the cerebellum contains several times more neurons than the cerebral cortex yet contributes comparatively little to conscious experience, which IIT explains through the cerebellum's modular, feedforward-heavy wiring yielding low Φ despite the neuron count. (3) The first answered that this is not independent confirmation, just a fit to what we already believed about the cerebellum on other grounds, and that the deeper problem is that Φ is defined over all possible partitions of a system, which is computationally intractable for anything brain-sized; nobody has actually verified these predictions on real neural tissue, so the theory's successes are plausibility arguments dressed up as calculations. (4) The second responded that intractability of exact computation is a practical limitation, not evidence of unfalsifiability in principle, and pointed to the perturbational complexity index -- a measure inspired by IIT that is used clinically to assess consciousness in unresponsive patients -- as a case where the theory generated a quantitative prediction that has actually been validated, rather than a post-hoc rationalization. Neither budged.
