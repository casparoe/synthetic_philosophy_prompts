A few of us are in a reading group working through old philosophy of mind papers, and last week's session on the frame problem turned into a whole argument with one guy in the group who does ML research. His take was that the frame problem is basically a non-issue now thanks to deep learning. The classic version, from McCarthy and Hayes, was that in a logic-based system you need explicit "frame axioms" listing everything that *doesn't* change when an action happens, and that blows up combinatorially -- which is part of why old-school symbolic AI never really scaled. But he pointed out that neural nets, and now LLMs, don't use hand-coded logical axioms at all, they just learn statistical structure from data, so there's no list of frame axioms to write down in the first place. His conclusion was that the deeper worry Dennett and Fodor raised -- that no finite system can figure out in real time which of its indefinitely many beliefs are even relevant to a given action or update, without some doomed exhaustive search -- basically evaporates once your doing things the deep learning way, since something like ChatGPT obviously makes "what matters here" judgments constantly without choking on it.

Is he basically right that scaling up neural nets dissolves the frame problem, or is there something off about that argument? I dont know the AI history side well enough to have pushed back properly in the moment, so I'd like to actually understand it before this comes up again.
