For my school's philosophy elective, we've been reading Mill's *On Liberty*, and our teacher periodically has us swap short essays and grade a classmate's response using her rubric, on the theory that writing the feedback teaches you the material as much as writing the essay did. I've been assigned the response below and want to be careful not to miss anything before I turn my grade in.

Here is the question my classmate answered:

Mill claims that the "sole end" warranting coercion of an individual against his will is the prevention of harm to others, and that his own good is never sufficient warrant on its own. Drawing on Mill's discussion of purely self-regarding conduct and on the corn-dealer example, explain (a) what kind of reaction, if any, Mill thinks is legitimate when someone's private conduct merely offends or disgusts other people without harming them, and (b) why Mill thinks the corn-dealer's accusers can justly be punished in one setting but not in another. Then explain briefly how this same line between harm and mere offense underwrites his argument for near-total freedom of opinion. (350 words)

And here is the answer:

Mill's harm principle holds that the only legitimate reason to coerce someone against their will, whether by law or by public opinion, is to prevent harm to other people; a person's own physical or moral good is never enough justification on its own. This is why Mill divides conduct into self-regarding actions, which mainly affect the person doing them, and other-regarding actions, which affect other people's interests, and why only the second kind can rightly be interfered with.

For purely self-regarding conduct, Mill doesn't think society has to stay completely silent just because it can't legislate. He recognizes that unconventional behavior can genuinely wound the feelings of neighbors and unsettle the general sense of decency in a community even when nobody's rights are technically violated, and he allows that once this kind of offense becomes widespread and strongly felt, it starts to function like a mild, informal version of harm that the community may legitimately act against, short of legal punishment. That is why the eccentric individual can still face real social pressure and disapproval without this being inconsistent with the harm principle: the collective discomfort produced by the conduct is itself doing the justificatory work, just below the threshold that would license law.

The corn-dealer passage works on similar logic. Mill says that the claim that corn-dealers starve the poor can be freely printed in a newspaper, but the same claim addressed to an angry crowd gathered outside a specific dealer's house can be punished, because in the second setting the words are likely to bring about real harm to that person. This fits with Mill's larger case for freedom of opinion in Chapter II, which is essentially a marketplace-of-ideas argument: since none of us can be certain in advance which side of a debate is true, the best way to reach the truth is to let every opinion, however false or dangerous it sounds, compete openly with the others, trusting that over time truth tends to win out over error. Restricting the newspaper article would interfere with that open competition for no good reason, while stopping the speech to the mob would not, since by then the "competition" has already given way to incitement.
