I'm a science-and-ideas journalist who spent nearly twenty years covering the public affairs office of a national research agency, and its longtime communications chief is retiring. Her old staff have asked me — the reporter who gave her the hardest time over the years — to toast her at the farewell dinner. She built her reputation less on saying false things than on the artful use of true ones: burying inconvenient findings in Friday-afternoon releases, answering the question she wished had been asked rather than the one that was, "clarifying" a misleading line instead of retracting it. Half the room is press staff who will catch every reference; the other half is family, who mainly want to hear that she did honorable work. Using Hannah Arendt's distinction, from essays like "Truth and Politics" and "Lying in Politics," between the deliberate lie and the subtler, more modern business of reshaping what counts as fact, write a five-minute toast that is warm and genuinely funny but does not, by Arendt's own standard, say anything false.
