I own a unit in a small condominium building a few blocks from campus that has, over the last decade, become housing mostly for graduate students and postdocs: roughly two-thirds of the units are owned by investors who live elsewhere and rent them out on ordinary leases, while a handful of owners, myself included, actually live in the building full-time. Under our bylaws, only unit owners are members of the association, one vote per unit regardless of who actually lives there, and at next month's meeting the board will vote on a proposed rule banning short-term, Airbnb-style rentals, on the grounds that constant turnover has increased noise, security concerns, and wear on the elevators and common areas. The people who will feel the day-to-day consequences of this rule most directly are the long-term tenants, who deal with the noise and the rotating strangers in the hallway right now and could see their rents rise later if the building becomes a more attractive long-term-rental property as a result of the policy, but tenants have no vote of any kind under the bylaws, and only the owners, several of whom have never spent a single night in the building, get to decide.

I raised this at a dinner with colleagues recently and was struck that everyone had a confident but different reaction. One person said the tenants obviously have a legitimate claim to some say, since they bear most of the real costs of the decision either way. Another said this is a non-issue: the tenants agreed to a lease under known rules, are free to leave if they dislike how the owners vote, and have no more claim to co-govern the building than a hotel guest has to vote on hotel policy. A third pointed out that if "whoever is substantially affected should get a say" is the right standard, we would also have to think about the residents of the building next door, who share our courtyard and complain about the very same noise, and maybe even the university's off-campus housing office, which steers many of these tenants to us in the first place and has its own stake in the policy. My own instinct is that the tenants are owed something the neighbors and the housing office are not, but when I try to say exactly why, without just presupposing the answer, I keep failing. My own field is philosophy of science, so this isn't a debate I have ready-made tools for from my teaching or research, but it has been nagging at me all week.

So I would like your take. Is there something genuinely unfair about a policy like this being decided entirely by the owners, or is "you knew the deal when you signed the lease" actually a complete answer, full stop? And if you think the tenants really are owed a vote, or at least a voice, is there a principled way to say how far that reasoning should extend, one that does not simply amount to the current owners generously deciding, on their own authority, how generous to be?
