I never had any formal training in philosophy — I left school at eighteen and have spent most of my working life doing something unrelated — but I've been preoccupied with the ethics of immigration policy for a long time, partly because I volunteer with a small charity that helps asylum seekers navigate the system here in Britain, and partly because I got tired of every conversation I have on the subject collapsing into slogans within a couple of exchanges. A while ago someone mentioned, almost as an aside, that Michael Dummett — whom I understood to be one of the great technical philosophers of the last century, known for his work on Frege and on the foundations of logic and mathematics — had also written a short book called "On Immigration and Refugees," and that this grew directly out of his own history of anti-racist campaigning, including his opposition to the 1968 Act that was rushed through Parliament specifically to keep Kenyan Asians who held British passports from entering the country. That combination struck me as strange enough — a hardcore logician turning his attention to the most bad-tempered subject in British public life — that I tracked down a copy, read it twice, and have now drafted a short piece of my own trying to state, in plain terms, what I take his central argument to be, and to test it against a case closer to home than his own examples.

As I've reconstructed it, Dummett's key move is to refuse the assumption that almost everyone on both sides of the debate grants for free: that a state simply has the right to exclude whoever it likes from its territory, so that the only real question is which exceptions — refugees, family members, needed workers — should be carved out of that right. He wants that default itself defended rather than assumed, and thinks that once you actually try to defend it, it turns out to be far weaker, and far more entangled with the receiving country's own history (including what it did to help create the conditions people are fleeing), than restrictionists usually admit. He isn't an open-borders absolutist, though: he allows that a state might justify some controls by appeal to real strains on housing, services, or social cohesion, but insists the burden of proof be flipped onto whoever wants to exclude, and points out how rarely actual British policy has been shaped by that kind of honest argument rather than by something closer to racial anxiety dressed up as border management. In my essay I try to run this move against the government's scheme (since scrapped, though I think the argument outlives the policy) to remove asylum seekers to Rwanda to have their claims processed and settled there: my claim is that the scheme only looks defensible if you smuggle back in exactly the assumption Dummett wants us to interrogate — that Britain's obligation to someone who has already reached its territory and asked for protection can simply be exported along with their case file.

What I would like from you is honest criticism rather than encouragement. First, tell me where I have flattened or misread Dummett's actual position — I am working from one reading of one short book with no way of checking myself against people who know his work properly. Second, tell me whether my extension of his argument to the Rwanda scheme genuinely follows from his framework, or whether I am just borrowing his authority for a conclusion of my own that he might not have accepted. Third, if there is a stronger objection to the whole approach — from a committed restrictionist, or from some other direction I haven't considered — that I'm missing, I would rather hear it from you now than be caught out by it later.
