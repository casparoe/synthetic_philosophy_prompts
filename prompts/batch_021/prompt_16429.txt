My uncle Hollis, a physicist who devoted his retirement to the metaphysics of personal identity, died in March and was cryopreserved within the week, as he'd arranged decades earlier. His cryonics circle is treating tonight's memorial as a long goodbye rather than an ending; my mother's side of the family, who consider the whole enterprise faintly absurd, are treating it simply as a funeral. Hollis foresaw this divide and left me notes rather than a script. I am not to promise the room that "he" will simply wake up one day: he spent years arguing, after Parfit, that this question may have no determinate answer. He liked to call his own suspension "my branch-line case," after the variant of the teletransporter thought experiment in which the original dies while a psychological continuer carries on elsewhere. But I am equally forbidden from treating this as ordinary death, or reaching for the soul-talk he found embarrassing at other people's funerals. I am the novelist in the family, not the philosopher, and I need a eulogy that takes his own view of what matters in survival seriously enough to satisfy both halves of that room.
