I have no formal training in philosophy, but I've spent a lot of my free time over the years reading about the philosophy of science on my own, and one thing that has always stuck with me is Karl Popper's uneasy relationship with Darwinism. As I understand it, he originally dismissed the theory of natural selection as being close to a tautology, flexible enough to explain almost any outcome after the fact rather than predict one in advance, though he partially revised that judgment later in life. Popper died in 1994, before some of the most striking real-time demonstrations of natural selection had fully played out. One example is Richard Lenski's decades-long laboratory experiment on E. coli, in which one population evolved a genuinely new ability to exploit a nutrient already present in its environment, but only after an earlier, seemingly inconsequential mutation had quietly set the stage for it many generations before. Given how demanding Popper's standards for genuine scientific testability were, what do you think he would have made of an experiment like this? Would he have accepted it as exactly the kind of rigorous, falsifiable test of Darwinism that he once felt was missing, or do you suspect he would have found some further reason to hold back?
