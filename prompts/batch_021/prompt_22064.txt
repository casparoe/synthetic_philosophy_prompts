So I went down a bit of a rabbit hole this week after someone in a comment section (I think it was under one of those old "notice your confusion" style posts, but I've lost the link now) brought up this thing called the dogmatism paradox. I've seen it credited to Kripke from some unpublished lectures back in the 60s, and separately to Gilbert Harman for writing it up properly, but honestly the lineage is fuzzy to me and I don't think it matters much for the argument itself. The setup goes: suppose I know that P. Then it seems like I'm already in a position to know that any future evidence against P is going to be misleading evidence, because P is true, so nothing that genuinely counts against it can exist — any apparent counterevidence has to be some kind of illusion or noise. And if I already know in advance that some future evidence would be misleading, it seems like I don't have to take it seriously on it's own merits when it actually shows up, I'm entitled to just discount it. String those two moves together and you get: knowing P now licenses you to dismiss, in advance, literally any future evidence against P, no matter how strong it looks when it arrives. That's obviously a terrible way to reason, basically a recipe for being unfalsifiable about anything your currently confident of, but each step on its own is annoyingly hard to just reject outright.

What I actually want is the diagnosis, not just the setup, since I feel like I've got the setup down fine already. Specifically: (1) where does the argument first go wrong, and is denying epistemic closure really the standard move, or is there a way to keep closure and block things somewhere else — like distinguishing "knowing in general that misleading evidence against P must exist" from "being entitled to dismiss this particular bit of evidence sight unseen"? (2) Does the paradox survive if you translate it into Bayesian terms, swapping "know that P" for something like "assign P a credence of .995" — or does moving to graded beliefs dissolve the problem, the way going from flat certainty to merely very confident is supposed to fix the trouble with assigning probability 1 to things? And (3) I keep seeing this get tangled up with Pryor's totally different-sounding use of "dogmatism," for the view that having an experience as of P gives you default justification for P without first ruling out skeptical scenarios — is that actually the same underlying issue wearing a costume, or just an unfortunate naming collision? An actual answer on all three would be great, not just a restatement of the puzzle back at me.
