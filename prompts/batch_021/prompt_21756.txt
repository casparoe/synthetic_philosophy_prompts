I co-host a small philosophy podcast, and we occasionally run a segment in which we script an imagined conversation with a philosopher who is no longer living, working strictly from positions they actually defended in print rather than inventing views for them. The next installment focuses on Michael Dummett -- not the Dummett of Frege scholarship and semantic anti-realism that most listeners already associate with the name, but the Dummett of *On Immigration and Refugees* and of decades spent campaigning against racist immigration law, including his role in founding the Joint Council for the Welfare of Immigrants. Because that side of his work is so much less discussed than his technical philosophy, I want the questions to double as a real introduction to it rather than a token nod before moving to safer ground.

Please draft nine or ten interview questions for this segment. Include at least one question that presses on how he separates a state's "in principle" right to control immigration from the claim that any actual government -- starting with his own, over the Commonwealth Immigrants Act 1968 -- has ever exercised that right for defensible, non-racist reasons; at least one that pushes on whether the moral distinction he relies on between refugees and economic migrants can really bear the weight his argument puts on it, given how mixed real people's motives for leaving usually are; and at least one that asks him to reconcile his willingness to grant that immigration may sometimes legitimately be restricted with his own history of campaigning against restriction. For each of these three, add a brief follow-up keyed to the answer he's most likely to give, so the follow-up presses into the likely soft spot rather than just repeating the question. For the remaining questions, I'd rather they surface the strongest objections his position has drawn from both sides -- from open-borders theorists who think he conceded too much, and from restrictionists who think his concessions are hollow -- than ask him anything he could answer by simply restating the book's thesis.
