For several years I have used the two-envelope paradox as something of a personal party trick: I ask someone to imagine choosing between two envelopes, having been told that one contains exactly twice as much money as the other, and then walk them through the familiar "proof," via naive expected-value reasoning, that they ought to switch no matter which envelope they initially picked. The trick usually lands, and I follow up with what I had always taken to be the standard debunking: that one cannot coherently hold, for every amount I might find in my own envelope, that the other envelope is twice as large with probability one-half and half as large with probability one-half, since no genuine probability distribution behaves that way for every possible value at once. Last month I tried this on a friend who works professionally as a Bayesian statistician, and she was not satisfied with my usual explanation. On a napkin, she sketched a prior for the smaller envelope's contents that only ever takes the values $5, $10, $20, $40, and so on (doubling at each step, with geometrically decreasing probability), and pointed out that if I happened to see $5 -- the smallest amount the prior allows -- I would know for certain that I was holding the smaller envelope, making switching a guaranteed win, which my blanket "switching is simply never justified" line could not accommodate. I did not have a good response at the time, and there is a rematch of sorts planned in a few weeks that I would very much like to be better prepared for.

My own background is in philosophy rather than statistics, so I can follow an argument reasonably well but am on shakier ground once the probability theory goes beyond an undergraduate acquaintance with Bayes' rule. What I would find most useful is a careful, unhurried explanation -- rather than the potted "it's a paradox because the math is illegitimate" version one finds within the first page of a search -- of exactly which step in the classic switching argument fails, and why no probability distribution over the contents of the smaller envelope can make it true, for literally every possible observed amount at once, that the other envelope is equally likely to be double or half. I would also like you to work through my friend's example in enough detail that I could reconstruct it myself, and then go further than she did: does the case for switching only ever apply to that smallest possible value, or can it also hold for some of the larger observed amounts, depending on how quickly the prior's probabilities decay? If you could build the intuition up in stages, starting from why the naive argument feels so compelling before introducing only as much formal probability as is strictly necessary, that would help most -- it is the underlying intuition I am missing, not a formal patch I could recite without really understanding it.
