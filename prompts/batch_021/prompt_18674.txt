I spent thirty-five years as a field biologist studying sexual selection in birds, and since retiring I have joined a philosophy of biology reading group. Last week's session on Zahavi's handicap principle led to a disagreement between two other members that I have not been able to settle myself.

The group had raised the diamond engagement ring as a stock case of costly signaling: expensive, and largely unrecoverable if the engagement fails, so only a genuinely committed partner is supposed to find it worth buying. (1) Frank called this a clean instance of the handicap principle: sheer cost is what makes the gesture informative. (2) Miriam disagreed: the version of the principle that actually survives as an evolutionarily stable strategy -- Grafen's reconstruction of it, worked out after Maynard Smith's objections to Zahavi's original argument -- requires cost to fall differentially on low-quality signalers; an identical flat cost does not sort honest signalers from fakers, it just taxes both alike. (3) Frank answered that the ring meets this once properly costed: an uncommitted buyer is statistically likelier to end up wasting the money, so his expected cost is already higher, even though the price tag is identical for everyone. (4) Miriam replied that this quietly swaps arguments: her point in (2) concerned differential psychological cost, while (3) instead relies on differential probability of loss -- a different mechanism, and one she does not think he can introduce without further defense.

I am comfortable enough with the biology, but I am still finding my feet with the philosophical apparatus, so I would welcome a more careful referee than I can currently be. Please go through the exchange move by move, say who has the better of it at each step, and address whether Miriam's charge that Frank switched mechanisms between (2) and (3) is fair to him -- and whether the ring example could be respecified to actually satisfy the differential-cost requirement she has in mind.
