And here is the student's answer:

Williamson wants to reject the idea that our own mental states are always transparent to us — the thesis that if you are in a mental state, you are thereby able to know that you are in it. He calls this luminosity, and argues against it using a sorites-like series of cases. Imagine a subject sitting in a room over the course of a single morning, at times t0 through tn. At t0 the room is freezing and they are clearly cold; the temperature then rises by an imperceptibly small amount at every subsequent instant, until at tn the room is warm and they no longer feel cold at all.

Suppose, for reductio, that "feels cold" is luminous, so that at every ti at which the subject feels cold, they also know that they feel cold. The crucial auxiliary premise is a margin for error principle. Because introspection of one's own bodily sensations is not infallible down to arbitrarily fine degrees, knowing at ti that one feels cold takes more than just feeling cold at ti: since ti and ti+1 are subjectively indistinguishable, the belief "I feel cold" formed at ti is safe, and hence knowledge, only if the subject would not have formed that very same belief had they not actually been cold — that is, only if it is also true that they feel cold at neighboring times like ti+1. So knowledge at ti that one feels cold requires actually feeling cold at ti+1 as well.

Chaining the luminosity assumption together with the margin for error principle generates the contradiction: the subject feels cold at t0, so by luminosity they know it at t0, so by margin for error they must feel cold at t1, so by luminosity they know that too, and so on up the series, forcing the conclusion that they still feel cold at tn — which is false by hypothesis. Since every premise besides luminosity is secure, luminosity is what has to go: there is some ti at which the subject feels cold without being in a position to know it.

This bears on the KK principle because knowing that p is itself just another condition a subject can be in, so nothing stops us from rerunning the very same argument with C = "knows that p," letting the subject's evidence for some p accumulate or degrade gradually across an analogous series of cases. The conclusion is exactly parallel: knowledge is not luminous either, and therefore KK is false — one's own knowledge that p can never be secured merely by reflecting on the fact that one knows it, so the KK principle collapses entirely as a description of self-knowledge.
