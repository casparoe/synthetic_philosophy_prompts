I'm a retired reproductive physiologist (thirty-some years running a lab on hormone cycles in sheep and a couple other ungulates, if anyone cares) and now that I've got time on my hands I've been chewing through philosophy of biology books instead of gardening like a sensible retiree. A friend of mine who actually has a philosophy degree keeps telling me I'm oversimplifying, so I wrote up some notes toward a short essay and would love a second opinion before I embarrass myself showing it to her. The core of it: I went back and reread Gould and Lewontin's old spandrels paper, then Elisabeth Lloyd's book arguing the female orgasm is a byproduct of shared male/female developmental wiring (the nipple analogy, following Symons), not an adaptation. Lloyd is pretty merciless with every adaptive story on offer -- Baker and Bellis's sperm-retention "upsuck" idea, pair-bonding, the lot -- holding each to a strict testability standard and finding it wanting, then lets her own preferred byproduct account in almost for free, as the default that wins whenever nobody else clears the bar.

My essay's claim is that this looks less like "the adaptationists lost on the evidence" and more like "whoever gets to define the null hypothesis wins," with spandrel-talk dressed up to make an assumption look like a conclusion. Except -- and this is were I get stuck -- I also vaguely recall George Williams arguing that adaptation should be treated as a "special, onerous" concept you only invoke when a simpler non-adaptive explanation genuinely fails, which would actually license exactly the asymmetric treatment Lloyd gives. So is my "double standard" complaint just me failing to appreciate a real and defensible asymmetry between adaptive and non-adaptive hypotheses, or is there something to it after all? I'd like comments on whether the argument actually holds together logically, whether I'm being fair to Lloyd, and honestly whether this is even a fresh point or something every philosopher of biology already sorted out back in the 90s.
