I've been down a bit of a rabbit hole researching a piece off the back of that Ramsey biography from a few years ago (the "sheer excess of powers" one), and I keep returning to this short essay Ramsey called "Epilogue" -- he read it to the Apostles back in 1925, and it only got published after he died at 26. The gist is that the sheer size of the universe shouldn't make anyone feel small or make life less meaningful, since stars can't think or love and thats what actually matters to him, not cubic light-years. Whats funny is that Ramsey knew and sparred with Bertrand Russell, who'd been preaching almost the exact opposite mood for decades -- that "unyielding despair" business from "A Free Man's Worship," where meaning has to be a defiant achievement built on top of a universe that's fundamentally indifferent to us, and which Russell was still restating right through the twenties.

For the piece, my editor wants an actual head-to-head, not just "huh, interesting contrast." Could you compare Ramsey's stance in the Epilogue with Russell's cosmic-pessimist humanism? I want to know what each of them takes to be the fact that's supposed to bear on meaning (the universe's size, its indifference to us, the eventual heat death, whatever it is) and why it does or doesn't, on their view, actually threaten meaning. Then give me your own verdict: does Ramsey actually earn his cheerfulness, or is he just quietly changing the subject on something Russell was genuinely onto? Pick a side and justify it -- don't just both-sides it for me.
