So a bit of background before the actual question: I'm a swe on a growth team, and a few months ago we swapped our internal A/B test dashboard from the old fixed-sample-size / p-value setup to a Bayesian one, mostly because the pitch from whoever championed it was "you don't need to precommit to a sample size anymore, just watch the posterior live and ship whenever you're confident." Two people on my team, Dana (data scientist) and Marcus (backend eng who's kind of a stats hobbyist on the side), got into a whole thing over Slack about whether that pitch actually holds up. It kicked off because we'd shipped a change after the dashboard hit "97% probability B beats A" around day 4 of everyone refreshing it constantly, and then a month later the real numbers showed basically nothing had happened. I've got zero formal background in stats or philosophy of science, just picked up A/B testing on the job, so I can't tell whose right, and it's been bugging me. Below is roughly how their argument went, paraphrased but I tried to keep it fair to both sides:

1. Dana said the peeking itself isn't some loophole, it's basically the whole point of doing it this way: the posterior only depends on the data you actually collected, not on when or why you decided to stop looking at it. The old rule about fixing your sample size in advance was only ever a patch for a problem that's specific to p-values, and that problem just doesn't apply once you're updating a posterior instead of running a significance test.

2. Marcus said that argument proves too much: if the stopping rule really is irrelevant, you could leave the dashboard open forever and eventually catch a random swing up to "97% B wins" out of pure noise, just because you keep checking. He said that's basically what happened here, there was no real effect, they got a lucky peek, and "the stopping rule doesn't matter" is exactly the reasoning that gets you there.

3. Dana admitted you could get a misleading read if you're patient (or unlucky) enough, but said that's true of literally any statistical method, a false positive is a false positive and it's not special to peeking. Once you've stopped, the posterior is still the correct summary of the evidence given the data and the prior, full stop. The real lesson from the failed launch is that "97% off one metric on day 4" was weak evidence being treated as strong, so they should've required a stricter bar or a minimum runtime, not that checking early was itself illegitimate.

4. Marcus said that's basically conceding his point: if how long you're willing to keep checking, and how often, changes how often you end up fooled, then swapping p-values for posteriors doesn't get you out of needing some rule about runtime and thresholds, it just hides that requirement behind different math. Calling it "no need to precommit to a sample size" when you still secretly need discipline about when you're allowed to stop is, in his words, "just rebranding the same problem with better PR."

Neither of them backed down and it fizzled into an agree-to-disagree in the thread.
