Here is the question as it was posed to the class:

"Henry Sidgwick was a founding member of the Society for Psychical Research and served as its first president, and together with F. W. H. Myers, Edmund Gurney, and his wife Eleanor Sidgwick, he devoted decades to the empirical investigation of telepathy, apparitions, and mediumship. Drawing on *The Methods of Ethics*, explain (a) what Sidgwick means by the 'dualism of practical reason,' and (b) why, given his own philosophical commitments, he took this dualism to give him a specifically philosophical — rather than merely personal or religious — motive for pursuing psychical research. Then (c) briefly assess, by Sidgwick's own lights, whether that research ultimately accomplished the philosophical work he needed it to do."

And here is the student's answer:

"Sidgwick's ethical theory in *The Methods of Ethics* examines three methods — egoistic hedonism, intuitionism, and utilitarianism — and asks how far they actually agree once each is made fully consistent. His conclusion is mostly optimistic: he argues that common-sense intuitionism, properly refined, converges with utilitarianism, so that 'do what conduces to the general happiness' turns out to be the deliverance of ordinary morality once its principles are cleaned up. The one place this harmony breaks down is between egoism and utilitarianism: Sidgwick cannot produce a purely rational proof that it is always irrational to prefer a smaller good for oneself over a greater good for everyone, or vice versa. Practical reason, on his view, speaks with two equally authoritative voices, and he calls this the dualism of practical reason. He treats this as more than a piece of intellectual bookkeeping, since without some way of closing the gap he thinks the whole cosmos of duty threatens to collapse into what he calls chaos, since morality would then rest on an arbitrary choice between rival axioms rather than a single rational foundation.

This is where the psychical research comes in. Sidgwick thought the only non-question-begging way to close the gap was to show that self-interest and the general good actually coincide, and the traditional way of guaranteeing that coincidence was a doctrine of personal immortality with future rewards and punishments. Since Sidgwick had already lost confidence in revealed religion as a young man — part of why he resigned his Trinity fellowship rather than go on subscribing to doctrines he no longer believed — he could not simply help himself to that premise on faith, so he treated the survival of death as an empirical question that rigorous investigation might settle either way, and pursued it with Myers and Gurney using the same evidentiary standards he demanded elsewhere in philosophy.

Judged by Sidgwick's own standards, the project succeeded. The Census of Hallucinations that he and Eleanor Sidgwick organized found apparitions coinciding with a real, contemporaneous death at a rate well above chance, which Sidgwick accepted as solid evidence of telepathy, and by the end of his life he had also become persuaded by the cross-correspondences — the fragmentary, puzzle-like scripts produced independently by mediums in different countries — that they showed purposive communication from beyond the grave. Between the two results, he took the immortality premise his ethics needed to have finally been secured empirically, so the dualism of practical reason, although famous as the great unresolved problem of his published work, seems to have been something he privately considered settled by the time he died."
