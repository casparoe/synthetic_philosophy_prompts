In the 1650s, Thomas Hobbes and the mathematician John Wallis waged a bitter public dispute that was ostensibly about Hobbes's claimed quadrature of the circle in De Corpore, but turned on a deeper disagreement about what geometrical demonstration really is. Hobbes held that geometry is the one true science available to us precisely because its objects are of our own making: a line is the path traced by a moving point, a circle the trace of a line rotating about one of its endpoints, and to demonstrate a property of a figure is to exhibit the causes of its generation, in keeping with his broader materialist view that to know something is to know the motions of the bodies that produce it. Wallis, developing his method of indivisibles and infinite series in the Arithmetica Infinitorum, held that genuine mathematical results could be secured through arithmetical and symbolic reasoning owing nothing to any such physical construction.

Write a dialogue between Hobbes and Wallis, set in the midst of their controversy, in which each tries to talk the other out of his conception of geometrical objects and proof. Make it a real exchange in which each responds to the other's actual arguments rather than restating his own position, and in which each has to answer the strongest objection available to his opponent -- for Hobbes, that his construction-based method delivered a quadrature Wallis could and did prove false; for Wallis, that his indivisibles and inductive use of infinite series were themselves open to the charge of resting on no rigorous demonstration at all. Neither figure should come off as a strawman; give each the best version of his case.
