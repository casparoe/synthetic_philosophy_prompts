Ok so I got into it pretty deep with my friend Marcus last night about penal substitution (the Christian atonement doctrine, not, like, criminal law) -- he's taking a philosophy of religion seminar this semester and brought up that David Lewis, yes THE modal realism guy, wrote this weird short paper near the end of his life basically asking whether the doctrine even makes sense. His whole move is to distinguish paying off someone's debt from serving someone's punishment: if I owe you $500, literally anyone can hand you $500 and the debt is gone, no residue, because debts are impersonal and fungible. But if I've committed some crime and deserve to be punished for it, Lewis says that's not fungible in the same way -- the whole point of retributive punishment is that it targets the specific person whose wrongdoing it is, so having someone else suffer in my place doesn't actually satisfy what justice was supposedly demanding, it just adds one more person's suffering to the world. He's got this example (I think it's actually from history?) of a "whipping boy" -- a servant kept around specifically to be beaten whenever the young prince misbehaved, since you weren't allowed to hit royalty -- and the obvious thought is that the prince never actually gets punished for anything, no matter how many times the whipping boy bleeds for him.

Marcus's take was that this basically proves penal substitution is conceptually broken, and that Christians who say the doctrine "makes sense" to them are quietly borrowing the debt picture for the logic while keeping the emotional payoff of the punishment picture. I pushed back that theres a way to maybe save it: what if divine justice isn't retributive in Lewis's narrow sense at all, but more like a cosmic ledger where some fixed quota of suffering has to occur somewhere to restore a balance -- if that's the model, it stops mattering who supplies the suffering, same as with the debt. Marcus said that just relocates the problem, because now "justice" is so impersonal that it's not clear why the guilty party's suffering would've been required in the first place, or why God -- who presumably wrote the rules -- couldn't just cancel the ledger the way he could forgive an ordinary debt. We went around on this for like an hour and neither of us landed a real knockout blow.

Does Lewis's debt-vs-punishment distinction succeed in showing that penal substitution, at least as most Christians who affirm it seem to understand it, is conceptually incoherent? Or is there some way of cashing out "justice being satisfied" that rescues the doctrine from his argument?
