Since retiring after forty years as an experimental physicist, I have been working through the classic papers in philosophy of mind, and Alan Turing's 1950 essay proposing the imitation game brought me up short. The ninth and final objection he addresses there is what he calls "the argument from extra-sensory perception": he states that the statistical evidence for telepathy is overwhelming, worries that a psychic human contestant could give the game away by guessing concealed cards far more often than chance allows, and proposes that the test be run in a telepathy-proof room to neutralize the effect. Given that parapsychology's evidential record has since collapsed under proper scrutiny, doesn't this mean Turing's proposal was calibrated against a phenomenon we now know to be illusory, leaving the imitation game resting on a false empirical premise? I would value your candid assessment of how much weight this should really carry against the imitation game as a serious criterion for machine intelligence.
