On the rationalist blogs I've followed for years, sorites-style puzzles are usually dissolved rather than solved: the standard line, not far from Yudkowsky's treatment of concepts as clusters in thing-space rather than sets with sharp necessary and sufficient conditions, is that "heap" and "bald" simply don't carve reality at a joint, so the paradox is a symptom of expecting language to do something it never promised. Curious how philosophers who refuse that dissolution actually handle things, I spent this year working through Timothy Williamson's Vagueness and Rosanna Keefe's Theories of Vagueness, and I've come away more interested in epistemicism and supervaluationism than I expected, and more confused about how much daylight is really left between them once you get past the slogans. Both positions reject the sorites' inductive premise instead of blaming excluded middle or the coherence of "heap" itself, and both stay close to classical logic; where they part ways is over what makes that premise merely seem true. Williamson says there is a perfectly determinate cut-off number of grains, unknowable to us because knowledge requires a margin for error that no vague term can supply. Fine and Keefe say no cut-off is a fact about the world at all: truth is "supertruth" across every admissible way of making "heap" precise, individual borderline sentences fall into gaps, and only certain "penumbral connections" among them are guaranteed to survive every specification. Each side also owes the other an answer to a standard objection: the supervaluationist has to explain why "admissible specification" isn't itself vague in a way that just relocates the sharp boundary to a metalinguistic level instead of removing it (the higher-order vagueness problem), and the epistemicist has to explain why the margin-for-error story isn't just borrowing its plausibility from the very "vagueness is secretly precision" picture that made epistemicism sound strange to begin with.

I'd like you to write a dialogue that actually stages this, rather than re-explaining the sorites paradox for a general audience. Picture two women who shared an office in grad school and have taken opposite sides ever since: one wrote her dissertation defending epistemicism, the other has spent a decade trying to make supervaluationism handle higher-order vagueness. Reunited at a small workshop on vagueness, they're still going at it after the last talk of the day has ended. Have each press the other on exactly the objection above rather than restating her own position: the supervaluationist should force the epistemicist to show that our ignorance of the cut-off is principled rather than merely asserted, and the epistemicist should force the supervaluationist to say something precise about what fixes the admissible specifications, if not, at bottom, more vagueness all the way up. Let them reach for whatever standard sorites cases help -- heaps, colour patches, "tall" -- make the exchange genuinely technical, and let each concede small points without abandoning her overall position. I don't want either of them to win, or to discover they agreed all along; I want two people who know this literature far better than I do, leaving the conversation with their views intact but with a sharper sense of exactly where the other one is weakest.
