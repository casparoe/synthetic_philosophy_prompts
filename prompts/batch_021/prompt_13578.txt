Was on a work call with two researchers for a piece on adaptationism -- she's an evo-bio postdoc, he's more the philosophy-of-science type -- and about ten minutes in they forgot I was there and started arguing with each other instead of answering my questions. The topic: whether the female orgasm is a "spandrel" in the Gould-Lewontin sense (a byproduct of some other adaptation, not itself selected for). Honestly can't tell who won.

(1) She said it's obviously a spandrel: the clitoris and penis come from the same tissue early in development, so the orgasm "wiring" comes along for free with the male version -- same as Gould's old point about male nipples, nobody thinks those are adaptations.

(2) He said shared origin doesn't settle current function -- structures get re-purposed constantly (insect wings maybe started as heat-regulating flaps, the panda's "thumb" is a wrist bone doing a new job), and theres apparently real variation across primates in when something orgasm-like happens that tracks mating and social setups, which is what you'd expect if selection kept tinkering with it afterward.

(3) She said sure, exaptation is real, but you can't just gesture at "selection could've reused it" without testing that against the null case that it's just riding along -- that's literally what Gould and Lewontin were complaining about, and the specific adaptive stories on offer here (sperm-retention, pair-bonding) don't hold up once anyone checks the physiology.

(4) He said the byproduct story gets the same free pass though: "it's just a spandrel" is equally untested, and it's a suspiciously convenient place to stop looking right when things get interesting.

Neither budged. Who was actually right, move by move -- and is there something more basic both of them are missing?
