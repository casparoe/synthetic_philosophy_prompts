I have been reading Turing's 1950 paper "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" closely on my own — I have no formal training in philosophy or computer science, but the paper is short enough that I felt I could work through the whole argument rather than just the famous bits everyone quotes. The part that stopped me was the ninth objection he considers, the one about extrasensory perception. He does not raise it as a joke: he says the statistical evidence for telepathy is, in his own words, "overwhelming," and he seriously proposes making the imitation game "telepathy-proof" by putting the contestants in a room that would block any such transmission. Everything else I have read on this treats the passage as an embarrassment to be quietly set aside, a period curiosity with nothing to do with the real argument. I have written a short essay arguing that this common reaction is mistaken, and I would like your comments on whether the argument I make actually works.

Here is the essay:

Turing sets up the imitation game so that the interrogator communicates with both contestants only through written or typewritten answers, ideally relayed by teleprinter, and he is explicit that this is meant to strip away every cue — tone of voice, handwriting, appearance — that could let the interrogator settle the question without engaging the contestant's ability to hold up a conversation. The test is only interesting because that channel has been narrowed to conversation and nothing else. My claim is that the extrasensory perception passage is not a departure from this design but an application of it. If Turing genuinely believed, on the strength of the parapsychological evidence available to him, that some people can transmit or receive information telepathically, then a telepathic contestant possesses exactly the kind of extra channel the teleprinter was introduced to shut down: a way of settling who is "really" the machine that has nothing to do with conversational skill. Given that belief, closing off telepathy is not an ad hoc patch on the test but the same move, applied consistently, that motivated putting the contestants in separate rooms in the first place. On this reading, the extrasensory perception passage is not a sign that Turing's operationalism collapses under an eccentric belief; it is a sign that the operationalism is being followed through rigorously, into a case the rest of us can ignore only because we do not share his estimate of the evidence.

I suspect there is a real disanalogy I am not weighing properly: we know exactly what physical arrangement keeps sound and sight from crossing between two rooms, but nobody, including Turing, had any idea what would keep telepathy from crossing between them, if it existed at all, which makes "telepathy-proof" a very different kind of stipulation from "connected only by teleprinter." I would like your view on whether that disanalogy actually sinks my reading, and more generally on whether I am forcing a unity onto the paper that is not really there. I would also welcome any correction on matters of fact about the paper itself, since I am working entirely from my own reading of it.
