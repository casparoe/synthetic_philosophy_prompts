(a) The probability that the first heads is on toss n is (1/2)^n, since you need n-1 tails followed by a heads. Multiplying by the payoff gives (1/2)^n * (-1)^(n+1) * 2^n/n, which simplifies to (-1)^(n+1)/n. Summing from n=1 to infinity gives 1 - 1/2 + 1/3 - 1/4 + ..., the alternating harmonic series, which converges to ln(2), about 0.693.

(b) This series is only conditionally convergent, not absolutely convergent, since the series of absolute values (1 + 1/2 + 1/3 + ...) is the harmonic series and diverges. By the Riemann rearrangement theorem, a conditionally convergent series can be reordered to sum to any value at all, or to diverge, just by adding up the exact same terms in a different order. Since there's no principled reason to sum the outcomes in the order n = 1, 2, 3, ... rather than some other order, the 0.693 from part (a) isn't privileged over any other number. So underneath it all, Pasadena's expected value is infinite in the same basic way St. Petersburg's is — both are just cases of expected value theory breaking down and blowing up once you look closely.

(c) Bernoulli's fix was to replace dollar payoffs with the utility of those dollars, using something like a logarithmic utility function, so the huge payoffs way out in the tail of the St. Petersburg game get squashed down enough for the sum to converge. Since it's the same kind of unboundedly growing payoff that's driving the problem in Pasadena, making the same Bernoulli-style switch to expected utility resolves the Pasadena paradox for the same reason it resolves St. Petersburg — it's really just the same fix, applied to a fancier game.
