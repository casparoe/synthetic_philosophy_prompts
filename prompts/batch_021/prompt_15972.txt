I knew Jean-Charles de Borda long before I encountered his voting theory: as a fellow physicist, through the repeating circle he built for the survey that fixed the length of the meter. Only in retirement, reading around the mathematics of elections, did I learn of his other legacy: a rank-order ballot that the French Academy of Sciences used for years to elect new members, until it became so obviously exploitable — a voter could favor their own candidate simply by ranking the strongest rival artificially low — that Napoleon, freshly admitted to the Academy himself, pushed to have it scrapped. Borda's own reported response to the objection was that his method was "intended only for honest men." His rival Condorcet wanted no part of that shrug: he was after a procedure that would reliably distill a group's collective judgment about which candidate was genuinely best, treating a vote as something closer to a noisy measurement of an underlying fact than a tally of arbitrary tastes, which is partly why he proposed his own scheme of pairwise comparisons instead. Suppose you could show Condorcet the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem (discovered, aptly enough for my new hobby, by a philosopher and an economist working independently) — which proves that once there are three or more options, every non-dictatorial voting rule whatsoever, his own pairwise method included, leaves some voter better off lying about their true ranking on some ballot. Would he have taken this as a refutation of his life's ambitions for the mathematics of voting, or might his own view of voters as fallible witnesses to a shared truth, rather than as mere holders of arbitrary preferences, point him toward some way of living with manipulability that a straightforward preference theorist like Borda would never have thought to look for?
