So I've been kind of obsessed with general relativity as a hobby for the last year or so (no formal physics or philosophy background, just youtube lectures and slowly working through a couple of textbooks), and I went down a rabbit hole reading about this "hole argument" thing that apparently bugged Einstein for a couple of years before he finished GR in 1915. The way I get it: say you've got a solution to the field equations with some region of totally empty space in it, no matter at all. You can smoothly warp the metric around inside just that one empty region while leaving literally everything outside it untouched, and you still end up with a mathematically valid solution to the same equations. Now if you think the points of spacetime are real things in their own right, independent of whatever field values happen to sit on them (I guess this is what's called "substantivalism," though I only know that from a footnote somewhere), then the exact same past and boundary data turns out to be compatible with two different "insides" for the hole. People treat this as a genuine threat to determinism in GR, not just some throwaway curiosity, which is what hooked me on it in the first place.

Here's the fix I talked myself into before reading much further, and I want to know if it actually holds up: isn't this basically like having two maps of the same country where all you did was swap which dot is labeled "New York" and which is labeled "Boston," but every road and distance between the dots stays exactly the same? Nothing physical changed, you just swapped the name tags on paper, so the two "solutions" to the hole thing aren't really rival futures, their the same physical situation written down twice. Once you say points don't come with some invisible name tag that isn't reflected in their relations to everything else, the paradox just evaporates and determinism is fine. Best part is it feels like I still get to say spacetime points are "real" in some sense, I just have to drop the idea that they carry identity over and above their relations.

I ran this by a friend who took one philosophy of science course years ago and he just said "it's not that simple" but couldn't really tell me why. So: is my map analogy actually doing the work I think it's doing, or is there a gap in it I'm not seeing? And if biting this bullet is basically fine, does it quietly turn me into some kind of relationalist about space and time, even though it doesnt feel like im denying spacetime points exist at all?
