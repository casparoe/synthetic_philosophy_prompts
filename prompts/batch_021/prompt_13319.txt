In my seminar on Hobbes's natural and civil philosophy, I posed the question below on Hobbes's philosophy of geometry, and one student's answer has left me undecided about how much credit it deserves. I would like your independent assessment before I finalize grades.

Question: Hobbes maintains that geometry is the most certain of the sciences because we ourselves construct its objects, and he extends this reasoning to claim that his civil philosophy can attain the same certainty as geometry. Explain (a) how Hobbes's materialist definitions of the point and the line depart from Euclid's and support this claim, (b) how the resulting argument for geometry's certainty is meant to carry over to civil philosophy, and (c) what bearing, if any, the Hobbes–Wallis dispute over the quadrature of the circle should have on our assessment of Hobbes's position.

Student's answer: "For Hobbes, geometry is uniquely certain among the sciences because we construct its objects ourselves and can therefore know their causes completely. This is tied to his materialism: since only bodies exist, a point cannot really be an entity with no extension at all, as Euclid's definition suggests; Hobbes instead redefines a point as a body whose magnitude is simply not considered in a given demonstration. Lines are then generated by the motion of such a point, surfaces by the motion of a line, and so on, so that geometrical figures are the traces left by bodies in motion rather than static abstract objects. Because we ourselves stipulate these motions, we have complete causal knowledge of the figures that result, which is exactly what Hobbes takes scientific knowledge to require. In effect, since we invent the very objects geometry studies, its truths are for Hobbes ultimately conventions we impose on the world, no different in kind from the rules of chess, and this is what guarantees their certainty. Hobbes then argues that civil philosophy can achieve the same certainty, because the commonwealth is likewise artificial: since sovereignty, law, and justice are instituted by covenant rather than found ready-made in nature, we can know their causes just as securely as we know the properties of a triangle we have drawn ourselves. This parallel is central to Hobbes's ambition of putting political philosophy on the same demonstrative footing as geometry. That whole picture, however, is undercut by the Hobbes–Wallis affair: once Wallis had conclusively refuted Hobbes's claimed quadrature of the circle, Hobbes revised his position and dropped the claim, tacitly conceding that Wallis's more modern, algebraic conception of geometry was superior to his own. Since Hobbes could not even correctly practice the science he took as his model, the construction-based argument for the certainty of civil philosophy loses much of its force."
