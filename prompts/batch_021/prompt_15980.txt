Question (25 points): "Explain Bernard Williams's distinction between internal and external reason statements, and reconstruct his argument for thinking that external reason statements are false or unintelligible. Then present one significant objection to Williams from the secondary literature and briefly assess how much force it has against him."

My classmate's answer:

"Williams distinguishes two kinds of reason statements. An internal reason statement, 'A has a reason to X,' is true only if A could arrive at X-ing by a sound process of deliberation starting from things already in A's 'subjective motivational set' (S) — roughly, A's desires, projects, and commitments. If nothing in S could ever lead A, by correct reasoning, to X, then it is false that A has a reason to X, however desirable X might look from the outside. An external reason statement, by contrast, claims that A has a reason to X regardless of what is in A's S: it is meant to hold for A simply in virtue of A's being a rational agent, independently of what A happens to want or care about. Moral philosophers who think that every rational being has a reason to keep promises or avoid cruelty whether or not they care about morality are committed to external reason statements of this kind.

Williams thinks external reason statements are false, or at best internal reason statements in disguise. His central argument is that reasons must be capable of explaining and motivating action, and only elements of an agent's existing motivational set (or things reachable from it by deliberation) can do this; 'pure reason' cannot manufacture a new motivation out of nothing. This is essentially the internalist thesis that a moral judgement only counts as a reason for an agent if the agent is thereby motivated to act on it: if I sincerely judge that I have a reason to do X, I must to that extent be moved to do it, and Williams denies that this connection can be secured by external, desire-independent considerations alone.

One influential objection comes from Christine Korsgaard, who appeals to an Aristotelian picture of practical wisdom: a properly brought-up agent, possessing the virtues, comes to see certain considerations as reasons for action as part of acquiring 'second nature,' so the reason is not simply read off a pre-existing desire, yet it is still capable of motivating a virtuous person. On this view, external reasons need not be inert in the way Williams suggests, because ethical upbringing can make an agent sensitive to reasons that were not already represented anywhere in their prior S.

Overall, Williams's argument is powerful but not fully conclusive: it works well against a reason that is supposed to move any agent whatsoever regardless of their psychology, but it is less clear that it defeats a more modest externalism that only requires reasons to be available to a suitably rational and well brought-up agent."
