A friend of mine and I had a long argument this week about a mutual friend, Priya, who has served as treasurer for our climbing club for the past two years without a single problem. The club started using a banking app that automatically flags and reverses any transaction outside a pre-approved list of vendors, and that requires a second signature for anything above a small threshold, after an earlier treasurer was caught quietly siphoning off dues. My friend's position, which he argued with real conviction, is that these controls mean we have no genuine evidence that Priya is honest: she has simply never had an unobstructed opportunity to take money for herself, so for all anyone knows she would do it without hesitation if the safeguards vanished tomorrow, and two years of immaculate books therefore tell us nothing at all about her character.

I found this conclusion hard to accept but harder to rebut. My instinct is that if someone behaves impeccably for two years, fully aware that the controls are watching, that ought to count as real evidence of good character, and that treating an untested, unguarded opportunity as the only acceptable proof of honesty sets a standard that almost nobody we would ordinarily call honest could actually satisfy. But I could not explain why a safeguard that would immediately catch and reverse any attempted theft should fail to undercut the inference to her character, especially once my friend pointed out that we would judge the reverse case quite differently: if dues had in fact gone missing right under the safeguard's nose, none of us would hesitate to call whoever was responsible dishonest, safeguard or not. We went back and forth for over an hour without either of us budging. I would like to know whether my friend has actually identified a real problem with how we attribute character to people who operate under this kind of constraint, or whether there's a good reason two years of clean behavior should count as real evidence regardless of the guardrails behind it.
