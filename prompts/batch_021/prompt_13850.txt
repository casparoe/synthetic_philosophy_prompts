I'm currently taking a seminar on democratic theory, and this week's session was built around Robert Goodin's defense of the all-affected interests principle as a solution to the boundary problem -- the question of who should count as part of the demos for any given collective decision. After section, a few of us kept debating it over coffee, and I came away thinking the principle actually settles the problem quite cleanly. Rather than relying on seemingly arbitrary facts like citizenship or residency to decide who gets a vote, we simply ask whose interests will be affected by the decision at hand and enfranchise exactly that group. Take a country's climate or energy policy as an example: because virtually everyone alive, and arguably future generations too, will be affected by it to some degree, the all-affected principle seems to hand us a determinate answer straightaway -- the demos for that decision is close to the whole of humanity, present and future -- rather than leaving the boundary question open.

Given that, I'm puzzled by why our other readings, including Sofia Näsström's piece, keep treating the boundary problem as one of the field's central unresolved puzzles even after the all-affected principle is on the table. Is there some further gap in the principle itself that I'm not seeing, or is the remaining disagreement really just about whether theorists are willing to accept its practical consequences -- something close to a global, intergenerational electorate for most significant policy questions -- rather than about whether it gives us a workable criterion for drawing the boundary in the first place?
