For the better part of thirty-five years I ran a laser spectroscopy lab, and like most working experimentalists I settled into a comfortable "shut up and calculate" attitude toward quantum foundations: the formalism matched what came off the photodiodes, and that was as far as my curiosity needed to travel. Retirement has finally given me time to read the interpretive literature I always set aside, and one argument keeps needling me: the "quantum suicide" thought experiment built on the many-worlds interpretation, according to which an observer who repeatedly submits to a device with a fifty percent quantum-mechanical chance of killing them outright should, from their own first-person perspective, expect to go on surviving indefinitely, since the only branches containing anyone able to notice anything are the ones in which they survived.

What keeps this from being a throwaway curiosity is that serious people disagree, in quite technical ways, about what the argument actually establishes. Tegmark is willing to treat the resulting "immortality" as a genuine, if practically worthless, consequence of no-collapse quantum mechanics. David Lewis, in an essay on the topic (I believe it's called something like "How Many Lives Has Schrödinger's Cat?", and which only appeared after his death) pressed hard on what would have to be true about chance and personal identity across branching for that reassurance to amount to more than a trick of perspective. And a recurring objection, which I've seen credited to Jacques Mallah among others, accuses the whole argument of a "branch-counting fallacy": a rational agent, so the charge goes, should weight branches by their vanishing Born-rule measure, not by the mere existence of a surviving branch. That dispute seems to feed into two further debates I don't feel equipped to referee on my own: the question, following what I understand is Peter Lewis's quantum-mechanical spin on the Sleeping Beauty problem, of how self-locating credence ought to behave under branching in the first place, and the question of whether the Deutsch-Wallace decision-theoretic derivation of the Born rule gives a many-worlds believer principled grounds to treat near-total loss of measure as being just as bad as ordinary death.

I would like you to write me an essay that actually adjudicates this dispute rather than merely surveying it. Lay out the strongest form of the quantum-immortality argument, the strongest objections to it (the branch-counting charge, and whichever considerations from self-locating belief and Everettian decision theory bear on it), and whatever work personal-identity theory is quietly doing underneath the disagreement. Then commit to a verdict: does a many-worlds-believing person have any good, reflectively defensible reason to fear death less than a single-world believer would, and should this bear at all on real decisions involving mortal risk? A qualified verdict is entirely acceptable -- something like "the argument is valid on its own terms but decision-theoretically toothless because X" would count as taking a side -- but I don't want the essay to end by simply reporting that both camps have a point.
