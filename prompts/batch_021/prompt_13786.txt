Last night, a friend and I had a long argument about Jain anekāntavāda that neither of us managed to resolve. My friend maintained that once one accepts that no single naya can express the whole truth about an object, one is committed to a thoroughgoing relativism, on which every philosophical system is simply "true from its own standpoint" and none can be judged mistaken. I resisted this conclusion, since classical Jain thinkers plainly used anekāntavāda to argue that specific rival doctrines—an extreme insistence on permanence, say, or on momentariness—were one-sided errors rather than partial truths, yet I could not quite explain why this does not just collapse into the relativism my friend was describing. Given how syādvāda's conditional predications and the distinction between a legitimate naya and a fallacious one (nayābhāsa) are actually supposed to function, does anekāntavāda amount to relativism about truth, and if not, what within the doctrine does the work of ruling standpoints out rather than treating them all as equally valid?
