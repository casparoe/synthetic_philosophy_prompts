I'm several chapters into a novel set in Newton's Cambridge, built around the year he sent Locke a manuscript on two corrupted verses of the New Testament and then, at the last moment, asked for it back. I've drafted the note below to anchor an eventual author's afterword, and I'd like your comments on it before I let it shape any scenes.

Newton and the Two Corruptions

In 1690, Isaac Newton sent John Locke a manuscript arguing that two of the New Testament's clearest proof-texts for the Trinity — the Johannine comma at 1 John 5:7 and the disputed reading of 1 Timothy 3:16 — were not apostolic at all, but insertions made by later, doctrinally motivated copyists. Newton's philology was careful: he traced the manuscript history, noted that the comma was absent from the earliest Greek witnesses and from the Church Fathers who would have had every reason to cite it in their own controversies had they known it, and concluded that orthodoxy had, at some point in the fourth century, edited its own evidence. He wanted the piece published, but anonymously and abroad — Locke was to arrange for it to be translated and placed with Jean Le Clerc in Holland. Then, with the manuscript nearly in Le Clerc's hands, Newton asked for it back.

The reversal looks like timidity, but I think it is better read as calculation. Newton was a fellow of Trinity College and the Lucasian Professor of Mathematics, a chair that carried an unusual dispensation, secured in 1675, excusing its holder from the ordination normally required of college fellows — a dispensation Newton needed because he could not in conscience have sworn to doctrines he had come to regard as fraudulent. To publish, even anonymously, was to gamble a career built on public orthodoxy against a private conviction — Arian, by most accounts, though he never claimed the label himself — that the whole Trinitarian settlement, Nicaea and Athanasius and everything built on them, rested on corrupted texts and, beneath that, on a corruption of the pure, monotheistic Christianity he believed the apostles had actually taught. This was not a side interest. It sat beside his chronological work on Daniel and Revelation, his dating of prophetic history, and his decades of alchemical research, all of which he seems to have treated as branches of one recovery project: stripping away centuries of accreted error to reach the uncorrupted original, whether in scripture, in prophecy, or in matter itself.

None of this became public in his lifetime. The theological manuscripts stayed in the family for nearly two centuries, were judged by the Cambridge committee that catalogued Newton's papers in the 1870s to have no scientific interest, and were finally dispersed at a Sotheby's sale in 1936; John Maynard Keynes bought much of the alchemical material himself and came away arguing that Newton was less the first of the modern rationalists than the last of the great magi, still working in a tradition that reached back to Babylon.

What I find useful, for the novel, is less the heresy itself than the discipline of its concealment: decades spent writing millions of words that could have ended his career, while remaining, to every outward appearance, a conforming and famously difficult Anglican professor. The corruption he thought he had found in the old texts, he reproduced, in miniature, in his own biography.

I'd value three things from you. First, flag anything that looks factually shaky or overstated — I'm especially unsure whether I've represented the link between the ordination dispensation and the Lucasian professorship correctly, and whether "Arian" is really the fair label for Newton's Christology rather than something more idiosyncratic. Second, quite apart from accuracy: does the note's closing claim — that concealment, not conviction, is the more dramatically useful fact about Newton — actually survive scrutiny, or does it flatten something more complicated? Third, if you had to point me toward two further details from Newton's theological manuscripts that I haven't used here, which would most reward a novelist's attention, and why?
