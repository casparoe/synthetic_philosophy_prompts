I've been working through Caspar Oesterheld's writings on evidential cooperation in large worlds (ECL) for a seminar paper on acausal reasoning in decision theory, and while I can reconstruct the basic mechanics, there is one structural piece of the argument I can't quite pin down. As I understand it, because my decision procedure is correlated, in the evidential sense, with the decision procedures of other agents with whom I have no causal contact, learning that "I" cooperate (say, by giving some weight to value systems other than my own) is evidence that suitably similar agents elsewhere do the same. An evidential decision theorist should therefore treat the situation as a kind of bargaining problem with unmet, causally isolated partners, generating something like gains from trade across radically different value systems.

What I keep getting stuck on is why the argument needs the world, or multiverse, to be large or infinite, rather than just relying on the correlation itself, which already seems to be doing all the real work in an ordinary superrationality case, such as a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma against a known psychological twin. Oesterheld and others treat "large world" as an essential premise rather than a colorful setting, and I would like help disentangling exactly what job it is doing. Is the point that a large or infinite population makes it close to certain that correlated partners holding a wide diversity of values actually exist somewhere, so the trade is not merely hypothetical? Is it instead about turning many individually shaky pairwise correlations into a single aggregated expected-value case that only becomes compelling in the limit of many potential partners? Or does "large world" also carry a further metaphysical commitment, tied to specific cosmological pictures such as a spatially infinite universe or a Tegmark-style multiverse, that a merely large-but-finite world would not secure? I would appreciate an explanation that keeps these candidate roles separate rather than treating "large world" as a single, unanalyzed modifier.
