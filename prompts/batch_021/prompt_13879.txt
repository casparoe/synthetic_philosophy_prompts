For the past two months, my school's team for the National High School Ethics Bowl has been preparing a case about a small rural clinic that runs out of a critical medication during a disaster and must decide whether to give its one remaining dose to a single patient who needs the whole amount to survive, or split it among several other patients who each need only a fraction of it. Almost everyone on the team assumed, without much discussion, that you obviously save the greater number, until our coach had us read John Taurek's 1977 paper "Should the Numbers Count?", which pushes back hard on exactly that assumption. As I understand Taurek's central move, he denies that the deaths of five people can simply be added together into one loss that outweighs the death of one person, since there is no single person for whom "five deaths" is a worse outcome than "one death" — each of the six people at risk stands to lose the very same thing, namely their own life, and Taurek doesn't think the mere existence of four other people with equally serious claims should count against the one. He then suggests that when you genuinely cannot save everyone, flipping a coin between the two options may respect each person's claim more fairly than automatically favoring the larger group.

This has split our team pretty sharply, and before our next practice round I would like a carefully reasoned outside perspective. Could you write me an essay that first lays out Taurek's argument in enough detail to show why a coin flip, rather than straightforward aggregation, might be thought to treat people as equals, and then puts that argument in dialogue with at least one serious reply from the philosophical literature — for instance, Derek Parfit's response in "Innumerate Ethics," or T. M. Scanlon's attempt, in What We Owe to Each Other, to justify letting numbers count within a contractualist framework? Please don't settle for a "there are good points on both sides" ending: I would like the essay to close by actually taking a side on whether, and under what conditions, the numbers should count, and to defend that verdict with argument rather than simply restating whichever intuition feels stronger.
