I've recently gone down a bit of a rabbit hole into the "cognitive penetrability of perception" debate in philosophy of mind, after a friend who's doing a PhD in cognitive science brought it up in the context of predictive-processing models of the brain. As I understand the setup, the question is whether beliefs, desires, and expectations can directly change the content of what we perceptually experience, rather than merely biasing the judgments we form afterward about what we saw. The examples I keep running into seem to pull in different directions: hills apparently looking steeper to people who are tired or wearing a heavy backpack, a shape with a well-known "natural" colour — a banana, a heart — looking faintly tinted with that colour even when it's actually rendered in flat gray, but also the hollow-face illusion, where a mask viewed from the inside still looks convex even once you're explicitly told, and can verify by touch, that it's concave. Philosophers seem to disagree sharply about which, if any, of these actually show cognition reaching into perception itself, as opposed to something else entirely.

What I'm specifically stuck on is a move I keep seeing skeptics of cognitive penetration make. They'll often concede that a given top-down effect is real, but then argue it doesn't demonstrate penetration of perception proper, because the effect is actually running through some other channel instead — a shift of attention, a memory-colour or perceptual-learning effect, or plain old post-perceptual judgment and response bias — rather than a change to early visual processing itself. When I first ran into this move it struck me as suspiciously convenient: any result that would count as evidence for penetration just gets reclassified as "not really perception" after the fact, which would seem to make the impenetrability thesis unfalsifiable by construction. I assume, though, that there's supposed to be some principled, independently motivated way of drawing that line, rather than this being pure post hoc rescue. Could you explain what that principled criterion is actually supposed to be — what would have to be true of an effect for it to count as genuine cognitive penetration of perceptual content rather than one of these alternative channels — and give me your honest sense of whether that line actually holds up against the examples above, or whether the skeptics really are just moving the goalposts?
