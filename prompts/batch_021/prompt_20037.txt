I sit on the scientific advisory panel of a foundation that funds frontier physics research. We are deciding on a $3 million proposal to build a satellite instrument precise enough to measure the large-scale curvature of space and settle which of two live, observationally tied cosmological models is correct: on Model A the universe is finite and comparatively modest in extent, so only a limited number of observers arise over its history; on Model B it is finite but many orders of magnitude larger, so the same underlying physics yields a proportionately larger population of observers. A philosopher on the panel wants us to decline the proposal: she argues that our own existence already confirms Model B with near-certainty, since finding ourselves in existence at all is far more to be expected if there are far more observers to be among -- so the satellite would spend three million dollars confirming what reflection on our own existence already tells us for free. Several scientists on the panel sense something is wrong with this argument but can't pin down what, and I recognize it as a version of what Bostrom called the presumptuous philosopher thought experiment. Please draft the memo I will circulate before Friday's vote: it should recommend whether to fund, decline, or conditionally fund the proposal, on what timeline, and it should engage her argument on its own terms rather than wave it away, being honest about what remains genuinely unresolved between self-sampling and self-indication approaches to this kind of reasoning.
