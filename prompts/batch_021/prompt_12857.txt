After a long career in experimental physics, I have spent much of my retirement reading my way through decision theory, and I find myself repeatedly stuck on Piccione and Rubinstein's absent-minded driver problem. A driver must take the second of two consecutive, indistinguishable highway exits to reach home: taking the first exit is worst (payoff 0), sailing past both is middling (payoff 1), and catching the second is best (payoff 4). Because he genuinely cannot tell the two intersections apart, his only available strategy is a single probability of continuing, applied identically regardless of which intersection he has actually reached. What I have been unable to resolve is why the "planning-optimal" probability — the one maximizing expected payoff as computed before setting off — should be treated as a substantively different quantity from the "action-optimal" probability that falls out of updating, at the intersection itself, on the odds that one is at the first exit rather than the second. In my own field, I am accustomed to ensemble averages and single-trajectory statistics agreeing once one is careful about what exactly is being averaged over; here it seems the very act of being careful is what generates the puzzle. Could you unpack, in some detail, exactly where and why these two ways of cashing out rationality can part company, and what this reveals about the coherence of self-locating belief more generally — including whether this problem is best understood as a cousin of the Sleeping Beauty puzzle or as making a genuinely separate point?
