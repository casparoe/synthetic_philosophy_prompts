I am a novelist nearing the end of a book whose central figure, Judith Ansel, is a legal philosopher retiring after more than forty years as the last unapologetic defender of legal positivism in her department—the sort of scholar who still corrects dinner guests who say some evil regime's decrees "weren't really law." I've been putting off drafting the retirement dinner, which is the scene the whole book has been quietly building toward. Roughly half the room will be jurisprudence colleagues who know exactly what is meant by the separability thesis and by "the grudge informer case"; the other half will be family and old friends who have heard Judith complain about a man named Fuller for decades without ever quite following why. Two tables away sits Owen Marsh, her oldest friend, sharpest critic, and lifelong champion of the natural-law side, who spent one ugly year in the 1970s telling anyone who would listen that Judith's first and most Hartian paper—which argued that a postwar German court had confused a Nazi statute's validity with its merits when it declared the grudge informer's law no law at all, and that the woman should instead have been convicted under an honestly retroactive statute—made Judith an apologist for tyranny. She was not, and everyone serious in the room now knows it, but the wound is old enough that the toast cannot pretend it never happened, and raw enough that it cannot be played for a cheap laugh.

What I need is the toast itself, delivered by Priya Okonkwo, Judith's favorite former doctoral student and now a rising scholar in her own right, running to about five minutes read aloud. It should be affectionate rather than reverent, and it has to get Judith's actual position right: positivism is a thesis about what makes a rule law, not a thesis that law deserves obedience regardless of what it commands, and I don't want the speech to flatten that distinction even in service of a laugh. Somewhere in it I need exactly one joke built on the title of Fuller's real reply to Hart, "Positivism and Fidelity to Law," repurposed as a line about Judith's own marriage, so that the jurisprudence contingent gets a genuine citation and the family gets a joke about fidelity. And it needs to let Owen's presence register—a sentence or two, no more, honest enough about the old accusation that anyone who remembers it will feel the speech has actually looked at it, before moving on toward something closer to affection than argument, since the scene right after the toast is the two of them finally talking again.

I'm not a legal philosopher myself, so I'd rather the substance be exactly right than merely atmospheric: nothing in the speech should be interchangeable with a paragraph from a general summary of the Hart-Fuller debate. Please give me the toast itself, in Priya's voice, rather than notes on how one might write it.
