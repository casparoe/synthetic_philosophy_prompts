I'm the staff scientist for a farm-animal-welfare coalition, and for the better part of a decade my job has been to write the "sentience" sections of our legislative testimony: the page or two, usually near the front of any bill we back, that establishes, before anyone argues about crate sizes or transport times, that hens and pigs are creatures capable of real suffering, and that this suffering is what gives the rest of the bill its moral weight. Two weeks ago, at a roundtable ahead of a markup session, a philosopher retained by the opposing trade association raised illusionism about phenomenal consciousness -- the view associated with Keith Frankish, with Daniel Dennett as a kind of patron saint -- and argued that since a serious and growing minority of philosophers of mind now hold that human beings are simply mistaken when they introspect a rich, ineffable "feel" to their own experience, our field's confident talk of animals "really suffering," as opposed to merely behaving as if in pain, is leaning on a folk-psychological category that the relevant experts are busy dismantling. Nobody at the table had a ready answer, and the meeting moved on to fecal scoring before anyone circled back to it.

It has been gnawing at me since, because if he is right, this is not a minor technicality: it would mean the entire premise I have built a career on -- that there is a real, felt difference between a hen who is suffering and one who is merely behaving a certain way -- does not actually refer to anything, in hens or in us. Before I am back at the table next month, I would like to understand how much of that inference actually follows from illusionism itself, and, assuming it does follow, how a welfare organization is even supposed to keep making its case once the "felt" part of felt suffering has been taken off the table.
