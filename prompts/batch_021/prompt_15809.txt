Over the past year, Huntington's disease has gone from a distant, unfortunate fact about my family history to a decision I now have to make about my own life. My mother was diagnosed last year, which means I have a fifty percent chance of carrying the same mutation myself, and a genetic test exists that would tell me, essentially with certainty, whether I will eventually develop the disease. There is currently no treatment that can prevent, slow, or delay its onset, so nothing about my medical care would change depending on the result; the only thing that would change is what I know about my own future.

I've been talking through whether to get tested with my sister, who is finishing a PhD in economics, and she brought up a result she called Good's theorem, which she summarized as showing that a rational, expected-utility-maximizing agent should never decline free information relevant to a decision they face, since they can always simply disregard it and act exactly as they would have otherwise; in expectation, such information can therefore only help you or leave you no worse off. Since I obviously care about my own peace of mind and about how I live out whatever time I have, she concluded that declining the test could never really be the rational choice for someone in my position.

This struck me as too quick, especially once a genetic counselor mentioned that most people who are at risk for Huntington's actually choose not to get tested when it's offered to them, which sits oddly with the idea that they are all simply making a mistake. I'd like you to help me get clear on whether my sister's use of the theorem actually holds up. Specifically, please construct the strongest case you can that Good's theorem does not show it is always rational to want cost-free information of this kind, by being precise about what the theorem assumes and pinpointing exactly where a situation like mine falls outside those assumptions.
