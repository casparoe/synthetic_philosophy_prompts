I'm assembling case-based dialogues for a seminar on the ethics of war, and I'd like one that stages the disagreement over the moral equality of combatants as sharply as possible. Write a dialogue between a traditionalist, following Walzer, who holds that combatants who comply with jus in bello are moral equals and equally legitimate targets whatever the justice of their cause, and a revisionist, following McMahan, who holds that liability to lethal force tracks whether one is actually fighting for a just cause, so that only unjust combatants are liable to be killed, no matter how scrupulously they observe the rules of war. Anchor the exchange in a single case: two soldiers, one fighting in an objectively just war and the other in an objectively unjust one, each fully compliant with the laws of war and, let us stipulate, entirely blameless for believing that his own side's cause is just. The revisionist must explain how a blameless soldier can be a legitimate target of lethal force for something he isn't culpable for, and the traditionalist must explain why treating the two soldiers identically doesn't simply paper over a real moral asymmetry between them; let the disagreement stand unresolved at the end.
