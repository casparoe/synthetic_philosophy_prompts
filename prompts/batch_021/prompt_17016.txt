I'm a PhD student in philosophy of science, and this term our Friday reading group has been working through Bayesian confirmation theory -- we just finished the section on the "problem of old evidence" in Earman's *Bayes or Bust?*, using the standard example of Mercury's perihelion: the ~43 arcsec/century anomaly in its orbit that Newtonian mechanics couldn't explain but that dropped straight out of Einstein's field equations once he worked them out in 1915. Afterwards two people in the group, Reza and Ilana, got into an argument over drinks that turned into a real back-and-forth, and I can't referee it myself, so hoping you can.

(1) Reza opened by saying the old-evidence case just refutes Bayesian conditionalization as a theory of confirmation, full stop: the community's credence in the perihelion data was already 1 (or near enough) decades before 1915, so conditionalizing on it literally cannot move anyone's credence in GR, and yet everyone agrees this was one of the most dramatic confirmations in the history of physics -- so the model gets its own field's most famous case exactly backwards. (2) Ilana said this moves too fast: what actually happened in November 1915 wasn't a change in anyone's credence in the perihelion data, it was the discovery that GR *entails* that exact number, so you should conditionalize on "GR entails E" rather than on E itself, and once you do that the confirmation shows up fine -- she said she thinks this is basically the standard "Garber" move here, though don't quote me on the name. (3) Rezas comeback was that this quietly gives up logical omniscience, the assumption baked into the whole conditionalization framework that an ideal agent already assigns credence 1 to every entailment among the propositions in their space -- and you don't get to just drop that and keep calling the result "conditionalization" without owing a totally new account of how learning a logical fact even works. (4) Ilana said that's a cost worth paying, since nobody serious thought real scientists were logically omniscient anyway, and everything else about the framework can stay as is. (5) Reza's last move was that it's not that cheap: if merely discovering that a theory entails already-known data is what does the confirming, then any theory you gerrymandered after the fact to entail that same data should get an identical boost, which seems to erase the distinction between GR's result and ordinary ad hoc curve-fitting -- and the only thing that seems to block that is some notion of how surprising or unforced the entailment is, which isn't a Bayesian notion at all.

Neither of them budged, and honestly I go back and forth every time I retell it. Could you go through moves (1) through (5) and say who's got the better of it at each stage, not just who wins overall -- and flag anything you think both of them are missing? And then stand back for a second: given move (5), is there actually a way to rescue a Bayesian treatment of old evidence, or does the exchange show that whatever's doing the confirming in cases like this isn't Bayesian updating at all?
