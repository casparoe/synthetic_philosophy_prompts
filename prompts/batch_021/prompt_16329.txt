After four decades spent measuring quantities that do not care what anyone believes about them, I retired into a branch of philosophy that has unsettled me in a way no laboratory result ever did: anthropic reasoning. Working through Bostrom's writing on observer-selection effects, I have found myself stuck on the disagreement between the Self-Sampling Assumption and the Self-Indication Assumption, and especially on his "presumptuous philosopher." In that thought experiment, two physicists regard two rival cosmological theories as equally well supported by the evidence and are preparing a costly experiment to decide between them, only for a philosopher to interrupt and insist that the theory implying vastly more observers in the universe must already be overwhelmingly more probable, purely because SIA has him reason as though he were drawn at random from all possible observers rather than all actual ones. What strikes me is that two people can agree on every physical fact and still be driven, by nothing but self-locating logic, to opposite conclusions about the universe. The "safer" assumption, SSA, is no refuge either, since it generates the Doomsday Argument, which hard-SF writers have already mined for fiction (Stephen Baxter's Manifold: Time, unless memory fails me). I would like to see the presumptuous philosopher given comparable treatment. Please write a short story, set in whatever milieu serves it best, in which the tension between SSA and SIA plays out through characters with genuine stakes in which cosmological theory turns out to be true, keeping the reasoning precise enough that a specialist would recognize exactly what is at issue, and without letting the philosophy collapse into exposition dumped on the reader.
