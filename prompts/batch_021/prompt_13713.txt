A friend and I spent two hours last weekend arguing about the surprise exam paradox: a teacher announces there will be a surprise exam next week; a student 'proves' by backward induction that no exam fitting that description can occur -- it can't fall on the last possible day, since by then it would be expected, so it can't fall on the second-to-last day either, and so on back to the first day -- yet the teacher gives one anyway and genuinely catches everyone off guard. My friend calls the argument invalid and dismissible; I think it points to something real, that the announcement behaves less like a false prediction than like a claim that becomes unknowable to its audience the moment it is believed.

The disagreement turned out to matter more than I expected. I sit on my school's academic policy committee, and a teacher has asked for approval to reinstate unannounced, graded 'surprise quizzes,' given at some point within a two-week unit, with the only promise being that students won't know which day it will fall on until it happens. The student council's formal objection runs exactly the backward-induction argument above and concludes that the policy is either incoherent or bound to violate its own terms; the teacher calls this 'a clever trick, not a real objection.' Draft the committee's decision memo -- approve, reject, or approve with conditions -- with reasoning precise enough that a sharp student couldn't just shrug it off, rather than one that simply asserts that surprises obviously happen in practice.
