I've spent a long time reading rationalist blogs and forums — LessWrong, Overcoming Bias, Slate Star Codex and its successor Astral Codex Ten — and for the past few years I've also helped run a small in-person meetup that grew out of that scene. After our most recent session, which was nominally about Bayesian updating, two of the regulars, Theo and Ines, ended up in an argument about Aumann's agreement theorem that started over drinks afterward and then ran for two more days in our group chat. I wasn't able to settle it myself and would appreciate an outside read on it, ideally one that goes move by move rather than just declaring an overall winner.

The argument went roughly like this. (1) Theo opened by noting that he and Ines have been disagreeing for weeks over how to price a specific question on Manifold Markets, that each considers the other honest and reasonably rational, and that Aumann's theorem shows two Bayesians who share a common prior can't remain in a state of common knowledge that their posteriors on the same event differ — so their continued disagreement, after each has explained their reasoning at length, must mean that one of them either isn't leveling with the other or isn't updating the way an honest Bayesian would. (2) Ines answered that the result leans entirely on the common-prior assumption, which is exactly what she denies the two of them share: she thinks they started out with different priors long before any of the present evidence came in, so nothing in the theorem obliges them to converge. (3) Theo pushed back that a "prior difference" not grounded in some actual asymmetry of information or experience is just the disagreement re-described rather than explained, and that if an unexplained prior difference is always available as an excuse, the theorem can never really bite. (4) Ines granted that this stings, but brought up what she called the Geanakoplos–Polemarchakis result: even holding the prior fixed, two people who only exchange their current bottom-line probabilities, without ever exchanging the reasons behind them, are guaranteed to converge after finitely many rounds — and to her that shows the "agreement" the theorem delivers is a much thinner thing than the honest, reasons-tracking agreement she and Theo are actually trying to reach. (5) Theo, invoking an argument he attributed to the economist Robin Hanson, replied that this is too quick: on the Bayesian picture, a rational person's stated posterior already summarizes everything in their private evidence that bears on the question, so trading numbers back and forth is exactly as informative as it needs to be — and Ines's resistance to that conclusion, he said, looks like exactly the kind of self-favoring bias that should make either of them suspicious of their own continued disagreement. (6) Ines closed by pointing out that this last move only works if each of them already has common knowledge that the other is honest, rational, and free of that very bias, which two actual people never fully have about each other, or even about themselves — so the persistence of their disagreement is just as consistent with "one of us is biased" as with "each of us has some reason, from the inside, to trust our own reasoning a little more than we can verify the other's."

Could you go through the exchange step by step, saying who has the better of it at each numbered move, and then flag anything relevant that neither Theo nor Ines raises? I'm especially curious whether there's a cleaner way to state what "common knowledge of posteriors" actually demands, since my suspicion is that part of why the two of them keep talking past each other is that they're quietly assuming different amounts of idealization about what that phrase requires.
