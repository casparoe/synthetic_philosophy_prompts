Over the past year, as I have moved a growing share of my savings into Bitcoin to hedge against currency depreciation at home, I have become increasingly preoccupied with the question of what money actually is. While auditing a history-of-economic-thought seminar, I discovered that George Berkeley devoted an entire late work, The Querist, to this very question: he argued that the wealth of a nation lies in the industry of its people rather than in gold or silver, and repeatedly floated the idea that money's value comes not from any intrinsic worth but purely from its function as a "ticket" or "counter" for recording and transferring claims on real goods and labor — a view he invoked to justify proposing a national bank that would issue paper credit backed by land rather than specie. Given that account, how do you think Berkeley would have assessed Bitcoin: would he have welcomed it as the logical extension of his own anti-bullionist theory of money, or would its lack of any state or public institution standing behind it — the very thing his proposed bank was meant to supply — have struck him as a fatal disqualification?
