I'm several years and one previous novel into a slow-moving historical novel about the last decade of Leibniz's life, and after months spent back inside the Monadology, the Discourse on Metaphysics, and his correspondence with Clarke, I've reached a chapter I've been putting off: a set of fictional letters in which my Leibniz is shown, by a young and slightly reckless correspondent, a plain account of quantum entanglement and Bell's theorem. I want these letters to read as Leibniz reasoning with his own equipment, not as a physicist's Leibniz-flavored commentary, so before I draft them I'd like help getting the underlying argument right; the period detail I can supply myself.

Here is the difficulty as I understand it, and where I'd like your help pressing on it. Leibniz held that monads are windowless and cannot causally act on one another at all; that the appearance of coordination between substances -- paradigmatically mind and body -- is explained by pre-established harmony rather than real interaction; and that, across his long dispute with Clarke and, behind him, Newton, unmediated action at a distance was scarcely more intelligible than the occult qualities the mechanical philosophy was supposed to have retired. Via the principle of sufficient reason and the doctrine of the complete concept, he also held that every true fact about a substance -- including whatever we'd now call its hidden variables -- is fixed in advance of its ever being observed. Bell's theorem is usually read as showing that you cannot keep all three of: locality (whatever exactly that should mean for someone writing well before relativity), the definiteness of a particle's properties before measurement, and the statistical independence of those properties from an experimenter's later choice of what to measure. Leibniz, on the reading above, is already committed to the first two, which leaves him exactly one way out if he wants to keep both -- denying that independence, which is what physicists now call superdeterminism. Is that really his only move, or does some other part of his system get him out of the corner first? And if superdeterminism really is where he ends up, does the principle of sufficient reason make that an easier position for him to hold than it is for a modern physicist, since he already has a candidate reason -- God's choice of the best of all possible worlds -- for exactly the kind of pre-arranged coincidence that makes superdeterminism look like a conspiracy theory to everyone else? I don't need a primer on Leibniz or on Bell's theorem; I need the strongest version of the argument he would actually make, including whichever part of his own system ends up under the most strain.
