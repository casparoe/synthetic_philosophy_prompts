I TA a graduate seminar on decision theory that spends a couple of weeks on moral uncertainty -- much of the material overlaps with stuff that gets discussed on the EA Forum and LessWrong, which is originally how I got interested in it. This week I'm grading responses to the seminar's discussion question, and I'd like an independent read on one of them before I finalize a mark, so I've tried to present it here without signalling my own view of it.

The question was:

Compare "My Favourite Theory" (MFT) and "Maximize Expected Choiceworthiness" (MEC) as decision procedures for an agent under moral uncertainty. (a) State the standard objection to MFT that motivates adopting MEC instead. (b) Identify the further problem that MEC faces which does not arise for ordinary expected-utility maximization under empirical uncertainty, and explain why it doesn't arise there. (c) Briefly describe the parliamentary-model proposal for handling the problem in (b), including its source, and assess whether it actually succeeds in avoiding that problem.

Here is the submitted answer:

(a) My Favourite Theory (MFT) instructs the agent to act on whichever moral theory currently has the highest credence and disregard the rest, regardless of how close the credences are or how much is at stake according to the theories that are outvoted. If I assign 0.51 credence to a theory that calls some act permissible and 0.49 to a theory that calls it a serious wrong, MFT tells me to go ahead as though the second theory did not exist. This produces sharp discontinuities in behavior around the 50% threshold even though credences themselves move continuously, and it throws away information about how strongly the losing theories object. MEC replaces this with a rule that weights each theory's verdict about how choiceworthy an option is by our credence in that theory and sums across theories, so that a theory with just under half our credence can still swing the decision if enough is at stake by its own lights.

(b) The catch is that summing in this way presupposes that the "choiceworthiness" scores different theories assign are already on a common scale, so that a utilitarian score of +8 and a Kantian score of +2 can be added together meaningfully. Ordinary expected-utility maximization under empirical uncertainty doesn't face this problem because every possible state of the world is evaluated by the same utility function; only the probabilities of the states, not the scale used to value outcomes, are in question. Under moral uncertainty, by contrast, both the credences and the yardsticks are in question at once. This is essentially the same difficulty as the interpersonal comparison of utility in welfare economics, which Arrow's impossibility theorem already demonstrated cannot be resolved in a way that is both principled and non-arbitrary, so it is unsurprising that no consensus procedure for intertheoretic comparison exists either.

(c) MacAskill's parliamentary model sidesteps this by not attempting cardinal comparisons at all. Each moral theory is treated as a delegate in an imagined parliament, holding a number of votes proportional to our credence in it; delegates then bargain and vote on which action to take, spending more of their voting weight on the choices their theory regards as most important and conceding on choices it regards as minor. Because this only requires each delegate to rank options and weigh how much it cares within its own theory, rather than across theories, it avoids the problem in (b) entirely and lets a low-credence theory with extreme stakes still exert proportionate influence, without our ever having to say how many units of utilitarian value a Kantian violation costs.
