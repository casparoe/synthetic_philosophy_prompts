I'm on the board of a small foundation, run along effective-altruist lines, that funds global-health and biosecurity work. At a recent retreat, our facilitator posed two paired grant choices: (1) a program certain to save 1,000 lives versus one with an 89% chance of saving 1,000, a 10% chance of saving 5,000, and a 1% chance of saving none; (2) an 11% chance of saving 1,000, otherwise nothing, versus a 10% chance of saving 5,000, otherwise nothing. Almost everyone took the certain option in (1) and the higher-expected-value gamble in (2) -- the classic Allais paradox, since no expected-utility function over lives saved can rationalize both choices at once. Some board members now want a standing rule that all grant comparisons respect the independence axiom, since tolerating the inconsistency leaves us exploitable across repackaged versions of the same choice; others reply that it's not obviously irrational for a funder making rare, high-stakes, non-repeatable bets to weight certainty heavily, citing decision theories like Buchak's risk-weighted expected utility that relax independence without lapsing into incoherence. Draft the memo I'll circulate to the board recommending whether we should bind our grantmaking to the independence axiom, giving a fair hearing to whichever side you don't recommend.
