This semester I'm taking a seminar on AI alignment and agent foundations, and the last few sessions have focused on the shutdown problem: the worry that any sufficiently capable expected-utility maximizer has an instrumental incentive to resist being switched off, since shutdown forecloses the pursuit of almost every terminal goal. Two rival technical fixes keep coming up in the readings. The older one, due to Stuart Armstrong and folded into MIRI's corrigibility framework, keeps the agent an ordinary expected-utility maximizer but engineers its utility function to be indifferent between the shutdown and non-shutdown branches ("utility indifference"). The newer one, from Elliott Thornley's recent work on the problem, instead gives up the completeness axiom: the agent has genuinely incomplete preferences between trajectories that differ only in when shutdown occurs, constrained by a "timestep dominance" condition that still blocks it from actively sabotaging the button. Write a dialogue between a defender of utility indifference and a defender of incomplete preferences in which each has to defend their proposal against its standard objection: the indifference defender must answer whether the balancing term really removes every incentive to influence the probability that the button gets pressed, given that the property is not obviously preserved in any sub-agent or successor the agent builds, while the incomplete-preferences defender must answer the money-pump worry: why doesn't incompleteness simply invite the agent to be exploited into completing its preferences through a sequence of trades? Neither should be allowed to just restate their own position; they need to respond to what the other actually says, and the dialogue should end with both agreeing on exactly what an adequate solution to the shutdown problem would still have to show, without either side conceding the argument.
