So I've spent the last couple weeks reading way too much 60s-90s philosophy of mind for a piece I'm working on about whether AI systems could ever have "real" mental states, and I keep running into Putnam's multiple realizability argument treated like a settled result -- pain can be realized in carbon or silicon or octopus ganglia, so it can't just BE C-fiber firing, so type-identity theory is basically dead and functionalism wins. Fine, sure. But then I dug up this Bechtel & Mundale paper from the late 90s that apparently a bunch of philosophers of neuroscience take very seriously, and its whole move is that the classic argument only looks compelling because it compares a coarsely-described mental kind (just "pain," full stop) against finely-described physical kinds (specific neurons, specific neurotransmitter pathways) -- and that when comparative neuroscientists actually line up brain structures across species at a consistent grain, like they do for visual cortex, the realizers turn out not to be all that multiple after all, just the same structure doing the same job with minor variations. My philosopher housemate thinks this is a genuine equivocation objection that basically defangs 40 years of functionalist orthodoxy; another friend thinks its just moving the goalposts, since "same grain" comparisons can be gerrymandered to get whatever answer you want going in.

Could you write me the strongest possible version of the Bechtel & Mundale-style argument -- i.e., that the textbook multiple-realizability case against reductive physicalism doesn't actually establish what Putnam and Fodor needed it to establish, because it trades on this grain-of-description equivocation? I don't want the octopus-pain stuff just restated as though it settles anything; I want the real case for why that argument is a lot less devastating to reductionism than it's usually given credit for, including an honest answer to the obvious "you're just gerrymandering the grain to get your preferred conclusion" comeback.
