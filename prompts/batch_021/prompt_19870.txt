I don't have any background in biology or philosophy beyond what I've picked up from books and documentaries, but I've spent more evenings than I'd like to admit over the past month reading about siphonophores, after seeing one drift past a submersible's camera in a nature documentary, and it's left me stuck on a question I can't work out on my own. As far as I understand it, something like a Portuguese man o' war is not, strictly speaking, a single animal: it is a colony of many genetically identical but physically and functionally distinct "zooids," each specialised for one task (floating, propulsion, feeding, stinging, reproduction), none of which is capable of surviving on its own, yet the colony as a whole swims, hunts, and reacts to threats in a coordinated way, with signals evidently passing between the zooids as though the colony had a single nervous system of its own. That's made me realise that whenever people argue about whether some animal is conscious, and how richly, they seem to be assuming there is already a single, well-bounded animal there for the question to be about. Siphonophores make me suspect that this assumption can simply fail, well before we even reach the harder question of what, if anything, it is like to be the thing in question.

What I would like from you is a different example than the siphonophore case above (a real organism or phenomenon, or a carefully constructed hypothetical, whichever makes the point better) that brings out this problem of individuating a subject as a distinct difficulty for theories of animal consciousness, separate from the more familiar question of which animals, if any, are conscious in the first place. Ideally the case would press on the boundary in a different way than colonial organisms do — for instance through extreme decentralisation within the nervous system, split or partly independent processing inside what we would ordinarily call a single animal, or some other structure that makes it genuinely unclear where one candidate "subject" ends and another begins. I would also appreciate it if you could say plainly whether the theories of consciousness I've come across in my reading, such as integrated information theory and global workspace theory, actually offer a principled way of drawing those boundaries (something I gather is sometimes called the "boundary problem" in that literature), or whether they mostly just assume a single centralised brain and so avoid engaging with cases like this altogether.
