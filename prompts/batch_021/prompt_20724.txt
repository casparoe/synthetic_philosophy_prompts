I'm teaching the last unit of my metaethics seminar right now and figured I'd end on evolutionary debunking arguments, since it's always the part that gets the most reaction out of students (it's cross-listed with biology this year, so I've also got a few EEB majors in there who were delighted to hear philosophy finally "catching up" with biology). Last week everyone read Sharon Street's "Darwinian Dilemma" paper, and coming out of discussion section basically the whole room had converged on one conclusion: since our moral psychology was shaped by selection pressures that had nothing to do with tracking any independent moral truth -- we feel loyalty to kin and disgust at incest because ancestors wired that way out-reproduced the ones who weren't, not because incest is Really Wrong -- it just follows that none of our moral judgments are true, full stop, and ethics is basically dressed-up animal behavior. My TA, who's usually pretty careful about this stuff, thinks that's basically the right takeaway too, which has started to make me doubt myself.

Here's my actual question: is there a real gap in the move from "this belief was produced by a process indifferent to independent moral truth" to "so no moral beliefs are true," or does that gap basically close once you accept the evolutionary story, and I've just been soft-pedaling the nihilist conclusion out of squeamishness for years? I'd like to give the class something sharper than "well, it's complicated" before their response papers are due friday, so if the inference really does have a hole in it, i'd like to be able to say exactly where it is.
