"Direct realism (sometimes called naïve realism) is the theory that we perceive physical objects directly, and that they really do have the properties we experience them as having - for example, their shape, size and colour exist in the object itself, independently of any perceiver.

The argument from illusion tries to show that this cannot be right. Consider a round coin that is tilted away from you: it looks elliptical, even though the coin itself has not changed shape at all. Since the coin cannot be both round and elliptical in the same respect at the same time, direct realists cannot say that what we are directly aware of is the coin having the elliptical property - there must be something else we are aware of that really is elliptical, which the sense-datum theorist calls a sense-datum. A similar problem shows up with the classic example of looking at a star: because the light has taken so long to reach us, the star could have already burned out, meaning what we are directly aware of cannot be the star itself. Because illusory experiences are subjectively indistinguishable from normal veridical ones, the argument concludes that all perception, not just the illusory cases, must really be an awareness of sense-data rather than of mind-independent objects directly. This is a big problem for direct realism, since sense-data are mind-dependent, so it looks like we never actually perceive the physical world directly at all.

Indirect realism accepts this conclusion, holding that we perceive mind-independent objects indirectly by being directly aware of sense-data which those objects cause in us and which normally resemble them. However, this creates a further problem: since all we are ever directly aware of is our own sense-data, we have no way of getting outside our own perceptions to check that they actually do resemble the objects that supposedly cause them, which threatens to lead to scepticism about the external world, or even to Berkeley's idealism, on which physical objects are reduced to collections of ideas.

Disjunctivism tries to stop the argument from illusion before it reaches this point, so that direct realism never has to accept sense-data in the first place. The key move is to deny that a veridical perception and a matching illusion or hallucination have to be the same fundamental kind of mental state just because they can seem alike. Disjunctivists point out that when you inspect a hallucination carefully enough, it never has quite the same phenomenal character as a genuine perception does - there is always some subtle difference, even where it's hard to put into words - and this is what allows them to treat 'it perceptually seems to me that there is a round coin here' as a disjunction: either I am veridically perceiving an actual coin (a direct, relational state that involves the real object), or I am undergoing an illusion or hallucination (a completely different kind of state, which does not involve any such object). Since the two disjuncts do not actually feel identical once you look closely, there is no need to explain away an appearance of sameness, and therefore no pressure to say both cases share a common sense-datum.

I think this is a fairly successful defence of direct realism, because it stops the inference from 'illusions and perceptions can seem alike' to 'there must be a shared sense-datum in both' from ever getting off the ground. That said, one weakness, raised by critics like Howard Robinson, is that disjunctivism still has to explain what is actually happening during a hallucination if it isn't an awareness of a sense-datum and isn't a relation to a real object either, which seems especially awkward given that a hallucination could in principle be produced by exactly the same brain activity as a veridical perception, suggesting they should be classed as the same type of mental state after all. Overall though, I think disjunctivism is the strongest response direct realism has to the argument from illusion, since the alternative is accepting sense-data and the scepticism that comes with them."
