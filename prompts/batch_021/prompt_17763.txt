A friend and I spent much of Sunday evening arguing about whether to combine our money into a single joint position in a prediction-market contract on an upcoming election, and the argument ended up turning into a rerun of a debate we'd already had in our decision theory seminar over Harsanyi's social aggregation theorem. My friend's view was that, since we are each individually rational in the von Neumann–Morgenstern sense, Harsanyi's theorem guarantees that our joint behavior can always be represented as maximizing some weighted sum of our two utility functions, so there is nothing philosophically fraught about acting as a single unit once we agree on the weights. I was not convinced, because our actual disagreement is not about how much we each value the payout relative to the stake, but about the probability we each assign to the candidate winning. That struck me as exactly the kind of disagreement Harsanyi's original proof was never built to handle, since it is stated for a fixed set of lotteries that both the individuals and "society" are assumed to already be ranking in common, which quietly presupposes agreement on the odds.

My friend countered that this is a minor technicality that surely gets patched once the theorem is restated in terms of subjective rather than objective probabilities, but I vaguely recall reading, possibly from the same seminar's reading list, that this extension does not go through smoothly at all: that once individuals are allowed genuinely different priors, combining the usual Pareto condition with the requirement that the social preference itself be expected-utility-rational forces an uncomfortable choice, something like either the individuals sharing a common prior after all, or the aggregation rule collapsing into a kind of dictatorship. Neither of us could reconstruct why that would be true, or what precisely goes wrong if you just plug heterogeneous beliefs into the same proof.

I would like your help settling this properly. Could you explain what actually breaks, mechanically, when you try to extend Harsanyi-style aggregation from a setting with common objective lotteries to one with individuals who hold genuinely different subjective beliefs? I would find this most useful if illustrated with the kind of bet that two such disagreeing individuals could unanimously want to make, and an account of why a social observer should find that unanimity suspicious rather than reassuring. I would also appreciate your judgment on whether any of the standard fixes on offer, such as imposing a common prior by fiat, weakening the Pareto condition, or simply having the aggregate defer to one person's beliefs, amounts to a genuine rescue of Harsanyi's conclusion, or is really just a way of quietly changing the question.
