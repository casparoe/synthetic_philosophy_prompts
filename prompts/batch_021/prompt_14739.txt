"Expressivists like Ayer and Blackburn deny that moral sentences describe facts; instead a sentence like 'lying is wrong' expresses the speaker's disapproval of lying, roughly the way 'boo, lying!' would. The problem Geach raised is that this works fine when the sentence is asserted on its own, but moral language also shows up unasserted, like this: 'If lying is wrong, then getting your little brother to lie is wrong. Lying is wrong. So, getting your little brother to lie is wrong.' That's a valid modus ponens, and it's valid no matter what 'wrong' means, so long as it means the same thing in both premises. But on the expressivist story the second premise expresses disapproval of lying, while the first premise doesn't -- you can assert the conditional without disapproving of lying at all. So it looks like 'lying is wrong' has to mean two different things for the argument to work, which would make it equivocal rather than valid, even though the argument is obviously fine. This is why it's called the Frege-Geach problem: Geach borrowed the point from Frege's sense/reference distinction, where 'the morning star' and 'the evening star' refer to the same planet under different senses -- similarly, 'wrong' was thought to keep the same reference across the two premises even while functioning totally differently depending on whether it's asserted.

Blackburn's quasi-realism tries to answer this by giving expressivism a story about complex sentences: asserting the conditional expresses a higher-order attitude, like disapproving of the combination 'approving of lying but not of getting your brother to lie,' and validity becomes a matter of which combinations of attitudes are inconsistent to hold together, rather than classical truth-preservation. Blackburn thinks this lets him go further and earn the right to call moral claims true or false, know moral facts, and so on, and he takes this to succeed so completely that quasi-realism ends up equivalent to realism in everything that matters -- the 'quasi' just marks a difference in emphasis, not in substance."
