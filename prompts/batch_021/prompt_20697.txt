Over the past year I have been visiting my father several afternoons a week in the memory care facility where he now lives, and watching his dementia progress has unsettled something I thought I understood reasonably well from an MA in philosophy I completed some years ago: Wittgenstein's On Certainty. Early on, his condition looked like ordinary forgetting — misplaced dates, half-remembered names, the sort of everyday fallibility that seems continuous with normal empirical belief. But there have now been several episodes in which he doesn't seem merely mistaken about some fact; for minutes at a stretch he appears to lose his grip on things closer to what Wittgenstein calls "hinges" — failing to register that my mother, married to him for fifty years, is who she says she is, or being unable to settle even roughly where he is or whose house this is — in a way that feels less like an unusually stubborn false belief than a total disorientation about what kind of question is even being asked.

Sitting with this has left me stuck on a question my coursework never equipped me to answer. Does the fact that hinge-like certainties can be dissolved by neurological deterioration show that they were, all along, just extremely deeply entrenched empirical beliefs — so that Wittgenstein's claim that hinges are exempt in principle from evidential support or refutation turns out to be false, undone by the thoroughly mundane empirical fact of neurodegeneration? Or can a genuinely Wittgensteinian hinge epistemology absorb this by insisting that what dementia destroys, in a case like his, is not a belief but the surrounding framework — closer to what Wittgenstein pictures as the bed of a river than to the water flowing over it — within which anything recognizable as belief, doubt, or evidence could get a foothold in the first place, so that the case is not a counterexample to the picture in On Certainty but a harrowing illustration of it?

I would like you to write a dialogue between two equally capable interlocutors on opposite sides of this question: one who argues that cases like my father's show hinge propositions to be ordinary, if unusually stable, empirical beliefs subject to genuine evidential defeat, and another who defends a non-evidentialist, framework-based reading of hinge propositions against precisely this kind of challenge. Please have each press the other on their most serious weak points — for instance, the worry that the framework view becomes unfalsifiable if every apparent counterexample can simply be redescribed as "collapse" rather than refutation, and the worry that the graded, sometimes fluctuating quality of insight one actually observes in dementia sits awkwardly with treating hinge-loss as all-or-nothing — rather than letting them talk past one another, and please don't let either side simply concede the point.
