There's a set-theoretic party trick, built on the Axiom of Choice, that occasionally circulates in AC-skeptic corners of the rationalist internet, and I've never gotten past finding it bewildering rather than actually illuminating. The setup: countably infinitely many prisoners are each assigned an arbitrary real number as a hat color by an adversary who need not use any probability distribution whatsoever. Every prisoner can see everyone else's number but not their own, and all must simultaneously guess their own number with no further communication once the hats are placed. The trick is to fix, in advance, a choice function that selects one representative sequence from every equivalence class of the relation of differing in only finitely many coordinates, defined on the space of all such sequences; each prisoner then works out, from what they can see, which class the true sequence must belong to, and guesses whatever value that class's representative takes at their own position. This guarantees that only finitely many prisoners are wrong, no matter how adversarially the hats are assigned. I can follow every step of the argument, but it still feels like the prisoners are conjuring genuine information about unseen values out of nothing. Could you walk me through the gut-level intuition for what the Axiom of Choice is actually doing here — why this isn't some illegitimate form of information transfer, and why the same idea can't be pushed further to get every prisoner right, or reproduced via an explicit, choice-free rule?
