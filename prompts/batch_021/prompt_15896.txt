In 1924, shortly before his death, Gottlob Frege kept a short political diary that survived among his papers and was not published until decades later. Its entries are starkly nationalist, antisemitic, and anti-democratic: they voice hostility to the Weimar Republic's parliamentary system and to universal suffrage, and endorse stripping Jews of political rights. The discovery reportedly dismayed Michael Dummett, who had done as much as anyone to canonize Frege as the founder of analytic philosophy of language, prizing him for insisting that logic and thought are objective and universal, independent of any thinker's psychology or nationality. Scholars have since disagreed about whether the diary should change how we read Frege's actual philosophical output -- his rejection of psychologism, his sense/reference distinction, his platonism about numbers and about Thoughts (Gedanken) as denizens of a "third realm," and his context principle -- or whether his logic and his politics belong in entirely separate compartments of the man.

Write an essay addressing whether, and if so how, Frege's political diary should bear on the assessment of his philosophy. Address both the general question of how a thinker's biography ought to affect our evaluation of their arguments, and the more specific question of whether there are substantive connections -- rather than mere coincidence -- between Frege's particular technical commitments and the outlook expressed in the diary. Weigh the strongest considerations on each side, then reach and defend a definite verdict rather than resting on a non-committal survey.
