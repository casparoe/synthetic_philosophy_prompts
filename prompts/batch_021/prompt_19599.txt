I'm the only member of my county's environmental review board without a science background, and I've spent weeks reading on my own to make sense of a dispute that just landed on our agenda. A limestone hill slated for quarry expansion is home to a small land snail found nowhere else. The state's staff biologist calls it an isolated, somewhat distinctive population of a common snail found on hills throughout the region; a biologist at the regional natural history museum insists it is a genuinely separate species. Both accept the same genetic and shell data; what divides them is which test should settle the question. The state biologist treats successful interbreeding as decisive, and captive trials suggest the two populations still could interbreed; the museum biologist treats long isolation and being reliably told apart from its relatives as decisive, making that interbreeding result beside the point. Under our ordinance, the quarry must fund a costly relocation and monitoring plan only if a distinct, at-risk species is present. Please draft the short memo I will bring to next month's meeting: it should recommend a specific course of action and time frame, and it should take seriously the state biologist's concern that if long isolation and being reliably told apart are enough by themselves, almost every isolated hill population becomes its own species.
