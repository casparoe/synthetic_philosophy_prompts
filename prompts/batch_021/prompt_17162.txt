I was recently appointed as the public member of the compact commission that administers water sharing on a river my state shares with its upstream neighbor -- a seat meant to represent water users outside the state agencies themselves, which landed on me because I run an irrigation-supply business along the river. The commission is structured like the interstate river compacts common across the American West, with commissioners appointed by each state's governor plus a federal representative who breaks deadlocks. For decades the compact simply split the river's flow by a fixed percentage negotiated in the 1950s, but a decade of declining snowpack has made that number politically and hydrologically untenable, so the commission's economics subcommittee has spent the past year drafting a permanent shortage-and-surplus-sharing protocol that would set each state's allocation as a function of whatever the total flow turns out to be in a given year, rather than renegotiating from scratch after every bad one.

The subcommittee has narrowed the protocol to two rival formulas, both anchored to the same disagreement point -- the diversions each state was actually taking before the compact -- but differing in what else they use. The Kalai-Smorodinsky candidate also takes each state's court-adjudicated maximum entitlement as its "ideal point," with the practical effect that any change in the river's flow from one year to the next gets split between the states in proportion to how much each one's entitlement exceeds what it was diverting before the compact. The Nash candidate ignores entitlements altogether and simply maximizes the product of each state's gain over the status quo, which works out to splitting any such change equally regardless of what the courts have said either state is owed. One camp argues that only the Kalai-Smorodinsky rule is monotonic -- it can never leave a state worse off in a wet year purely because the other state's paper entitlement happened to grow -- and that ignoring entitlements after all that litigation is unprincipled. The other camp argues that this is exactly the problem: since the Kalai-Smorodinsky allocation shifts every time an entitlement is relitigated, it gives both states a standing incentive to keep inflating their claims in court, and that it's Nash's independence of irrelevant alternatives, not Kalai-Smorodinsky's monotonicity, that actually protects the protocol's stability over the coming decades.

As the newest commissioner, and the only one without a legal or engineering background, I've been asked to write the memo the full commission will vote on at next month's meeting. Could you draft it for me? I need it to recommend one of the two formulas -- or a principled hybrid, with a built-in review after some fixed number of years given how unsettled the hydrology still is -- rather than simply listing considerations on both sides, to explain the monotonicity-versus-independence-of-irrelevant-alternatives tradeoff in language my fellow commissioners can follow without a background in bargaining theory, and to take the strongest version of whichever side you don't recommend seriously rather than caricaturing it.
