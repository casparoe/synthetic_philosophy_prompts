So two students in my existentialism seminar got into a whole thing after we covered the bad faith chapter, and I can't stop replaying it. It started over Maya's cousin Ray, who quit accounting last year to drive long-haul trucks and has been telling everyone "I finally realized I was always meant to be out on the road -- the desk job was never really me."

Theo said that's bad faith by the book: Ray's turning a decision into a discovery so he doesn't have to feel the groundlessness of having chosen it, same move as the waiter who treats "waiter" as his nature instead of a role he's performing right now and could stop performing any second.

Maya said no -- Sartre isn't against essence-talk as such, only against denying that the "essence" is held up by an ongoing choice. Nothing stops Ray from describing his life in terms of a true self so long as he'd admit, if pushed, that he could quit trucking tomorrow. Otherwise every "this is who I really am" story ever told counts as bad faith, which trivializes the idea.

Theo answered that the concession-under-pressure test is too easy to pass and beside the point: Ray isn't just using loose narrative shorthand, he's using the "always meant to be" framing specifically so he won't feel the anxiety of the choice, and that's the flight from freedom Sartre's actually after, whatever Ray would say if you cross-examined him about hypotheticals later.

Maya replied that Theo's collapsing two seperate things: taking up your facticity (your talents, your layoff, whatever) as material for a project is fine, maybe unavoidable -- the bad faith is specifically the extra claim that the project was fixed in advance, that there was never really a choice at all. Ray could be making that extra claim, or just making small talk at a barbecue, and "it calms his anxiety" doesn't tell you which, since plenty of true, non-self-deceived beliefs calm anxiety too.

Could you go through this move by move and say who's got the better of it at each step, not just an overall winner, and flag anything you think both of them are missing entirely? I'm especially curious whether "it calms his anxiety" can even count as evidence here, given that on Sartre's picture there's no hidden part of Ray that already knows the truth and is keeping it from the rest of him.
