A colleague of mine, Warren, is retiring after thirty-some years on the bench, and I've been roped into giving a short toast at his send-off dinner next month. Warren's basically the reason I ended up teaching philosophy of law and not just straight ethics -- we co-taught a law-and-philosophy seminar for almost a decade -- and his whole shtick with incoming clerks was always that one line of Pascal's, "justice without force is powerless, force without justice is tyrannical," which he'd trot out as basically his entire judicial philosophy in eight words. What most of the room won't know, but a solid chunk of the seminar alumni definately will, is that Pascal doesn't stop there -- right after that quote he gets into the much less comforting idea that people obey laws mainly because they're told the laws are just, when the actual reason is that they're established and backed by force, and that it would be dangerous to announce this out loud; and theres that other bit of his about how what's a crime on one side of a river is perfectly legal on the other, which is clearly doing the same debunking work. Warren himself set me straight on this over lunch a few years back -- he was adamant that the debunking reading is the real Pascal and the cozy pull-quote is basically bait, and he loved watching first-year clerks' faces fall when he walked them through it. Which makes it a little strange to toast a man who thinks "justice" might mostly be force with better PR, except that for thirty years he was also just an unusually scrupulous, fair judge, and I don't think those two things are actually in tension for him -- I just have to make that case in five minutes to a room that's two-thirds family and retired court staff who've never read a page of the Pensées.

So here's what I'm after: a toast, five minutes or so, that treats the darker half of Pascal (the concealment point, the river, the whole "justice as dressed-up force" business) as the actual engine of the thing rather than a bit of colour I toss in for the philosophy crowd -- but that still reads as a warm, funny tribute to Warren rather than a seminar, and definitely doesn't come across as accusing him of spending his career running a very dignified con. If you can land one genuine laugh in there, even better.
