I spent thirty years running an experimental immunology lab and have, since retiring, joined my university's research-integrity committee, which rules on allegations of data fabrication and falsification. As at most US institutions, our policy tracks the federal definition: a misconduct finding requires that the conduct was committed intentionally, knowingly, or recklessly, and it explicitly excludes honest error. We are weighing a case in which a postdoctoral researcher discarded a cluster of "outlier" readings without documenting the exclusions; had they been kept in, they would have undercut a result that went on to a high-profile publication and considerable press coverage. He maintains this was routine data cleaning, of a sort he has done many times on projects nobody remembers. Several committee members are convinced the exclusions were deliberate precisely because so much now rides on the result being real, and some recent reading in experimental philosophy has left me suspecting our deliberations are a live instance of the Knobe effect, whereby people are far readier to call a side effect "intentional" the worse its consequences turn out to be, even holding the evidence about the agent's actual state of mind fixed. I doubt we can simply resolve to disregard the stakes, since a notion of intent purged of all outcome-sensitivity may not be available to us, or even the right thing to aim for. Please draft the memo I will bring to the committee: it should recommend both how to resolve this particular case and what standing protocol we should adopt afterward, and it should take seriously the strongest argument for letting the stakes inform the judgment before explaining why the committee should or shouldn't be persuaded by it.
