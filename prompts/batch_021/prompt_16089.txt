Kurt Gödel held two distinctive, well-documented private convictions that he never had the chance to bring to bear on today's technology. In letters to his mother, he laid out a "rationalistic" argument for life after death: since the world is ordered by reason, it would amount to a kind of cosmic waste for beings with humanity's capacity for open-ended intellectual and moral development to have that development permanently cut short by bodily death, so some form of continued existence should be expected — though he was careful to note that this need not match any particular religion's picture of an afterlife, and might even be realized through natural laws not yet understood. Separately, in his 1951 Gibbs lecture, he used the incompleteness theorems to argue for a disjunction he took to have real philosophical bite: either the human mind's mathematical powers outstrip those of any possible finite algorithmic machine, or there exist mathematical truths that no mind could ever discover; and he privately leaned toward the first horn, doubting that a mind could be nothing more than a rule-governed mechanism.

Taking these two commitments as your evidence base, how do you think Gödel would have judged contemporary proposals for mind uploading or whole-brain emulation as a technological route to surviving death? Would he most likely have welcomed such proposals as a plausible, naturalistic fulfillment of the continued development his rationalist argument demands, or would his suspicion that minds outrun mechanism have led him to dismiss an uploaded emulation as merely a functioning copy rather than the person's actual continuation? Work through the tension between these two strands of his thought rather than resolving it too quickly in either direction.
