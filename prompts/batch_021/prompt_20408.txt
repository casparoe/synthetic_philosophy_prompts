Ok so I've fallen down a decision theory rabbit hole again (this keeps happening, ever since I started lurking on LW back in the day) and this week's obsession is the "Death in Damascus" case. My friend Dave, who's way more into the formal side of this stuff than I am, brought it up during our reading group and now I can't stop poking at it, mostly because I think his preferred fix is too quick.

Setup for anyone who hasn't run into this one: you're in the marketplace, Death gives you a meaningful look, and you know you're marked for tonight in one of two cities, Damascus or Aleppo. You get to choose which city to be in, but Death is basically a perfect predictor of your choice and will show up wherever he predicted — so your pick has no causal influence on him, and yet it's obviously not independent of whether you survive. I think this originally comes from a Gibbard and Harper paper meant to vindicate causal decision theory over the evidential kind, but the part that actually keeps me up at night is the "ratifiability" objection to it: if a rational choice has to be an act you'd still endorse after learning you're about to perform it, then whichever city causal decision theory recommends immediately stops being recommendable, since Death predicting correctly just got more likely, so you should switch — and then the same thing happens to the new pick, forever. Dave's answer is "just randomize, mixed acts can be ratifiable even when pure ones can't," but I've also seen people argue for dropping the ratifiability requirement entirely, switching to evidential decision theory, or going full functional/logical decision theory, where Death's prediction depends on your decision algorithm rather than being a seperate causal or correlational fact about you.

What I want is an essay that actually adjudicates this instead of just listing positions like a wikipedia page. Explain clearly why the cycling argument is supposed to be a specific problem for causal decision theory (not just "predictors are spooky"), then go through the proposed fixes and say which ones genuinely dissolve the problem versus which ones just relocate it somewhere else. End on an actual verdict about what a rational agent should do standing in that market square — a conditional answer is fine ("randomize if X, otherwise it doesn't matter because Y") as long as you commit to it instead of concluding that it depends on which theory you already liked going in.
