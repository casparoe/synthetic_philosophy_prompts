I'm about two-thirds through a novel narrated by a woman recovering from a long illness, and one of the threads I keep returning to is her unreliable grip on her own inner states: whether the fear she reports feeling is the fear she's actually feeling, whether a childhood memory she now recalls "with total clarity" is being recalled or quietly reconstructed as she goes. Wanting some intellectual ballast for this before the prose gets too impressionistic, I went down a rabbit hole into the epistemology of self-knowledge and ended up reading about Timothy Williamson's case against "luminosity" in Knowledge and Its Limits — the thesis that certain conditions, paradigmatically phenomenal ones, are such that whenever you're in them you're automatically in a position to know you're in them. As I understand it, his argument uses a margin-for-error style thought experiment (something like a person very gradually shifting from clearly feeling cold to clearly not feeling cold) to conclude that almost no non-trivial condition is luminous in this sense, and that the same reasoning threatens the KK principle — the idea that knowing something puts you in a position to know that you know it — for any cognizer whose powers of discrimination are less than perfect.

What I'd like from you is a dialogue, pitched at a reasonably high level of sophistication, between two epistemologists of comparable ability: one who wants to salvage some version of luminosity or KK, at least for phenomenal self-knowledge, and one who presses the Williamsonian case against them. I don't want them simply trading prepared statements — each move should be a genuine response to whatever the other person has just said, and long enough that each side gets to develop a point rather than just land one-liners. I'd especially like the exchange to work through whether the margin-for-error principle driving the anti-luminosity argument is something a defender of introspective privilege is entitled to simply reject — say, on the grounds that self-knowledge of one's own current states is a different kind of thing from perceptual knowledge of the world, and not answerable to the same safety-style constraints — or whether that move is really just ad hoc; and whether conceding that luminosity fails only "at the margins" is a stable fallback position or already gives up what made the Cartesian picture attractive to begin with. It doesn't need to end in agreement — if anything, it's more useful to me if it doesn't — but each side should land at least one point the other can't easily wave away.
