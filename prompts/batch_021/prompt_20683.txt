Somebody pointed me to a paper of Aumann's from the mid-90s where he proves that common knowledge of rationality in a game of perfect information -- the standard illustration is the centipede game -- actually forces the backward induction outcome, i.e. the first player bails out on the very first move instead of letting the pot grow. Seemed pretty neat until I fell down a rabbithole into the Binmore/Reny objection: if a player ever actually reaches move 5 or wherever, she's got direct evidence her opponent isn't rational (a rational opponent would've stopped at move 1), so what's she even supposed to believe once she's there, and in what sense was rationality ever "common knowledge" if the game itself can produce a move that falsifies it? Feels like the proof might be smuggling in exactly the counterfactual it can't actually support, but I can't tell if that's a real hole or just me being confused about counterfactuals. Can you write me a dialogue between two epistemic game theorists hashing this out, maybe over drinks after a seminar -- one defending Aumann's result on its own terms, the other pushing the off-path-belief objection as hard as it'll go?
