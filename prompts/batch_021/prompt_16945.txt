I'm a backend engineer with no formal philosophy training, but a coworker who minored in philosophy kept sending me Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy links after I got curious about the theory behind the Bayesian A/B-testing tools we use at work, and that's how I fell into something called the "problem of old evidence" in Bayesian epistemology. The setup, first raised by the philosopher Clark Glymour around 1980 as far as I can tell, is this: on the standard Bayesian picture, evidence E confirms a hypothesis H exactly when learning E raises your credence in H, i.e., P(H|E) > P(H). That works fine when E is genuinely new, but it seems to break down for one of the most celebrated confirmations in the history of physics. The anomalous precession of Mercury's perihelion — roughly 43 arcseconds per century that Newtonian mechanics couldn't account for — had been precisely measured since the 1850s, decades before Einstein wrote down general relativity in 1915. Since everyone, including Einstein, already assigned that fact probability 1 long before GR existed, naive conditionalization implies that learning "GR entails this fact" couldn't have raised anyone's credence in GR by so much as a decimal point — yet it was, and still is, treated as one of GR's most important early confirmations. From some further reading I gather there are a few competing patches on offer: treating the discovery that H logically entails E as itself new information for agents who aren't perfectly logically omniscient, asking counterfactually what your credence in H would have been had you not already known E, or insisting that what really matters is whether E was actually used in constructing H rather than merely whether E predates H. None of these seems to have won out as the consensus fix, and I gather some people think the whole episode shows that treating confirmation as a purely probabilistic relation between propositions is hopeless.

This stopped feeling like a purely academic puzzle when I noticed it describes something my own team does constantly. After a production incident, we write up an exhaustive timeline of exactly what happened, so that by the time anyone proposes a root cause, every fact the explanation will need to account for is already known with certainty. A couple of months ago we had a spell of intermittent 500 errors that only appeared during a narrow deploy window, and a senior engineer proposed, after the fact, that it was a race between a Redis cache-invalidation job and a feature-flag config reload — a hypothesis that, once stated, entailed every detail we had already logged. The room treated the fit as strong confirmation, and we closed the postmortem without ever reproducing the failure or checking any prediction of the hypothesis that we hadn't already observed. It struck me that this is structurally the same move as the Mercury case: a hypothesis constructed after, and in order to account for, evidence that already had probability 1 for everyone in the room.

Could you lay out the most important implications of the problem of old evidence, and of the proposed fixes to it, for how my team should actually treat root-cause hypotheses like this one? Concretely, I'd like to know whether any of the standard Bayesian responses give a principled reason to be less confident in an explanation that was essentially reverse-engineered from a fully-known timeline than we'd be in one that made and passed some prediction we hadn't already checked, or whether my discomfort is really tracking a different, non-Bayesian issue — something more like the absence of held-out predictions — that I'd be mislabeling by calling it "the problem of old evidence" at all. I'd also be curious whether the Mercury case is really as clean an analogy to our postmortem as it first seemed to me, or whether there's some disanalogy between the two that I'm missing.
