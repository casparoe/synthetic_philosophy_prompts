I'm plotting a novel in which two secondary characters — a Daoist-trained hospice chaplain and a hospital bioethicist — spend a long night together arguing about whether death is something to be feared. Before I draft the scene in my own prose, I want to see their disagreement worked out in full. Please write a dialogue in which the chaplain defends something close to Zhuangzi's position: that death is only a further transformation within one continuous process, so that there is no fixed self left over for it to happen to, drawing on his own imagery where it helps — his refusal, say, to grieve his wife in the ordinary way once he reflects on the transformations of qi, or the skull he questions on the road, which later answers him in a dream. The bioethicist should defend something close to Thomas Nagel's deprivation account of why death is bad for the one who dies, including his attempt to explain why we do not dread our nonexistence before birth the way we dread our nonexistence after death. Have each press the other's strongest objection instead of simply restating their own view — in particular, whether a comparative, deprivation-based notion of harm can get any grip at all once the self is understood, Zhuangzi-style, as no more than a temporary knot in a larger process — and don't let either side win too easily: I want a genuine exchange, with each interlocutor as sharp as the other.
