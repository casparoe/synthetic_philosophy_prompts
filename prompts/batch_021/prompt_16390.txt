"Frege opens 'On Sense and Reference' by noting that a statement like 'a = a' is trivial and can be known just by understanding it, whereas a true statement of the form 'a = b' — e.g., 'the Morning Star is the Evening Star' — can be a real extension of our knowledge. And here's the student's answer, copied exactly as written in the booklet:

"(a) Frege noticed that 'a=a' just tells us an object is self-identical, so anyone who understands it at all knows its true — it can't teach us anything. But 'a=b' can be a real discovery, like finding out the Morning Star and the Evening Star are the same object (Venus). If identity just related the objects themselves, this difference would be mysterious, since 'a=a' and 'a=b' would both just say Venus is the same as itself. Frege's fix is to say names have both a reference — the object they pick out — and a sense, the particular way that object is presented. 'The Morning Star' and 'The Evening Star' have the same reference but different senses, since they present Venus differently (as the last star visible at dawn vs. the first visible at dusk), which is why 'a=b' can be informative: it tells us two different presentations latch onto one object, something you couldn't know just from grasping the words. The sense of a name is really the mental picture a speaker forms on hearing it, and since two people's pictures can differ, this also explains why the same sentence can strike different people as more or less informative.

(b) Frege extends this by saying that in 'Hammurabi believed the Morning Star was visible at dawn,' the embedded clause isn't just reporting a relation between Hammurabi and Venus — if it were, then since the Morning Star just is the Evening Star, it would immediately follow that Hammurabi also believed the Evening Star was visible at dawn, which need not be true. So within the 'that'-clause the words contribute their ordinary sense rather than their ordinary reference: Hammurabi's belief is a relation to a Thought built from the sense of 'Morning Star,' and someone who only grasps the sense of 'Evening Star' hasn't grasped that Thought, even though the two names converge on the same planet. This is what Frege calls a referentially opaque context, and it's why substituting co-referring terms there isn't guaranteed to preserve truth-value.

(c) One well-known problem is that this generates a regress: for double embeddings like 'Jones believes Hammurabi believed the Morning Star was visible,' the inner clause has to shift reference again, so you'd need a 'sense of a sense' for every extra layer of embedding, and it's not obvious this ever bottoms out in something we actually grasp."
