I'm a final-year philosophy major currently taking a seminar cross-listed with the economics department on the philosophical foundations of game theory. We recently spent a week working directly through Aumann's 1995 argument that common knowledge of rationality implies the backward-induction outcome, using the centipede game as the running illustration, and I only really came away understanding why the result counts as paradoxical, rather than merely surprising, after that discussion. Our department's graduate philosophy society records a short interview podcast a few times each term, and I offered to write the questions for the next episode. The guest is a philosopher who works on the foundations of game theory and is well known for defending the Aumann line even against the standard complaint that a sufficiently long centipede game makes immediate defection look absurd; when pressed on what a player should conclude after an opponent deviates from the backward-induction path, her go-to move is to say that "off the path, there is nothing to believe," since on her view fully rational players never actually reach those nodes in the first place.

Please draft eight to ten interview questions that do not simply give her room to repeat that line. I would like at least one question that presses directly on the off-path belief problem itself: a player who does see her opponent make a backward-induction-forbidden move still has to choose some response, so "there is nothing to believe" cannot really be where the matter ends. I would like at least one question asking whether Aumann's notion of rationality is doing genuine explanatory work here, or whether it is simply built so as to yield backward induction as its conclusion. I would also like a question built around the regress inside the argument itself: the reasoning meant to justify the very first player's defecting right away depends on claims about what would happen at rounds several moves later -- rounds that, if that same reasoning is correct, are never actually played out -- and I'm not sure how a defender of the Aumann line is supposed to hold both of those thoughts at once. Finally, please include a question that raises the experimental finding that actual subjects in centipede games mostly do not defect immediately and that cooperation instead unravels gradually, asking her to say plainly whether that is merely a fact about human psychology her view can set aside, or a genuine problem for treating backward induction as a requirement of rationality rather than a description of ideal play. For each of these, add a short follow-up question keyed to the answer she is most likely to give, and close the list with a question asking what kind of evidence or argument, if any, would actually lead her to abandon the Aumann line rather than restate it more carefully.
