Question (15 points). The sentence "Water is H2O" is standardly treated as an example of a truth that is necessary yet knowable only a posteriori. (a) Explain why "water" and "H2O" should be understood as rigid designators, and how this feature secures the necessity of the identity claim, if it is true. [5 points] (b) Explain why this necessity is compatible with the fact that the identity was discovered empirically rather than known in advance. [5 points] (c) Kripke also discusses cases with the opposite profile: truths that are knowable a priori yet only contingently true. Using his own example, explain how such a case is possible, and say what it shows about the relation between the necessary/contingent distinction and the a priori/a posteriori distinction. [5 points]

Student's answer: (a) A rigid designator is an expression that refers to the same object—or, for a natural-kind term, the same kind of stuff—in every possible world in which that object or kind exists. Kripke argues that ordinary proper names are rigid designators, and Putnam extends the same idea to natural-kind terms such as "water" and "gold" with his Twin Earth thought experiment: a liquid on a distant planet that looks and behaves exactly like water but has the composition XYZ rather than H2O is not water, because "water" rigidly picks out whatever kind actually satisfies the description originally used to introduce the term—the clear, drinkable liquid that fills our lakes and rivers. Once chemists established that the stuff satisfying this description is H2O, that description had done its job of fixing the reference of "water"; and, since fixing the reference this way also fixes what the word means, "water" and "H2O" turned out to be two names for one and the same thing. Since "water" and "H2O" are both rigid designators of that single substance, "water is H2O" is true in every possible world in which it is true at all—it is necessary, on the same grounds that make "Hesperus is Phosphorus" necessary once we see that "Hesperus" and "Phosphorus" are just two names for the planet Venus. (b) This necessity is compatible with a posteriori knowledge because necessity and apriority answer different questions. Necessity is a metaphysical matter of whether a state of affairs could have been otherwise; apriority is an epistemological matter of whether a truth can be known independently of empirical investigation. Nothing in our ordinary concept of water, examined from the armchair, reveals its microstructure—only laboratory chemistry could show that the watery stuff around us is H2O. So the identity is necessary, yet it had to be discovered a posteriori. (c) Kripke pairs this necessary a posteriori case with one of the opposite profile: a statement that is contingent yet knowable a priori. His example is the standard meter stick S, once kept in Paris. Suppose we introduce the phrase "one meter" by stipulating that it denotes the length of S at a fixed time t0. Then the sentence "S is one meter long at t0" can be known a priori, since it simply records the stipulation by which we fixed the reference of "one meter," rather than any measurement of S. And because that same stipulation is what fixes what "one meter" denotes once and for all, the sentence turns out to be necessary as well as a priori: S could not have failed to be a meter long at t0, since "one meter" just means whatever length S has at that moment. This shows that the necessary/contingent distinction and the a priori/a posteriori distinction cut across one another rather than lining up in a single pair of categories.
