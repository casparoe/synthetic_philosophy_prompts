Quick one for a piece I'm working on about whether utilitarianism can be "mathematically proven" -- Harsanyi's aggregation theorem keeps getting waved around (especially by EA-adjacent writers) as basically forcing you into utilitarianism once you accept a couple of pretty innocuous-looking axioms: Pareto, plus expected-utility axioms for both individuals and society. Somethings bugging me about it though. The theorem's only about preferences over lotteries, and I've got a hunch the Pareto step is quietly assuming nobody should care HOW a good outcome gets produced (a fair coin toss vs. just handing the good thing to the same person every time), so long as the expected utility comes out equal. Can you make the strongest version of that objection, ideally with a small concrete case, showing exactly where the "innocent" axioms end up smuggling in a rejection of process fairness? Not looking for the standard defense of Harsanyi here -- I want the best case against the theorem actually delivering what people claim it delivers.
