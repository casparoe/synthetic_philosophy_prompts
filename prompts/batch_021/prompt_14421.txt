Question: H.L.A. Hart argued that theories of punishment need to distinguish the "general justifying aim" of the practice from questions about its "distribution." Explain this distinction and explain why Hart thought it let him combine consequentialist and retributivist commitments into a single theory.

Student answer: Hart's move is to split two questions people usually run together: why do we punish at all, and who gets punished (and how much). The first question -- the general justifying aim -- he answers in consequentialist terms: punishment is worth having as an institution because it deters crime and protects people, so its point is forward-looking. But if that's the whole story you get the classic problem for utilitarians: nothing about deterrence requires punishing only the guilty, so in principle a utilitarian could end up endorsing punishment of an innocent person if it deterred crime just as well. Hart's fix is that distribution is governed by an independent, retributive rule: a person is to be punished if and only if they're guilty of an offense, and the punishment should be proportionate to it. Utilitarians had tried to dodge the innocent-person problem before by just saying that punishing an innocent person wouldn't really be "punishment" by definition -- Hart calls this the definitional stop, and takes it to basically settle the issue, since once guilt is built into the very definition of punishment, a utilitarian can never be recommending punishing the innocent. So on Hart's view you end up with a genuinely mixed theory: forward-looking and aggregative about why we have the practice at all, backward-looking and desert-based about who it can be used on and how much. It's a similar move to the one Rawls makes with punishment in "Two Concepts of Rules," where the rules of a practice get justified on utilitarian grounds even though applying the rules in any particular case looks like straightforward backward-looking retributivism.
