As someone who spends my working hours fine-tuning language models but has never formally studied philosophy, I recently came across the following passage from Leibniz's Monadology (§17) while reading around the debate over whether LLMs genuinely understand anything; it was cited as an early ancestor of Chinese-Room-style objections to machine understanding. Here it is, as it is usually quoted:

"It must be confessed, moreover, that perception, and that which depends upon it, are inexplicable by mechanical causes, that is to say, by figures and motions. And, supposing that there were a machine so constructed as to think, feel, and have perception, we might conceive of it as increased in size while retaining the same proportions, so that one might be able to enter into it as into a mill. That being so, we should, on examining its interior, find only parts which work one upon another, and never anything by which to explain a perception. Thus it is in a simple substance, and not in a compound or a machine, that perception must be sought for."

I can picture the mill, but I cannot quite reconstruct the argument it is meant to establish. Is Leibniz simply reporting that inspecting a mechanism's parts never turns up anything that visibly looks like perception -- which would amount to a fairly weak argument from ignorance -- or is there a more principled reason here for why perception in particular could never be located in interacting parts, however they are arranged? Relatedly, I want to know whether the argument on its own rules out the possibility that perception or understanding belongs to an organized whole rather than to any of its non-perceiving parts (the move later known as the 'systems reply' to the Chinese Room), or whether that exclusion only follows once you add Leibniz's independent claim that composites, unlike simple substances, are mere aggregates without genuine unity. If it is the latter, I would like to know what, if anything, would need to be added to the argument before it could actually bear on whether something like an LLM understands the text it processes, rather than just restating the old intuition that machines cannot really think.
