As the token philosophy professor on my university's Honorary Degrees Committee -- normally the most boring assignment in the building -- I've ended up refereeing a real fight this year over our strongest nominee: a retired four-star general. I'll keep some specifics vague since this is a live case, but his nomination letter is built around decades of genuinely admirable humanitarian and disaster-relief work since he left active duty. The complication is from earlier in his career: he was the officer who planned and ordered a campaign against a small country's hydroelectric dams and irrigation systems, correctly calculating that wrecking the food supply would collapse the government's will to keep fighting far faster than continued conventional warfare would. It worked -- the war ended almost a year early -- but at the cost of an estimated several thousand civilian deaths from the resulting famine, deaths his own planning documents had predicted almost to the number.

One of my colleagues on the committee, a Kantian who, I happen to know, assigns Anscombe's old pamphlet against Oxford's honorary degree for Truman every year in her intro course, is making essentially Anscombe's argument -- "choosing to kill the innocent as a means to your ends is always murder," she keeps saying, no matter how good the end turns out to be or how carefully you distinguish intended from merely foreseen -- and her point is that a university conferring an honor can't just wave that off as somebody else's moral bookkeeping. Two other members think that logic, taken seriously, would disqualify half the military figures any university has ever honored, and suspect there's a principled disanalogy with the Truman case but can't quite locate it, beyond just liking the humanitarian work more. (My partner's advice over dinner was just to table it, which is not an argument, however tempting.) I need to circulate a short written opinion before we vote Friday, recommending we proceed with the nomination, postpone it, or withdraw it -- with real reasons attached, not "reasonable people disagree" -- and I'd like it to actually sit with the Anscombe-style objection rather then breeze past it on the way to whatever conclusion is more convenient.
