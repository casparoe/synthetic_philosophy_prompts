So my partner is a physicist and a few months ago I got dragged into their book club, which has honestly become a whole thing for me. We just finished Carlo Rovelli's "The Order of Time," and I could not stop thinking about how much it echoes an essay by Borges called "A New Refutation of Time," which I actually wrote a chunk of my undergrad thesis on years ago. Rovelli's basic move (as I understood it — someone correct me if I'm garbling the physics) is that there's no "time" variable at the most fundamental level of reality at all; what we experience as flow and direction is really a byproduct of entropy and the fact that we're big, blurry, coarse-grained observers who can only track some variables and not others. He and Alain Connes even have this "thermal time" idea, where time just falls out of picking a thermodynamic state rather than being built into physics from the start.

Heres what's been nagging at me: Borges, leaning on Berkeley and then Hume, argued that once you give up substance behind matter and a persisting self behind perceptions, succession itself looses its footing — each moment becomes self-sufficient, and "time" is basically a trick that language, habit, and memory play on us. But famously he doesn't let himself off the hook: the essay ends with him admitting that, however good the argument is, he's still tragically stuck living inside time, like a river that is also the thing being swept along by it. I keep flip-flopping on whether Rovelli's picture is doing the same move as Borges, just in physics instead of epistemology, or whether it's actually much weaker, since Rovelli still wants entropy, thermodynamic gradients, and "the universe" to be robustly, mind-independently real even once time is gone — which sounds exactly like the kind of leftover substance Borges's argument was trying to get rid of along with time.

Could you write up Borges's actual reaction to Rovelli's argument — not a book report about him, but him actually reasoning it through, in something like his own idiom if you can manage it? I want to know whether he'd take "The Order of Time" as basically vindicating, in the language of thermodynamics, what he'd already shown with Hume and Berkeley decades earlier, or whether he'd say Rovelli is being too timid, quietly smuggling back in exactly the kind of "real" furniture of the world that a consistent idealist has to give up along with time. Please actually engage with the structure of his argument in the essay rather than doing the usual "Borges liked labyrinths and infinite libraries" thing — that's not really what this essay is about anyway.
