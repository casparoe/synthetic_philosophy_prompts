I'm a novelist, and for reasons that have more to do with community service than any real statistical training, I've spent the last three years on the five-member selection committee for the Whitmore Fellowship, a small but well-regarded prize that funds a year of stipend and mentorship for two emerging writers annually, one in fiction and one in poetry. Submissions are read blind, with no names or biographical notes attached, though we separately collect some background information at intake, including whether an applicant holds an MFA, purely so the board can audit its own process later.

That auditing became urgent last month, after a literary blog accused the fellowship of favoring MFA-credentialed applicants, noting that across our last six cycles we accepted about 18% of applicants with an MFA and only about 8% of applicants without one. A board member who spent his career as a biostatistician went back through the genre-level numbers and found the reverse pattern within each track: non-MFA applicants were accepted at 6% in fiction against 5% for MFA applicants, and at 25% in poetry against 20% for MFA applicants. He also confirmed that fiction, by far our most oversubscribed category, draws the great majority of its applicants from writers without an MFA, while poetry, where we can fund a much larger share of what comes in, draws mostly from applicants who have one. He and one ally on the committee call this a textbook Simpson's paradox and consider the matter closed: publish the breakdown, change nothing. The other two members, while not disputing his arithmetic, worry that this amounts to using a statistical technicality to wave away a genuine disparity, especially since non-MFA writers may cluster in fiction partly because the workshop training that makes a poetry manuscript competitive is something an MFA disproportionately provides.

We vote on the report's public language in three weeks, and I've been asked to circulate a memo beforehand recommending what we do. Could you draft it? I'd like it to reach a specific recommendation, covering both what the report should say and whether anything about next year's selection process should change, and I'd like it to take the second camp's worry seriously on its own terms rather than treat it as a simple failure to understand the statistics. It's exactly the kind of quietly consequential institutional judgment call I'd like to render honestly in a novel one day, but for now I need it settled for real.
