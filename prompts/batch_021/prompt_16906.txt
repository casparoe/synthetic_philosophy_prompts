For the past three years I've been part of my school's Environmental Action Committee, and this spring our principal finally agreed to let the whole student body vote, during an all-school assembly, on whether next year's $40,000 discretionary grant from the school foundation should go toward rooftop solar panels instead of repaving the west parking lot. I've been asked to give the closing speech right before we open the vote, and I would like it to actually change some minds rather than just make six hundred people feel briefly guilty before recess.

The complication is that last semester I took our school's one-semester Philosophy elective, taught by Dr. Alvarez, who also happens to be the Committee's faculty advisor and will be standing at the back of the gym for the entire assembly. For a short unit on obligations to future generations, she had us read the chapter on the Non-Identity Problem from Derek Parfit's Reasons and Persons, including his example of the fourteen-year-old girl and his comparison of a resource policy of "Depletion" against one of "Conservation." I mentioned to some committee friends afterward that I wasn't sure I could still, in good conscience, give the speech everyone is expecting, which is some version of "if we don't act now, we are condemning our own children and grandchildren to a ruined planet," and they mostly told me I was overthinking a four-minute pep talk. But once you take the Non-Identity Problem seriously, that sentence stops being obviously true: exactly which people get born decades from now depends on countless downstream effects of whichever choice we make today, so the actual people living with a worse climate would never have existed at all under the better policy — different people entirely would exist instead — and if their lives are still worth living, it is genuinely unclear that our choice harms them, even though the choice still feels wrong.

Could you write me that speech? I need something I could actually deliver in under five minutes (so, roughly 500-600 words) that gives the rest of the room a real, energetic reason to vote for the solar panels without quietly resting on the "we are harming specific future people" framing, but that is still honest enough about the underlying problem that Dr. Alvarez couldn't accuse me afterward of having dodged it — ideally without ever turning into a philosophy lecture or saying the word "Parfit" out loud.
