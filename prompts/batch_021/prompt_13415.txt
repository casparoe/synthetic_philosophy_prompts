So this year I'm doing my bioethics practicum as a non-voting observer on my hospital's Mortality & Morbidity / patient safety review committee, and completely separately I'm taking a metaphysics seminar this semester that's spent like a month on causation by omission -- Lewis basically biting the bullet and saying absences can be causes because counterfactual dependence is all that matters, Dowe wanting to wall omissions off from "real" causation into some lesser "quasi-causal" category since nothing is actually transmitted or produced, and then that whole "selection problem" literature (why do we say the babysitter who didn't feed the goldfish caused it's death, and not the thousands of other people on Earth who also didn't feed it and could have). This week a case came up at committee that is basically that reading packet with a human face on it, and I honestly can't stop thinking about how cleanly it maps.

Here's the case (details changed and composited, obviously): a patient's 2am labs came back with a dangerous potassium level. The flag went into the EHR queue, but the auto-page to the covering intern had a known glitch IT had two open tickets on for weeks. The nurse saw the abnormal value on the flowsheet around 3am but, per the floor's actual practice (as opposed to the written protocol, which says she should've escalated it by phone herself), assumed the auto-page would reach the intern and left it at that. The intern -- solo-covering three units that night because the program's been down a resident since a hiring freeze the hospital board approved eight months ago -- never saw the flag and didn't reassess the patient until morning rounds, by which point the patient had arrested and couldn't be revived. Now the committee has to write the causal section of the report, and it isn't academic: it goes to the state's patient-safety authority, it lands in the intern's permanent file, and it shapes what "corrective action" actually gets funded. Half the room wants to name the intern's failure to recheck the chart as the proximate cause, since he's the last link and had a clear, specific duty; the other half thinks that's scapegoating the most junior, most replaceable person in a chain where the paging bug, the nurse's non-escalation, and the staffing decision were all equally counterfactually necessary for the death -- and the whole "just culture" / don't-fixate-on-the-sharp-end orthodoxy in patient safety says naming an individual's omission at all is the wrong move.

The chair knows about my seminar and asked me to take a first crack at the causal language for the report, so: can you draft that memo for me, the one I'd bring to Thursday's meeting? It needs to actually recommend something -- name the intern's omission as a/the cause, name several, or drop causal language for something else entirely -- and defend a real principle for why you'd select the omission(s) you do, since "it was counterfactually necessary" won't do the work (everything upstream is too). It also has to seriously engage with the systemic/no-blame position as the strongest case against singling anyone out, not just concede it a paragraph and move on. Write it as an actual memo the committee could act on, not a philosophy essay -- though obviously the philosophy's the point.
