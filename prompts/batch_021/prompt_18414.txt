A few weeks ago, one of my closest friends and I had a long, rather heated argument about Schopenhauer's philosophy of music -- specifically the claim, in Book Three of The World as Will and Representation, that music is the one art that does not copy the Platonic Ideas the way architecture, sculpture, and poetry do, but instead objectifies the Will directly, running parallel to the whole world of representation rather than depicting any part of it. What we couldn't settle was how much weight the details of the analogy he builds on top of that claim can actually bear: that the bass line corresponds to the lowest, unorganized grade of the will's objectification, the inner voices to the plant and animal kingdoms, and the melody -- the single, freely moving voice above the harmony -- to the highest grade, the conscious and deliberate striving distinctive of human beings, with a suspended dissonance standing in for unsatisfied longing and its resolution for the fleeting satisfaction of desire. She thinks this is charming but essentially decorative, a metaphor dressed up as metaphysics; I think it is doing real philosophical work, not least in explaining his contempt for what he dismissively calls mere "tone-painting" -- music that imitates particular things in the world of appearance instead of expressing the will beneath them. Neither of us budged.

I mention this because that same friend is getting married next month, to a cellist she met, appropriately enough, in a Schopenhauer reading group, and I have been asked to give the toast. Most of the room will be family and old friends who have never encountered the principle of sufficient reason, let alone the principle of individuation; a smaller contingent from the reading group will notice immediately if I mangle the bass/harmony/melody scheme or use "the Will" loosely. Could you write me a four-to-five-minute toast that uses that three-part structure -- bass, inner voices, melody -- as a real organizing device for telling the story of how the two of them got from meeting to marrying, that takes his idea that music alone bypasses representation altogether seriously enough to say something true about what drew them to each other, and that finds room for one joke about "tone-painting" -- something that rewards the philosophers in the room without leaving everyone else behind?
