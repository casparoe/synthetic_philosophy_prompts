Over the last several months I have been teaching myself philosophy of language in my spare time, mostly by following citation trails through Wikipedia and the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy after work, and I recently got stuck on something inside Donald Davidson's idea of "radical interpretation." As I understand it, Davidson argued that when you try to work out what a complete stranger's utterances mean, you have no independent way of knowing their beliefs or their language in advance, so you effectively have to solve for both at the same time, and the only way to get the process off the ground is to assume the speaker is, on the whole, rational and mostly right about the world — what he called the principle of charity. That much felt intuitive to me. But then I came across a reference to a philosopher named Richard Grandy, who apparently thought that "maximize truth and consistency" was not quite the right rule to interpret by, and proposed something called the principle of humanity instead, which is supposed to care more about whether the beliefs and desires we attribute to someone make ordinary human psychological sense than about whether they come out literally true. I have been trying for a while to picture an actual situation in which the two principles would pull apart and license different interpretations of the very same words, and I cannot quite manage it: maximizing truth and being "humanly reasonable" seem to line up in just about every example I can think of.

Could you compare the two principles properly for me — not just define them side by side, but explain what the real disagreement between them consists in — and, ideally, construct one concrete case in which a strictly charity-guided interpreter and a humanity-guided interpreter would end up assigning different meanings or beliefs to the same utterance? I would also be curious which of the two you think is ultimately the more defensible constraint to place on interpretation, or whether both should be replaced by something else entirely, and why.
