I'm teaching a seminar on conditionals this term, and we finally got to Lewis's 1976 triviality result for indicative conditionals -- the proof that if you take the equation P(A > B) = P(B|A) (the Stalnaker/Adams thesis, more or less) seriously as holding across the board, you can derive that A and B end up probabilistically independent for basically any A and B, which is absurd. I walked the class through the algebra on the board: conditionalize on B and you get P(A>B | B) = 1, conditionalize on ¬B and you get P(A>B | ¬B) = 0, run the law of total probability and out pops P(A>B) = P(B), which combined with the thesis itself gives you P(B|A) = P(B). Everyone nodded along at each step, but afterward a student asked "okay, but why does that have to happen, in plain English," and I realized my honest answer was basically "because the math says so," which isn't great pedagogy.

So here's what I'm after: can you give me an intuitive, mostly symbol-free story for why the result holds? I'd especially love it if you zeroed in on why the proof needs the thesis to keep holding after you conditionalize on B and on ¬B seperately -- not just for the agent's actual, current credence function -- since that seems to be where all the real work happens, but I can't quite articulate why that's the crucial ingredient rather than just a technical convenience Lewis throws in. And as a bonus, if you've got a clean way of explaining why the argument needs a genuinely "rich" space of propositions to get off the ground (rather than just two disjoint ones), I'd love to hear it -- that's the bit I need for the inevitable follow-up question about whether restricting the algebra is a real way out for people who still want to hold onto the thesis.
