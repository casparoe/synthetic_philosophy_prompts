I spent my career as a comparative physiologist (mostly amphibians, some fish), retired a few years back, and lately I've fallen properly down a philosophy rabbit hole -- currently stuck on the brain-death literature, which my niece (she's in med school) got me started on. It scratches the same itch the species problem used to scratch for me professionally: everyone's confident there's a real fact being tracked, nobody agrees on the criterion, and which criterion you pick changes real cases on the ground.

Here's my rough list of the live contenders for what actually makes a human organism dead:
1. the classic whole-brain view -- death is the irreversible loss of the organism's "integrative unity," seated in the brain
2. the Presidents Council's 2008 patch-job -- forget integration, death is loosing the capacity for the basic "vital work" of a living thing (openness to the world, acting on it, needing to)
3. the higher-brain/neocortical view -- what matters is irreversible loss of the capacity for consciousness, brainstem be damned
4. the plain old circulatory-respiratory criterion, which is what donation-after-circulatory-death actually runs on in practice
5. Shewmon's objection -- correctly-diagnosed "whole-brain-dead" bodies keep on healing wounds, fighting infections, even gestating, for years, so the integration story is just bad biology
6. the "legal fiction" view -- brain death was never meant to track real biological death, it's a useful category so transplant programs can operate within the dead-donor rule
