I organize a small interdisciplinary colloquium on the foundations of mathematics, and next month's invited speaker is a logician known for arguing against the grain of her field: she thinks the working mathematical community's near-total confidence in the consistency of Peano Arithmetic -- let alone ZFC -- rests on much thinner epistemic ground than practitioners like to admit, and that ultrafinitist-style worries about a hidden inconsistency deserve to be treated as a live possibility rather than a historical curiosity. The topic came up at a departmental reading group a few weeks ago, where we worked through Gentzen's 1936 consistency proof and someone brought up Edward Nelson's brief, later-retracted 2011 claim to have actually proved Peano Arithmetic inconsistent; the discussion was interesting but stayed fairly superficial, and I would like the colloquium Q&A to go further.

Could you draft eight to ten interview questions for her that do not just invite a restatement of the position? I want at least one question on the regress generated by Gödel's second incompleteness theorem: since no proof of PA's consistency can be carried out in a system no more secure than PA itself, what does she think is actually entitled to ground our confidence, if not proof; one on whether Gentzen's move of trading arithmetical unprovability for transfinite induction up to ε₀ amounts to any real epistemic gain or simply relocates the worry to less familiar territory; one on how she calibrates our graded confidence across PA, ZFC, and ZFC augmented with large cardinal axioms, given that even the striking near-linear ordering of these theories by consistency strength is itself an unproved empirical regularity rather than a theorem; and one on what she thinks the Nelson affair actually establishes, given that the error was caught within weeks by ordinary expert scrutiny rather than lingering undetected. For each of these four, please add a brief follow-up question keyed to the response she is most likely to give, and close the full set with a question asking her to specify what, short of an impossible proof of consistency, would actually count as evidence against her own skepticism.
