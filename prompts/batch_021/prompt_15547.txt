So this one's a bit personal and I could use some help finding the right words. My old mentor Walt — the guy who trained me back when I was a cub reporter, long before I drifted into covering science and ideas full time — died last week, and his family has asked me to give the eulogy this Saturday. Here's the complicated part. Walt was a legendary investigations guy, the kind who broke a huge story about kickbacks on the county water contracts back in the 90s purely because he refused to believe a press release just because it came on official letterhead. That exact same instinct — don't trust the official story, follow the money, ask who benefits — is what sent him, in the last stretch of his life, down the 9/11-truther hole and then, worse, into some genuinely unhinged covid stuff, to the point where a couple of his oldest newsroom buddies stopped picking up when he called. The room on Saturday is going to be split down the middle: half his old colleagues, hardnosed empiricist types who will visibly wince at anything that sounds like it's excusing where he ended up, and half his kids and a couple of the online friends from his last years, who loved him and mostly still share his views, and who'll wince right back if I make their dad sound like a cautionary tale.

I've been reading around on the epistemology of conspiracy theories for a piece I'm working on, and there's an idea that keeps nagging at me — that you can't just wave away "conspiracy theorizing" as a single category, since some conspiracy theories turn out to be dead right (Watergate being the classic one), so whatever seperates the good ones from the bad ones has to be something more specific, like whether a theory can actually be falsified by new evidence versus one that's built so any piece of counter-evidence just gets absorbed as further proof of the cover-up, growing to explain it's own refutation. That distinction feels like the only honest hook for this eulogy — it lets me honor the instinct that made him a genuinely great reporter while being truthful about the very specific way it curdled at the end, without the whole thing reading as either a takedown or a whitewash. Could you write the eulogy for me? Keep it to about five minutes read aloud, have it actually lean on that "falsifiable vs. self-sealing" distinction to do some work rather than just gesturing vaguely at "he always asked questions," and pitch it so his old newsroom colleagues are nodding along and his kids don't feel like they're being lectured at their own father's funeral.
