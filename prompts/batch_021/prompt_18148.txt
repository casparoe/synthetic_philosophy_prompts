Here is the student's answer: "Bentham's manuscripts on sexual irregularity extend the logic of the felicific calculus to conduct that the criminal law of his day treated as a capital offense, above all male homosexual acts, which he sometimes discussed under the heading of 'paederasty.' His basic move is that punishment is itself a pain, so on the principle of utility it must be justified by a proportionate prevention of pain elsewhere; since private acts between consenting parties produce no harm to third parties, the pain inflicted by the law, and by the social stigma and blackmail it enables, is pure loss, unredeemed by any offsetting gain. He works through the standard justifications for punishing such conduct -- that it offends God, that it damages the parties themselves, and that it threatens the population by diverting men from procreative sex -- and rejects each in turn: the theological argument smuggles in a standard external to utility, since we have no way of reading God's will except through utility itself; the paternalist argument cannot show that the pleasure taken is outweighed by any self-regarding harm; and the population argument fails empirically, since engaging in these acts does not stop a man from also marrying and procreating. He treats the visceral disgust most people feel toward this conduct as an instance of what he elsewhere calls the principle of sympathy and antipathy, a pseudo-principle that dresses up unreasoned feeling as though it were a reason, and Bentham included this very discussion in the published Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation as a worked illustration of that critique. Running beneath the whole discussion is Bentham's insistence that individuals hold a natural right to determine the private uses of their own bodies, a right the legislator has no standing to override merely because the majority happens to find its exercise distasteful. On this basis Bentham arrives at a position -- decriminalization of private, consensual 'irregularities' -- that anticipates Mill's harm principle by roughly seventy years, though unlike Mill he reaches it through the arithmetic of pleasure and pain rather than through any appeal to liberty as such."
