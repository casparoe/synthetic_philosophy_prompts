I'm the token philosopher on the board of a smallish foundation that funds neglected animal-welfare research -- mostly the usual farmed-animal stuff, but we started a "wild animal welfare" grant line last year and it's already causing headaches at the board level. The proposal in front of us this cycle is from a team of wildlife biologists asking for three years of funding to pilot an immunocontraceptive vaccine, delivered by dart so no capture is needed, on a fenced deer population that's been overshooting its range for a decade. There's no hunting allowed on the land and the predators that used to keep the herd in check are long gone, so every hard winter a good chunk of the herd dies slow of starvation. The PI is upfront in the cover letter that the real pitch isn't "habitat management," it's animal welfare, plain and simple: fewer deer suffering a bad death, and contraception is a lot less invasive then darting them with something lethal or trapping-and-relocating.

Our board is properly split, and not along the lines I expected going in. A couple of members think that once you grant that wild animals' suffering counts morally at all, and once you notice that humans caused this particular mess (we're the ones who shot out the wolves and cougars generations back), funding this is close to a no brainer -- whats the alternative, letting them starve because it's "natural"? But my two ecologist board members have a worry that isn't just "don't play god" squeamishness: contraception messes with herd age structure and behavior in ways that ripple into browsing pressure and then into everything else living on that land, and once a funder like us is in the business of optimizing wild population sizes for aggregate welfare, there's no obvious principled place to stop short of managing every suffering-causing population we can get a dart gun near. I went into this meeting assuming I'd find that second worry a bit precious, and I'm annoyed to report it's landed harder than I expected.

Could you draft the memo I need to bring to next month's board meeting? I need an actual recommendation -- fund, decline, or fund with specific conditions -- not a survey of considerations, and it has to take the ecologists' epistemic-humility worry seriously on its own terms rather than waving it off as status-quo bias, since that's the exact objection I'll get grilled on. A page or so is fine; it just needs to be something I can defend line by line when I'm asked hard questions about it.
