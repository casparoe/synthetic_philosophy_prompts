And if I'm remembering the secondary reading right, the very last thing he's in the middle of arguing, right before the manuscript cuts off, is why women shouldn't get full political rights. There's something so perfectly awful about that for a book that I can't stop thinking about it.

But what's actually been bugging me is the argument itself, the part before it breaks off. Spinoza's whole political philosophy runs on this idea that your natural "right" to something just IS your power to actually get or do it, no more and no less. Okay, fine. I tried explaining this to my book club last week and immediately got stuck: if right just tracks power like that, why doesn't whoever (or whatever faction) happens to have the most power simply have the most right to rule alone, more or less the way Hobbes lands on his sovereign? Spinoza is adament that this same power-is-right premise favors spreading power out across the whole multitude instead, and calls that arrangement the most "natural" and stable one, but I don't actually get the mechanism that gets him from the metaphysics to that political conclusion rather than to some kind of strongman logic. Could you walk me through how that argument is actually supposed to work, in some real detail? And while you're at it, I'd love your take on whether the unfinished bit about women is a genuine inconsistency in the framework, or whether -- more uncomfortably -- it actually follows from the same logic he uses to justify democracy in the first place.
