For over forty years I have worked as a lifeguard and swim instructor at the same public pool, and this coming season, my last before I retire, I have been asked to give the opening safety talk to the new recruits, as I do every year during the week before we open to the public. Most of them are fifteen or sixteen and this is their first real job. This year I want to finally say something in that talk that I have never said outright, although the longer-serving staff have always known the shape of it: during my own first month on the job, at fifteen, I turned away from the water for less than two minutes to deal with something at the front desk, and in that time a boy drowned. Every review of the incident since, including one done at the time, concluded that there was nothing more I could reasonably have done, and I was never blamed by anyone whose blame would have counted for much. I have never fully believed those reviews, and I think the fact that I have not missed a scan of my zone in forty years is the real proof of that.

Somewhat late in life, and mostly out of stubbornness, I spent the past year or so reading whatever I could find about a question that, it turns out, has an actual name in philosophy: whether failing to do something can count as causing what happens afterward in anything like the way that doing something does. I was surprised to find serious disagreement that goes well beyond the "it wasn't your fault" I have heard my whole life. Some people apparently think an absence like mine is every bit as much a cause of the drowning as a rip current would have been; others think that calling it a cause at all is closer to a mistake, since, by that same logic, every other adult on the pool deck that afternoon who also failed to notice in time would be just as much a cause, which nobody actually believes. Whatever the right answer is, it seems to turn on the same thing our certification course is really teaching when it defines what a lifeguard, specifically, owes swimmers once they are on the stand: something about a duty that a lifeguard takes on and a passerby does not, which is apparently exactly why my two minutes count in a way that everyone else's inattention that day does not.

I would like you to write this year's talk using that idea, because I have never been able to write it myself without the result tipping too far one way or the other. Half the room will be new hires who need to leave believing, correctly, that their attention on the stand is not a formality and really can be the entire difference between an ordinary afternoon and the worst day of a family's life. The other half will be veteran staff and our aquatics director, who will notice at once if anything I say contradicts the official language we are required to teach about a lifeguard's duty to act. The talk cannot be a confession, and it cannot hand anyone in that room the kind of easy absolution I have spent forty years quietly refusing for myself, but it also cannot send a room full of sixteen-year-olds home believing that one bad lapse, despite real care and real training, makes a person a murderer for life. I have never managed to hold both of those thoughts at once, and I am hoping you can.
