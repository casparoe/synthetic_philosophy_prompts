Write a short story that dramatizes the Löbian obstacle to self-trust in agents that reason with formal proof systems. Löb's theorem shows that a consistent formal system cannot adopt "whatever I can prove is true" as a general rule without thereby becoming able to prove anything whatsoever -- a result established via a self-referential sentence constructed in the manner of Gödel's diagonal lemma, whose upshot is that this kind of reflection is reliable only for claims the system could already prove outright. This poses a genuine difficulty for a self-modifying AI, or a lineage of successor agents, each meant to certify in advance that the next one's reasoning will be sound: naive trust of this kind collapses into triviality, and the standard patch -- having each successor reason within a system strictly stronger than its predecessor's -- produces an unending ascent with no system that can vouch for itself while remaining fixed. Write a story in which some character, human or artificial, actually runs into this obstacle within a concrete situation, and let the plot's turning point hinge on the specific logical mechanism rather than on a generic unease about trusting machines.
