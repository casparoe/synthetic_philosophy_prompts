I'm a philosophy PhD student working in formal epistemology, and part of my dissertation concerns the "problem of logical omniscience" that standard Bayesian models of rational belief inherit from possible-worlds semantics. This term a reading group I'm part of (mostly logicians and computer scientists) worked through Garrabrant et al.'s "Logical Induction" paper, and I think I have a decent handle on the basic machinery: sentences are treated like assets in a market, a "trader" is a bounded arbitrageur betting on those assets according to some computable strategy, and the resulting sequence of prices counts as a logical inductor just in case no trader drawn from some restricted, efficiently computable class can drive its own worth to infinity as trading continues without bound. What I have not managed to explain to myself in a satisfying way is how this framework avoids the self-reference trouble that, as far as I understand, sank earlier attempts at this problem. My understanding is that if you try to build a single probability function, expressible in the very language it is grading, that satisfies an exact reflective/self-referential coherence principle, you can diagonalize against it and reconstruct something like a probabilistic analogue of Tarski's undefinability of truth. Logical induction is supposed to sidestep this, but every explanation I've worked out on my own just amounts to repeating "it's only required to hold asymptotically," which feels like relabeling the mystery rather than resolving it.

Could you explain, with real technical precision rather than analogy alone, why the shift from an exact fixed-point condition to the "no efficiently computable trader can achieve unbounded profit in the limit" criterion genuinely defuses the diagonalization argument instead of just deferring it? I am especially interested in two things: first, what work is being done by restricting the exploiting traders to a polynomial-time-computable class rather than to all computable functions or all functions simpliciter (i.e., why the argument breaks if you either loosen or tighten that class); and second, why it is acceptable for the market's price on a given self-referential sentence to be inaccurate, or even to fail to converge, at every finite stage, provided that the inaccuracy is never efficiently detectable and exploitable. A contrast case would help too: if one instead tried to define a single non-asymptotic assignment obeying an exact reflection principle, where precisely does the construction fail? I would like to come away able to reconstruct the core argument myself, rather than just finding it plausible when someone else walks through it.
