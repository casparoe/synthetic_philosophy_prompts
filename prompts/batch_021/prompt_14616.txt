Maple, my three-year-old golden retriever, has developed a habit I can't stop turning over in my head, partly because I have the novelist's occupational tic of mining ordinary life for material whenever a manuscript stalls. I taught her to fetch in our narrow back garden using a single battered yellow tennis ball, and within a couple of weeks she was flawless at it: I'd throw, she'd sprint out, retrieve, and deliver the ball to my feet, every single time, no exceptions. Last month I brought her to a large dog park for the first time, and something strange happened. I threw a green ball I'd packed for the occasion, and instead of chasing it, Maple bolted straight past it toward a stranger's dog that happened to be gnawing on an old yellow tennis ball, and tried to wrestle it away from him. She did this all afternoon: whenever a yellow ball was anywhere in view, she went for it, regardless of what I had actually just thrown, and she did so with the same brisk, tail-up assurance she shows when she's getting a trick exactly right, not the hesitating look she gives when a command genuinely stumps her.

I mentioned all this at a dinner with fellow novelists a few weeks later, half-hoping someone with a stray background in animal behavior might have a sharper account than the shrug I'd gotten from a friend at the park itself, who'd suggested Maple simply "isn't that bright" and that I should retrain her in more locations until the habit breaks. That answer has never quite satisfied me. Maple has no trouble learning far harder tricks elsewhere, so this doesn't look like a case of her failing to do something; it looks like a case of her doing something, fluently and wrongly, with total conviction that she's succeeding. That distinction is what keeps nagging at me, and I don't think my park friend's fix would even touch it, since retraining her across more yards might just teach her a wider rule that is still, underneath, tracking the wrong feature of the situation.

What I would love from you is a precise, conceptual account of what's actually happening when a confidently and competently executed piece of behavior turns out to have latched onto the wrong target in this way, and why that ought to count as a distinctive kind of failure, quite different in kind from simply lacking a skill. I'd also be curious whether you think this is really a quirk peculiar to dogs and back-garden training, or an instance of something much more general, liable to appear in essentially any learner, human, animal, or otherwise, that is trained by trial and error inside an environment narrower than the one it will eventually have to act in. And if you have a view on it, I would welcome a practical suggestion too: is there anything I could have done differently in the garden to keep this from happening in the first place, or is some version of this confusion simply unavoidable whenever training takes place somewhere smaller than the world?
