For my school's philosophy club, a few of us have been reading A.J. Ayer, the twentieth-century British logical positivist who spent much of his career arguing that any claim admitting no conceivable method of verification is not simply unproven but strictly meaningless, and who used this principle to dismiss most traditional talk of an afterlife as empty rather than false. What made the reading more interesting than I expected is that Ayer himself had a well-documented near-death experience in 1988, after his heart stopped for several minutes during a hospital stay, and that he wrote about it afterward in a way that only very slightly loosened his lifelong rejection of any prospect of surviving death; he still refused to treat the experience as genuine evidence for a soul or a God, even though it clearly unsettled him. My friends and I ended up arguing about how someone with Ayer's specific commitments, rather than a generic "skeptic," would respond to ideas that only became live philosophical debates well after his death in 1989, such as whole-brain emulation, mind uploading, and cryonic revival, all of which are often described today as secular, technological routes to surviving death.

What I would like your help with is working out, across a few well-developed paragraphs, the position you think Ayer would most plausibly have taken on mind uploading and cryonic revival specifically. Unlike claims about souls or heaven, these scenarios seem at least in principle testable, since an uploaded or revived mind could presumably be interrogated about its own experience, so I am curious whether you think Ayer's verification principle would actually count in favor of treating such claims as meaningful, or whether he would say that the deeper question of whether the resulting entity is really the same person, rather than merely a psychological copy, is exactly the sort of unverifiable metaphysical question his framework was designed to dissolve. Please ground your answer in his specific views on personal identity and physicalism, such as how he handled memory-based criteria of identity in his later work, and in the cautious, hedging way he actually responded to his own brush with death, rather than simply asserting that he would have been dismissive because he was famous for being a skeptic.
