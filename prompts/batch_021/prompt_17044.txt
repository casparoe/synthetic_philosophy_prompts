So I'm TAing a history of economic thought seminar and we just covered Keynes's 1930 piece "Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren." Everyone's takeaway is always the same tired point about the 15-hour week not panning out, which feels like low hanging fruit at this point. What I keep chewing on instead is the weirder part of the argument -- the bit where he says avarice, usury and precaution have to stay "our gods for a little longer still" to get us through the "tunnel of economic necessity," but once we're through it we're supposed to flip back to treating avarice as a vice and loving money as a "disgusting morbidity." There's also that throwaway line about the man who doesn't really love his cat, only his cat's kittens' kittens -- his example of the pointless future-directed "purposiveness" we're eventually meant to be cured of. If Keynes came back today and you showed him the FIRE movement, LinkedIn hustle-culture influencers, and effective altruism's longtermist wing, do you think he'd read all that as confirming his psychology (people who never got the all-clear to stop grasping), or is there something going on there that his framework just doesnt have room for? Which of his specific claims do you think hold up best, and which look the most naive in hindsight?
