As part of my dual-enrollment Intro to Philosophy course, we're required to peer-grade a classmate's short-answer homework using a rubric our instructor provides, and I'd like a second opinion before I submit my score.

The assignment question was: "William Molyneux posed the following question to Locke: a person blind from birth has learned to distinguish a cube from a globe by touch alone. If that person were suddenly made to see, could they, before touching either object, say by sight alone which is the cube and which is the globe? (a) Explain how Locke and Molyneux themselves answered this question, and why. (b) Leibniz and Berkeley both responded to Locke's discussion of the problem. Explain how each one's position compares to Locke's, and on what grounds each agrees or disagrees with him. (c) Briefly explain how modern experiments on newly sighted patients (e.g., research associated with Project Prakash) have actually tested this question, and what their results show."

Here is my classmate's answer:

"No — Molyneux and Locke both held that a newly sighted person could not tell the shapes apart by sight alone. Their reasoning was that touch and sight give us separate, unconnected trains of ideas: the blind man has learned to attach the word 'cube' to a certain tactile sensation, but he has never had any experience connecting that sensation to a visual appearance, so he has nothing to go on. He would need to see and touch the objects together at least once before the association could form.

Leibniz and Berkeley are usually mentioned together as the two classic responses to Locke, but they actually land in different places. Berkeley, in the New Theory of Vision, agreed with Locke's 'no,' but for a stronger reason: he held that visible shape and tangible shape aren't even the same property picked up by two different senses — they are entirely different kinds of ideas that we have merely learned, through habit, to associate with each other, which is a stronger claim than Locke's own argument requires. Leibniz, in the New Essays, disagreed with Locke and held that the newly sighted person would tell the shapes apart right away, since a globe presents the same appearance from every angle while a cube does not, so the difference would be visually obvious to anyone with working eyes.

Modern research has actually tested this question directly. Project Prakash, which provides cataract-removal surgery to congenitally blind children in India, let researchers run real Molyneux's-problem experiments for the first time. Held and colleagues found that newly sighted patients performed at chance when asked to pick out, by sight alone, a shape they had previously only touched, and this cross-modal gap persisted even after further visual experience — vision and touch never fully integrated for shape. This is a clean, direct vindication of the empiricist side of the debate: Locke and Berkeley were right, and Leibniz's rationalist confidence that reason alone could bridge the senses turned out to be empirically false."
