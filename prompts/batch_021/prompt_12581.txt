Since retiring from a career in condensed matter physics, I've joined a small philosophy discussion group that meets monthly to work through things we missed the first time around. At our last session, nominally on presentism versus eternalism, two members, Miriam and Gerald, got into a disagreement the rest of us couldn't settle, and I'd value an outside judgment on it.

(1) Miriam pressed the relativity-of-simultaneity argument for eternalism: two inertial observers in relative motion who merely pass each other on the street have differently tilted planes of simultaneity, so a distant event already on one observer's "now" can still lie on the other's future (this is essentially Penrose's case of two pedestrians and a fleet-launch decision near Andromeda). Since no frame is privileged, there is no frame-independent global "now" for presentism's shared present to be: either every such event is real, or none is.

(2) Gerald replied that this refutes only a global, frame-wide "now," not presentism itself: a presentist can instead say that what's real for you is fixed entirely by your own past light cone, a relation that comes out the same in every frame since it never invokes a simultaneity slice at all, so presentism escapes the argument untouched.

(3) Miriam answered that this "escape" concedes the point: if presentness is relativized separately to every single point, with no further fact about which points are jointly "now," there is no single present moment left to carry the metaphysical weight presentism needs -- only the block universe's light-cone structure dressed in tensed vocabulary.

(4) Gerald responded that giving up a universe-wide simultaneous present is not giving up presentism, since relativity itself is the independent reason to drop exactly that assumption; insisting that "real" presentism must retain it just begs the question by holding the view to its pre-relativistic formulation rather than to its best available descendant.

Neither side budged. Both of them have the physics right; it's the metaphysics I can't referee myself. Could you work through moves (1) through (4), indicate who has the better of each specific step, and close with whatever you think both of them are still missing?
