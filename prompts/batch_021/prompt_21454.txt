I'm on my high school's Ethics Bowl team, and while we were preparing our case packet on the ethics of war for the regional tournament next month, two of my teammates, Leah and Marcus, got into a disagreement during practice that our coach never quite managed to settle before we ran out of time. He had assigned us Michael Walzer's defense of what he calls the "moral equality of soldiers," along with a section of Jeff McMahan's book *Killing in War* arguing against it, and the two of them ended up on opposite sides, going back and forth for almost twenty minutes. I wrote down the exchange as carefully as I could because I want to bring a clear answer back to practice, but I honestly cannot tell who has the better of it at each stage.

(1) Marcus opened by defending Walzer's position: he argued that we cannot require an ordinary soldier, or even most generals, to correctly determine whether their side's war has just cause (jus ad bellum) before they are permitted to fight, since that determination depends on historical, legal, and strategic judgments well beyond what anyone on a battlefield can reasonably be expected to make. So, he concluded, soldiers on both sides who abide by the laws of war (jus in bello) should count as moral equals: equally entitled to kill enemy combatants, and equally entitled to protection if captured.

(2) Leah replied that whether soldiers can figure out the justice of their cause is beside the point, because the real question is whether they actually have the right to kill, and that has to depend on facts about the cause itself. If a war lacks just cause, she said, the soldiers fighting for it are, whatever they sincerely believe, engaged in objectively impermissible killing -- in the same way that sincerely but wrongly believing you are under attack does not give you a genuine right to shoot first.

(3) Marcus countered that this proves too much: an eighteen-year-old conscript who was drafted, fed nothing but state propaganda, and would be executed for desertion is now, on Leah's view, just as legitimate a target for lethal force as the head of state who started the war, which seems to erase any moral difference between the architects of an unjust war and its most coerced participants.

(4) Leah said that objection confuses two different questions: whether someone is a legitimate target for defensive force, and how blameworthy they are for becoming one. The conscript can be nearly blameless, she said, given the threats and deception used against him, while still being liable to defensive force, because liability tracks the objective danger you pose to people who have done nothing to forfeit their right not to be attacked, not how much you personally deserve blame for posing it.

(5) Marcus responded that this move relocates the problem rather than solving it: if liability doesn't require culpability, Leah's view owes us some account of how much responsibility for a threat is enough to make someone a fair target, and any line she draws looks bad. Draw it low, he said, and you license lethal force against people barely more responsible than someone acting in their sleep; draw it high enough to exclude the conscript, and you can no longer say most soldiers in actual wars, who fought under some version of exactly this pressure, were ever legitimate targets in the first place -- which was supposed to be the whole point of rejecting Walzer.

Neither of them backed down, and it was clear both had actually done the reading rather than just repeating slogans.
