A few weeks ago I had a long, fairly heated argument with my friend Leo about whether Aristotle actually has anything worth calling an economic theory, which started after our philosophy reading group spent a session on the Nicomachean Ethics' treatment of exchange and justice. We didn't get anywhere, but the same territory came up again, more sharply, last week between two other people in the group, Marisol and Devin, while I mostly just listened, and I'd like a considered outside opinion on their exchange, since I honestly couldn't tell who was getting the better of it.

The trigger was Aristotle's claim, in the Politics, that of all the ways of getting wealth, lending money at interest is the most unnatural, since money's proper function is to serve as a medium for exchanging goods people actually need, whereas interest produces currency directly from currency, with nothing made or exchanged in between - Aristotle even leans on the fact that the Greek word for interest, tokos, also means "offspring," as though interest were money unnaturally breeding. (1) Marisol opened by calling this a category mistake wearing a biology costume: a productive loan funds a real venture - a workshop, a voyage - that wouldn't otherwise happen, so what the lender collects back is compensation for supplying capital exactly when the borrower needed it and didn't have it, plus a premium for the risk that the venture fails, which is precisely the kind of genuine contribution Aristotle says is missing. (2) Devin replied that this defends interest against a complaint Aristotle isn't quite making: Aristotle doesn't object to profit as such, since trade undertaken to meet a household's genuine needs is for him perfectly natural; what worries him is wealth-getting (chrematistike, in his terms) that has no natural stopping point because it has slipped loose of any use it serves, and a fixed-interest loan, unlike a share in a venture's actual profits, is owed in full whether the venture succeeds or fails - so it looks less like payment for a real service than a claim on the borrower's future output that neither tracks, nor shares the risk of, any output actually produced. (3) Marisol shot back that this rescue proves too much: a landlord's rent is likewise owed whether or not the tenant's business succeeds, yet Aristotle's discussion of natural and unnatural ways of acquiring wealth never so much as mentions rent, let alone singles it out the way it does interest - so either "shares the risk" isn't really the principle doing the work for Aristotle, or his catalogue of what's natural is incomplete in a way that should make us suspicious it tracks anything principled at all. (4) Devin granted that Aristotle never says how rent would fare under his own view, but insisted that only shows a gap in Aristotle's application of a good idea, not a defect in the idea itself, and that "guaranteed extraction versus shared risk" should be judged on its own merits regardless of how thoroughly Aristotle happened to apply it.

Could you go through this move by move and say who has the better of it at each of the four numbered points, rather than just declaring an overall winner, and flag anything important that neither of them raised? I'm especially interested in whether Devin's "gap, not a flaw" move at point (4) is doing real philosophical work, or is just a way of not conceding what point (3) actually showed.
