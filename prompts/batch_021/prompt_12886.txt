I'm a PhD student in economics working on decision theory under risk, and I'd like help pressure-testing an argument before I build a term paper around it. The Kelly criterion directs a repeated bettor to maximize expected log wealth, equivalently the long-run geometric growth rate, and Breiman's theorem shows that this strategy will, with probability one, eventually outperform any other strategy given enough plays. Some now read this as grounding an independent norm of "geometric rationality": because wealth compounds multiplicatively, what a single persisting agent actually experiences across repeated plays is the time-average growth rate, not the expected value across a hypothetical ensemble of parallel agents. This is the ergodicity-economics argument associated with Ole Peters, which claims to derive the log-optimal rule from the dynamics of the gamble itself, rather than positing log utility as a preference. Paul Samuelson's classic rebuttal was that this simply reintroduces log utility by the back door, with no stronger claim to rationality, however many times the bet is repeated, than any other concave utility function already permitted by the expected-utility axioms.

Compare these two positions directly. Does the ergodicity/time-average argument supply a genuinely independent justification for treating geometric growth-rate maximization as uniquely rational, or does Samuelson's charge of smuggled-in log utility survive against it essentially intact? Justify your answer, and say what, if anything, a defender of geometric rationality would need to add to escape the charge.
