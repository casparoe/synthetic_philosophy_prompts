I'm a high school senior writing an independent research paper on bias in criminal-justice algorithms, and the more I read about the famous fight over the COMPAS recidivism tool, the more it looks like a clash between two definitions of "fair" rather than a factual dispute, since both sides' statistics turned out to be correct. ProPublica's 2016 investigation found that Black defendants who did not reoffend were classified as high-risk far more often than white defendants who did not reoffend, meaning the false positive rates differed by race. Northpointe, the company behind COMPAS, countered that the tool was fair because it was well calibrated: a given risk score corresponded to roughly the same probability of reoffending regardless of race. The impossibility results proven by Kleinberg, Mullainathan, and Raghavan, and separately by Chouldechova, show why both sides could be right about their own numbers: whenever two groups have different base rates for the outcome being predicted, no score can be calibrated within groups and have equal error rates across groups at the same time, short of a perfect predictor. Please write an essay that first explains, in your own words, why unequal base rates force this trade-off, and then weighs the normative case for prioritizing calibration versus error-rate balance when the two conflict in a setting like pretrial risk assessment. Don't settle for concluding that it simply depends on one's values -- defend a specific verdict, and say whether that verdict would change if the same kind of risk score were used to determine eligibility for a beneficial program, such as a public-health outreach service, rather than to decide whether to detain someone.
