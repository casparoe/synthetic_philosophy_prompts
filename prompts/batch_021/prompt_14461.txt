I spent most of my career running a biochemistry lab, and now that I'm retired I've been using the free time to finally read all the philosophy I always felt a bit guilty about skipping. Lately I've been on a Frege kick, partly because predicate logic is about the closest thing in that world to the kind of rigor I was used to, and partly because an old colleague loaned me a biography over coffee last month that got me completely sidetracked from the actual logic into the man's life. Turns out that in the spring of 1924, a little over a year before he died, Frege kept a diary for a few weeks that then sat unpublished for decades until historians dug it up, and its full of genuinely nasty stuff - wanting Jews stripped of political rights, pining for some strongman to sweep away the Weimar parliament, that whole ugly package. From what I've read, this really shook people like Michael Dummett, who'd spent a chunk of his career treating Frege as basically the patron saint of analytic philosophy and then had to work out in public what he actually thought about that.

What I can't settle in my own head is how this stacks up against the other big case everyone brings up, Heidegger and his actual Nazi party card. My sense as a total outsider is that the two feel different in kind: with Heidegger, apparently plenty of serious scholars think notions like authenticity, historicity, and the whole business about a people's shared destiny really do feed into the fascism, so that the two are woven together rather than just sitting side by side. Frege's case looks totally different to me - I can't find any thread connecting the sense/reference distinction, or the logicist project in the Grundlagen, to his opinions about Jews and parliaments, its more like two sealed rooms in the same house. But I've been burned before assuming two things in a field I didn't know were unrelated, when people who actually knew the field told me otherwise, so I don't fully trust that instinct here.

So here's what I'd actually like from you: compare the two cases properly instead of just taking my hunch at face value. For both Frege and Heidegger, lay out the strongest version of the argument that the repugnant politics is genuinely continuous with the core philosophical work, and the strongest version of the argument that it's a compartmentalized personal failing that leaves the technical philosophy untouched. Then tell me, with actual reasons, in which of the two cases the "entanglement" story is more defensible - I'd like a real argued verdict, not a diplomatic tie.
