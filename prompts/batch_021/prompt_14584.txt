I spent the better part of four decades doing experimental work in condensed-matter physics, and it is only since retiring that I have had the leisure to read philosophy properly rather than in stolen hours. A few colleagues from the old department and I now meet monthly under the mild pretext of a "reading group," and this term we have been working through Leibniz: the Discourse on Metaphysics, the ensuing correspondence with Arnauld, and the closing fable of the Theodicy, in which Sextus Tarquinius is shown, within a vast representation of all the possible worlds, the quiet and happy life he might have led had he never wronged Lucretia. Leibniz is emphatic that this untroubled Sextus is not our Sextus under a different history, but a wholly distinct possible individual who simply never makes it into existence; in the letters to Arnauld he says much the same of Adam, insisting that an Adam who had not sinned would not be "our" Adam at all, since each individual substance's complete concept already contains, from the outset, every predicate that will ever be true of it, including the entirety of its relations to everything else in its world.

This has obvious resonances with the twentieth-century dispute between David Lewis's counterpart theory, on which particulars are confined to a single possible world and cross-world talk about what they "might have been" is handled through resembling but numerically distinct counterparts, and the rival transworld identity view—associated with Kripke's treatment of proper names as rigid designators and defended explicitly against Lewis by Plantinga—on which one and the same individual literally exists, with different properties, in more than one world. On the face of it, Leibniz looks like a forerunner of Lewis: no individual turns up twice across worlds, and cross-world comparison is handled by resemblance among distinct complete concepts rather than by identity. Two things complicate that picture, though. First, Leibniz's other worlds are not concrete universes on a par with the actual one, as Lewis's are, but unrealized alternatives within the divine understanding, so whatever "reality" his other-worldly Sextuses have is of a quite different order. Second, his so-called superessentialism—the thesis, apparently forced on him by the complete concept doctrine together with the identity of indiscernibles and the principle of sufficient reason, that literally every predicate of an individual is essential to it—looks considerably more restrictive than Lewis's counterpart relation, which is just similarity, and can be loosened or tightened by context without having to track every property at all. And yet Leibniz also occasionally speaks, in the letters to Arnauld, of a "vague" or incomplete Adam, conceived under only a handful of general predicates, of whom it seems to remain an open question whether he sins—loose talk that sounds considerably closer to ordinary transworld-identity intuitions than his official doctrine ought to permit.

I would value your considered reconstruction of where this actually leaves him. Given his real commitments—the complete concept doctrine, the identity of indiscernibles, the principle of sufficient reason, and a conception of possibility grounded in the divine understanding rather than in concrete plurality—would Leibniz's system, honestly followed through, place him on the counterpart-theory side of the modern debate, on the transworld-identity side, or somewhere neither label quite fits? I would especially welcome your view on how his position would fare against Kripke's well-known complaint that a mere counterpart, however similar, is simply the wrong individual to ground claims about what might have happened to someone in particular, and against Plantinga's related charge that counterpart theory changes the subject away from genuine de re modality—and on whether the "vague Adam" passages are best read as a harmless simplification for the sake of argument, or as a real fault line between Leibniz's official metaphysics and the way he actually reasons about possibility when he is not being careful. Two of us in the group are convinced that Leibniz becomes basically a Lewisian once you strip away the theology; a third insists the superessentialism makes him something stricter and stranger than either modern position has room for. I have not managed to convince myself of either.
