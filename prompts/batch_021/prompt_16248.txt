I'm finalizing the reading schedule for my grad seminar on consequentialism and effective altruism, and next week I've got Lenman's old paper "Consequentialism and Cluelessness" paired with Greaves's much later piece that tries to carve the problem up into "simple" versus "complex" cluelessness. I've taught this pairing three or four times now and every single time some sharp student asks a version of the same question that I've never managed to answer as cleanly as I'd like: did Greaves actually solve Lenman's problem, or did she just give it a more precise name and leave it sitting exactly where it was?

So I want you to do the comparison properly for me, not just "here's Lenman's view, here's Greaves's view" side by side. Specifically: work out where Lenman's central worry -- that basically any act ripples forward and, within a few generations, changes who even gets concieved, so that we can never actually know whether we've made the long run better or worse -- lands inside Greaves's taxonomy. Is that meant to be a "simple" cluelessness case we're licensed to just bracket because the unforeseeable effects wash out symmetrically between our options, or is it "complex" cluelessness, where we've got some reason to think the effects are skewed one way but nothing like a usable probability to act on?

And then give me an actual verdict, with the reasoning attached rather than a wishy-washy "both have a point": does Greaves's simple/complex machinery give act consequentialists a real way of living with cluelessness, or is it mostly bookkeeping that relocates Lenman's original worry into the "complex" bin and leaves it just as unsolved as it was before?
