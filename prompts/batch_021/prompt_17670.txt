And here's the student's answer, copied exactly:

"Kuhn's claim is that once a field goes through a genuine scientific revolution, the old and new paradigms don't share a common set of standards, problems, or vocabulary, so a proponent of one and a proponent of the other are, in some sense, talking past each other rather than disagreeing about the same thing. The standard illustration is the chemical revolution: phlogiston theorists and oxygen theorists weren't just wrong about each other's experiments, a term like "element" didn't even pick out the same kind of thing for the two camps, so there's no neutral description of the data that both sides could read the right answer off of. Kuhn's other go-to comparison is a Gestalt switch, like the duck-rabbit: you don't get argued into seeing the second figure, you just suddenly see it, and afterwards it's hard to fully recover the first way of seeing. Since the two paradigms share no common measure, there's no neutral standpoint left from which to weigh their claims against each other -- the choice between them isn't ultimately the kind of thing that has a rational verdict, only a kind of conversion that spreads through a community one scientist at a time.

This is exactly what drew fire from critics like Lakatos, who read Kuhn as making theory-choice a matter of mob psychology rather than argument, and Kuhn clearly found the charge damaging. You can see him responding to it directly later on: the 1969 postscript adds the exemplar/disciplinary-matrix distinction to clean up the notion of "paradigm," and by the essays of the 80s and 90s Kuhn had essentially given up the incommensurability thesis in response to the criticism, since he'd come to see that it couldn't be made consistent with preserving any rational element in theory-choice at all. So the later essays are best read as Kuhn conceding the force of the Lakatos-style objection, not as a mere clarification of a position he'd held all along."
