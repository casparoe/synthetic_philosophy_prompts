So this is going to sound like a weirdly specific thing to be stuck on, but I just started my ICU rotation this month (I'm a third-year med student) and sat in on a case conference about DCD -- donation after circulatory death -- that I haven't been able to shake since. Quick background for context: when a family decides to withdraw life support and the patient isn't brain dead, once the heart stops there's a mandatory "hands-off" waiting period -- somewhere between 2 and 5 minutes depending on the hospital's protocol, from what I can tell -- before the surgical team is allowed to go in and start recovering organs, because you're not supposed to take organs from someone until they're actually, legally dead. It's also made me look differently at the organ-donor box I checked on my license without thinking twice about it years ago.

Anyway, two of the critical-care fellows in the room got into a real back-and-forth about whether that waiting period tracks anything real, and I honestly can't tell who won. Ravi's position was that the wait is fine because after that point, circulatory function has *permanently* stopped -- nobody in the room is going to attempt resuscitation, the family doesn't want it, so for all practical purposes it's over, and "permanent" cessation is exactly what the accepted standards call for. Dana pushed back hard: she said this quietly swaps "irreversible" for "permanent," and those aren't the same thing -- there's documented cases of hearts restarting on their own a few minutes after apparent asystole, so the heart's ability to restart hasn't actually vanished, we've just agreed not to act on it. She said if the exact same heart, in the exact same physical state, counts as "alive" in a room where the team keeps working it and "dead" in a room where they've agreed not to, then whether the patient is dead depends on our intentions about what happens next rather than facts about the body, which seemed backwards to her for something this basic. Ravi shot back that literally every definition of death has some conventional piece baked in -- even brain death involves picks about which tests to run and when -- so you can't dock DCD for having a conventional element without junking the brain-death standard too. Dana granted that some convention is unavoidable, but said theres a difference between a convention about how we verify a fact thats already there and a convention that makes the fact itself depend on our plans, and that permanence is the second kind, which is what actually bothered her.

They got paged away before it went any further. I'd also love help sharpening the distinction Dana reached for at the end, between conventions-about-verifying and conventions-that-constitute -- it feels like it's doing a lot of the real work here, but she never quite nailed it down and neither can I.
