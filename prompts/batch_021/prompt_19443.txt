I've spent more of the last decade reading Astral Codex Ten, LessWrong, and the Alignment Forum than is probably good for me, and one side effect is that I can no longer sit on my hospital's clinical ethics committee without noticing when a routine-looking proposal is quietly reenacting a debate I've only ever seen conducted about artificial agents. The case now before us: our palliative care service wants to pilot a decision-support tool for patients who arrive incapacitated, with no advance directive, and either no available surrogate or a surrogate whose account of the patient's wishes the team has independent reason to doubt. Rather than resting the decision solely on family members' recollections of what the patient would have wanted — which the surrogate decision-making literature finds to be only modestly better than chance even when a surrogate is available — the tool would infer her likely treatment preferences from her own documented behavior: prior refusals and consents in the chart, medication adherence, earlier goals-of-care conversations, and, where a family member consents to share it, a broader digital footprint. In effect, it tries to do for one incapacitated patient what inverse reinforcement learning tries to do for an artificial agent: recover the preference function that best rationalizes a history of choices.

That analogy is exactly what unsettles me about the proposal, and it's splitting the committee along lines that have nothing to do with the usual bioethics factions. The tool's advocates treat it as a natural, more systematic version of the substituted-judgment standard we already apply informally. But I keep coming back to the classic Ng–Russell point that a great many reward functions, including degenerate ones, are consistent with any fixed pattern of behavior, so that recovering "the" preference behind it requires smuggling in assumptions about how rational, informed, or unbiased the person was — and to the further, more recent point, associated with work like Armstrong and Mindermann's on value learning, that no amount of preferring simpler explanations rescues you here, since a trivial "preference" paired with a sufficiently detailed model of the patient's biases fits the record just as well as whatever decomposition the tool's designers intended. If that's right, the tool's confident output about what she "really" wants may just encode its designers' unstated assumptions about her rationality, dressed up as a discovery about her. Against this, our actual alternative is not some ideal of unmediated access to the patient's will but a demonstrably imperfect family member's guess, and a structured tool — used as one input rather than a verdict — might still be a real improvement on that, especially in the cases where no family member is available to guess at all.

Could you draft the short written opinion I'll circulate to the committee before we vote? It should recommend a concrete disposition — approve the pilot, reject it, or approve it subject to specific conditions — with a realistic time frame or review point attached, and it should engage the identifiability worry above on its merits rather than either waving it away as too abstract for a bedside decision or treating it as an automatic veto. I'd like the reasoning laid out so that a committee member on either side could see exactly which point they would have to rebut in order to change the recommendation.
