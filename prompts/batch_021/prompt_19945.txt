So two people in my decision theory reading group (I'm in philosophy, most of them are econ PhDs) got into an argument about the centipede game that's stuck with me. Quick refresher: two players alternate "take" (end the game, get the bigger slice) or "pass" (pot grows, control passes to the other), for a fixed number of rounds. Backward induction says the last mover should take, so the second-to-last mover anticipates that and takes too, and it unravels back to round one -- so "rational" players defect immediately even though passing longer leaves both better off.

Kevin's claim: this is a genuine paradox, not just an unfortunate result. The unraveling only works if, at every node, the mover still trusts the other to reason correctly from here on. But you only reach round 4 or 5 because someone already deviated from what that reasoning predicted -- so being there is itself evidence against the assumption the argument needs in order to keep going. It undercuts it's own premises.

Mara's fix: stop treating it as reasoning that unfolds turn by turn. Fix a complete strategy for every node in advance, and ask which plans are consistent with common knowledge that both players are rational. Only "take everywhere" qualifies -- anything else requires expecting someone to do something they wouldn't actually want once that moment came, which contradicts common knowledge of rationality from the start. No updating needed, no "are we off-path" -- uncomfortable, not incoherent.

Kevin's comeback: that doesn't show round 5 gives zero information, it just defines the question away -- you've made "what should I believe right now" unaskable, not answered it.

Mara's reply: what's rational against a fallible opponent who might deviate again is a fine question, it's just a different game than the idealized one backward induction is actually a theorem about.

Neither budged, and both seemed to have a real point. Go through this move by move, say who's got the better of each individual exchange -- not just who wins overall -- and flag anything you think they're both missing or talking past each other on.
