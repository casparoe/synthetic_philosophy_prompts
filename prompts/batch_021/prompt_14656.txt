At a friend's birthday dinner a few weeks ago, someone told a story that somehow turned into a whole argument between two other people at the table, and I still can't decide who actually won each point (this group does this constantly, we can't just enjoy a dinner). The story: our friend Marco collects whiskey, and his local specialty shop runs a "trade-up" program where you hand over a bottle you own plus a small cash top-up for a different bottle on the shelf. Over a couple months Marco did three trades with them: he traded his everyday sipping bottle for a smokier one, because he decided the smoky one is better for evenings (+$15), then traded the smoky one for a rare, hard-to-find bottle, because that's better for gifting/showing off (+$10), then traded the rare one back for the exact everyday bottle he'd started with, because for casual weeknights he actually likes that one better than the fancy rare bottle (+$20). End result: he owns exactly what he started with, and he's $45 poorer.

Nora's take was that this is a textbook case of Marco getting money-pumped -- his preferences form a loop (smoky beats everyday, rare beats smoky, everyday beats rare), and any shop that noticed could walk him around that loop forever and slowly drain his wallet. Owen pushed back: he said that's not a fair read, because Marco wasn't ranking three things on one scale, he was using three different, perfectly legitimate criteria ("better for evenings," "better for gifting," "better for weeknights"), so there's no real inconsistency, just context-dependent taste -- no different from liking coffee more than tea in the morning and the reverse at night. Nora replied that the context story doesn't actually explain what happened: if those three preferences were genuinely independent and legitimate, there'd be no way to walk Marco in a circle that costs him money for literally nothing in return; the existence of a money-losing loop is proof that the "different criteria" were secretly functioning as one inconsistent ranking the whole time. Owen's comeback was that even granting its a loop, calling Marco irrational full stop assumes he'd keep falling for it -- but he wouldn't: the moment the shop offered to trade him back to the smoky bottle a second time, he'd clock the pattern and refuse, even though in the moment he might still feel like he prefers that trade. So at most, Owen said, this shows Marco made one $45 mistake before catching on, not that there's some deep flaw in him a clever bookie could exploit indefinitely -- noticing a pattern and stopping is just ordinary good sense, not evidence of some irrational core.

Neither of them budged, and I'd rather get an actual adjudication than a tiebreaker vote.
