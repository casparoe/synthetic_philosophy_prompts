I'm about two-thirds into a campus novel, and there's one scene I keep chickening out of writing. My protagonist's dissertation advisor, Renata, is a metaphysician whose whole career rests on the idea that "grounding" is a single, primitive relation running through the whole of reality — the one thing that makes it true that the mental is grounded in the physical, that a disposition is grounded in its categorical basis, that a set is grounded in its members, all as instances of the same underlying "in virtue of." At the hotel bar during a conference she gets cornered by Dov, her old grad-school rival, who's spent the last decade arguing that this whole "Big-G Grounding" project is basically hollow: for him there's no single primitive doing that work, just a pile of perfectly respectable "small-g" relations (composition, set-membership, functional realization, and so on) that don't even share a logic with each other, so bundling them into one relation is worse than useless — it's the kind of move that lets you smuggle in a transitivity inference across a chain of relations that were never transitive to begin with.

I sat through a real version of this fight in a reading group in grad school and remember it being definately electric, but I never got philosophically fluent enough myself to reconstruct the actual moves, and I refuse to put two cardboard cutouts on the page who just restate there thesis at each other for a page and a half. Could you write me the scene — dialogue only, maybe 600-900 words, minimal stage direction — where Renata and Dov actually go at it, each one responding to what the other just said instead of talking past each other? I'd like it to work in something like Dov's "no work for a theory of grounding" complaint and the transitivity worry, plus Renata's strongest comeback to the charge that Big-G Grounding is just an unclear placeholder standing in for whatever small-g relation happens to be doing the real work underneath. It's fine, honestly preferred, if neither of them "wins" by the end, so long as a grad student eavesdropping two stools down could tell you exactly what the fight was actually about.
