I've spent a long time reading around the rationalist blogosphere -- Overcoming Bias, the old LessWrong sequences, the decision-theory subthreads that pop up periodically on Astral Codex Ten -- and I've noticed that every discussion of the Allais paradox seems to resolve the same way. Someone lays out the two choice problems, observes that most people's preferences violate the independence axiom, and then closes the case with a money-pump or dynamic-consistency argument -- in the spirit of Eliezer Yudkowsky's old "Zut Allais!" post, if I have the title right -- to the effect that anyone with those preferences can be guaranteed to lose money, by their own lights, across a suitably designed sequence of choices. The moral is always that the preferences are what need fixing, not the axiom.

What that treatment leaves out is that the inference from dynamic consistency to independence was directly and technically contested. Mark Machina's generalized expected-utility framework allows the indifference curves over the probability triangle to fan out rather than remain parallel, which reproduces the common-consequence-effect pattern behind the Allais choices while still preserving stochastic dominance and continuity. And Machina's own reply to the money-pump argument was that it only succeeds given a further, substantive premise -- what the dynamic-choice literature calls consequentialism about subplans: the idea that a rational agent must value any continuation strategy purely as a function of the reduced lottery over final outcomes it yields, regardless of the path or the forgone branches that led there. Deny that premise, and the threatened exploitation doesn't obviously go through.

I'd like a dialogue that stages this disagreement at the level where it actually lives, rather than at the level of "some people have an intuition and are simply confused." One interlocutor should treat the independence axiom as non-negotiable and press the money-pump/dynamic-consistency argument in its strongest form, arguing as though consequentialism about subplans were just obviously part of what rationality requires. The other should defend the Allais choices as fully rational along Machina-style lines and, when the money pump is put on the table, should do something with it rather than shrug: explain why rejecting consequentialism blocks the exploitation, and concede plainly what that rejection costs. Let the first speaker land a genuine return blow -- for instance, by asking whether non-consequentialist evaluation is really doing anything different from just committing to a plan in advance and refusing to reconsider it, McClennen-style -- and close the exchange with each side stating, in a single sentence, what result or argument would actually change their mind. Something like fifteen to twenty exchanges should give the disagreement room to breathe; I'd rather you keep it tightly focused on this one crux than let it drift into the neighboring paradoxes (Ellsberg, St. Petersburg) that tend to get invoked in the same conversations but really turn on different axioms.
