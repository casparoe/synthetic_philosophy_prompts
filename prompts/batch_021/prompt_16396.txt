I have been reading rationalist blogs and forums for years, and I have picked up the habit of analyzing everyday situations in something like their idiom. An incident from last winter, though, left me with a disagreement I still have not resolved to my own satisfaction, and I would value an outside perspective on it.

While backcountry skiing alone, well outside cell range, I badly injured my knee in a sudden whiteout and could not walk out under my own power. I was eventually found by a local guide who happened to be out on his own snowmobile that day and had no affiliation with any official rescue service. He told me conditions were genuinely dangerous, given recent avalanche activity nearby, and that towing an injured stranger back to the trailhead was a real risk he was not willing to take for nothing; his default, he said, would be to radio the professional rescue service and simply wait with me at a distance until it arrived, though he was not confident I would still be in good shape by the time it did. He offered to take me down himself, but only for a substantial sum, payable once we were both off the mountain. I had no cash on me, no phone signal, and nothing to give him there and then, so all I could do was tell him I would transfer the money once I was home. He studied me for a long moment, apparently trying to judge whether I was the kind of person who would actually pay once the danger had passed and he had no way of enforcing anything. Only then did he agree to help, mentioning, a little bitterly, that he had been stiffed this way before.

I made it home safely and transferred the money that same day, without a second thought. When I recounted the story to a friend, she insisted I had acted irrationally: by the time I was home, the guide had no contract, no way of finding me, and nothing further to gain or lose from my decision either way, so paying him amounted to burning money for a feeling. I disagreed, but I could not explain why in terms she would accept, since she takes "rational" to mean whatever best serves you going forward, given the situation you actually find yourself in at the time you act. Was she right that, once I was safely home, keeping my word had become purely a matter of decency rather than rationality? Or is there a way of thinking about rationality on which paying was the more rational choice all along - and if so, what exactly does it say about how to act in situations like this one?
