I spent thirty-odd years as an experimental physicist before I retired, and these days I fill my mornings working through decision theory and social choice papers instead of lab notebooks -- turns out reasoning about ensembles and averages isn't so different whether you're doing statistical mechanics or welfare economics. Lately I've been chewing on Harsanyi's aggregation theorem, and specifically on the old objection from Peter Diamond (1967-ish, if memory serves) that goes roughly like this: there are two identical people and one indivisible good, and a planner can (a) give the good to person 1 outright, (b) give it to person 2 outright, or (c) flip a fair coin and hand the good to whoever wins the toss. Write down symmetric utilities -- 1 util for getting the good, 0 for not getting it -- and the expected sum of utilities comes out to exactly 1 under all three options. So any Harsanyi-style planner, whos committed to the expected-utility axioms plus respecting individual preferences ex ante, more or less has to be indifferent between a, b and c. Almost nobody actually feels that way though -- flipping the coin seems obviously fairer than just handing the good to person 1 for no particular reason, even though nothing about expected total utility distinguishes the two.

I brought this up with a friend from my chess club who did his PhD in economics, and he waved the whole thing away as a classic ex-ante/ex-post confusion -- once you've fixed the probabilities correctly, he says, theres nothing left to explain, and the fairness intuition is just people illegitimately smuggling in facts about the realized outcome after the coin's already landed. I'm not fully convinced that settles it, but I also can't quite put my finger on why not. So could you make the strongest case you can for the other side -- that the fairness intuition in Diamond-style examples is tracking something real, and that we should therefore reject, or at least heavily qualify, Harsanyi's route from expected-utility axioms to utilitarian aggregation? Please actually engage with the "you're just confusing ex-ante and ex-post" comeback rather than talking past it; I want a real argument, not a survey of who believes what.
