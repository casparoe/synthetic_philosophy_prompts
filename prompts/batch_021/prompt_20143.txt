Several years ago, not long before my aunt died, I made her a promise at her bedside that I've never told another living soul about — it doesn't really matter what it was — and I still fully intend to keep it, even though she's gone now and there is no one left on earth who could ever find out whether I do. Turning that over in my head is what got me reading old philosophy on my own, starting with the part of Hume's Treatise (Book Three, Part Two, where he deals with justice) that takes up the question of why a promise obligates anyone at all. What struck me is that he doesn't think this can be explained just by saying I "willed" myself into an obligation when I spoke the words, since on his view a private mental act can no more manufacture a real duty out of thin air than wishing could move a piece of furniture; the most he'll allow is that promising works purely as an artificial social convention, useful the way the rules of a shared practice are useful, which people have reason to uphold and to resent others for breaking once everyone understands how much they all gain from being able to rely on one another's word.

More recently I worked my way through T. M. Scanlon's very different account in What We Owe to Each Other, built around what he calls Principle F, where the whole "convention" framing drops out entirely. On his story, what actually generates the obligation isn't any shared social practice but the plain fact that I deliberately gave my aunt assurance, led her to form a specific expectation about what I would do, and could easily have avoided creating that expectation if I hadn't wanted to reassure her — so going back on my word now would amount to a kind of retroactive abuse of the trust I asked her to place in me at the time.

Neither story sits comfortably with my actual case. Hume's convention seems to need an ongoing practice between people who can still hold each other to it, and there's no one left to do that here; Scanlon's account seems to need an expectation that could still, in some sense, be let down, and my aunt is no longer around to be disappointed by anything. Could you walk me through, in real depth rather than a quick contrast, exactly how each of these two theories is supposed to generate the binding force of an ordinary promise — and then tell me whether either one, strictly on its own terms, has the resources to explain why mine still feels binding? If one of them can secretly handle it and I'm just missing the move, I'd like to see that move spelled out; and if you think both genuinely fail here, I'd like to understand precisely why, rather than being told it's just an unresolved hard case.
