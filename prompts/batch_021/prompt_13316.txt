For my "Ethics of Algorithms" class this semester (crosslisted between philosophy and CS, so we get readings from both sides and I honestly don't know who to blame for the workload) we just spent two weeks on the impossibility results in algorithmic fairness, specifically the Kleinberg-Mullainathan-Raghavan paper and the Chouldechova one from around the same time, showing that you basically can't have calibration within groups and equalized false positive/negative rates across groups at the same time, unless the base rates happen to be equal or your model is a perfect predictor (which never happens). My professor kept comparing it to Arrow's theorem, except for classifiers instead of voting rules — like, it's not a bug you can just code your way out of, its a genuine mathematical impossibility.

This actually hit close to home because my university just rolled out a predictive risk score within my college (only a few thousand students, not the whole university) that flags people "at risk of dropping out" so advisors know who to call in for extra check-ins. A friend on student senate told me the advising office is really proud that the tool is "well calibrated AND equitable across race and income," and after this week's lectures I'm pretty sure those two things can't both be fully true at once in any meaningful sense. Could you go through the most important implications of these impossibility results for a situation like this? Mainly I'm trying to figure out what advisors and administrators should actually be prioritizing once they accept they can't satisfy every fairness criterion simultaneously, and whether there's some principled way to pick which trade-off to make, or if that choice is really just a political/values judgment wearing a lab coat.
