I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, but I got pulled into this topic through an unlikely route: a few months ago I read Fukuyama's "The End of History and the Last Man," which leans heavily on the Hegelian idea that history is basically the working-out of mutual recognition between free citizens, ending in something like the liberal-democratic state. Curious where that idea actually came from, I went and read parts of the Philosophy of Right itself, and was surprised to find, in the section on international law (I think it's around paragraph 324, though I could be misremembering the number), Hegel arguing something closer to the opposite of what I expected: that a condition of lasting, "perpetual" peace between states would actually corrupt or stagnate a people's ethical life, and comparing war's role to the way wind keeps a body of water from turning foul. It reads like a direct answer to Kant's "Perpetual Peace" essay and its proposal of a federation of republican states that gradually renders war obsolete, though I honestly can't tell how much of that connection Hegel makes explicit versus how much I'm filling in myself.

I brought this up with a couple of friends over drinks and we argued about it for an hour without getting anywhere, so I'd like to see the exchange handled properly by people who actually know the material cold. Could you write me a dialogue between a contemporary Hegelian, defending the claim that war plays a genuine, positive role in the ethical life of states and that a lasting peace is neither achievable nor obviously desirable, and a contemporary Kantian cosmopolitan, defending the federation-of-states project against that charge? A few things I'd like built in: I don't want the Hegelian to come across as simply glorifying war -- my understanding is that Hegel's actual position is narrower than that, denying that war is a pure evil without going so far as to say it's simply good -- and I don't want the Kantian to just repeat that peace is preferable without engaging the stagnation argument on its own terms. Somewhere in the exchange, I'd like the Kantian to have to face the dilemma that a federation with no power to enforce its own rulings still seems to depend on the same contingent goodwill between sovereign states that produces wars in the first place, while a federation with real enforcement power starts to look like exactly the kind of world-state Kant himself worried would decay into something like "soulless despotism." And I'd like the Hegelian, in turn, to have to answer for the fact that this argument predates industrialized warfare, so it's fair to ask whether "war keeps a people ethically healthy" survives contact with what wars actually turned out to look like. Neither character should be a pushover for the other -- I want to see exactly where their disagreement stops being about definitions and starts being a real, substantive bet about how states and history actually work.
