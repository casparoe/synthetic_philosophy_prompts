I've been running a grad seminar on theories of truth this term, and last week's session on dialetheism turned into a proper argument that's still bugging me, so I wanted an outside opinion on it. Everyone in the room agreed that Priest's whole pitch rests heavily on the "revenge" problem: gap theories like Kripke's fixed-point construction, or Tarski-style hierarchies that just refuse to let the liar sentence be well-formed in the first place, say in effect that the liar is neither true nor false, but then "this sentence is not true" comes roaring back as a strengthened liar the theory can't classify without contradiction. Priest's dialetheism is supposed to dodge this because it can just say the strengthened liar is both true and false (a glut) and stop there, no further hierachy needed -- which is meant to be the decisive advantage over its rivals.

One of my students wasn't buying it, though. She argued that Priest's Logic of Paradox can't actually express "just false" (false and not also true, as opposed to a glut) using ordinary paraconsistent negation, since gluts already make ¬P true whenever P is a glut too. So if the dialetheist ever wants some further device -- an "exclusion negation," or some kind of determinacy operator -- to say anything is exclusively false, she claimed you can build a new strengthened liar out of that device instead ("this sentence is exclusively false"), which LP can't happily absorb as just another glut without gutting the whole point of the device. If she's right, the "we alone escape revenge" selling point looks a lot less special, and I haven't been able to talk her out of it.

So here's what I want: an essay that actually settles this instead of just surveying the terrain. Can dialetheism give a principled answer to the exclusion-negation revenge worry, or does it just relocate the problem one level up like everyone else? And once you weigh that against the independent cost of restricting disjunctive syllogism and explosion for everyday reasoning, is embracing true contradictions still the most rational response to the liar specifically, compared to a paracomplete or contextualist rival that just accepts its own version of revenge as a brute cost? Don't give me "it's complicated" as a conclusion -- I want an actual verdict, hedged if you like (something like "dialetheism wins for the liar, but the case doesn't generalize to the sorites or to Hegelian contradictions in change and motion" would count as committing), but a verdict.
