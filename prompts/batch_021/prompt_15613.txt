My sister is getting married next month, and she has asked me, her older brother, to give the toast at the reception. I have no background in philosophy of any kind — I install and service HVAC systems for a living — but four years ago, stuck doing paperwork for two months after breaking my ankle on a job, I picked up a secondhand copy of Wittgenstein's On Certainty at a library sale, and it is genuinely the only book that has ever changed how I think about anything. The idea that stuck with me is his claim that some of what we call our certainties are not beliefs we have checked out and confirmed at all, but more like the hinges a door is hung on: there is a passage, 341 I think, though I would not swear to the number, where he says something close to that. The hinge itself is never argued for or doubted, because it is the very thing that has to stay still before any arguing or doubting about anything else can get started. I gather from things I have read since that "hinge proposition" is a phrase later philosophers attached to the idea rather than his own term, but the image of the hinge is definitely his.

Here is why this matters for the toast, and why I am asking you rather than improvising it myself. My sister and her fiancé actually broke off their engagement once, about three years ago, and did not speak for the better part of a year before finding their way back to each other. Everyone on our side of the family remembers this clearly and has been quietly wondering, in the run-up to the wedding, whether this time is genuinely different or whether she has simply stopped letting herself ask the question. His side of the family and most of the other guests have no idea any of this happened, and would be confused, or worse, unsettled, by a toast that addressed doubt too directly. I do not want to lie to either half of the room, and I refuse to stand up and promise that the two of them will never doubt each other again, because that is not true of any marriage, and every married person in that room will know it is not true. But the hinge idea gives me something honest to say instead: that finding their way back to each other was not a matter of finally gathering enough evidence to settle the question for good, the way you would settle a bet, but closer to the two of them deciding, together, where to fix the hinge, so that a shared life — doubts, arguments, and all — becomes possible in the first place, rather than something a future doubt could quietly pull out from under them.

Could you write the toast for me? It should run about three to four minutes read aloud, be genuinely funny in at least a couple of places the way a good wedding toast should be, and end on an actual toast that people can raise their glasses to. It must never once say "Wittgenstein," "epistemology," or "hinge" out loud, since my sister would never forgive me, and it has to work at two levels simultaneously: as a warm, ordinary wedding toast for guests who know none of this history, and as something quietly more pointed for the relatives who do, so that they leave reassured without ever being told, in so many words, what they had been worried about. I may have bent the philosophy slightly out of shape explaining it to you, since I have no one to check my reading against, so if I have gotten Wittgenstein wrong, feel free to fix it, as long as whatever replaces it still does the same work.
