I got roped into serving on my small town's arts & culture commission last year (I'm a software engineer by day; they just needed warm bodies who weren't already on three other committees), and we've got a genuinely tricky vote coming up this Tuesday. A local nonprofit wants the town to kick in $180k toward converting our old half-derelict grange hall into a real community theater -- proper seating, a lighting rig, the works. On paper it's a slam dunk: a drama program for the schools, maybe a little tourism, and it fixes up a building thats currently just pigeons. But at the last public meeting, one of our commissioners, a retired classics professor named Feldman, stood up and basically gave us Rousseau's letter to d'Alembert from memory as his objection. His argument, as best I can reconstruct it afterward: theater trains citizens to be passive spectators who feel like they've discharged some moral duty just by getting misty-eyed at a fictional injustice onstage, instead of ever having to actually do anything about a real one; actors make a living professionally pretending to be people they're not, which he thinks corrodes their sense (and eventually the audience's sense) of having a stable, authentic self at all; and -- this is the part that actually got under my skin -- he argued all this is basically harmless in a huge anonymous city, but our town is small enough that people still know each other and show up to things together, and that's exactly the kind of fragile communal fabric that theater quietly erodes by swapping shared participation for paid spectating. He kept contrasting it with our Fourth of July parade, where everyone's either marching or watching people they actually know, versus a theater where you just sit in the dark and watch strangers pretend.

Half the commission thinks Feldman's a lovable crank and we should just fund the renovation already; the other half (including, annoyingly, me, a little) can't quite shake the feeling he's onto something, even though $180k isn't gonna unravel the social fabric of a town of 6,000 people on its own. I'm the one who has to write the actual recommendation memo that goes out to the full commission before Tuesday's vote, and it needs to land on one concrete option -- fund it, don't fund it, or fund it with specific conditions and a timeline -- while actually engaging with Feldman's argument instead of just writing "commissioner Feldman raised concerns about civic values" and moving on, because he will 100% notice if I dodge it. Can you draft that memo for me?
