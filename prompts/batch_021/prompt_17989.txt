My grandma passed away last month, and a couple weeks later my aunt went to see a medium and came back utterly convinced grandma had "come through" with details she swore no one could've fed her. I don't buy it, but it sent me down a rabbit hole reading about the history of people trying to test this stuff rigorously instead of just believing or debunking on vibes, and thats how I re-discovered Henry Sidgwick. I only knew him as the hyper-serious utilitarian from The Methods of Ethics (hes kind of a patron saint in the EA corner of the internet I hang around), so it was a genuine shock to learn he was the founding president of the Society for Psychical Research and spent a good chunk of his later career running careful experiments on telepathy and mediums -- including the Creery sisters case, which looked genuinely impressive until stricter controls made a signalling code look like the likelier explanation -- and yet he never let himself announce a firm conclusion either way, even though by most accounts he wanted very badly for some of it to pan out, since he thought his own "dualism of practical reason" problem might only get resolved if the universe turned out to have some kind of moral order behind it after all.

Given that specific track record -- the scrupulousness, the personal stake, the way the Creery case burned him -- what do you think Sidgwick would make of the Daryl Bem "feeling the future" precognition studies and the replication crisis/preregistration reforms that followed in psychology? Would he read it as the Creery pattern repeating itself, or is there something about it that would've actually moved him a bit? I'm way less interested in generic "Victorian guy reacts to modern science" takes than in what his actual methodology and psychology predict he'd say here.
