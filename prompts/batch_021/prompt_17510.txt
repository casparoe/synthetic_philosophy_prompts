I cover science and ideas for a living, and I'm drafting a long-form piece on naturalist philosophers who tried -- or conspicuously declined -- to bring ethics inside their picture of knowledge. Below is my attempt to reconstruct what W. V. O. Quine is up to in "On the Nature of Moral Values," a short, rarely discussed 1978 essay that gets at most a footnote in most treatments of his work. I'd like your comments before I take it any further.

Quine spent decades arguing that even mathematics and logic ultimately answer, however indirectly, to the tribunal of sensory experience, with observation sentences -- utterances like "It's raining," pinned tightly enough to shared stimulation that any two competent speakers on the scene will assent to them together -- serving as the periphery where the whole edifice of theory touches down and gets corrected. Everything else is free construction, justified only by how well it coheres with the rest of the web and with that observational edge.

"On the Nature of Moral Values" reads like Quine wondering, briefly and without much confidence, whether ethics could be built the same way. Our snap reactions to particular cases -- that this was cruel, that a promise ought to have been kept -- would play the part of moral observation sentences, and general principles would be more like theoretical posits: instruments for organizing those reactions, answerable to them holistically rather than one at a time, and revisable exactly as a law of physics is revisable once enough anomalies pile up against it.

I've supplied the phrase "moral observation sentence" myself; the essay is compressed enough that Quine never quite puts it that plainly. What he does say, and what keeps him well short of any considered moral realism, is that the whole point of an observation sentence in his epistemology is that it forces convergence: shared stimulation guarantees that competent speakers agree on it whatever their private theories. Our immediate moral reactions carry no such guarantee -- two witnesses to the same act can come away with opposite verdicts, with nothing playing the role that shared retinal stimulation plays for a weather report. Quine leaves the essay without saying whether this means moral discourse fails to be fact-stating at all, or merely that its facts, if it has any, are checked far more locally and unreliably than science's are.

I'd value your reaction on three points: first, whether this is a fair reconstruction of the essay's actual argument, or whether I've imported more machinery from "Word and Object" than the piece itself supports; second, whether there is a more standard scholarly reading of where the essay lands -- tentative skepticism, quietism, something else entirely -- that I should be measuring my version against; and third, whether "the naturalist who balks at his own method" is the sharpest story available here, or whether I'm missing an angle a specialist would find more interesting.
