I teach a graduate seminar on early modern political philosophy, and the session on Pascal's Pensées ended in a dispute between two of my students, Elena and Marcus, that spilled into my office hours two days later and that neither has let go of since. We had been working through the cluster of fragments where Pascal argues that justice without force is powerless and force without justice is tyrannical, and that because justice is endlessly disputable while force is not, human beings have never managed to make what is just strong, only to make what is strong pass for just. Elena's claim was that this fragment, read together with Pascal's remarks on custom -- that any law traced back to its actual origin loses its authority, and that the wise deliberately hide this from ordinary people because it is dangerous to let them suspect the laws are anything but laws -- shows that Pascal is a debunker at bottom: force that entrenches itself simply gets called "justice," and the theological framing around this is window dressing. Marcus pushed back, reading the same fragments as making an epistemic claim rather than a debunking one: because reason is corrupted after the Fall, Pascal thinks, we cannot reliably track true justice at all (hence his remark that a river or a mountain range can reverse an entire jurisprudence, so that what counts as justice on one side is punished as crime on the other), and custom backed by force fills the resulting vacuum as a practical necessity that Pascal treats as regrettable, not as a covert redefinition of the word "justice."

Elena answered that the distinction is cosmetic: if true justice can never actually be accessed, then for every purpose that matters politically, talk of justice simply collapses into talk of force, so the theology does no independent work. Marcus replied that Pascal's own fragment about a soldier who would be a murderer for killing a man on this side of a river, yet is called brave and just for killing an identical man on the far side, shows that Pascal still recognizes something we can call injustice in a boundary that positive law treats as decisive -- a kind of negative moral knowledge no pure conventionalist should be entitled to. Elena's rejoinder was that spotting an arbitrary line is just as available to a conventionalist as to anyone else, and she returned to the concealment fragment: if the wise privately know the laws aren't really just while making sure everyone else believes they are, that is exactly the ideology-managing picture she had described from the start, not evidence against it. Neither would concede, and we never got to whether any of this bears on Pascal's further, more conservative conclusion that people should generally obey existing laws rather than press these very arguments into grounds for resistance.

Could you adjudicate this exchange move by move -- saying who has the better case at each specific step rather than just declaring an overall winner -- and flag anything significant that both of them seem to be overlooking? I would also like your own view on which reading comes closer to Pascal's considered position: that justice normatively reduces to force, that justice is real but practically inaccessible so force merely substitutes for it, or some third option that neither of them formulated. And finally, does this interpretive dispute actually matter for Pascal's conservative counsel of obedience, or does he land in the same place regardless of which reading is correct?
