Was grabbing lunch with a coworker who's doing a philosophy MA on the side, and she brought up this thing called the "bootstrapping problem" in epistemology, which apparently also gets talked about under the label "easy knowledge." As soon as she described it my brain went straight to compiler bootstrapping, and now I can't shake the feeling that this is a problem CS people solved decades ago and philosophers just havent caught up.

The setup she gave me: say you've got a belief source you've never independently verified, classic example being a fuel gauge in your car. Every time it reads "full" you just trust it, and after enough rounds of doing this you can inductively conclude "yep, this gauge is reliable" — but you never once checked the actual tank level in a way that didn't already depend on trusting the gauge. Apparently a bunch of epistemologists think that's illegitimate, too "easy" to count as real knowledge of the gauges reliability, even in a world where the gauge happens to genuinely be accurate the whole time. But that's just... a self-hosting compiler, right? Stage 1 (built with whatever compiler you already had lying around) compiles the source to get stage 2, stage 2 compiles the same source again to get stage 3, you diff stage 2 against stage 3, call it a fixed point, and ship it. Nobody thinks GCC's correctness is philosophically suspect because of this. Even that famous Ken Thompson hack, where a compiler backdoors itself and every future version of itself, basically gets treated as a fun security anecdote, not evidence that bootstrapping is broken as a way of grounding trust.

So genuinely, why do epistemologists keep treating this as some deep unresolved puzzle for perception and instruments, when software engineering ran essentially the same experiment at scale and it worked out totally fine? Feels like "is bootstrapping legit" got answered by like, 1980s compiler people, and the philosophy literature just never got the memo.
