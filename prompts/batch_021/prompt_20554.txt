The neurologists treating my father — three months post cardiac arrest, currently classified as being in a state of unresponsive wakefulness (what used to be called a vegetative state) — have started talking about running an EEG protocol in the tradition of Adrian Owen's covert-awareness work, to check for what's now called cognitive-motor dissociation: neural responses to commands like "imagine playing tennis" despite the total absence of any outward movement. Waiting on that has pushed me into a lot of reading on theories of consciousness, and I've ended up preoccupied with illusionism, the view Keith Frankish has systematized and that Dennett held in a more eliminativist register — on which phenomenal consciousness, the intrinsic, ineffable "what-it's-likeness" of experience, doesn't exist at all; there is only an introspective mechanism that misrepresents certain physical or functional states as having those properties.

What I've actually drafted a short essay on is a specific objection sometimes called (if I'm remembering the source correctly — I think it's from a paper by François Kammerer, but I read it secondhand and won't vouch for the citation) the paradox of standard illusionism. Galen Strawson pressed a version of it in the New York Review of Books: if illusionists grant that it seems to us that experience has phenomenal qualities, the concession is already fatal, since a seeming just is a phenomenal state, so denying phenomenal consciousness while claiming its absence is disguised from us is self-undermining. My essay reconstructs the standard illusionist reply — that the "seeming" is nothing but a representational or functional event, a mechanism classifying some target state as intrinsically qualitative, and representing something as F doesn't require the representing state itself to be F — and then argues that this reply is valid but essentially stipulative: it saves illusionism only by quietly redefining "seeming" so that it no longer refers to whatever it was that made phenomenal consciousness look undeniable in the first place.

I'd value your comments on three things: first, whether I'm rendering the Frankish/Strawson dispute (and the rejoinder) accurately or garbling it somewhere; second, whether my "stipulation" charge actually lands, or whether there's a stronger illusionist response I'm missing — I suspect Chalmers's "meta-problem of consciousness" framing is relevant here and haven't worked out exactly how; and third, and this is the part I most want candor rather than reassurance on, whether it even makes sense to ask what illusionism implies for a case like my father's, where there's no introspective report to be misled by, only externally measured responses to commands — or whether that's a category error that conflates a metaphysical thesis about the nature of experience with an epistemic question about detecting responsiveness.
