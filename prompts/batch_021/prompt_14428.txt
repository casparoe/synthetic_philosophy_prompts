Last night my friend Marcus and I got into a long argument over dinner that neither of us managed to resolve, and I would like an outside opinion on who was right. Marcus has been on something of a behavioral-economics kick lately, and he tried out a pair of choices on me to see how I would react, presenting each one as though I actually had to decide on the spot. In the first, I could take a guaranteed $50,000, or gamble instead on an 89% chance of $50,000, a 10% chance of $250,000, and a 1% chance of nothing; I took the guaranteed money without much hesitation. In the second, there was no safe option on the table at all: I had to choose between an 11% chance of $50,000 (and an 89% chance of nothing) or a 10% chance of $250,000 (and a 90% chance of nothing), and this time I went for the bigger prize despite its slightly worse odds of paying out anything whatsoever.

Marcus then walked me through the numbers and showed that these two choices cannot both come from maximizing an expected payoff under any single way of converting dollars into how much I personally value them. As best I could follow the algebra, preferring certainty in the first choice commits me, on pain of inconsistency, to preferring the 11%-chance option over the 10%-chance option in the second choice as well — yet I preferred the 10%-chance option instead. He takes this to be a clean demonstration that I was simply being irrational somewhere, and not merely unusually cautious in one moment and unusually bold in the next, since he insists that no single, consistent attitude toward risk or money could generate both of my actual preferences at once. What bothers me is that, even now, days later, both choices still feel entirely right to me, and I do not feel the slightest pull to revise either one just because someone has shown me they do not fit together on paper.

Was Marcus correct that there is no coherent, rational way to hold onto both preferences at once, or is there a serious theory of rational choice under risk on which this pattern is not a mistake at all? And if there turns out to be a respectable way of defending both choices, I would also like to understand what it costs: would holding both preferences at once leave someone in my position open to being deliberately maneuvered out of money by a counterpart who understood the structure of my own preferences better than I did?
