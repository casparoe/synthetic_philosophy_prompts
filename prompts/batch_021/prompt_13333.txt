This term I'm enrolled in a seminar on Rawls's political liberalism, and for the last two weeks we have been working closely through his essay "The Idea of Public Reason Revisited." What struck several of us in seminar is that Rawls, who is usually careful not to let the apparatus of public reason dictate substantive verdicts on contested political questions, makes a pointed exception for abortion: in a footnote, he argues that a reasonable balance of three political values—due respect for human life, the orderly reproduction of political society over time, and the equality of women as equal citizens—yields a woman's right to decide on abortion during at least the first trimester, and that any comprehensive doctrine that denies this right is, to that extent, unreasonable. One classmate insisted this footnote is basically a throwaway remark that shouldn't be read as revealing anything systematic about Rawls's commitments, while another argued it is a direct application of machinery he took very seriously elsewhere, which is what got me wondering how far that machinery would actually travel.

Specifically, I would like your best reconstruction of what Rawls would have said about Dobbs v. Jackson Women's Health Organization (2022) and its aftermath—the overturning of Roe v. Wade and Planned Parenthood v. Casey, the return of abortion regulation to the states, and the subsequent enactment in several states of bans that reach well before any first-trimester line, often defended by legislators in explicitly religious terms. I'm not looking for a generic liberal reaction, but for an answer that actually engages Rawls's own apparatus. Does the "proviso" attached to his "wide view of public political culture" (which permits comprehensive doctrines into political debate provided proper political reasons are supplied in due course) plausibly cover this kind of legislative advocacy? And is the analogy some people draw between this advocacy and Rawls's own treatment of the abolitionists and the civil rights movement actually apt, given that those movements were pressing against an unjust status quo rather than unwinding an already-settled element of constitutional practice—or does that comparison just relocate the disagreement, since which side counts as defending an unjust status quo here is precisely what is contested? Please work through this as precisely as you can from what Rawls actually committed himself to in these texts, rather than defaulting to a plausible-sounding guess based only on his general reputation as a liberal egalitarian.
