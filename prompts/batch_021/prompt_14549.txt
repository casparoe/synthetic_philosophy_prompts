I'm about two-thirds through a novel where a side character — a reclusive, slightly unhinged mathematician — actually builds a version of Thomson's lamp: she rigs it to switch on, then off after half of whatever time is left, then on after half of what's left after that, and so on, so it supposedly flips infinitely many times inside two minutes flat, and she's convinced that if the thing actually completes, it'll tell her something true about infinity (possibly it'll also fry the wiring in her apartment, but that's a seperate problem). I'd only ever known the lamp as a fun trivia-paradox before researching it for the book, and it turns out there's a real, unresolved-feeling fight underneath it: Thomson's original 1954 argument was that the lamp can't coherently end up on OR off, which he took to show something's actually incoherent about completing that whole infinite run of switch-flips, not just that we don't happen to know the answer. Benacerraf's reply a few years later (1962, if you want the receipts) was basically "not so fast" — that Thomson never actually derived a contradiction, only that the description of the switching never pins down what state the lamp is in exactly at the two-minute mark, which is a much smaller and less dramatic problem than "supertasks are impossible."

My housemate, who did a philosophy minor and loves being right about this stuff, insists Benacerraf just wins outright and the "paradox" was always a trick of description rather than a real one. I'm less convinced, partly because of a wrinkle I half-remember from some rabbit-hole reading: if you allow lamp-like "infinity machines" at all, you seem to get devices that could settle things that are supposed to be undecidable (checking every case of some open conjecture, one per flip, faster and faster forever) just by running to completion, and that feels like it should count against the whole setup being coherent in the first place, not merely against us knowing the final state. Could you write me a dialogue between two philosophers standing in front of the finished lamp, arguing about what, if anything, just happened — one who thinks Thomson basically had it right and something has genuinely gone wrong, and one who thinks Benacerraf closed the case and there's nothing left to explain? I want real back-and-forth over several rounds, with each one actually answering the other's last point (including that undecidability wrinkle, if you think it belongs in the argument at all), and neither should get an easy out — they can end the scene still disagreeing, but each side should land at least one blow the other has to take seriously.
