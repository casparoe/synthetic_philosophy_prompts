"On the standard Bayesian picture, E confirms H just when P(H|E) > P(H), which (by Bayes' theorem) is equivalent to P(E|H) > P(E). But if E is old evidence -- something the scientist already knows for sure -- then P(E) = 1, and conditionalizing on something you already assign probability 1 can't move your credence in H at all: P(H|E) = P(H). So on the letter of the theory, old evidence can never confirm anything, which seems totally backwards given real science. The textbook case is Einstein's general relativity: physicists had been stuck for decades on why the deflection of starlight round the sun didn't match Newtonian predictions, and when Einstein's theory nailed the observed figure, people took it as a huge point in GR's favor even though the anomaly long predated the theory. One popular fix denies that agents are logically omniscient, i.e. that everyone instantly sees every consequence of every hypothesis. If a scientist doesn't yet realize H entails E, discovering that entailment is itself news -- and Garber's version of the fix cashes this out by asking what your credence in H would have been before E got absorbed into the background, basically a hypothetical prior computed as if E hadn't already been taken for granted. That gives you a legitimate before/after gap to work with, so E (or rather, learning about its logical connection to H) can confirm H after all. The obvious problem is that there's no principled way to say exactly how confident a scientist 'would have' been in H absent E -- it's a counterfactual that doesn't correspond to anything the scientist actually went through, so critics worry the whole move just amounts to picking whatever prior gets you the answer you already wanted."
