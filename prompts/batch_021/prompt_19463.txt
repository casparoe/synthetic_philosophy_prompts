I've been dipping into the philosophy of vagueness after an argument with a coworker about when a pull request becomes "too large to review." Our linter flags anything over a fixed line-count threshold, but everyone on the team agrees that the real property we care about — "too large" — doesn't actually snap into existence at one specific line, which felt suspiciously like the classic Sorites paradox about heaps of sand, where removing one grain at a time never turns a heap into a non-heap, yet eventually you're left with nothing. Looking into how philosophers actually handle this, I kept running into two positions presented as the leading rivals: epistemicism, which I gather is the view Timothy Williamson is best known for, and supervaluationism.

The picture I've pieced together from a handful of summaries is that both theories are ways of softening classical logic's assumption that a predicate like "heap" or "too large" has one exact on/off boundary, and that underneath the jargon they actually agree on the basic diagnosis — there's no single sharp cutoff to be found anywhere — while disagreeing only on the mechanics underneath: epistemicism says the cutoff is real but permanently hidden from us, while supervaluationism says there's a whole family of equally legitimate ways to draw the line, so "borderline" cases simply fall between them. That makes the two feel like close cousins to me, almost like two ways of handling a threshold-based classifier in code: one where a true cutoff score exists but can never be measured precisely, and one where you just return "undefined" for a whole band of scores near the boundary. Is that a fair way to carve up the disagreement, or is it flattening something more fundamental — particularly when it comes to whether either theory really manages to avoid committing to a sharp boundary existing somewhere, even if neither can say exactly where?
