I recently joined the small grants committee of a longtermist research fund that supports early-career work in decision theory and AI safety, and the first proposal I've had to help evaluate has left the committee genuinely split. The applicant wants a year of funding to work on "acausal trade": the idea, rooted in old discussions of Newcomb's problem and superrationality, that agents who cannot causally interact at all — distant civilizations elsewhere in a large or infinite universe, or future superintelligences we will never observe — can still benefit from cooperating with one another, because sufficiently correlated decision-making means that one agent's choice is genuine evidence about what the other will choose. Some in the field now call this "evidential cooperation in large worlds" rather than acausal trade, but the applicant's proposal is explicit that the goal is unchanged: work out formal conditions under which this kind of cooperation is warranted, and from there derive concrete guidance about how present-day actors, including AI labs, should behave now for the sake of agents they can never verify cooperated back.

Roughly half the committee thinks this is legitimate, if unusually abstract, decision theory: they point out that non-causal decision theories (evidential decision theory, and more recent functional and logical variants) are an active area of academic work, that superrationality among correlated reasoners already has real standing in that literature rather than being some fringe invention, and that rejecting the proposal just because its conclusions sound strange would mean rejecting a style of reasoning we already accept in other guises. The other half thinks the whole research program is unfalsifiable by construction: it requires assuming both that relevantly similar agents exist elsewhere and that we can know enough about their values and reasoning to coordinate with them, and no possible observation could ever confirm or disconfirm any of it. They also doubt the word "trade" is earning its keep, since ordinary trade involves some way of checking whether the other side held up its end, and nothing plays that role once causal contact is off the table entirely. One member summed up her objection by calling the whole idea "a Pascal's mugging that pays out in decision-theory papers instead of money." I've been asked to draft the committee's decision memo ahead of next week's meeting, since as the newest member I apparently drew the short straw.

Could you write that memo for me? I need a clear recommendation — fund, decline, or fund with specific, stated conditions — together with the reasoning behind it, written so that whichever side loses can still see its strongest argument was taken seriously. If the right answer is that the proposal should be narrowed or reframed rather than simply accepted or rejected outright, please say what a fundable version would need to demonstrate.
