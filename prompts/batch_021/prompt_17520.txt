Here is the student's answer, verbatim:

"Both Aquinas and John of Salisbury take up the problem of the unjust ruler, but they frame the question somewhat differently. Aquinas distinguishes a tyrant who has usurped power without any legitimate title from a ruler who was legitimately instituted but subsequently governs for his own advantage rather than the common good. In the first case, because the usurper never held rightful authority to begin with, resisting him does not amount to breaking any obligation of obedience the community owed him; in the second case, Aquinas urges much greater caution, since the community's own prior consent to this ruler's authority is at stake.

Aquinas's fullest treatment of the practical dangers of tyrannicide comes in the Summa Theologiae, II-II, q. 42, where he argues that private citizens should not take it upon themselves to kill a tyrant: an attempt is at least as likely to fail as to succeed, a failed attempt typically provokes the tyrant into greater cruelty toward his subjects, and allowing individuals to decide for themselves who counts as a tyrant sets a dangerous precedent that threatens legitimate rulers along with illegitimate ones. Removal of the tyrant, if it is to happen at all, should instead come from whatever public authority originally instituted him or, lacking that, from an appeal to God, on the model of Israel's deliverance from its oppressors.

On this point, Aquinas's position converges closely with John of Salisbury's in the Policraticus, where tyrannicide is likewise treated as a praiseworthy service to the community rather than merely a permissible one. Both writers point to the same Old Testament precedent, Ehud's killing of the Moabite king Eglon, as evidence that God himself sanctions the killing of oppressors, and both conclude that a private individual who kills a tyrant is not just excused but deserving of the community's gratitude."
