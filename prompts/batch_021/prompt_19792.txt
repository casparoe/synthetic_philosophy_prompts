Question: Frankfurt (1969) asks us to consider a case in which Black wants Jones to perform a certain action. Black can predict Jones's decisions by monitoring a neural sign that reliably precedes them, and Black will intervene to make Jones perform the action only if the sign indicates that Jones is about to decide otherwise. As it happens, Jones decides entirely on his own to perform the action Black wants, so Black never intervenes. (a) Explain how this case is meant to refute the Principle of Alternative Possibilities (PAP). (b) Explain the "dilemma defense" against Frankfurt's argument, associated with Widerker and Ginet, which turns on the causal status of the prior sign. (c) Assess whether the dilemma defense succeeds against the version of the case just described.

Student's answer:

Frankfurt's case is designed to separate an agent's being morally responsible for what she does from her having been able to do otherwise. Black is a counterfactual intervener: he stands ready to manipulate Jones into performing the action Black wants, but only if Jones shows some prior sign of being about to decide differently. Because Jones decides on his own, in just the way Black wants, Black never actually steps in, and the action together with the reasoning behind it is entirely Jones's own. Yet Jones could not have done otherwise, since any attempt to do so would have triggered Black's intervention. Frankfurt takes this to show that PAP is false: Jones is responsible even though he lacks alternative possibilities. Since the classical incompatibilist argument that determinism rules out responsibility proceeds via PAP (determinism rules out alternative possibilities; responsibility requires alternative possibilities; therefore determinism rules out responsibility), showing that PAP is false removes a load-bearing premise of that argument, and thereby shows that moral responsibility is compatible with determinism after all.

The main worry about this argument is what Widerker and Ginet call the dilemma defense, which focuses on the prior sign that cues Black. Suppose first that the sign is causally sufficient for Jones's decision, so that there is a deterministic connection between sign and choice. Then Jones's actual decision is itself causally determined, and a libertarian, who holds that a free and responsible choice must be undetermined, will simply deny that Jones's decision was free in the first place; the case then just assumes, without argument, that a determined choice can be free and responsible, which is exactly what is at issue. Suppose instead that the sign is not causally sufficient, but only a reliable, non-necessitating indicator. Then nothing guarantees that the sign will be followed by the anticipated decision, so it remains open, right up to the moment of choice, that Jones could have decided differently, and PAP is not refuted after all. Either way, the case cannot do the work Frankfurt needs it to do.

I think the dilemma defense succeeds even against the more sophisticated, indeterministic version of the case. The main attempt to block the second horn is the flicker-of-freedom strategy, which points out that even on the indeterministic reading of the sign, Jones retains some alternative, if only the bare possibility that the sign fails to occur. Fischer has defended this strategy at length, arguing that such flickers are exactly the sort of alternative a defender of PAP needs, since it remains true, right up until Jones decides, that things could have gone differently. If Fischer is right about this, then the indeterministic version of the case does leave Jones with a relevant alternative possibility, so the second horn of the dilemma holds, and the case is not, after all, a counterexample to PAP.
