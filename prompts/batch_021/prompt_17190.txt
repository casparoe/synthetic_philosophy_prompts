Every year, my high school brings in an outside theater company to perform a play during a mandatory all-school assembly, usually on something heavy like bullying, drunk driving, or suicide, followed by a short discussion led by the guidance counselors. This year's play was about a student who takes his own life after being bullied online, and it was genuinely well written and well acted: by the end, most of the auditorium, including guys who normally act tough, was crying, and a few people even went up afterward to hug the actors. For the rest of that day, the school felt noticeably different, quieter and kinder than usual. But within about a week, I noticed some of the very same people who had been visibly shaken during the assembly were back to making the kind of comments the play was supposed to be about, sometimes toward the same classmates they usually target.

This isn't a one-time thing; it happens after almost every one of these assemblies, year after year, regardless of the topic. I keep going back and forth on how to make sense of it. Part of me thinks it has to count for something that a room full of teenagers genuinely felt someone else's pain, even if only for an hour. But another part of me suspects the crying and hugging might work against their own purpose, as if getting to feel that moved and that compassionate during the performance somehow substitutes for actually treating people better afterward, so that the play changes nothing, or even leaves people feeling like they've already done their part. I brought this up with my debate coach, who said I was reading way too much into a school assembly, but it still seems like there's a real question underneath it about whether being emotionally affected by a staged story carries any moral weight on its own, apart from whatever behavior does or doesn't follow from it. I'd be curious how you would think through whether there's actually something hollow about that kind of reaction, or whether I'm just overanalyzing a pretty normal part of being human.
