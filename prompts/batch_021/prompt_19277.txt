For the past few months I've been taking notes, half against my better judgment, on my own family for a novel I'm drafting about a household coming apart around a parent's decline. Eighteen months ago my mother was diagnosed with a frontal-lobe condition that has made her sharp and dismissive in ways she never used to be around other people — cutting remarks about my brother's divorce, an offhand cruelty toward grandchildren she once doted on. My brother has largely stopped visiting. His position is that it makes no sense to resent a symptom, and that the only honest response now is to treat her outbursts the way one treats bad weather: something to be worked around, not taken personally or held against her.

I find I can't quite come around to his view, though I've had trouble saying why, since we don't disagree about any of the medical facts. What unsettles me is something her neurologist mentioned almost in passing — that this kind of disinhibition rarely invents new sentiments from nothing; more often it just dissolves the tact that used to keep older ones private. So when she says something cutting now, I no longer know whether to lay it at the door of the disease or of her, unfiltered perhaps for the first time in years, and I'm not convinced the two descriptions are even cleanly separable, or that separating them would tell me how I'm entitled to feel about being on the receiving end.

I raised the dilemma with my writing group, half expecting it to be useful only as material, and found them fairly evenly split, which is part of why I would like a more careful outside opinion. My brother thinks the diagnosis simply settles the matter, and that continuing to feel wronged is a kind of failure to update on the evidence. I suspect that something would be lost — some way of still taking her seriously as a person I'm answerable to, rather than a situation I'm managing — if I talked myself all the way out of the hurt entirely. I'd value your view on who is closer to right, and on whether it should matter for how the rest of us are entitled to feel toward her now — whether the disease is manufacturing her cruelty, or only unmasking it.
