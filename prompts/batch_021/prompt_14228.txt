I write novels and collect first-edition books as a private indulgence, and a sequence of trades I made across a string of estate sales last year still troubles me. I owned a worn first edition, valuable to me chiefly for a previous owner's marginalia. A dealer persuaded me to exchange it for a pristine, signed later printing, plus a modest sum on my part, since the signature mattered more to me than the marginalia. Some months afterward, the same dealer offered a first edition bearing a well-known printer's misprint, in return for the signed copy and a further sum, the misprint being rarer still. At the following sale, he proposed trading that misprinted copy back for my original, marginalia-bearing copy, plus one more payment, since I confessed I had missed it after all. Only when I reviewed my accounts did I realize that three individually sensible trades had brought me back to exactly where I started — only now out of pocket. Friends call this carelessness, but I suspect something more structured was at work. Where, if anywhere, should the irrationality be located here: in any single trade, in the relation among the three, or in the way I was choosing across time rather than all at once? And short of refusing every future offer on principle, is there a genuinely defensible way to guard against it?
