I've been reporting out a longer piece on laws of nature for the magazine's ideas section, and I sat in on a philosophy of science conference panel last month where one of the speakers breezily called herself "a happy Humean" about laws, which threw me a bit given what I thought that label actually commits you to. From what I've read (mostly Lewis on "Humean supervenience" and the "best system" account of lawhood), Humeans about laws are basically taking Hume's classic line on causation all the way through: there's no necessary connection out there in nature, just one particular fact after another, so a "law" ends up being nothing more than a label for a pattern that happens to hold, with nothing underneath forcing it or explaining why that pattern holds rather than some other one. If thats the right reading, doesnt it follow that Humeans are stuck admitting physics doesn't actually explain anything -- its just very elaborate bookkeeping of coincidences -- and that they have no non-circular answer to Hume's problem of induction, i.e. no real reason the pattern should keep holding tomorrow?

Given that, I'm confused why so many serious, non-crank philosophers of science actually sign up to be Humeans about laws instead of treating that as a reductio and jumping over to the necessitarians -- Armstrong, Dretske, Tooley -- who think laws are real relations of necessitation between universals and so seem to dodge the whole mess. Is there some move available to Lewis-style Humeans that gets them out of that skeptical hole, or is "physics doesn't really explain anything and induction is ungrounded" just the bullet they've knowingly chosen to bite? Trying to get this right rather than strawman one side of the debate for the piece.
