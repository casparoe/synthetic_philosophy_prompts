I am currently writing a historical novel that includes Frederick Douglass as a viewpoint character, and I have reached the chapter dramatizing his break with Garrison over how the Constitution ought to be read. Rather than have some other character explain the dispute to him, I want the disagreement to emerge through Douglass's own reasoning, so I have written the paragraph below as a piece of interior monologue set around 1851. Before I build the rest of the chapter on it, I would like your comments on whether the intellectual substance actually holds up.

"He had said as much himself, more times than he could count: that a compact drafted to protect the property of slaveholders could not be redeemed by clever reading; that its clauses — three-fifths of all other persons, the promise to deliver up any person held to service escaping into a free state, the twenty years' grace allowed to the trade in human beings — described a bargain with the slaveholder as plainly as if he had signed his own name to the page. A covenant with death, Garrison called it, and an agreement with hell, and for a decade Douglass had said the same, in his own words, on his own platforms, because it had seemed simply true.

What he could no longer get past was a smaller fact, the kind no stump speech had room for. In three separate places the men at Philadelphia might have named the thing outright, and in three separate places they had reached for a euphemism instead. Not slave. Not slavery. Person held to service or labor. Other persons. Spooner had built a great structure on that reticence: he argued that no enactment repugnant to natural justice was law at all, whatever had been voted on it — that slavery had never been lawful anywhere, Constitution or no Constitution. Douglass could not follow him quite that far; it asked the word law to do work no court, and not many consciences, would grant it. His own claim was narrower, and he thought harder to answer. Whatever certain delegates had privately intended — and he did not doubt that some had intended exactly the bondage that existed — the words they had actually submitted, and the states had actually ratified, did not say slave. They said person. A reader bound to those words rather than to what dead men had meant by them was bound to read person as person, and to resolve every silence, every euphemism, on the side of the liberty the Preamble had promised, not the property the slave codes assumed.

Garrison would call this a lawyer's trick wearing the face of a moral discovery. Douglass had begun to think it was the only kind of moral discovery left to a people who had to go on living, for now, under one government and one text."

Specifically, I would appreciate your views on three things:

1. Whether I have actually kept Douglass's argument distinct from Lysander Spooner's, or have quietly had Douglass make Spooner's argument without noticing. I want the reader to sense he is doing something narrower and more defensible than Spooner, but I am not certain I have located the real difference correctly.
2. Whether Garrison is getting a fair hearing here, or whether I have given him only the weakest form of his objection because it was dramatically convenient. If there is a sharper reply available to him, I would like to know what it is even if I decide not to use it.
3. Whether anything else in the passage is simply wrong, overstated, or anachronistic by the standards of the actual constitutional debate among abolitionists — including, if it is a problem, my having compressed what was in fact a gradual, letter- and editorial-driven change of mind into a single private moment of realization.

I would rather hear blunt criticism now than after the manuscript is finished.
