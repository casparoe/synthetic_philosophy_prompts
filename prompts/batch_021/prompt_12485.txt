In my school's senior moral theology elective, we recently spent two weeks on Augustine's writings on lying, working from translated excerpts of both De Mendacio (On Lying) and the later, shorter Contra Mendacium (Against Lying), which he wrote after a correspondent named Consentius suggested that someone from the Church infiltrate the Priscillianist heretics under a false identity in order to expose them, a plan Augustine flatly rejected. One detail from these texts that I keep turning over is that Augustine does not treat every lie as equally sinful: he lays out a graded scale of roughly eight kinds of lies, from lies told in religious teaching, which he ranks as worst, down to lies that harm absolutely no one and even help someone else survive, which he ranks as least bad. And yet no matter how far down that scale you go, the lie never actually becomes permissible. Even a lie that would save an innocent person's life, or protect someone's chastity, and that would harm no one at all, is still, on his account, a sin, just an extremely minor one. This strikes me as strange, since the whole scale seems to be organized around how much harm a lie causes and how much good it does, so I don't see why enough good on the "help" side is allowed to change how bad the sin is without ever being allowed to change whether it's a sin at all. When I asked my teacher about this, she said it isn't really a contradiction once you understand what "degrees of sin" are tracking for Augustine, but she moved on to the next reading before fully explaining what she meant, and I haven't been able to reconstruct the argument on my own.

Please argue that Augustine's position here is in fact internally consistent: that grading lies on a scale of severity while still refusing to ever let even the most beneficial, harmless lie count as permissible is not an arbitrary double standard, but follows naturally from something coherent in his underlying views on the will, sin, and the relative ranking of goods. I'm not looking for a general defense of "lying is always wrong" (I can find versions of that argument anywhere); what I want is the specific logical move that lets Augustine hold onto both the graded scale and the absolute line at once, without either one undercutting the other.
