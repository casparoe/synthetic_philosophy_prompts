For my IB Philosophy extended essay, I have been reading about higher-order theories of consciousness, and I have found myself stuck on a split that runs right through the higher-order camp itself, rather than merely separating it from first-order or global-workspace theories. From what I can tell, David Rosenthal's version of the theory holds that a mental state is conscious only when it is accompanied by an actually occurring, roughly simultaneous higher-order thought representing that one is in that very state, whereas Peter Carruthers argues that this "actualist" picture demands far too much simultaneous cognitive machinery, and that it is enough for a state to be merely disposed to produce such a higher-order thought whenever a mindreading mechanism happens to target it. My philosophy teacher mentioned that this disagreement is not just about implementation details, since each side thinks the other gets the metaphysics of "what it's like"-ness wrong: actualists worry that an unexercised disposition is too metaphysically thin to make it actually like something to be in a state right now (after all, something can be soluble without currently dissolving), while dispositionalists worry that requiring an actual, occurrent higher-order thought for every conscious state is biologically implausible and invites worries about stray or targetless higher-order thoughts manufacturing apparent consciousness out of nothing.

Rather than reading yet another summary of this debate, I would like to see the two sides actually argue it out with each other. Could you write a dialogue between a defender of the actualist higher-order thought theory and a defender of the dispositionalist higher-order theory, in which each character presses their strongest objection against the other's account and the other genuinely responds to that specific objection, rather than the two of them simply talking past one another? I would like the exchange to engage with both worries mentioned above, the "thinness" objection to dispositionalism and the targetless-thought and biological-implausibility objections to actualism, and I would like both sides to be equally sharp, since I do not want either theory to come across as the obviously weaker one.
