In a graduate seminar on philosophy of religion, we spent the last two sessions on Luis de Molina's doctrine of middle knowledge and its use by contemporary philosophers like Alvin Plantinga, Thomas Flint, and William Lane Craig to reconcile meticulous divine providence with libertarian human freedom. I think I have the basic three-fold structure down: natural knowledge covers all necessary truths and bare possibilities, middle knowledge covers the counterfactuals of creaturely freedom (what any possible free creature would do in any possible circumstance), and free knowledge covers what actually happens once God decrees which world to create. But a few of us in the reading group came away convinced that the view is actually less friendly to creaturely freedom than it's advertised to be, not more.

Here is the worry. Since God is the one whose knowledge occupies the "middle" moment, it seems to follow that God is also the one who fixes which counterfactual is true for each possible creature in each possible circumstance—for instance, that Peter, placed in circumstances C, would freely deny Christ three times. If God is thereby authoring the entire "menu" of creaturely dispositions before he ever gets to the further step of deciding which circumstances to actualize, then the creature's supposedly free choice looks doubly scripted: scripted once at the level of which counterfactuals hold, and scripted again at the level of which of those counterfactuals gets actualized. That would make Molinism a more thoroughgoing determinism than the Calvinist decree, since at least Calvinists don't dress their determinism up in the vocabulary of counterfactual freedom. Is this a fair criticism, and if it isn't, exactly where does it misdescribe the role middle knowledge is meant to play in Molina's system?
