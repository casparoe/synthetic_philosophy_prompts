Okay, this is going to sound niche but bear with me. I sit on the curriculum committee that oversees our business school's required "Ethics and Professional Character" course, and this Thursday we have to vote on whether to keep it, substantially revise it, or scrap it in favor of the "behavioral ethics" course our compliance-and-ethics office has been lobbying for over the last two years (think Bazerman-and-Tenbrunsel "Blind Spots" style stuff - decision checklists, situational/environmental design, basically zero talk of character or virtue). The trigger is that our external program reviewers report landed last week, and it leans hard on the situationist critique: it cites the Milgram obedience studies, the seminary-student "Good Samaritan" experiment, even that old Hartshorne and May honesty study where kids were scrupulously honest in one setting and cheated freely in another, all to argue that the course is built on a folk-psychological premise - that people have stable character traits which predict behavior across situations - that decades of social psychology has more or less demolished. Their recommendation is basically: nice virtue-based curriculum, shame if it doesn't survive contact with the literature.

Funny enough, I had almost this exact fight two nights ago with a friend who's finishing her PhD in social psych. I tried defending the course by saying something like "but virtue for Aristotle already involves practical wisdom, which is sensitive to the particulars of a situation, so it's not like the theory predicts people act identically regardless of context," and she said that's just a post-hoc move that makes the theory unfalsifiable - keep adding "unless the situation calls for something else" and no amount of data could ever count against it. Neither of us budged, and now I have to write something more careful than what I improvised over a second glass of wine.

Could you draft the actual memo I'd bring to committee? I want one clear recommendation - keep, revise, or replace the course - argued for on the merits, not a "both sides have a point" shrug. But whichever way it comes out, I want the loosing side taken seriously: if the recommendation is to keep or revise, it has to engage honestly with the situationist evidence instead of hand-waving it away; if it's to replace the course, it can't just strawman virtue ethics as assuming people act identically no matter the setting. And if there's an actual philosophical answer to my friend's "unfalsifiable" charge - something sturdier than what I came up with at the bar - please work it in, because that's exactly the kind of thing that would actually land with this committee.
