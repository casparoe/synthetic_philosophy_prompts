I am several chapters into a novel set almost entirely inside a small, self-contained pocket universe containing nothing at all but two perfectly matched spheres: identical in size, mass, composition, and every other quality I can think to specify, suspended in empty space at a fixed distance from one another, with no third object and no outside observer to break the symmetry between them. A friend with a philosophy background, hearing me describe the setting at dinner, mentioned that I had more or less reinvented Max Black's famous thought experiment about the identity of indiscernibles, so I went back and read the original paper to make sure I was building my cosmology on solid ground. My understanding, going in, was that Black's spheres are meant to show that Leibniz's principle fails as a general metaphysical truth: that two things can share literally every property, including every relation each bears to the other, and still be two things rather than one.

The trouble is that the longer I sit with it, the less sure I am that I have not talked myself into the opposite conclusion. If my two spheres really do share every property without exception, it seems to follow directly from Leibniz's principle that they cannot be two distinct things at all, only a single thing redundantly described twice over -- in which case my premise of twin, indistinguishable planets would quietly collapse into a story about one oddly self-referential planet, and Black's thought experiment would end up vindicating the identity of indiscernibles rather than refuting it, which is the opposite of how I have always seen it summarized. Before I build any more of the novel on this foundation, I would like to know where my reasoning has gone wrong, what the thought experiment is actually supposed to establish about the principle, and why philosophers apparently still treat the case as live and contested rather than settled decisively in one direction or the other.
