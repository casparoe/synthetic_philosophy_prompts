And here's the student's answer, typed up exactly as submitted:

Anscombe's pamphlet, "Mr Truman's Degree," protested Oxford's plan to give Truman an honorary doctorate, and she was one of only a couple of dons who actually voted against it in Convocation. Her central claim is that Truman is guilty of murder for ordering the bombing of Hiroshima and Nagasaki. The key move is that this wasn't just killing a lot of civilians badly, it was killing them on purpose as the whole point of the strategy: the aim was to terrorize the Japanese government into surrendering by annihilating whole cities, so the civilian deaths were the means to victory rather than an unintended cost of it. She draws on the double-effect distinction between intended and merely foreseen harm, a distinction she first worked out in this pamphlet and would go on to refine in her later, more technical writing on intention, to argue that killing enemy combatants, or even unintentionally killing some civilians while bombing a genuine military target, could in principle be defensible, but deliberately targeting the innocent can't be, however good the end. Since the number of lives the bombing was projected to save by shortening the war was large, the deeper problem is really one of proportionality: the civilian toll was wildly out of proportion to any legitimate military aim, so no utilitarian calculation could have justified it either. This is why, two years later in "Modern Moral Philosophy," Anscombe coins the term "consequentialism" for exactly the kind of thinking Truman's defenders were using: that if the consequences are good enough, no act, including killing the innocent, is absolutely off limits. She thinks a moral theory with no exceptionless prohibitions of this kind isn't worth serious engagement, which is part of why she's usually read as reviving an Aristotelian rather than Kantian alternative to consequentialism.
