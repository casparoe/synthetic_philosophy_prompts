In a seminar on the philosophy of psychiatry, we have spent the last few weeks on Ian Hacking's account of the "looping effects" of human kinds, developed at length in books such as Rewriting the Soul and Mad Travelers. As I understand it, the central move is to distinguish classifications of what Hacking calls indifferent kinds — sorting a sample as quartz, or as an isotope of carbon, does nothing to the sample — from classifications of interactive kinds, where sorting a person into a psychiatric or behavioral category can change how that person experiences and describes themselves. That change in self-understanding can alter conduct, which can alter the very population the category was meant to pick out, which then pressures the clinicians, researchers, or institutions maintaining the category to revise it, changing the classified population again, and so on. Hacking's two central case studies are multiple personality disorder and the nineteenth-century diagnosis of dissociative "fugue," though he also uses the mechanism, under the heading "making up people," to argue that some classifications do not just redescribe a pre-existing kind of person but bring a genuinely new way of being a person into existence.

One thing our instructor keeps stressing is that this is meant to be a stronger and more specific claim than ordinary labeling theory or self-fulfilling prophecy: it is not just that being labeled changes someone's behavior, but that the label, criterion, or diagnostic category itself gets rewritten as a result, in a genuine two-way loop rather than a one-way effect of classifier on classified.

With that distinction in mind, I would like you to write a short story, roughly 1,200 to 2,000 words, that dramatizes the looping structure as plot rather than having a character explain it in dialogue. You may build the story around a real category in the spirit of the ones above, treated with whatever imaginative license fiction allows, or invent a plausible category of your own (a near-future one is fine). Either way, please carry the loop through at least two full turns: an act of naming or classifying; a change in how the classified come to understand or conduct themselves, shown through incident rather than asserted outright; and a subsequent revision of the category, or of the institution enforcing it, that is visibly a response to that change — with some sense, by the end, that the cycle will continue rather than settle into a stable, final description. Setting, era, and cast are entirely up to you; what matters most is that the looping mechanism itself, and not merely its more familiar cousin of stigma or self-fulfilling prophecy, comes through clearly.
