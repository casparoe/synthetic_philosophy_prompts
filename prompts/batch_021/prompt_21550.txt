Two colleagues of mine in our philosophy-of-religion reading group got into a running disagreement after we discussed David Lewis's short paper on penal substitution, and I'd like an outside read on who had the better of it.

Claire opened with Lewis's own thought experiment: an offender is justly sentenced to prison or execution, an innocent friend volunteers to undergo the sentence instead, and everyone agrees this changes nothing -- no court would free a murderer because someone else was executed in his place, and if the switch were somehow carried out in secret, the offender would still have to be punished once it came to light. Since Christian penal substitution theories of the atonement hold that essentially this happened in the case of Christ, Claire argued that the doctrine rests on a notion of substitutable punishment we reject in every other context.

Ben objected that this proves too much, because we plainly don't reject it in every other context: when an offender is sentenced to pay a serious fine -- serious enough that some would rather serve time -- and a friend pays it for him, almost no one objects, even though the structure is identical: an innocent party absorbing, in the guilty party's place, a loss meant to fall on the guilty party. So the blanket claim that substitutable punishment is unintelligible can't be right, and a defender of the doctrine could try arguing that Christ's case patterns with the fine cases rather than the prison cases.

Claire replied that the fine case is a poor model for the atonement, because fines can serve a compensatory function -- restoring some loss -- so it doesn't matter who supplies the money, whereas imprisonment and execution aim at retribution, which by its nature requires that the guilty party specifically be the one to suffer. Since the doctrine holds that Christ satisfied retributive justice rather than merely compensating someone, the fine case doesn't carry over to it.

Ben answered that the compensation story doesn't fit the facts: serious fines are paid to the court, not the victim, and we refuse substitution just as firmly for crimes like murder, where compensation was never even possible -- so whatever explains our tolerance for fine-substitution, it isn't compensation. He proposed instead that we tolerate it only because it's practically impossible to stop -- nothing prevents a friend from simply gifting the offender the money beforehand -- so our "acceptance" may be nothing but resignation to a loophole that cannot practically be closed, not any real endorsement of substitution.

I found merit on both sides but couldn't adjudicate the exchange myself. Could you go through it step by step and say who has the better of it at each point? I'm especially interested in whether Ben's closing "resignation" diagnosis actually holds up, or whether there's a further consideration neither of them raised that bears on it. Finally, tell me what you think the whole exchange shows about whether penal substitution theories of the atonement are, in the end, intelligible.
