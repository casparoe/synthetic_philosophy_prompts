I'm a software engineer on the loan-underwriting team at a fintech company. During our quarterly model-risk review, two of our data scientists, Devon and Marisol, got into a disagreement about the model's fairness audit that I haven't been able to stop thinking about. Some background: the model is well calibrated within each of the two demographic groups our compliance team tracks under fair-lending rules -- predicted default probabilities match actual default rates equally well in both (I'll just call them Group A and Group B) -- but its false-positive rate, meaning creditworthy applicants denied anyway, is nearly twice as high in Group B as in Group A.

1. Devon said the calibration result settles the fairness question: once a score means the same thing regardless of group, the model isn't the source of the disparity -- the gap in false positives just reflects a real difference in the groups' underlying risk profiles.
2. Marisol said calibration is fully compatible with wildly unequal error rates whenever base rates differ, which is exactly what's happening here, and it's no comfort to a creditworthy applicant in Group B, denied at twice the rate of an equally creditworthy applicant in Group A, to be told the score "means the same thing."
3. Devon answered that the only way to equalize the false-positive rates is a more lenient approval threshold for Group B than Group A -- deciding applications partly on group membership, the very thing fair-lending law exists to prevent -- whereas their current single, group-blind threshold doesn't look at group membership at all.
4. Marisol answered that the threshold is group-blind in name only, since the underlying score is already built from features correlated with group membership -- keeping one threshold isn't a neutral default, it's a choice to accept the disparate outcome instead of the disparate treatment.
5. Devon replied that this proves too much, since it would justify adjusting for any group difference found anywhere in the model, and that Marisol still hadn't said why equalized error rates, rather than calibration, should win -- especially since you provably cannot have both once the groups' base rates differ.
6. Marisol said she wasn't claiming equalized error rates is uniquely correct either -- only that Devon doesn't get to call the status quo neutral and put the entire burden of proof on the alternative. If it's really a forced trade-off between two legitimate notions of fairness, somebody already made that value judgment, silently, by shipping calibration-plus-one-threshold as the default design.

Nobody at the review changed their mind, and I don't know this literature well enough to know who's actually right. Please go through the six points one at a time and say whether each person is basically correct, correct but overstating something, or mistaken, and why -- then close with your overall verdict on whose position holds up better, and note what, if anything, both of them are missing.
