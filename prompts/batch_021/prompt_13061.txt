I've been drafting notes toward a LessWrong post on the problem of logical omniscience, and I would value comments before I try to turn it into something postable. The core of the argument runs as follows.

Standard Bayesian coherence, as delivered by the Dutch book argument, requires an agent to assign credence 1 to every logical or mathematical truth and identical credences to any two logically equivalent sentences, since a bookie who can see the equivalence can otherwise construct a sure loss regardless of what the agent itself has managed to prove. This is routinely flagged as an idealization that no bounded reasoner can actually satisfy. My worry is that this concedes too little: the trouble isn't merely that bounded agents fall short of an otherwise sound ideal, but that the ideal is itself confused, since it treats an agent's position partway through a proof as a mere embarrassment rather than as something any adequate theory of rationality has to represent.

Logical induction is usually offered as the fix: a computable procedure for pricing logical sentences such that no efficiently computable trader can extract unbounded profit from the sequence over time, with the prices provably converging to respect logical relationships in the limit. But this feels to me like it changes the subject rather than answers it. It tells us which pricing policy resists exploitation in the long run; it doesn't obviously tell us whether the particular number the policy outputs today, about some conjecture nobody has settled, is itself justified, as against merely destined to look reasonable in hindsight once the market converges. That looks more like a Reichenbach-style pragmatic vindication than a solution to the original problem.

Is this a fair reading of what logical induction is meant to establish, or am I collapsing two questions that the framework was never meant to answer at once? And is the "confused ideal" objection to classical coherence a familiar move I should be crediting to someone, rather than presenting as my own?
