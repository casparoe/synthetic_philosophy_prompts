A friend and I just spent an hour arguing about Carnap's politics. I argued that the 1929 Vienna Circle manifesto he co-signed with Neurath and Hahn -- which ties the 'scientific world-conception' to rationally reorganizing economic and social life -- shows that logical positivism was always covertly technocratic: if value talk is cognitively empty, a 'scientific' planning body can't admit it's choosing ends at all, so it just repackages the choice as a neutral technical finding. My friend replied that this has it backwards: Carnap's own fact/value distinction should make a planning body keep its value choices explicit and revisable, not let expertise decide them by default.

This isn't just academic: I sit on my university's Committee on Research Metrics, which must decide by next month whether to replace departmental peer review with a 'Research Impact Index' -- citations, grant income, and a 'societal impact' score, combined into one formula -- for allocating a $2M discretionary fund. Supporters call it objective; critics say the weighting smuggles in a contestable view of scholarship's purpose. Draft the committee's decision memo -- adopt, reject, or adopt with conditions -- with reasons the administration could act on, and a fair hearing for whichever side loses.
