I sit on the seven-member Charter Review Commission for our city of roughly 130,000 residents. The City Council has asked us to evaluate a citizen petition proposing that noncitizen residents — people who hold permanent legal residency but have not naturalized — be permitted to vote in municipal elections (city council, mayor, and local ballot measures, though not state or national elections) once they have lived in the city for at least a year. The Commission has split three ways, along lines that cut across the usual immigration debate. One view holds that anyone whose interests are substantially affected by the city's decisions has a claim to a vote, and sees no principled reason to stop at long-term noncitizen residents rather than extending the same logic to commuters, seasonal workers, or absentee landlords who own property here. A second view wants to ground the franchise instead in whether someone is actually subject to the city's coercive authority — its policing, zoning, and tax enforcement — as a matter of daily life, which noncitizen residents clearly are but the absentee stakeholders are not. A third view holds that the ballot is a privilege of full civic membership, conferred nationally through naturalization, and that a city is not entitled to extend it to those who lack that status, whatever the merits of the other two positions. Our legal counsel added, half as a joke, that however we draw the line, the seven of us were appointed by a Council that was itself elected by the current, citizen-only electorate, so no one on the Commission is obviously in a position to redraw the boundaries of that electorate without already assuming an answer to who belongs to it.

The Council wants our advisory report before the petition goes to referendum or dies in committee next month, and I have been asked to produce the first draft. It should reach a concrete recommendation — adopting the amendment as written, adopting some modified version (a different residency threshold, a sunset clause requiring review after a fixed number of election cycles, and so on), or declining to advance it — together with a realistic timeline for whatever comes next. Rather than resting the case on how other cities have handled similar petitions, I would like the recommendation to engage on the merits with the strongest form of whichever position it ultimately sets aside, including our legal counsel's worry about circularity.
