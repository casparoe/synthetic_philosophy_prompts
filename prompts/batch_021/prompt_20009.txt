In my decision theory seminar, we have been examining Gibbard and Harper's "Death in Damascus" case, in which a man learns that Death is coming for him, flees Damascus for Aleppo, and finds Death waiting there anyway, because Death is an infallible predictor of exactly where the man will go. Since the case is constructed so that the man dies whichever city he chooses, it strikes me as essentially a decision-theoretic repackaging of the fatalist moral behind "Appointment in Samarra": once an infallible power has already anticipated your choice, deliberating about what to do cannot actually change anything, so the "choice" is only nominal. Given that, why do decision theorists continue to treat Death in Damascus as posing a live, unresolved problem for causal decision theory, rather than simply retiring it as a vivid illustration of a fatalism whose refutation is already well established in the philosophical literature?
