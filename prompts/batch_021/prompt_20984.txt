So last night my friend Priya and I got into an actual argument, not just banter, about whether Einstein counts as a socialist in any serious sense or if that's just a fun trivia fact people repeat without knowing what's actually in "Why Socialism?" Her position is that the essay is basically vibes — "competition makes people rotten to each other, therefore socialism" — with none of the actual Marxist machinery (no labor theory of value, no class struggle, nothing about historical materialism), so calling him a socialist "properly speaking" is like calling someone a Stoic because they once said "it's fine" through gritted teeth. I think that's a little unfair, because he's not just griping about meanness, he's making a specific argument about planned vs. anarchic production, and he's weirdly upfront for a manifesto-adjacent essay about the risk that a planned economy just recreates tyranny in bureaucratic form if you don't deliberately build in checks on it. We went around for like two hours and didnt land anywhere, which would be fine except I actually need to think straight about this by Saturday.

Here's why: my old advisor Frank is retiring after 38 years in the physics department and I got asked to give the toast at his dinner. Frank has been in the same democratic-socialist reading group since grad school and has quoted that essay at every department holiday party for as long as anyone can remember, to the point where it's a running bit that if you call him "basically a Marxist" or "a Communist" at the wrong moment he will actually stop the conversation and correct you on the difference, at length. The room is going to be half physics people who've only ever known him as a very kind experimentalist and treat the politics as a lovable quirk, and half his reading-group comrades who will absolutely clock it if I flatten his views into either "harmless old man with a hobby" or "secret radical" — since by his own lights (and Einstein's) neither of those is right. Can you write me the toast? Something like five minutes, warm and genuinely funny in places, but with the Einstein material actually doing work in it rather than being a name-drop — I want it to reflect what's specific and a bit surprising about the actual position (the planning-vs-anarchy stuff, the individual-freedom-and-bureaucracy worry, not just "markets bad") in a way that lands for the reading-group crowd without turning into a lecture that leaves my aunt checking her phone.
