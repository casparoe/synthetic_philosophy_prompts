Last weekend I got into a long, increasingly heated argument with a friend who is finishing a PhD in logic, after she mentioned, almost in passing, that she thinks some contradictions are actually true: not true in some loose, figurative sense, but genuinely, literally both true and false, and that classical logic is simply wrong to rule this out across the board. I pushed back for the better part of two hours, mostly by insisting that this was self-evidently absurd, and although she never talked me into agreeing with her, she did convince me that the position is far more carefully built than I had given it credit for, and that most of the objections I threw at her were ones she had clearly heard before and already had a ready answer to. The view, I've since learned, is called dialetheism, and it comes paired with a paraconsistent logic designed to stop a contradiction from letting you validly infer absolutely anything.

As it happens, I record a small philosophy interview podcast, and I have just booked a guest who defends more or less the same view my friend does: that certain self-referential sentences, such as the liar sentence, are what dialetheists call dialetheias, and that classical logic has to give way to something paraconsistent to house that fact without the whole system collapsing into triviality. I would rather not spend the interview re-running the exchange I already lost, so I would like help drafting eight to ten questions that go well past having the guest simply restate the position. I want at least one question that presses on the sheer incredulity most people feel toward the claim that a single sentence can be both true and false, and asks why we should trust the formal apparatus built to accommodate that claim more than the intuition it overrides; at least one on how switching off the rule that a contradiction implies anything is supposed to work without quietly costing us other everyday moves, like ruling a possibility out by deriving an absurdity from it; and at least one on the "revenge" worry that any apparatus rich enough to say a sentence is both true and false seems to let you construct a new sentence, using that very apparatus, that breaks in exactly the same way one level up. For each of these three, and for whichever other questions you include, please add a short, pointed follow-up that anticipates the answer the guest is most likely to give and doesn't let them simply retreat to the line my friend already used on me.
