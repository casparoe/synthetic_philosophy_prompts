Over the past several months I have been working on scaling laws for a large language model training pipeline, and I keep encountering loose talk about "emergence": the claim that certain capabilities appear abruptly once a model crosses some parameter or compute threshold, even though nothing qualitatively new has been added to the architecture. There is now also an influential rebuttal contending that many of these apparent jumps are artifacts of discontinuous evaluation metrics and disappear once smoother metrics are used instead. A physicist colleague of mine insists that this whole debate rehearses, in a much cruder form, a far older and more precise dispute in philosophy of physics over phase transitions. On one side is Batterman's argument that, because sharp phase transitions are strictly defined only in the thermodynamic limit, reduction to the statistical mechanics of any actual finite-particle system fails not merely in practice but in principle. On the other is Butterfield's rejoinder that this is too quick, since finite systems already exhibit the relevant behavior asymptotically as the limit is approached, so that emergence and reduction turn out to be compatible after all. I would like you to reconstruct this dispute with enough precision that I can judge for myself whether the machine-learning case is structurally analogous to what is really at stake between Batterman and Butterfield, or whether it merely borrows the vocabulary of emergence while remaining, underneath, a fairly mundane instance of epistemic surprise.
