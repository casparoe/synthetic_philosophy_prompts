In the Metaphysics of Morals (6:443), Kant writes, in Mary Gregor's translation:

"With regard to the animate but non-rational part of creation, violent and cruel treatment of animals is far more intimately opposed to a human being's duty to himself, and he has a duty to refrain from this; for it dulls his shared feelings of their suffering and so weakens and gradually uproots a natural predisposition that is very serviceable to morality in one's relations with other people. Even gratitude for the long service of an old horse or dog (just as if they were members of the household) belongs indirectly to a human being's duty with regard to these animals; considered as a direct duty, however, it is always only a duty of the human being to himself."

Elsewhere in this same discussion, Kant maintains that a human being "has duties only to human beings" and that whatever we owe concerning animals are duties merely "with regard to" them, never duties "to" them. Yet the passage above asks us to feel gratitude toward a faithful old horse or dog and to treat it as though it were a member of the household — an attitude that seems inherently other-directed in a way that pure self-regarding concern is not. Explain how, on Kant's own terms, such gratitude could ultimately amount to nothing more than a duty to oneself, and assess whether this reconciliation succeeds, or whether the language of gratitude covertly commits Kant to treating the animal as more than a mere means after all.
