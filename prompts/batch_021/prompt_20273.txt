I've spent the last several months teaching myself the philosophy of probability out of pure curiosity -- I never studied philosophy formally, but I enjoy sitting with an argument until it actually clicks rather than just nodding along. What pulled me in was the Principal Principle, which I gather traces back to David Lewis: the idea that if you know the objective chance of some event is x, your personal degree of belief in it should also be x, provided you don't have "inadmissible" information (the classic illustration being something like a crystal ball or a time traveler telling you how things actually turn out). Stated like that, it barely sounds like it needs a name -- of course your credence in a fair coin landing heads should be one-half. But once I followed this into Lewis's own metaphysics of chance, things stopped being obvious.

As I understand it, Lewis wanted chance to be thoroughly Humean: the chances at any moment are whatever falls out of the single "best system" that summarizes the complete pattern of events across the entire history of the world -- past and future -- once that system is scored for simplicity, strength, and, for chance specifically, how well it "fits" the actual sequence of outcomes. The trouble, which I've seen referred to somewhere as Lewis's own "big bad bug," is that this appears to make chance self-undermining: a chancy process running today could, with some small but perfectly genuine chance, produce a future course of history so far out of line with the "expected" pattern that a different chance function would end up as part of the best system instead. In other words, the very probabilities supposedly governing the process all along could get overwritten, after the fact, by the low-probability outcome they themselves permitted. That seems to leave the Principal Principle in a strange spot: it's not obvious what exactly I'm being told to defer to, if the chance I defer to today has some chance of turning out not to have been "the chance" once the future is in.

I raised this with a couple of friends from a small informal reading group I'm part of -- none of us has any academic training -- but we only managed to restate the puzzle to each other in different words. I know Lewis proposed a fix, usually called the New Principal Principle, in a later paper responding to his critics, but every explanation of it I've tracked down either hand-waves the details or buries them in notation I can't unpack on my own. Could you walk me through this properly: (1) why undermining is a genuine problem for the original principle and not just a technicality; (2) what the New Principal Principle actually changes about the way we're supposed to defer to chance, and whether that amounts to a real solution or more of a quiet redefinition that dodges the original question; and (3) whether a non-Humean picture of chance -- a primitivist or propensity view, say, where chances aren't hostage to the pattern of the whole history -- avoids this mess altogether, and if so, whether that counts as a genuine point in its favor rather than just a different set of costs? I'd like to come away actually understanding the shape of the disagreement, not just with a list of names attached to positions.
