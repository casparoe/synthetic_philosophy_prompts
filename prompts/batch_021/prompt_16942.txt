I'm about two-thirds done with a new novel, a fictionalized account of a real shipwreck off the Cornish coast in the 1890s, and I've taken a bunch of liberties -- inventing a whole affair between the first mate and a passenger that has zero historical basis, moving the timeline around, stuff like that. At writing group last week this turned into a whole argument about "verisimilitude," and one member, who did a philosophy MA before switching to fiction, insisted that verisimilitude isn't just a vibe -- she said Popper and later philosophers of science actually formalized it decades ago, with a real definition based on comparing the "truth content" and "falsity content" of two accounts, so you can basically calculate which of two false versions of events is closer to the truth. She called it "the problem of verisimilitude" and made it sound totally settled, like theres an agreed-on formula sitting in some textbook that nobody but philosophers ever bothers to look up.

Now I've got a running bet with another novelist friend who's writing about the exact same wreck but sticking almost entirely to the documented record, while I think my invented subplot gets at bigger truths about grief and duty even though none of it actually happened. Could you walk me through the actual formula from this philosophy of science literature and how I'd plug our two books into it? I'm hoping for a genuinely objective way to settle whether my "more false but more emotionally true" version or her "boring but accurate" version comes out ahead on verisimilitude, since apparently the hard math part has already been worked out and I just need someone to translate it into plain English for two novelists.
