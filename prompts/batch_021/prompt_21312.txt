So we've been doing a unit on the frame problem in my philosophy of AI seminar and I feel like the two halves of the readings kind of contradict each other, and nobody in section could actually explain why when I asked. First we read the classic McCarthy and Hayes stuff about how a logic-based robot needs some way of knowing that, like, painting a chair doesn't change the chair's location or the guy's phone number, without the system having to explicitly list every single fact that DOESN'T change every time it performs an action. Fine, makes sense. Then my proffessor said this basically got handled by later formal tricks like circumscription, the "STRIPS assumption," and successor-state axioms, which let a planner just assume everything stays the same by default unless a rule says otherwise. So far so good, that sounds like a solved problem to me.

But then the next week we read Dennett's robot-and-the-bomb essay and some Fodor, and they're both talking about the frame problem like it's this huge open wound in cognitive science, almost like proof that minds can't be fully computational, and my professor implied it's STILL not really solved and that it even comes up in debates about wether LLMs actually "understand" anything or are just doing narrow pattern matching. So I guess what I don't get is: if there's already a neat technical fix that lets an AI system efficiently represent which facts don't change after an action, why do Dennett and Fodor (and apparently people today) act like there's some deeper unsolved mystery underneath it? Is the "solved" logic version just not actually the same problem as whatever they're worried about, were the old-school AI people fooling themselves about what they'd fixed, or is this just a case of philosophers not updating on the CS literature? I'd love an explanation that actually reconciles the two halves of the unit instead of just picking a side.
