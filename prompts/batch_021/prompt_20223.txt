I've served for three years on the Reproductive Genetics Ethics Advisory Committee at the fertility practice where I work as a patient-care coordinator, and until last month the job was fairly sedate: reviewing consent-form language, approving counseling protocols for known-carrier couples, that kind of thing. That changed when the genetics laboratory we contract with for embryo biopsies notified us that it can now generate a supplementary polygenic report using the same genome-wide genotyping data already produced for every biopsied embryo, so no additional biopsy is required. The core report scores embryos for adult-onset polygenic diseases (type 2 diabetes, several cancers, schizophrenia); an optional module adds non-disease trait scores, including predicted adult height and an "educational attainment" score that functions in the research literature as a rough proxy for cognitive ability. Within the week, a couple with no known carrier status, doing IVF purely for unrelated infertility reasons, requested the full report including the trait module, explaining that they'd read about embryo-screening startups in the news and wanted the scores as a tiebreaker among several embryos that are otherwise chromosomally normal and comparable on disease risk.

I've been asked to draft the opinion the committee will vote on next Wednesday, and we're genuinely split. Our consent policy says only that genetic testing is offered "to reduce the risk of serious genetic disease and to support a healthy pregnancy," language nobody thought to sharpen before now. Roughly half the committee wants to instruct the lab to suppress the trait module outright, on the view that ranking embryos by predicted height or intelligence is enhancement rather than medicine and not something a fertility practice should be in the business of facilitating. The other half points out that the underlying sequencing has already been performed, the data already exists, the couple are the legal decision-makers for their own embryos, and it looks paternalistic to withhold information we could give them just because we're uneasy about their reasons for wanting it. There's also a wrinkle I only learned of while preparing for this: the trait and disease scores aren't cleanly separable in the underlying genetics — the genetic variants behind a high educational-attainment score are apparently also linked to a modestly elevated risk of bipolar disorder in the same population — so "report the disease scores but not the trait scores" may not even be a coherent instruction to give the lab, whatever we decide in principle.

Could you draft the short memo I'll circulate beforehand? I need an actual recommendation — for this couple's pending request and for a standing policy on future ones — rather than a call for more study, and I'd like it to engage honestly with the autonomy-and-paternalism objection rather than dispose of it in a sentence, since that's precisely what I'll have to defend on Wednesday.
