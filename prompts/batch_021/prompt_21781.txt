So I'm TAing for a Victorian intellectual history seminar this term and also trying to make headway on my own dissertation chapter on Sidgwick, and I keep circling the same question without landing anywhere. Everyone who reads the Methods of Ethics remembers the ending: Sidgwick basically admits that egoism and universalistic hedonism both look rationally binding to him, that he can't find any further principle inside secular ethics to adjudicate between them, and that the only thing that would close this "dualism of practical reason" is some further fact about the universe -- a moral government of things, or personal immortality -- guaranteeing that duty and self-interest ultimately coincide. Fine, that part's in every anthology. What gets left out of basically all the intro-level treatments is that this is the same guy who was the first president of the Society for Psychical Research when it got going in 1882, and who spent something like the next two decades of his life sitting through seance after seance and chasing hard evidence for telepathy and survival of death, right alongside Myers, Gurney, and his own wife Eleanor.

I keep going back and forth on how seriously to take the link between these two projects. One reading, which is basically Bart Schultz's line in his big biography, is that the psychical research isn't a hobby bolted onto the ethics -- its the natural next move for someone who got to the end of the Methods and refused to either fake a resolution or just shrug. If the dualism can only be closed by facts about a moral order or an afterlife, and you've already shown (with the fellowship resignation and everything) that you won't assert what you can't defend, then going and actually looking for the evidence is the only intellectually honest option left. The competing reading says this gives Sidgwick's life way too much credit for coherence -- that the SPR stuff is better explained by grief and the general Victorian crisis of faith, that Myers and Gurney were driving the agenda for their own reasons, and that reading it back into the argument of the Methods is a flattering just-so story rather than actual philosophy.

Could you write me an essay that actually stakes out a position on this instead of hedging? Go through both readings in their strongest form, engage with the real structure of the dualism argument itself rather than just gesturing at "he was worried about morality and religion," and land on an actual verdict about whether Sidgwick's psychical research should count as a rational continuation of his ethics or as something that needs to be explained on other grounds. A qualified answer is fine, but I want something I could actually quote in a footnote, not "there are considerations on both sides."
