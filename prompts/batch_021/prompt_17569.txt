I am at work on a novel that turns, in part, on a running argument between two characters: an evolutionary biologist and the family she has married into, whose wealth expresses itself almost entirely through visible, extravagant, and faintly absurd waste. She keeps telling them, half in jest, that their behavior is just a peacock's tail by other means. Before I let her make that case on the page with a straight face, I would like to know whether it actually holds up. Compare Amotz Zahavi's handicap principle with Thorstein Veblen's account of conspicuous consumption: both treat costly, seemingly wasteful display as a way of conveying real information about the displayer, but do the two accounts rest on the same underlying logic? Zahavi's signals are supposed to remain honest only because bearing their cost is disproportionately harder for a low-quality individual than for a high-quality one — does Veblen's theory depend on anything comparable, or is its force owed to something looser, such as emulation and invidious comparison, that doesn't actually require cost to track quality in that way? Tell me which of the two theories would hold up better if reconstructed as a model of a stable, honest signal, and at what point, precisely, my biologist's analogy should start to strain.
