I spent forty years as a nuclear physicist, working daily with half-lives and decay constants, and it is only since retiring that I have started reading philosophy of probability in earnest. I have been working through John Venn's *The Logic of Chance* (1866), whose frequentism grounds every probability statement in the relative frequency of an attribute across an actual or hypothetical series of similar cases. Venn died in 1923: he certainly knew radioactive decay as a statistical law, since Rutherford and Soddy had established it two decades earlier, but he did not live to see quantum mechanics harden, later in that same decade, into the claim that an individual atom's decay is not merely unpredicted but uncaused, nor to see Popper propose his propensity interpretation, in the 1950s, largely in order to make sense of that very claim. I would like your best reconstruction of how Venn, reasoning strictly from the commitments of his own book rather than from frequentism in the abstract, would have responded to Popper on this point. Would he insist that the single decaying atom is simply a member of a well-defined series, so that nothing in his theory need change, or would genuine single-case indeterminism have forced him to concede that frequency alone cannot capture what physicists mean by the probability of that decay?
