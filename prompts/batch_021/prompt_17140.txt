After forty years as an experimental physicist, I retired three years ago and have since thrown myself into philosophy with the slightly overcompensating enthusiasm of the late convert. This past term I sat in on a small non-credit seminar on classical Indian epistemology at the university near me, and when I would not stop talking on the course forum about how neatly the Nyāya school's four pramāṇas map onto the different ways we validate a measurement in the laboratory — a direct reading, an inference from an indirect signal, a calibration against a known standard, and simple reliance on a trusted colleague's reported result — the instructor, half in jest, asked whether I would help her grade a batch of short-answer responses to a single question. I agreed, on the condition that I do it properly rather than just skim for the gist, which is really why I am writing to you: I would rather lean on a second opinion than trust my own still-thin grasp of the secondary literature.

The question the students were set was this: "Explain why the Naiyāyikas treat upamāna as a fourth, independent pramāṇa rather than reducing it to a special case of anumāna or śabda. Then explain the Nyāya position on the extrinsic ascertainment of a cognition's validity (parataḥ prāmāṇya), contrasting it with the rival Mīmāṃsā view that a cognition's validity is grasped intrinsically, in and through the cognition itself (svataḥ prāmāṇya). Illustrate with an example of your choosing."

Below, reproduced verbatim, is the answer submitted by one of the students:

"The Nyāya school lists four pramāṇas, or means by which pramā (veridical cognition) is produced: pratyakṣa (perception), anumāna (inference), upamāna (comparison), and śabda (verbal testimony). Each is treated as irreducible: none of the four can simply be analyzed away into one of the others, which is part of what makes Nyāya's epistemology a form of pluralism about the sources of knowledge rather than a reductive foundationalism built on perception alone.

Upamāna is the hardest of the four to place, and the Naiyāyikas' stock example shows why they nonetheless insisted on its independence. A man who has never seen a gavaya (a kind of wild ox) is told by a forest-dweller that a gavaya is an animal similar to a cow. Later, coming upon such an animal in the forest, he recognizes it as a gavaya. What upamāna supplies, on the Nyāya analysis, is not the perception of the animal — pratyakṣa already does that — but the further piece of knowledge that the word 'gavaya' denotes this animal, a fact about the relation between a name and its object that the traveler could not have had before this encounter. Since neither the original testimony, which asserted only a resemblance and not that this particular animal answers to the word, nor the bare perception of the animal contains that fact, the Naiyāyikas conclude that the traveler is, in effect, inferring the word's application from the noted resemblance, so that upamāna operates as a hybrid of testimony and inference rather than as a wholly autonomous faculty in its own right.

The school is just as distinctive on how a cognition's own truth becomes known. Being the tradition most invested in inference and formal proof, the Naiyāyikas held that a cognition certifies itself: once a cognition of the form 'this is water' occurs, its veridicality is grasped in the very having of it, with no further, independent cognition required (svataḥ prāmāṇya). It was the Mīmāṃsakas, so anxious to secure the unchallengeable authority of Vedic injunction, who denied this and insisted that validity is always established from outside the cognition — typically by appeal to successful practice (pravṛtti-sāmarthya). So if I see water in a well and judge that it is there, the Mīmāṃsaka holds that I only know my perception was veridical once I have gone and drawn water from the well and found my thirst satisfied, whereas for the Naiyāyika the perception was already self-certifying the instant it occurred."
