I'm midway through a stats PhD doing applied causal inference (heterogeneous treatment effects, the usual Pearl/Rubin toolkit), and my advisor roped a few of us into a reading group on the philosophical foundations of all the do-calculus stuff we use without ever really thinking about it. We just spent two sessions on Woodward's interventionist theory of causation and I got stuck on something nobody in the group could really resolve. Woodward cashes out "X causes Y" via what would happen to Y under a hypothetical ideal intervention on X — basically the do-operator wearing a philosopher's coat. But "intervention" is itself spelled out in causal terms: it has to be something that causes X directly, that doesn't affect Y through any route besides X, and that isn't correlated with Y's other causes. So isn't the whole analysis just causation defined in terms of causation, wearing a disguise? Someone in the group said "yeah, that's a known objection, Woodward has a move here," but couldn't reconstruct what the move actually was.

What I'm really curious about is whether Lewis's older counterfactual story is any better off, or if it's got basically the same skeleton, just buried deeper. On the face of it Lewis doesn't need any of this — just a similarity ranking over possible worlds, no causal graph required. But then there's that whole business about ruling out backtracking counterfactuals via the asymmetry of overdetermination — a little local miracle right before the divergence point, rather than one big miracle to fake up a different past — which is exactly the built-in asymmetry that lets him get the causal direction right in the first place. And that smells suspiciously like its doing the same job as Woodward's conditions on interventions, just in different vocabulary. Could you actually walk me through, in an intuitive rather than purely technical way: (a) why the circularity charge against interventionism is supposed to have real bite, and what Woodward's best reply to it is, and (b) whether the counterfactual theory genuinely dodges an analogous problem or just relocates it into the similarity metric? If a concrete case can show where the two theories actually come apart on this, rather than just redescribing the same verdict in different words, that would help a ton — it doesn't need to be a novel example, the standard preemption/overdetermination cases are fine.
