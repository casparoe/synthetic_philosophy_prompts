Paul Humphreys's paradox is widely taken to be decisive against the propensity interpretation of probability. Propensities are supposed to be single-case, forward-directed causal tendencies of a chance setup to produce an outcome; yet if propensities are to be genuine probabilities that satisfy the full probability calculus, Bayes's theorem forces us to countenance a "reverse" propensity of a later effect to bring about its own earlier cause. Please argue that a propensity theorist can meet this challenge and preserve a substantive, causal notion of propensity, without simply denying that propensities must obey the standard axioms of probability.
