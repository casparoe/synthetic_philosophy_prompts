Two of my students, Nadia and Colm, got into a real argument after seminar last week over the Allais paradox and I haven't been able to stop thinking about it since. I'd put the classic case on the board:

Choice 1: (A) $1 million for sure, or (B) 10% chance of $5 million, 89% chance of $1 million, 1% chance of nothing.
Choice 2: (C) 11% chance of $1 million, 89% chance of nothing, or (D) 10% chance of $5 million, 90% chance of nothing.

Like most people, both of them said they'd take A over B and D over C. Then it got heated:

(1) Nadia said the combo is just a mistake: you can do the algebra in about four lines and show no expected utility function can rank both pairs that way, so whatever the psychological story behind it, it's a straightforward violation of independence, full stop.
(2) Colm said the algebra only shows the pattern isn't EU-representable, not that it's irrational -- Allais's whole point was that independence is what should be doubted, not the preferences, since giving up a sure thing for a 1% shot at nothing is categorically different from choosing between two already-risky bets, and caring about that difference is exactly the sort of thing a rational agent is allowed to do.
(3) Nadia came back with the money-pump: if the common part of two gambles is allowed to swing your ranking like that, someone can walk you through a sequence of trades that leaves you strictly worse off then where you started, guaranteed, and thats a lot worse than just failing some formal axiom.
(4) Colm answered that the pump only works if you're stuck evaluating each choice in isolation and can't refuse the next trade once you see where it's headed -- he brought up Machina's non-expected-utility theory, which keeps dominance and transitivity but drops independence precisely so Allais-style choices don't have to be exploitable that way.

Neither of them budged, and I couldn't tell you whether Colm actually defused the money-pump worry in (4) or just relocated it, or what Nadia would even say back if he did. Can you go through the exchange move by move and say who's right (or more right) at each step, not just who wins overall?
