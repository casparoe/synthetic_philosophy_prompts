Since retiring from a long career in experimental solid-state physics, I have taken up a fairly systematic reading program in decision theory, and I recently spent some time working through the money-pump argument for the transitivity of preference. As I understand it: if I strictly prefer A to B, B to C, and C to A, and I am willing to pay a small sum whenever I can trade what I currently hold for something I prefer more, an enterprising bookie can walk me through the cycle A→C→B→A, collecting a fee at each step, until I arrive back at my starting point poorer by three trades' worth of fees, with further losses accumulating each time the cycle is repeated. What appeals to me about this argument is that it is explicitly diachronic rather than synchronic: rather than simply observing that an intransitive ranking cannot be represented by a single real-valued utility function, it purports to show the trouble unfolding concretely over a sequence of choices through time.

I raised this at a small philosophy discussion group I have joined since retiring, and the analogy that occurred to me is a stability argument in dynamics: a system displaced from equilibrium dissipates energy until it settles down, and it seemed to me that the money pump was showing something similarly law-like about agents displaced from transitivity — namely, that they must be drained of resources given enough iterations. Yet I recall that Amos Tversky's classic experiments on intransitive choice among gambles found ordinary subjects violating transitivity in a stable, reproducible way, without any evident sign that these same subjects were being progressively fleeced in their everyday economic lives. If the money-pump argument really does establish what I take it to establish, shouldn't real intransitive preferences be rare, fleeting, or self-correcting, rather than a robust experimental finding? I would welcome your view on whether my physical analogy holds up, and if it does not, precisely where it breaks down.
