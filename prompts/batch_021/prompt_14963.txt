Since retiring after thirty-odd years doing research in condensed-matter physics, I have been teaching myself formal epistemology, largely on my own, and I recently spent several weeks working through the "accuracy-first" program because it appealed to me immediately. Rather than Dutch-book arguments, which only bite if someone can be induced to accept a particular system of bets, Jim Joyce's 1998 proposal was to show that credences violating the probability axioms are simply less accurate than some coherent rival: guaranteed to track the truth worse in every possible world, regardless of which world happens to be actual. That struck me as the sort of "objective" argument I was trained to trust, since it doesn't depend on anyone's psychology or betting behaviour, only on comparing doxastic states against the facts.

The more I read about how the theorem is actually secured, though, the less non-pragmatic it started to look to me. Joyce's original proof rested on a short list of formal conditions that any legitimate way of measuring inaccuracy was supposed to satisfy, and in 2002 Patrick Maher showed that those conditions were too permissive: he produced a scoring rule meeting every one of Joyce's stated conditions for which some incoherent credence function nevertheless escaped domination by every coherent alternative. The repair that is now standard, due to Predd and coauthors, published in 2009 in, appropriately enough, an IEEE information-theory journal, drops Joyce's bespoke conditions and instead proves the dominance theorem geometrically for the whole class of continuous strictly proper scoring rules at once, by treating credence functions as points in a real vector space and appealing to the existence of a nearest point in a convex set. As mathematics I find this beautiful and entirely convincing. Philosophically, though, I can't quite shake a worry. The comparison at the heart of the theorem — that one credence function is more accurate than another at a given possible world — never sums or averages over anything, and so is refreshingly probability-free; but "strictly proper," which is now doing all the work of picking out which scores are even eligible to run the argument, is defined by requiring that a credence function's own expected score, weighting the score at each world by the credence function's own credence in that world, be uniquely minimized by reporting itself rather than any rival. That is exactly the notion of "expected" at work in ordinary decision theory.

I would like you to argue, as rigorously as you can, for the following claim: that because the class of "legitimate" scoring rules is fixed by this expectation-theoretic requirement, the real normative work in the accuracy-dominance argument has quietly migrated from the pointwise, assumption-light dominance comparison into the prior choice of which scores are allowed into the argument at all — so that the resulting vindication of probabilism is not, in the end, independent of broadly pragmatic, decision-theoretic assumptions about how to weigh possibilities against one another, contrary to how the argument is usually advertised. Please state the strongest reply available to an accuracy-first epistemologist on this point (for instance, some argument that immodesty, or self-recommendation, is itself required on purely alethic grounds and carries no pragmatic content whatsoever) before explaining why, in your view, it does not succeed.
