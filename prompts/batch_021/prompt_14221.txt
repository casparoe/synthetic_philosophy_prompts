So my friend Marco and I got into a two-hour argument on my porch last weekend about something dumb that turned out to be genuinely interesting: does a wedding ceremony actually DO anything, beyond being a nice party? Marco's take was that if two people are already committed, the ceremony is just theatre — you'd get the same result if they individually texted everyone "hey, we're married now." I pushed back with the blue-eyed islanders puzzle and the coordinated-attack-problem/two-generals thing, arguing there's a real gap between everyone seperately knowing something (mutual knowledge) and it being common knowledge, where everyone knows that everyone knows that everyone knows, etc, and that a public ceremony where the whole room watches everyone else watch the vows is basically the cheapest way normal people ever get to manufacture actual common knowledge — which is why it licenses stuff (in-laws suddenly acting like in-laws, whatever) that a round of individual phone calls never could. Marco wouldn't fully concede, his counter was that the classic puzzles also need common knowledge of everyone's rationality, not just of the fact itself, and that assuming a room of half-drunk wedding guests are perfect logicians who'll instantly act on new information is doing a LOT of quiet work in my argument. We never really resolved it.

Anyway, I'm now actually giving the toast at my best friend's wedding in three weeks (she met her fiancé in a decision theory reading group in grad school, so honestly this isn't even a stretch for this particular crowd) and I want to build it around this idea for real, instead of just handing the nerds in the room a fun fact. I dont want a toast that explains common knowledge as trivia — I want it to actually try to DO the thing the visitor's announcement does in the islanders puzzle: say out loud, in front of everyone, something true that everyone already privately believes about the two of them, so that the saying of it is the moment it turns from something everyone individually thinks into something everyone in the room knows everyone else thinks too. Problem is half the guests are her old rationalist-meetup friends who will clock exactly what I'm doing, and the other half is extended family who have never heard the phrase "epistemic logic" in their life and just want a nice toast about two people who love each other. Can you write me that toast? It needs to be toastable out loud in about 3-4 minutes, genuinely funny in at least one place, and land as love, not as a lecture.
