ok so this week's rabbit hole: I finally read the actual Gongsun Long "White Horse" dialogue (3rd-century-BCE Chinese logician, the so-called School of Names guy) instead of just the one-liner everyone in LW/ACX-adjacent spaces uses as their go-to "look how silly ancient sophists were" example — "a white horse is not a horse," ha ha. turns out the real text stacks three or four distinct-ish arguments on top of each other for that conclusion, and honestly some of them seem like they might be doing real work? here's the chunk that's been rattling around in my head (I stitched this together from a couple translations and smoothed the archaic bits, so don't quote me on exact wording, but the logic should be intact):

Objector: Can one maintain that "a white horse is not a horse"?
Gongsun Long: One can. "Horse" is what we use to name a shape. "White" is what we use to name a color. Naming a color is not naming a shape. So a white horse is not a horse.

Objector: If there are white horses, you can't say there are no horses. Doesn't that make a white horse a horse?
Gongsun Long: If a man wants a horse, a yellow or a black horse will answer his want. If he wants a white horse, a yellow or black horse will not answer it. If "white horse" really were "horse," what's wanted in the two cases would be one and the same thing, and it shouldn't matter whether the horse is yellow or white. But it plainly does matter, so a white horse is not a horse.

...and later: A horse certainly has some color, which is why there can be white horses at all. If a horse had no color, there would be just "horse" and nothing more — how would you even pick out a white one? "White horse" is "horse" joined to "white," and horse-joined-to-white isn't just "horse."

what's bugging me: is the first move (naming shape vs naming color) actually a different argument from the second one (the "yellow/black horse satisfies 'seeking a horse' but not 'seeking a white horse'" bit), or is it the same equivocation between "is" as identity and "is" as class-membership, just repackaged twice? and that last bit, where "horse" gets treated like some bare colorless universal that "white" then attaches to — is that a genuine point about combining properties, or is he quietly assuming his conclusion (that any modifier changes identity) in order to prove it? been trying to figure out if theres an actual defensible logical/semantic thesis buried in here or if its just very committed wordplay, and I can't fully convince myself either way. a steelman plus a diagnosis of where (if anywhere) it goes wrong would be great.
