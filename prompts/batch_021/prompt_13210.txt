A close friend of mine, a logician who works on tiling agents -- on whether a self-modifying reasoning system can ever fully vouch for a successor's reliability from inside its own proof system, given what Löb's theorem seems to forbid -- is getting married next month, and I have been asked to give the toast. We have never once resolved our long-running argument about whether this "Löbian obstacle" is a permanent limit on self-trust or just an artifact of demanding too formal a notion of trust in the first place, and we had it out again, inconclusively, only last week. In a few weeks she will stand up and promise, in the first person, that her future self will love her husband faithfully for the rest of her life -- exactly the kind of unconditional claim about one's future conduct that, on her own account of the obstacle, cannot be secured from within the very system making the promise. Half the room will be her logic and AI-safety colleagues, who will catch that immediately; the other half will be both families, who will simply be listening for a toast about love. Please write a four-minute toast that lets the Löbian obstacle genuinely constrain what it can say: it should be the reason the speech cannot simply assert that she will obviously live happily ever after and has to find something truer to say instead, without letting Löb's theorem collapse into a generic point about nobody being able to predict the future.
