I'm a retired solid-state physicist (spent almost forty years doing low-temperature transport measurements before I hung up my cryostat for good), and since retiring I've somehow ended up in a philosophy of science reading group at the local university, mostly out of boredom and a nagging feeling that I used a lot of tools during my career I never really understood at a deep level. We just spent two sessions on Wigner's old essay about the "unreasonable effectiveness" of mathematics, and it kicked off an arguement in the group that never really got resolved, so I'm hoping you can help me stage it properly.

The thing that struck me rereading Wigner is that his actual claim is narrower and stranger than "math is useful" -- it's that a law extracted from a small, none-too-precise set of observations (his go-to example is Newton's law of gravitation, fit to Kepler's rough planetary data) later turns out to hold to a precision and over a range wildly beyond anything that went into deriving it, and he thinks that borders on a miracle with no rational explanation. When I brought up Hamming's rebuttal from years later -- the one arguing that a good chunk of the "miracle" dissolves once you factor in selection effects, mathematicians and physicists quietly co-evolving their tools to fit each other, and evolution tuning our mathematical intuitions to macroscopic reality in the first place -- half the group thought that basically dissolves the puzzle, and the other half thought its a dodge that doesn't touch the hard cases, like Dirac's equation coughing up antimatter before anyone had ever seen it, or Riemann's geometry sitting on the shelf for sixty-odd years before Einstein needed exactly that tool for general relativity. Somebody also brought up a more recent, much more skeptical piece (in the IEEE Proceedings, I think, though I might be misremembering the venue) arguing the whole "unreasonable effectiveness" framing is survivorship bias dressed up as metaphysics -- for every Dirac equation there's a three-body problem or a turbulence equation we still can't solve in closed form, and nobody keeps score on those.

So here's my request: write me a dialogue between two people who both clearly know this material cold -- one defending something close to Wigner's own view that the fit between math and physics points to something genuinely mysterious about the structure of reality, and the other pushing the deflationary story (selection effects, co-evolution, survivorship bias, evolved cognition) as hard as it can go without just refusing to engage with the hard cases. I want real back-and-forth, where each of them has to answer the other's actual best example rather than just restating their own position -- and I'd love for the antimatter case in particular to get fought over properly, since that's the one none of us in the group could resolve. If you can manage it, don't end with a tidy resolution -- instead have each side name one specific thing that would actually change their mind.
