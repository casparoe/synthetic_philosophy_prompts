I'm working on a novel set in Oxford in the 1650s, and one of my minor characters is a young mathematician who gets dragged into the Hobbes-Wallis circle-squaring feud (I've been mining Jesseph's book on the whole saga — "Squaring the Circle," I think its called — for research, and its a genuinely wild story). I want to give her a scene where she writes a letter to Hobbes going after him philosophically rather than just mathematically, but my writing group keeps telling me the philosophy bit is "too in the weeds" and I should cut it, which has only made me more determined to get it actually right before I decide whether to keep it.

Here's the setup, as I understand it: Hobbes thought geometry was the one science human beings had actually managed to make certain, and his reason for that wasn't "it's simple" — it was that we construct the objects ourselves (a circle is just whatever you get when a line sweeps around one fixed end), so we know their causes completely, and for Hobbes, science just is knowledge of causes reasoned out correctly from definitions. He then wanted civil philosophy to inherit that same certainty, since commonwealths, sovereignty, and obligation are also things we make, by covenant, rather than facts about the world we merely stumble on — so political conclusions should in principle be just as demonstrable as geometric ones, if people reasoned as carefully as geometers do.

What I want her letter to argue, and what I'd like your help actually building rather than just gesturing at, is this: Hobbes spent literally decades insisting he'd squared the circle, got correctly and publicly demolished by Wallis over and over, and never once conceded the point. I don't want her to use that as a cheap "you were bad at maths" jab — I want the strongest possible version of the argument that his own record as a geometer is a real counterexample to his theory of what grounds geometrical certainty, and that this specifically threatens the civil philosophy project rather than just being an embarrassing footnote. So please make that case rigorously, engaging with his actual causal "maker's knowledge" account of geometric demonstration, rather than resting on the easier, more generic objection that politics is just messier than mathematics.
