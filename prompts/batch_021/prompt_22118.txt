I'm a PhD student in computational social science, currently placed as the embedded methods advisor to my county's Pretrial Justice Reform Commission through a data-science fellowship my department runs with the county government. The commission is close to a vote on whether to adopt a new algorithmic risk instrument -- the vendor calls it the Meridian Score -- to help judges set bail and release conditions, and the pilot audit has split the room in a way that I don't think either side has fully reckoned with.

Here is the shape of the dispute. Meridian is calibrated by race: within each of its ten risk deciles, Black and white defendants assigned to that decile go on to be rearrested at essentially the same rate (decile 7, for instance, runs about 54% for both groups). But the county's underlying rearrest base rates differ sharply by race -- roughly 46% versus 26% -- and once you calibrate under that condition, the errors stop being symmetric: among defendants who are not rearrested, the false-positive rate is about 41% for Black defendants versus 21% for white defendants, while among defendants who are rearrested, the false-negative rate runs the other way, about 24% versus 47%. The commission's data-equity subcommittee wants Meridian rejected, or else sent back for race-specific decile cutoffs until that false-positive gap closes. The vendor, backed by two commissioners, argues Meridian is already fair by the one metric that is actually well-defined here -- calibration -- and that closing the error-rate gap is only possible by degrading accuracy overall or by setting different thresholds by race, which outside counsel has separately warned could amount to an explicit racial classification inviting an equal-protection challenge. I recall there is a real result behind this exact bind -- something like the Kleinberg-Mullainathan-Raghavan/Chouldechova theorem on the incompatibility of calibration and error-rate balance when base rates differ -- but no one on the commission, myself included, has worked through what it should actually imply for our vote.

I need to write the memo the full commission will act on next month, and I would rather get the reasoning right than just referee the room myself. Could you draft it as an actual decision memo -- a recommendation to adopt, reject, or adopt-with-conditions, with a concrete timeline for whichever you choose -- rather than a balanced survey of considerations? Please also engage honestly with whichever side you end up recommending against: if you recommend adoption, take seriously the subcommittee's worry that being "equally accurate on average" is compatible with quietly loading the cost of the tool's mistakes onto one group; if you recommend rejection or conditions, take seriously the vendor's point that this trade-off is not a defect specific to Meridian but a mathematical feature of scoring any two groups with unequal base rates, so no rival product would escape it either.
