For an elective on philosophy of language at my school, we recently covered Quine's discussion of radical translation in Word and Object, and the "gavagai" case has stayed with me ever since. In the thought experiment, a linguist visiting a community whose language is unrelated to any language she already knows hears a native speaker exclaim "Gavagai!" every time a rabbit darts past, and provisionally translates the word as "rabbit." As I understand Quine's argument, though, this translation is only one of several that fit the evidence equally well: "undetached rabbit part," "temporal stage of a rabbit," and "instance of rabbithood" would all be assented to and dissented from on exactly the same occasions, so no amount of pointing, prompting, or patient fieldwork could ever tell the linguist which one the speaker actually has in mind. I spend a fair amount of my free time on linguistics olympiad puzzles (the NACLO/IOL kind), so this example fascinates me, but every version I have tried to invent on my own just swaps in a different animal for the rabbit, which my teacher pointed out does not actually show that I understand why the indeterminacy arises in the first place.

Could you construct a genuinely new example of this same kind of indeterminacy, one that has nothing to do with rabbits, other animals, or any single pointable-at creature, and lay out at least three rival translations that would be tied to exactly the same pattern of assent and dissent no matter how much evidence a field linguist collected? I would like the explanation to spell out concretely why no further questioning or observation could break the tie between your rival translations, the way Quine's own argument does for "gavagai." If you are able, I would also be curious whether your example is, at bottom, just a repackaged version of the original whole-object/part/stage/kind ambiguity, or whether it draws on some other source of indeterminacy entirely; I have a nagging suspicion, from a stray comment I read somewhere, that Quine actually considered the rabbit case one of the easier and less radical illustrations of his overall thesis, and I have never seen anyone spell out what a harder version would look like.
