In my epistemology seminar this term, during the unit on knowledge and practical reason, we've been working through the disagreement between contextualism about knowledge attributions and pragmatic encroachment on knowledge (sometimes called subject-sensitive invariantism), mainly via DeRose's bank cases and Jason Stanley's work on practical interests. After one session, two classmates of mine, Owen and Farah, ended up arguing about it over coffee, and I listened closely enough to reconstruct roughly how it went. I have a short response paper due on this exact debate and would like an outside verdict, since I honestly can't tell where, if anywhere, either of them goes wrong.

The case that started it: take two versions of the same person, Renata, with identical evidence — she was at First National two Saturdays ago and it was open. In the low-stakes version, it's an ordinary Friday, and she tells her roommate, "I know the bank's open tomorrow," without bothering to check further. In the high-stakes version, her evidence hasn't changed at all, but her rent check has to clear by Monday or she'll be evicted, and now she says, "actually, I'm not sure — banks change their hours, we should call first." (1) Owen said this pair is a textbook case for contextualism: 'knows' expresses a stricter or looser epistemic standard depending on what's conversationally at stake, so both of Renata's utterances can be true as spoken even though her evidence and belief never change. (2) Farah said contextualism gets the data right but the mechanism wrong: what changes isn't the meaning of 'knows,' but whether Renata's true belief actually amounts to knowledge, since how much she stands to lose is itself part of what knowledge requires — and she added that the two views can be told apart by looking at cases where whoever is judging Renata's knowledge faces different stakes than Renata herself, which she said plain contextualism handles less naturally than her view does. (3) Owen shot back that this can't be right, because it makes whether someone knows something depend on morally arbitrary facts like their bank balance or how badly they need the check to clear — the wrong kind of thing to bear on whether a belief is knowledge, as opposed to on whether it's wise to act on it. (4) Farah answered that this objection doesn't really add anything: it just restates, as though it were an argument, the assumption that only truth-related factors like evidence and reliability can bear on knowledge, and that assumption is exactly what the bank cases were supposed to call into question.

Could you go through their exchange move by move and say, for each of the four numbered moves, whether it succeeds, fails, or is more complicated than either of them seems to realize, and why? Please close with a verdict on its own line, in exactly this format: \verdict{Owen}, \verdict{Farah}, or \verdict{Draw} — whoever, if anyone, had the better of this particular exchange, bearing in mind that this needn't be the same question as which of the two theories is, all things considered, more defensible.
