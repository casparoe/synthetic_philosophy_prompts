For a while now I've been reading about decision theory on forums like LessWrong, and one idea I keep coming back to is the notion of a "surrogate goal" as a defense against acausal threats. As I understand it, a sufficiently advanced mind could be blackmailed by another mind it will never meet or causally affect, simply because that other mind can predict or simulate how it would react to the threat of some catastrophic outcome. The proposed defense is for the mind to publicly, and quite sincerely, commit to caring just as much about some cheap and arbitrary stand-in goal, so that a rational extortionist targets the stand-in instead of anything that actually matters to it — its real values stay safe precisely because it would never yield on those. What I can't stop wondering about is what this does to a mind that has to sustain the pretence convincingly, possibly for a very long time. Can caring "as if" stay inert forever, or does it eventually become real caring? I'd like a short story, in the tradition of writers like Greg Egan or Ted Chiang, about one such mind — human, artificial, or something else entirely — at the moment it finally has to find out which. Please let this emerge through what happens rather than through a character explaining the theory out loud.
