Here is the question, worth 15 points: "In Anarchy, State, and Utopia, Nozick uses the Wilt Chamberlain example to argue against patterned principles of distributive justice. (a) Explain Nozick's distinction between historical principles and end-result (patterned) principles of justice. (b) Reconstruct the Wilt Chamberlain example and explain what it is supposed to show. (c) State the conclusion Nozick draws from the example, and explain how it bears on patterned conceptions of justice such as the difference principle."

And here, verbatim, is the student's answer: "Nozick distinguishes historical principles of justice, on which whether a distribution is just depends on the process that produced it, from end-result (patterned) principles, on which justice is fixed entirely by the shape of the distribution at a given time -- for instance, whether everyone has an equal share, or whether shares instead track need, IQ, or moral merit. Nozick's own entitlement theory is historical rather than patterned: a distribution is just if it arose from a just prior distribution through legitimate acts of acquisition and transfer, and it is patterned theories such as Rawls's difference principle that are the target of his critique. Wilt Chamberlain is supposed to show that no patterned distribution can remain stable if people are free to do as they like with what the pattern assigns them. Suppose a patterned distribution D1 has been reached in which everyone holds an equal amount of money. Wilt Chamberlain is an outstanding basketball player, and a large number of fans are each willing to pay an extra 25 cents at the gate specifically to watch him play, with that money going directly to him. Over a season, a million fans do this, so Wilt ends up with $250,000 -- far more than anyone else -- and the resulting distribution D2 no longer fits the original pattern. Since each fan was entitled to their 25 cents under D1 and each gave it up voluntarily, Nozick concludes that D2 is just: Wilt has earned this money through his talent and his effort and thus deserves the proceeds, so confiscating it to restore D1 would violate his right to what he deserves. From this, Nozick concludes that liberty upsets patterns: any attempt to keep a distribution patterned requires either continually blocking voluntary transfers or continually redistributing after the fact, and either policy amounts to the same kind of ongoing interference in people's lives as forcing Wilt to spend part of his season working for others without pay. This is why Nozick holds that no patterned or end-state principle, including the difference principle, can be sustained without unacceptable and continuous violations of liberty."
