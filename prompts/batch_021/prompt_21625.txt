I am writing a courtroom drama whose defense attorney wins over a skeptical jury not by contesting the evidence but by telling one vivid, entirely truthful story about her client's childhood, quietly setting aside the drier statistical case for reasonable doubt she has also prepared. An early reader insists this makes her a manipulator; I have been arguing it is simply good advocacy, but I would like to test that instinct against the philosophical literature. The standard view locates the wrongness of manipulation in its bypassing the target's rational agency rather than engaging it, yet rhetoricians since Aristotle have treated the deliberate arousal of emotion as a legitimate, even indispensable, tool of persuasion rather than a corruption of it. Is there a principled way of saying when a truthful but emotionally engineered appeal like my attorney's tips over into manipulation, or does the "bypassing rational agency" criterion simply break down for this entire category of cases?
