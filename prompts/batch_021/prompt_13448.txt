Odd request coming out of a lunchtime argument with a colleague, but here goes. I'm rebuilding the natural theology unit for my Phil of Religion course and had planned to present Craig's kalam argument and the "argument from contingency" (Leibniz's version mainly, with a nod to Aquinas's Third Way and Samuel Clarke) as basically two flavors of the same underlying move: infinite regresses are impossible, so there has to be a first or necessary member of the chain, call it God. My colleague pushed back that they're not just similar, they're basically the same argument at different stages of maturity — Craig just gets to cite the Big Bang for the premise that the universe began to exist, whereas Leibniz was stuck arguing philosophically that an infinite regress of causes can't be the whole explanation, so really both arguments live or die on whether there was a first member of the causal chain, and kalam just has better evidence for that shared premise.

That explanation didnt sit right with me, but its been a while since I taught this material carefully and I couldn't articulate the objection cleanly on the spot, which was mildly embarrassing in front of a room of adjuncts. My hazy memory is that the contingency argument is specifically built so that it doesn't matter whether the universe had a beginning at all — that it's supposed to go through even under the assumption of a past-eternal universe, because the puzzle it's targeting is why anything contingent exists right now, not why the causal chain started, whereas that same assumption (a past-eternal universe) would straightforwardly sink the kalam argument. Is that actually right? And if so, can you spell out precisely why an infinite past is compatible with, or maybe even irrelevant to, the logic of the contingency argument but fatal to kalam — precisely enough that I can stop my students from collapsing every cosmological argument into "everything needs a cause, therefore God" on the midterm?
