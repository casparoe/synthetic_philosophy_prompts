At dinner, two friends of mine got into an argument after reading a popular explainer claiming that a proof of 'P = NP' would make human creativity more or less obsolete, since finding a great proof, poem, or invention would become just as easy as recognizing one once you had it in front of you.

(1) Elena defended the claim: since 'easy to check' is essentially what it means for a problem to be in NP, a constructive proof that P = NP would give us an efficient way to generate strong candidate proofs, poems, and inventions, not merely to evaluate ones we already have in hand.

(2) Marco objected that this overstates matters: even a correct proof that P = NP need not yield a usable algorithm, since the polynomial-time procedure it guarantees could carry such an enormous exponent or constant that it would be useless on any input a person could ever care about, leaving actual creative practice untouched.

(3) Elena replied that this misses the real point: even a purely non-constructive, practically worthless proof would still show that 'finding' and 'checking' are the same kind of task, differing only in degree rather than in kind, and that fact alone is what should trouble anyone who thinks creativity is special.

(4) Marco answered that the trouble starts earlier than that: nobody has shown that judging a poem or proof 'great' is even the sort of yes-or-no question with a short, efficiently checkable certificate that P vs NP is about in the first place, so the whole comparison quietly assumes that creative judgment can be formalized that way.
