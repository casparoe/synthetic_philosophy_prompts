I have been working my way through the more obscure political writings of famous analytic philosophers for a feature on thinkers who stepped well outside their usual technical territory, and one of the stranger finds has been Michael Dummett's short 2001 book On Immigration and Refugees: a polemical, activist tract from someone otherwise known for his work on Frege and the realism/anti-realism debate, written after years spent campaigning against Britain's immigration laws. A good part of his case, as I read it, turns on rebutting Michael Walzer's well-known argument in Spheres of Justice, where Walzer treats a political community's right to decide who may join it as continuous with a club's or a family's right to choose its own members, and treats that power of closure not as something requiring justification from some prior principle of justice, but as the very precondition that makes domestic distributive justice possible in the first place.

I would like you to reconstruct and compare these two positions in some depth, since a quick read makes them look like they barely engage with one another. Lay out, as precisely as you can: (a) why Walzer thinks the right to control membership has to be treated as basic rather than derived, and (b) Dummett's central objections to that view, including the concession he appears to make that a state may still regulate the pace of immigration for practical reasons such as housing or infrastructure. Then give me your own verdict, not merely a summary of the disagreement: does Dummett's objection actually defeat Walzer's argument, or does Dummett's own position, once that concession is factored in, collapse into a more grudging version of the very communitarian picture he is attacking? And does separating refugees from ordinary economic migrants rescue either author's framework, or does that distinction create fresh problems for both? I am not looking for a rehearsal of the generic open-borders-versus-closed-borders debate; I want an assessment of how these two specific arguments hold up against one another.
