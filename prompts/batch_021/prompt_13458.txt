Since retiring after a forty-year career in experimental physics, I have turned to philosophy, and I recently ran into a move in the literature on perception that puzzles me. The argument from illusion turns on the fact that a veridical perception and a subjectively matching illusion or hallucination are indistinguishable from the inside; disjunctivists resist the usual inference to some common underlying kind of mental state, insisting instead that the veridical and non-veridical cases are, at bottom, fundamentally different in kind. But if such cases are introspectively indiscriminable, and could in principle even be produced by identical proximate neural activity (imagine cortical stimulation engineered to duplicate exactly the brain state underlying some genuine perception), then it is hard to see what could even in principle count as evidence for or against the claimed difference in kind. Doesn't this make the disjunctivist's distinction unfalsifiable by construction — a stipulative device for shielding direct realism from the sense-datum theorist's argument, rather than a substantive discovery about the nature of experience?
