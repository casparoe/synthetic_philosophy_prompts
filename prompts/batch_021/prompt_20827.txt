Since retiring three years ago after a career as an experimental condensed-matter physicist, I have been working my way through parts of the philosophical literature for which I never found the leisure while I was still running a lab. A former colleague, now working in decision theory, recently pointed me toward Robert Aumann's 1976 paper "Agreeing to Disagree," and I have spent the last several weeks going through it alongside some of the surrounding literature on common knowledge. The theorem, and the "meet"-of-partitions construction behind it, strike me as one of the more elegant short arguments I have come across outside physics, but the longer I sit with it, the less sure I am what it actually establishes about real disagreement, as opposed to what it establishes about a very particular and rather narrow epistemic setup.

What I cannot quite make peace with is this. Given a common prior, the theorem forces two agents' posterior probabilities for the same event to coincide once those posteriors are common knowledge — yet nowhere in the proof do the agents need to learn, or need it become common knowledge, what private signal or evidence actually generated the other's number; only the bare posterior value itself has to be common knowledge. All the work seems to be done by an averaging argument over a single cell of the meet partition, applied to two numbers that have already absorbed, for the purposes of that one event, everything relevant about each agent's private information. This has the feel of getting a substantive conclusion for free. I suspect my unease is tracking something real about what "common knowledge of a posterior" quietly builds in that mere common knowledge of the other's raw evidence would not automatically provide. I would be grateful if you could explain, with as much of the formal machinery as is needed to be precise but pitched for someone comfortable with probability and new to epistemic logic, why bare common knowledge of the two numbers already suffices to force agreement — and, in doing so, tell me whether this is best read as revealing something philosophically modest (a fact about an extremely demanding and rarely realized epistemic state) or as a genuinely substantive constraint on rational disagreement among real people who merely announce their opinions to one another.

As a closely related matter, I would also welcome your view on how much of the theorem's bite is really being supplied by the common-prior assumption rather than by the common-knowledge apparatus itself. Is there any principled, non-circular way of telling apart, from the outside, two people who share a prior and differ only in the information they have received, so that the theorem applies to them, from two people who simply started out with different priors, so that it does not? Or does that distinction end up doing most of the philosophical work that popular summaries of the theorem usually credit to rationality and common knowledge alone?
