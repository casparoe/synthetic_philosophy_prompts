A friend and I spent most of a long train ride arguing about Fitch's Paradox of Knowability, which she had just encountered in a philosophy of language seminar and was eager to explain to me. She laid out the proof carefully: define a proposition as "knowable" if it is possibly known by someone at some time; assume, as many anti-realists about truth want to, that every truth is knowable in this sense; and add two very modest-looking principles, that knowledge entails truth and that knowing a conjunction requires knowing each of its conjuncts. From just this much, she showed, it follows that if even a single truth happens to be one that nobody actually knows, a contradiction results -- so the seemingly modest thesis that every truth is knowable collapses into the absurd claim that every truth is already known.

She was captivated by this and drew a much stronger moral from it: that the theorem gives us a rigorous, wholly non-empirical demonstration that reality must contain specific, substantive facts -- she kept returning to the intrinsic nature of consciousness, and to what the physical world is "really like" independently of any observer -- that are not merely unknown to us for now but are, in principle, forever beyond the grasp of any inquirer, however idealized. In her view this is categorically different from ordinary scientific humility about hard, open problems, precisely because it would not be a hunch but something proven. I have no training in logic and could not quite articulate what felt wrong about that last step, though something did, and we never settled it. Is she right that Fitch's result licenses this kind of targeted pessimism about which of our open questions might be permanently unanswerable, or is she reading into the theorem a conclusion that the proof itself does not actually support? I would like you to reconstruct the argument closely enough to say exactly where, if at all, her gloss outruns what is demonstrated.
