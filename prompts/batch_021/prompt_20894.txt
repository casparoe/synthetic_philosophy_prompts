So this happened at a department dinner a few weeks back after we hosted a job talk, and I've been chewing on it since. Two people at the table -- Alina, who works on normative ethics, and Ben, who's more of a formal-logic guy -- got into it about whether "genuine" moral dilemmas (situations where someone's really obligated to do two things they can't both do) are even a coherent possibility, or whether that's just confused talk for "hard choice." Alina made up this case on the spot: a volunteer paramedic in a rural county with exactly one ambulance gets two calls at once -- a teenager from a car wreck on one side of town, an old man having a heart attack on the other -- and there's no way to reach both in time. She goes with the teenager (slightly better odds), the old man dies alone, everyone at the table agreed she made the right call, but apparently she's never stopped feeling guilty specifically about the old man, like she owed him something she didn't pay.

Here's roughly how the argument went (I wrote it up after, so it's paraphrased but I think fair to both sides): (1) Alina said this is exactly what a genuine dilemma looks like -- she was really obligated to both patients, couldn't discharge both obligations, and the fact that lasting guilt is the fitting reaction here (not just some emotional glitch) is basically what "moral residue" means. (2) Ben said no -- since she literally couldn't save both, and ought implies can, she can't have been under two live, real obligations right up to the end; at most one of them was still "on" once the conflict became unavoidable, and the guilt over the other patient is just leftover feeling, not proof anything real went undischarged. (3) Alina came back that Ben's move only works if "ought" agglomerates -- if "she ought to help the teenager" plus "she ought to help the old man" together entail "she ought to help both" -- and that's exactly the principle people who beleive in genuine dilemmas deny, so he can't use it to disprove dilemmas without just assuming his conclusion. (4) Ben said fine, but you don't get to drop agglomeration only in the cases that are inconvenient for you -- if it fails here there'd better be some principled story about why, or "genuine dilemma" just becomes a label for "conflict that still feels bad afterward," which surely isnt what either of them means by the term.

I couldn't referee this in the moment and I'm still not sure who came out ahead. Could you go through moves 1 through 4 and, for each one, say whether it's basically right, basically wrong, or right about something while missing something else -- and then tell me what, if anything, both of them are overlooking that would actually help settle (or dissolve) the dispute? I'm less interested in a final "Alina wins" / "Ben wins" than in the move-by-move stuff, though a one-line overall take at the end is fine too.
