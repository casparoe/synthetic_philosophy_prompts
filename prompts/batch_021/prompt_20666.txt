I've been reading around the intellectual prehistory of existential-risk thinking, and I keep returning to John von Neumann's 1955 essay for Fortune magazine, "Can We Survive Technology?" What strikes me about the piece is that his worry is not really about any single weapon or invention. It is about the shrinking interval between major technological upheavals, which he thought was outrunning any society's capacity to develop the political and institutional maturity each upheaval demands. He is also unusually blunt about there being no stable endpoint to this problem: a treaty, an inspection regime, or a destroyed prototype can buy time, but nothing can retire the underlying danger for good, since a piece of technical knowledge, once it exists, cannot be made to un-exist.

The example he reaches for is not the hydrogen bomb but something that now reads as startlingly prescient: the deliberate modification of climate and weather, which he thought might be achievable at relatively modest cost and would be far harder to detect, attribute, or negotiate over than a nuclear test. This same cast of mind is often linked to a recollection by his collaborator Stanislaw Ulam, who in a memorial piece written shortly after von Neumann's death described him musing about a rate of technological change heading toward a kind of essential "singularity" -- reportedly the first use of that word in anything like its present sense.

I'd like you to write a short story of roughly 1,000 to 2,000 words that takes this specific diagnosis seriously, rather than reaching for the more familiar "our inventions turn on us" plot. You might set it in the 1950s, with a von-Neumann-like advisor trying to make a skeptical committee grasp why no policy will finally settle the matter; or in a near future in which climate control has actually become a usable weapon and someone has to decide what, if anything, can still be done about it; or at any other point along that same trajectory that appeals to you. What matters to me is that the story sits with the specific bind von Neumann describes -- not that catastrophe is inevitable, but that safety is never more than provisional -- so that whoever holds real power in the story has to act without the comfort of a final fix.
