I sit on my university's Graduate Emergency Fund committee as the student representative, and a philosophy seminar I'm taking this term on practical rationality keeps coming to mind, since this dispute is exactly the kind of case where being predictably willing to do something produces a better outcome than actually being free to decide once the moment has passed. The Fund wires same-day, no-collateral cash to graduate students hit by a crisis abroad -- a robbery, a missed last flight -- on nothing but a short phone interview and a signed pledge to repay within six months, since ordinary loan paperwork is too slow for what the Fund exists to handle. Repayment has always been voluntary, resting on our caseworker's read of who will actually follow through once they're safely home, but repayment has collapsed this year, and the comptroller now wants every future advance conditioned on a promissory note enforceable through a transcript hold. Other committee members say this defeats the Fund's purpose: a note takes days to arrange and cannot help someone who needs cash tonight, and future applicants shouldn't be constrained by other recipients' defaults. Please draft the memo I'll bring to next week's vote: recommend one concrete policy going forward, including how and when to review it, and give the comptroller's proposal as serious a hearing as its critics get, regardless of which way the memo comes down.
