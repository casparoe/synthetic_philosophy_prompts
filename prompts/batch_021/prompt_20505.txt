I went down a bit of an AI-safety/game-theory rabbit hole recently and kept running into this idea people call the "commitment races problem." The rough version (correct me if I'm garbling it) is: if agents can make credible, irrevocable commitments before they actually sit down to bargain — Schelling's old point that burning your bridges can be a winning move, since now the other side just has to accommodate you — then everyone has a huge incentive to commit as early and as aggressively as possible, before the other side locks something in first. That's great if your the only one playing that game, but if both sides reason this way (and reason about the other side reasoning this way, and so on) you can end up racing each other into mutually locked-in demands that leave everyone worse off than if they'd both just waited a bit and talked first. Kind of reminds me of the doomsday-machine gag in Dr. Strangelove, except instead of one secret device you've got two competing ones getting rushed into production.

I mentioned this to a friend who's way more into decision theory than me, and she wasn't convinced this is a genuine dilemma rather than just a description of bad bargaining — her intuition is that a sufficiently smart agent just wouldnt fall for the temptation to commit early. So now I want to see the actual disagreement played out rather than just gesture at both sides in the abstract. Write a dialogue between two extremely sophisticated bargaining agents (make them AIs, aliens, idealized rational actors, whatever framing you find cleanest) in the moments right before they open negotiations with each other for the very first time. One of them is dead set on immediately sending an irrevocable, maximally aggressive commitment before the other can preempt them; the other has thought hard about the commitment-race dynamic and is trying to talk them out of it. Don't let either one win by default — the aggressive one needs real comebacks to the "but what if we both do this" worry, and the cautious one needs to offer an actual alternative, not just "let's not." I want them actually responding to each other's specific points rather than talking past one another.
