I'm a junior at my high school, where I serve on our Student Government Association's Bylaws and Elections Committee. Our SGA runs on a fairly formal written charter and something close to Robert's Rules of Order, so committee recommendations actually get debated and voted on by the full Senate rather than rubber-stamped, which is why I've been asked to draft an actual memo before our meeting this Thursday.

The situation is this: our outgoing SGA president wants her final act in office to be an amendment she's calling the Informed Voter Initiative. Under it, every student keeps exactly the voting rights they have now, but any student who attends all three candidate forums and passes a short, fact-based quiz on the candidates' actual platforms (not their slogans or social media presence) gets a second vote added to their tally in SGA elections and referenda. She got the idea partly from an excerpt of John Stuart Mill arguing for what he called plural voting that we read in AP Government, and partly from her frustration that our last major referendum, on the vending machine contract, seemed to get decided by whichever side had the funnier poster campaign rather than by the actual terms either side was proposing. Our faculty advisor, who studied political theory before she started teaching, mentioned that this is essentially a small-scale version of what political philosophers call epistocracy, and that whether anything like it can ever be justified is a genuinely live and unresolved debate rather than something with an obvious answer. Roughly half the committee thinks this is a clever, low-stakes way to reward students for actually being informed, since nobody loses the vote they already have. The other half thinks it's elitist no matter how it's packaged, since attending three after-school forums is much easier for students without a job, a sports practice, or a late bus to catch, and since it effectively tells some of our classmates that their political judgment counts for less than other students'. One committee member who's currently taking AP Statistics also brought up something she called Condorcet's jury theorem, which she thought might actually cut against the amendment even for someone who cares only about the group reaching the objectively better decision and not about fairness at all -- though none of us could reconstruct her reasoning well enough to know if she's right.

Could you draft the memo I'll bring to Thursday's meeting? It should give the committee a clear recommendation -- adopt the amendment, reject it, or adopt some specific modified version of it -- along with a realistic timeline, since spring elections are six weeks out and any change to how ballots are counted needs to be finalized and explained to the student body well before then. Please make sure the memo genuinely engages with the strongest argument on whichever side it doesn't end up recommending, instead of raising it only to wave it away, and please also address directly whether my classmate's instinct about Condorcet's jury theorem actually holds up here.
