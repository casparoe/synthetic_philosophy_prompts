So I spent my career running an insect physiology lab, and now that I'm retired I've been sitting in on a philosophy of mind reading group at the university a couple evenings a month, mostly cause everyone my age plays golf and I'd rather argue about qualia. Two of the regulars got into a proper row a few weeks back over whether honeybees feel pain, which as far as I can tell is really a proxy fight over where conscious experience starts on the tree of life. Afterward I wrote down the gist of it because I wanted to work out who actually had the better of it.

(1) Meg, who still runs a bee lab, opened by pointing out that injured bees show exactly the pattern we'd accept as good behavioral evidence of pain in a mammal: they trade off getting at a good reward against avoiding a noxious stimulus, they guard an injured leg and stop using it, and theres that old finding that agitated bees get more "pessimistic" on an ambiguous-cue judgment task afterward, like a mild insect version of anxious affect. (2) Dave, a postdoc who works on animal minds, said none of that requires anything to be felt -- you can get all of it out of layered nociceptive reflexes with local modulation, and without something doing the job a thalamocortical global workspace does for us, there's no stage for that information to be broadcast onto, so it stays sub-personal, the way a withdrawal reflex does in a decorticate animal. (3) Meg answered that his "workspace" requirement was reverse-engineered from the one species we're already sure about, so of course bees fail it, and that Ginsburg and Jablonka's unlimited-associative-learning marker (UAL) is a better-motivated line, and bees clearly pass that one. (4) Dave came back that some architectural constraint has to do real work, or the same style of argument crowns anything that shows negative feedback and trade-offs, including root systems and thermostats, so function alone can't be enough. (5) Meg then went to precaution: given how lopsided the moral costs are between wrongly hurting something sentient and being needlessly careful with something that isn't, a marker like UAL should carry real weight even without certainty, and Dave's insistence on brainlike wiring is just favoritism for things built the way we are. (6) Dave's last word was that precaution can't be a free pass either -- pushed far enough it commits you to expensive conclusions on pretty thin evidence, and "the asymmetry favors caution" doesn't say how much evidence should be enough to trigger it, which is exactly the line Meg still needs if she wants to stop short of pitying the thermostat too.

Neither one budged, and I dont trust my own read on this anymore since I only really know the biology, not the philosophy of mind.
