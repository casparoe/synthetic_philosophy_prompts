Me and a coworker got into a whole lunch argument about the "whose values" problem for AI alignment and I want an actual verdict out of this, not a survey of opinions. Basically, a bunch of the alignment writeups (Anthropic's constitution posts, OpenAI's model spec, that kind of thing) talk about wanting the model to stay neutral on contested moral/political/religious stuff instead of baking in the company's own views, kind of like how a liberal state is supposed to stay neutral between different "comprehensive doctrines" rather then officially endorsing one religion or moral code (had to look that term up, pretty sure its Rawls). Except apparently even Rawls only promises neutrality of aim, not neutrality of effect, since he admits that whatever rules you pick, some ways of life are going to flourish under them and others are going to wither. That seems like an even bigger problem for AI companies than for a state, because literally every RLHF reward signal, every hardcoded refusal, every "as an AI I don't hold opinions on X" dodge is already a substantive value call baked into the weights -- there's no neutral default config sitting there waiting to be discovered.

And you can't just dodge the whole mess by aggregating what people actually want instead (majority vote of users, population-weighted preferences, whatever), because doesn't that run into the same kind of wall Arrow's impossibility theorem puts up for voting systems -- like a CAP theorem for preference aggregation, where you can't get everything you'd want (respects everyone's input, no dictator, consistent rankings, etc) at the same time? I vaguely remember Anthropic actually tried crowdsourcing public input for one version of Claude's constitution a while back, which is a real attempt at taking the aggregation route seriously, but I don't know if that gets around the theoretical problem or just relocates it a level up, since now the fight is over who designs the survey, how the questions get worded, and who counts as a legitimate stakeholder.

So here's the actual ask: write an essay that lays out the strongest case for treating liberal, Rawls-style neutrality as the right ideal for an aligned AI to aim at, the strongest case that this is incoherent or flatly impossible given how these systems actually get built (not just "hard in practice"), and how the aggregation alternative fares given its own impossibility problems -- then actually pick a side instead of landing on "both raise good points." If you conclude something like "aim-neutrality is still coherent and worth keeping as a regulative ideal even though effect-neutrality is a lost cause," or "no, companies should just openly own a substantive value stance and argue for it on the merits," or some other specific verdict entirely, great -- just commit to one and defend it properly.
