Daniel Bernoulli's 1738 resolution of the St. Petersburg paradox proposed that a rational agent should evaluate a risky prospect by its "moral expectation" -- what we would now call expected utility -- rather than by its expected monetary value, and that this utility grows logarithmically with wealth because of diminishing marginal utility. Two centuries later, John Kelly's criterion arrived at the same logarithmic objective from a different direction: as the bet size that maximizes the long-run geometric growth rate of wealth under repeated, independent wagers. This convergence has become the focus of a real dispute: Paul Samuelson argued -- in one attack written entirely in one-syllable words -- that treating log-utility, or "Kelly betting," as uniquely rational for everyone, rather than as merely one utility function among many, illegitimately smuggles a repeated-game growth argument into what should be a question about individual risk preference; Ole Peters's more recent "ergodicity economics," by contrast, claims that Bernoulli's original argument was already implicitly a time-average growth argument, and that mainstream expected-utility theory -- including Samuelson's version of it -- has misread Bernoulli ever since as fundamentally being about risk aversion rather than about the non-ergodicity of multiplicative wealth dynamics.

Based on the actual structure and stated motivation of Bernoulli's own argument, rather than on these later reconstructions of it, which side of this dispute do you think Bernoulli himself would have found more persuasive, and why? Please ground your answer in specifics of his 1738 reasoning -- for instance, whether he was analyzing a one-off gamble or repeated play, and what work the diminishing-marginal-utility assumption is doing for him that a pure growth-rate argument would not need.
