Okay so I've been on a bit of a decision theory kick lately (my older brother sent me a video on Newcomb's problem a few weeks ago and now I can't stop) and that's how I found this older puzzle called "Death in Damascus" from a Gibbard and Harper paper. The setup: a guy runs into Death in Damascus, Death tells him "I am coming for you tomorrow," so the guy panics and sneaks off to Aleppo overnight -- except it turns out Death already had an appointment with him in Aleppo, totally independent of the whole Damascus encounter. Most retellings just leave it as a spooky "you can't escape fate" story, but going down the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy rabbit hole, I learned the actual decision-theory problem is way weirder than that: if Death is basically a perfect predictor of where you'll end up, then under evidential decision theory, whichever city you currently intend to go to becomes evidence Death will be there, so you should switch -- except the second you switch, the new city looks just as dangerous for the exact same reason, so you'd switch back, forever. Apparently this means neither option is "ratifiable," which basically means there's no choice that still looks best to you once you've actually committed to it.

What I want from you is a brand new example with that exact same structure -- no ratifiable act, endless flip-flopping under evidential decision theory -- but set somewhere completely ordinary, with zero death, fate, or supernatural stuff involved. Something about school, sports, money, whatever, you pick. It needs some kind of predictor or signal thats correlated with which option you go with (not literal omniscient Death, obviously), two choices that are otherwise identical in payoff, and the property that committing to either one is exactly what makes evidential decision theory recommend the other one instead. If you can, throw in a sentence on why causal decision theory doesn't get stuck in the same loop in your example. Please don't just hand me Newcomb's problem with the names swapped out, I've already seen that one -- I want something that genuinely has this flip-flop/non-ratifiability thing going on, not just "there's a predictor, spooky."
