I spent 40 years as an entomologist before retiring, and I've somehow ended up on my old professional society's common names committee -- the one that renamed the gypsy moth to the spongy moth a while back because "gypsy" is a slur for the Roma. We've now got a backlog of other species whose common names echo slurs for other groups, and the committee is deadlocked: half want a blanket rule (any word that's ever a slur for a human group gets pulled from a species name, no exceptions), the other half says thats overreach since nobody's calling an actual person the word, its just a label on a bug or a weed. Since retiring I've been reading philosophy of language for fun, and I think the real question buried in here is whether a slur's ugly meaning is baked into the word itself, so it travels along no matter what it gets attached to, or whether the ugliness is really about what a speaker's doing when they aim the word at a person -- in which case a moth might not even be the kind of thing the word could target. Could you draft the memo I'll bring to next month's meeting? It needs to land on an actual rule the committee could vote on, not "use your judgement," and it has to take the "it's just a bug, let it go" camp seriously instead of steamrolling them.
