Benjamin Franklin's attempt at what he called "moral perfection," recounted in Part Two of his Autobiography, keeps resurfacing on the rationalist blogs and forums I follow, almost always cited as an early ancestor of the modern habit tracker: the list of thirteen named virtues, each reduced to a short operational precept, the little book with a page per virtue and a grid of days on which lapses are marked as black spots, and the weekly rotation that gives one virtue close attention while the rest are left to take their ordinary course. A friend and I got into an argument recently about whether this scheme is really doing the same thing as the much older theory of habituation it seems to be gesturing at, namely Aristotle's in the Nicomachean Ethics — the claim that a stable virtuous character is built up gradually through repeated practice, the way one becomes a builder by building, rather than installed all at once by resolution or insight. Neither of us could settle it, so I'd like a more careful answer than we managed between us.

I'd like you to compare Franklin's method, and the conception of virtue behind it, with Aristotle's. The two seem to agree on the basic mechanism — practice over sudden resolution — but to disagree about nearly everything surrounding it. Franklin selects and orders his thirteen virtues by their usefulness to him (mastering Order, for instance, is meant to free up the time and attention needed for the rest of the project), frames the whole enterprise as instrumental to something like worldly and personal felicity, and has nothing resembling Aristotle's doctrine of the mean or the role of phronesis in judging, case by case, what a given virtue actually calls for in a given situation. He also, by his own admission, never quite gets there: he reports giving up on fully mastering Order in much the way a man who sets out to grind an axe perfectly bright eventually stops and decides he likes it well enough still speckled. Aristotle's virtuous person, by contrast, is not merely useful to himself or others as a means to some further end; the exercise of virtue is supposed to be partly constitutive of eudaimonia itself, and practical wisdom is built into the very definition of a virtuous act rather than omitted from the scheme entirely.

So: is Franklin's project best read as a genuine, if homespun, instance of Aristotelian habituation — or does the missing machinery (the mean relative to us, phronesis, virtue as partly constitutive of the good life rather than merely a means to it) make it a different kind of enterprise altogether, closer to a checklist-driven self-improvement regimen than to anything Aristotle would recognize as moral education? I'd like your own considered verdict on which of the two gives the more defensible account of how a virtuous character actually gets built, together with your reasons for it, rather than an even-handed summary that declines to choose.
