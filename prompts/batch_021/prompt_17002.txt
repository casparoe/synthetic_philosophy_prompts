For my AP Research project this year, I've been reading about mechanistic interpretability in large language models, and I keep getting stuck on a disagreement that the papers themselves never quite settle. On one side, researchers at labs like Anthropic have used sparse autoencoders to extract individual "features" from inside models like Claude — most famously, a direction in the network's activations that tracked the Golden Gate Bridge so reliably that artificially amplifying it made the model steer almost every answer back toward the bridge. Work like this is usually described as literally discovering what a model's internal representations are, the same way a neuroscientist might discover that a real neuron responds to edges in an image. On the other side, I've read skeptics who argue that "features" and "circuits" are really just a convenient vocabulary we invent to compress an enormous, alien matrix of numbers into something human-sized, and that changing your analysis method can make a representation appear or disappear — for instance, the board-state representation inside Othello-GPT reportedly only becomes linearly readable once researchers swap their probe from tracking "black versus white" to tracking "my pieces versus my opponent's." That leaves me unsure whether the representation was really in the network all along, or whether it only exists relative to whichever probe a researcher happens to pick.

When I brought this up with my philosophy teacher, she said it sounded a lot like the older scientific realism debate over whether unobservable posits like electrons or genes are genuinely out there or are just convenient fictions that happen to make our theories predictively successful. I'd like to understand that comparison properly: could you compare the realism debate over neural network representations to the general scientific realism debate in philosophy of science, addressing both where the analogy holds and where it breaks down? I'm especially curious whether moves like the "no miracles" argument and worries about underdetermination carry over cleanly to the interpretability case, whether Dennett's notion of "real patterns" offers a good middle path between the two camps, and whether there is something about neural networks specifically, as opposed to electrons, that makes the question of whether their representations are "real" easier or harder to settle.
