In a graduate seminar on practical reason I am teaching this term, we spent a session and a half on Bernard Williams's "Internal and External Reasons," and I came away wanting a sharper treatment of one thread in it than either the assigned secondary readings or my students' seminar papers managed to give. Williams holds that a statement of the form "A has a reason to X" is true, in his internalist sense, only if A could arrive at a motivation to X by a sound deliberative route from her existing subjective motivational set S — her actual desires, projects, and evaluations, as refined by correct information, the removal of error, and a fuller appreciation of what her existing projects actually involve. An external reason statement claims something stronger, which Williams thinks turns out to be unintelligible on inspection: that A has reason to X even though nothing reachable from S by any sound deliberative route would move her to it. He treats the resulting picture — that an agent with nothing in S bearing on others' interests may simply have no reason, and not merely no inclination, to spare them cruelty, a situation to be managed through education, sanction, and sentiment rather than through insisting he has a reason he cannot recognize — as basically correct, if unwelcome.

Two objections to this picture deserve to be met head-on rather than gestured at. First, the "sound deliberative route" clause has to carry a great deal of weight, since Williams allows deliberation to include vivid imaginative rehearsal of consequences, new information, and reflection on what one's existing commitments actually entail; this invites the worry that the clause is elastic enough to smuggle almost any consideration back in as "internal" after all, so that the internal/external distinction stops doing real philosophical work. John McDowell's response goes the opposite way: he denies that a virtuous agent's perception of what a situation calls for is simply an extension of whatever was already latent, if unexercised, in her prior motivational set, and treats this as his route to insisting that external reasons remain intelligible after all. Second, internalism seems to leave morality's categorical pretensions — the thought that the callous man has an actual reason, not merely a socially inconvenient disposition, to stop being callous — looking like wishful thinking rather than a discovery, which is exactly what rationalists about practical reason, from Kant's heirs onward, have refused to concede.

Write an essay that states Williams's argument for internalism with enough precision that a reader could reconstruct the sound-deliberative-route clause unaided, weighs the two objections above against the strongest replies available to an internalist, and then defends a definite verdict on whether internal reasons exhaust the space of genuine practical reasons. A conditional verdict along the lines of "internalism survives only if the deliberative-route clause is read to permit/exclude such-and-such" is a perfectly acceptable way of committing, but the essay may not close by simply reporting that both sides have a point.
