So a few months back I sold a box of my old manuscript drafts — three novels' worth, complete with wine stains and a genuinely embarrassing number of crossed-out chapter titles — to a university archive, and the check they cut me was honestly kind of obscene compared to what I make per copy in royalties, for text that is, word for word, identical to what's sitting in every paperback on a shelf somewhere. I mentioned this at dinner to a friend doing a philosophy MA, and she pointed me toward Nelson Goodman's old "autographic" vs "allographic" distinction: paintings are autographic, meaning a perfect physical duplicate of a Vermeer is just a forgery, not a second genuine Vermeer (basically the whole Van Meegeren scandal), while texts and musical scores are allographic — any typo-free copy of my novel is just as "genuine" an instance of the work as my own coffee-ringed draft, no aura required. Goodman apparently goes even further and argues a forged painting can matter aesthetically even while its still perceptually indistinguishable from the real thing, because we might later train ourselves to see the difference.

Here's what's been bugging me since: if literature really is allographic in Goodman's sense, why does everybody treat things like the Hitler Diaries fiasco, or Mark Hofmann's forged Mormon documents, or Macpherson's fake "Ossian" poems, as genuine scandals instead of a shrug? The words on the page are what they are no matter who supposedly wrote them, so by Goodman's own logic nothing should be aesthetically lost once the real authorship comes out — and yet something obviously does feel lost. Maybe that's fine for Goodman, since these are cases of fraudulent misattribution, a seperate phenomenon his theory was never trying to explain, so his account comes through unscathed. Or maybe the reaction to literary hoaxes shows the autographic/allographic line doesn't actually do the work he wants it to, and a text's history of production can diminish it after all.

Could you write me an essay that actually picks a side here instead of hedging? Lay out the strongest version of Goodman's position (including his reasons for thinking undetectable authenticity can still matter), the strongest case that literary hoaxes are a genuine counterexample, and then tell me who wins and why — something like "Goodman's right about paintings but his framework just doesn't extend to texts" totally counts as a real verdict, as long as you actually defend it. And if there's room, throw in a paragraph on whether an AI trained to churn out a "new" novel in a dead writer's style is more like the Vermeer case or the Ossian one.
