I sit on the five-person steering committee of a roughly 150-member giving circle that grew out of my local effective altruism meetup. Each December, members submit ranked ballots over a shortlist of four or five cause areas (this year: AI safety, global health, farmed-animal welfare, and pandemic preparedness), and the tally decides how our pooled discretionary fund is split for the coming year. We've used instant-runoff voting since the circle's founding, mainly because it's what one of our founders happened to have read about, but this past December it produced a genuine embarrassment: pandemic preparedness picked up a late surge of first-preference support after a well-attended talk, and would have won outright under the previous count, but the extra ballots changed the order in which options were eliminated, and it lost instead. Enough of us have since read about Arrow's impossibility theorem -- mostly filtered through old LessWrong and EA Forum threads rather than Arrow's original 1951 book -- to recognize this as a structural feature rather than a fluke, and the committee is now split roughly down the middle. One faction wants to switch to a Condorcet-consistent method, such as ranked pairs or the Schulze method, or to approval voting; the other insists that since Arrow's theorem already proves no ranked system can simultaneously satisfy independence of irrelevant alternatives, the Pareto principle, and non-dictatorship, swapping methods just trades one arbitrary pathology for another, so we might as well keep instant-runoff, or even save ourselves the trouble and revert to plain plurality. A couple of the approval-voting advocates go further and claim they can dodge the theorem altogether, since approval and score voting take cardinal ratings rather than rankings as input; the other faction dismisses this as a rhetorical trick rather than a genuine escape.

I need to circulate a short decision memo before our January meeting, at which the committee will vote on a bylaws amendment fixing our method for future cycles. Could you draft it? It should recommend one specific mechanism -- or explicitly recommend keeping instant-runoff, if that turns out to be the right call -- together with a concrete timeline for adopting it, and it needs to take the "Arrow's theorem means it's all arbitrary" position seriously, steelmanning it rather than caricaturing it, and explaining exactly where that inference holds up and where it breaks down -- including the cardinal-versus-ordinal wrinkle -- rather than just gesturing at the theorem and picking a favorite.
