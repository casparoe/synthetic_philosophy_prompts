I sit on the incident review board for a small company that runs autonomous shuttle buses around a hospital campus and the retirement community next door - slow, golf-cart speeds, but full-size vehicles, and by regulation there's always a human "attendant" onboard who can grab the manual controls and override the automation. I landed on this board mostly because I'm in compliance and everyone else dodged the extra meetings, so fair warning, I'm not the engineer in the room.

Two weeks ago a jogger stepped out from between parked cars right as our shuttle came up on a crosswalk. The attendant grabbed the controls and braked hard, which stopped us in time but also threw a seated passenger forward hard enough that she needed a few stitches. Should've been a routine incident report, except when engineering pulled the logs they found the shuttle's own automatic emergency braking had independently spotted the jogger and was about 0.3 seconds from executing an identical braking maneuver when the attendant's manual input got there first. So: the exact same stop, at the exact same force, was happening no matter what he did - even if he'd gone easy on the controls it wouldn't have mattered, the automation would've slammed the brakes a beat later regardless, apparently that braking profile is fixed once it's triggered.

Now the board's split down the middle over how to write this up. Half want the report to not name the attendant as the cause of the injury, since, in there view, he didn't actually change what happened, so it feels off to hang it on his choice. The other half think that's a dodge dressed up as fairness - he made a real judgment call under real pressure, which is literally the job we pay him to do, and "the backup would've done it too" shouldn't get him off the hook. One of our engineers, who apparently minored in philosophy years ago, said this is basically a real thing philosophers argue about - something called a Frankfurt case, about whether you can be responsible for something you couldn't have avoided either way - and I've now spent way more of this week reading about that than sleeping. I don't want to just summarize the literature at the board on Wednesday, I want an actual recommendation. Could you draft the short memo I'll bring in: a concrete call on how we characterize his role in the incident report, whether and how we retrain him, and on what timeline, backed by reasons the "he made no difference" half of the board could actually engage with, rather than a memo that just asserts he acted freely so he's on the hook?
