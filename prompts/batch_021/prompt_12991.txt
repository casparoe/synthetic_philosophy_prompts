I sit on the governing council of my national philosophical association. After a bruising controversy over a disinvited speaker, a bloc of council members wants to amend our bylaws so that the association's newly adopted statement on open inquiry can be weakened or repealed only by a two-thirds vote of the full membership, not the simple council majority that adopted it -- an entrenchment clause of the kind constitutions use to shield provisions from ordinary repeal. They defend it in explicitly Homeric terms: like Ulysses ordering his crew to lash him to the mast, the council should tie its own hands now, while it can still think clearly, against a future council that might yield to momentary pressure. Opponents reply that this isn't really like Ulysses and the sirens, since there's no neutral standpoint from which the present council's judgment counts as the association's sober self and a future council's counts as mere weakness of will; indeed, they add, a proposal drafted in the heat of one episode may itself be the impulsive move such devices are meant to guard against. Please draft the memo I'll circulate to the council recommending whether to adopt, reject, or modify the proposal, specifying a threshold and any sunset or review clause you'd attach, and taking the "whose weakness of will is this, really" objection seriously rather than dismissing it.
