"Frankfurt's case is meant to refute PAP, the idea that you're only responsible for an act if you could have done otherwise. In the case, a neurologist named Black wants Jones to vote for the Democrat. Black has implanted a device in Jones's brain that can monitor what Jones is about to do; if Jones is about to vote Democrat anyway, the device does nothing, but if Jones started to show signs he was going to vote Republican instead, the device would kick in and force him to vote Democrat. As it happens, Jones decides all on his own to vote Democrat, so the device never has to activate. Frankfurt says Jones is just as responsible for voting Democrat as he'd be if Black weren't there at all, since the actual sequence of events is identical either way. But Jones couldn't have done otherwise, because if he'd tried, Black would've stepped in. So responsibility doesn't require alternative possibilities, and PAP is false.

Some philosophers weren't convinced and gave the 'flicker of freedom' reply. They pointed out there's still a tiny alternative possibility left: Jones could at least have shown the initial sign of wanting to vote Republican, even though he couldn't have gone on to actually vote Republican. So there's a "flicker" of freedom, a thin alternative, that could still be what grounds Jones's responsibility, so PAP survives in a watered-down form. Fischer's response is that this flicker isn't good enough to do the job, because Jones has no control over whether the sign happens - it's just an involuntary brain event, not something Jones chooses or recognizes as a reason. Since the alternative isn't "robust" in this way, it can't be what actually makes Jones responsible. This is why Fischer says what really grounds responsibility is regulative control: the actual mechanism producing the action has to be appropriately responsive to reasons, whether or not Jones could've done otherwise. That's the heart of semi-compatibilism: moral responsibility is compatible with determinism because regulative control is compatible with determinism, even if genuine alternative possibilities aren't.

Mele and Robb tried to make the Frankfurt case even stronger by getting rid of the flicker completely. In their version, instead of a device that watches for a sign and reacts, there are two separate deterministic processes running from the start - one is Jones's own, one is Black's backup - and a "buffer" keeps them from interacting until the same decision comes out at the end regardless of which process is doing the causing, so Jones's decision ends up overdetermined by both processes at once. Because neither process depends on reading a sign from Jones first, there's no flicker at all left for critics to point to, which was meant to make this an even cleaner counterexample to PAP than the original."
