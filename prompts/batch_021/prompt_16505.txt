Fitch's paradox shows that the knowability principle — the thesis that every truth is, in principle, knowable — combined with the modest assumption that at least one truth is never actually known, entails the absurd conclusion that every truth is known. This result threatens any semantic anti-realism that ties truth to our capacity to verify it. One well-known escape, due to Neil Tennant, restricts the knowability principle to what he calls "Cartesian" propositions: those not rendered false by the very act of being known, which excludes troublemakers like "p, and no one knows that p." Write a dialogue, set in the question period after a colloquium talk, between an anti-realist who defends Tennant's restriction and a realist who objects that it preserves the letter of the knowability principle only by abandoning its spirit, since carving out an exception for every proposition that generates trouble leaves no principled thesis behind. Each side should engage the other's specific moves, and the anti-realist should offer a substantive, non-circular rationale for the restriction rather than simply insisting that it is well-motivated.
