Question given to the student: In "The Criteria of Negro Art" (1926), Du Bois argues that "all art is propaganda and ever must be." Explain what he means by this, including how he connects beauty to truth and to "right." Then briefly contrast his view with Alain Locke's.

Student's answer: In "The Criteria of Negro Art," Du Bois's central claim is that art is never neutral -- whether or not the artist intends it, every work of art advances some picture of how the world is or should be, so Black artists should stop apologizing for using their work to advance racial justice and start doing it openly and well. He doesnt think this makes beauty unimportant: he explicitly links beauty to truth and to "right" (i.e. justice), arguing that art which is dishonest about Black life, or which flatters white readers' comfortable stereotypes, can't really be beautiful either, however polished. Since he treats "all art is propaganda" as basically a two-way street, it also follows for him that all propaganda -- a pamphlet, a poster, a piece of NAACP agitprop -- deserves to be judged by the same artistic standards as a novel or a painting, not dismissed as some lesser, non-art category.

Locke represents the opposing camp in this debate: an "art for art's sake" position holding that the artist's whole job is craft and beauty, and that pulling race or politics into the work at all can only cheapen it. Where Du Bois wants Black art to be frankly propagandistic, Locke wants it kept free of any social purpose whatsoever.
