I teach ancient philosophy, including a graduate seminar built around Aristotle's *Politics* and *Nicomachean Ethics*, and this year I am also the philosophy department's appointee to my university's Committee on Investor Responsibility, which advises the trustees on ethical questions raised by the endowment's holdings. At last month's meeting, the committee took up a proposal to divest a modest fixed-income position in a small-dollar consumer lender whose short-term loans carry effective annual rates well into the triple digits, on the grounds that its borrowers are drawn overwhelmingly from exactly the kind of financially precarious households our own financial-aid office exists to help.

I made what I intended as a substantive point rather than a rhetorical flourish. Aristotle, in distinguishing the natural use of money from chrematistike, the unlimited pursuit of wealth for its own sake, singles out lending at interest — tokos, a word that also means "offspring" — as the most unnatural species of acquisition, precisely because it treats money, which he elsewhere argues is nothing more than a conventional measure of need, as though it could breed the way a living thing does. A colleague from the business school replied that this objection, if it works at all, condemns every bond and every mortgage the endowment holds alongside the loan in question, since all of them are, in exactly Aristotle's sense, money breeding money; if the argument proves anything, she said, it proves far too much, and whatever is actually objectionable about this particular lender has to be locatable somewhere other than the bare charging of interest. I don't have a ready answer, and I will admit that even the Scholastics who inherited this argument eventually allowed charging for genuine risk of loss, which already looks like a concession of her point.

I have been asked to produce the memo the committee will vote on next week, and I would like a draft to work from before I write my own. Our charter allows exactly three recommendations: full divestment, continued holding with no special conditions, or a formal engagement process with named conditions and a deadline for revisiting the question. Please draft that memo. I want it to actually resolve, rather than diplomatically avoid, whether anything in Aristotle's argument — or in some more defensible descendant of it — survives my colleague's objection and can do real work distinguishing this lender from an ordinary bond, and I want it to end with one recommendation among the three and a concrete timeline.
