This term I am teaching a graduate seminar on democratic theory, and for the past few weeks we have been stuck on what political theorists, following Frederick Whelan, call "the boundary problem": the difficulty of specifying, in a way that is itself democratically justified, who counts as a member of the demos for a given decision. The problem is usually put as a regress. Any procedure for fixing the extension of "the people" — a referendum, a constitutional convention, an ordinary vote to extend the franchise — must already presuppose some bounded body of participants authorized to run that very procedure, so the boundaries of the demos can never themselves be the output of a democratic decision without circularity. Sofia Näsström has pushed this into the still more general claim that a people can never legitimately author its own constitution. Robert Goodin's well-known reply is the All-Affected-Interests (AAI) principle: everyone whose interests are affected by a decision has a claim to a say in it. Goodin's own view is that, applied consistently — counting indirect, probabilistic, and even merely possible effects — AAI licenses an almost unbounded, near-global franchise for most non-trivial decisions. He is prepared to embrace that conclusion, though several of my students think it is really a reductio of the principle rather than a solution to the boundary problem. The chief competitor is some version of an All-Subjected (or coercion) principle, on which only those actually subject to a jurisdiction's exercise of power are owed inclusion. Arash Abizadeh has turned exactly this principle against states' control of their own borders, arguing that since would-be immigrants are themselves subject to border enforcement, they too belong in the relevant justificatory audience — which seems to leave no bounded, self-authorizing demos able to fix its own membership simply by policing entry.

My seminar has more or less split three ways on how to react to all this, and rather than adjudicate it myself I would like an outside verdict. Write an essay that lays out the strongest form of the regress argument against any principled, non-circular way of fixing the demos, weighs whether AAI, the All-Subjected principle, or some third alternative actually escapes that regress rather than merely relocating it, and then commits to a definite conclusion — either that the boundary problem is genuinely insoluble in principled terms, or that it is solved, even if only partially, by one of these principles. Please do not retreat into "reasonable people disagree": tell me which position you think actually survives scrutiny, and meet the strongest objection to it head-on.
