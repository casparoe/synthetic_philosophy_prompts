I had a long, slightly heated argument with a friend last night about effective altruism after she brought up Peter Singer's drowning-child argument. Her position was that once you accept Singer's reasoning, the whole concept of "supererogation" — acts that are morally good but not required — basically collapses: whatever produces the most good is simply obligatory, and anything less than that is a moral failure, so extraordinary charitable giving (or any other "above and beyond" act) was never really optional in the first place, just misdescribed by common sense. I felt this was too quick but couldn't pin down exactly where the argument goes wrong. Is she right that taking utilitarian-style reasoning seriously means there's no real room left for supererogatory acts, or is there a way to preserve that category even if something like Singer's argument is basically sound?
