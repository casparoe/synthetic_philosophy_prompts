I am several drafts into a novel that braids together three timelines — a grandmother's girlhood, her middle age, and her granddaughter's present — and rather than leave the book's temporal architecture to intuition, I've spent much of the last month reading in the philosophy of time so that I understand what I'm actually committing myself to before I commit a few hundred pages to it. I mentioned my reading to a friend who did a philosophy degree years ago, and she seemed uneasy with where I'd landed but couldn't quite say why, which is partly why I'm bringing the question here.

Here is where I've landed. The debate over whether objects endure (persisting by being wholly present at every moment of their existence) or perdure (persisting as a series of temporal parts, the way a road is made of segments laid end to end) seems to me to just be the persistence theorist's way of restating the debate between presentism and eternalism. If, as I intend for this book, past, present, and future are all equally real — a block universe in which the grandmother's girlhood hasn't vanished but merely sits elsewhere on the timeline — then surely I'm already committed to perdurantism: to treating "the grandmother" as a four-dimensional whole of which the girl and the old woman are only different temporal parts, rather than as a single, wholly-present person who simply bore different properties at different times. Endurantism, on this reading, is really just what a presentist is entitled to believe, since only someone who thinks there is a single privileged "now" could sincerely say that an entire object is wholly present within it. The two debates, in other words, strike me as the same debate wearing two vocabularies.

Before I let that equivalence quietly organize the whole manuscript — including a subplot in which the older grandmother has to decide whether she can still feel guilty for something the girl did decades earlier, which seems to me to hinge on whether they are really the same "wholly present" person or merely two segments of one worm — I would like a sanity check. Have I understood the relationship between these two debates correctly, or am I collapsing two questions that philosophers of time are careful to keep separate?
