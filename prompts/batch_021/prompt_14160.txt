The question as set: "In their 1979 paper, Gould and Lewontin use the mosaics decorating the spandrels of the cathedral of San Marco in Venice to mount a critique of what they call the adaptationist programme. In 400–600 words: (a) explain the spandrel analogy and what it is meant to show about biological traits; (b) explain what the 'Panglossian paradigm' refers to and why they chose that label; (c) identify at least two of the non-adaptive alternatives to natural selection that they think working adaptationists neglect; and (d) briefly describe one substantive criticism that has been raised against their argument since 1979."

The student's answer, verbatim: "Gould and Lewontin open with an architectural example: the four spandrels beneath the dome of San Marco in Venice, the roughly triangular spaces that appear wherever two rounded arches meet at right angles to support a dome above them. Because Byzantine artists filled each spandrel with an elaborate mosaic of one of the four evangelists, it is tempting to think the space was designed specifically to hold that image, but the spandrel is really just a necessary architectural by-product of mounting a round dome on round arches: you cannot build the one without generating the other. Gould and Lewontin use this as an analogy for biological traits: some features of organisms are not themselves targets of selection but incidental by-products of how other, genuinely adaptive structures are built, and biologists risk inventing a plausible function for the by-product when it never had one at all. In a companion paper, they name such by-product traits 'exaptations,' to distinguish them sharply from adaptations proper.

They call the assumption that every trait must be adaptive the 'Panglossian paradigm,' after Voltaire's Dr. Pangloss in Candide, who is convinced that this is the best of all possible worlds and that every feature of it, down to the shape of a nose, must therefore serve some evident purpose. Gould and Lewontin think working biologists often reason the same way: because a trait exists, and organisms are so well put together overall, its existence is treated as sufficient evidence that natural selection built it for its current use, without asking whether some other process might explain it just as well.

Two of the alternatives they highlight are pleiotropy, in which a gene selected for one effect also produces an unselected side-effect elsewhere in the body, and developmental or architectural constraints, in which the pathway by which an embryo is built forecloses certain variations outright, so a trait can persist for structural rather than adaptive reasons. Both possibilities, they argue, are routinely skipped over in favor of an adaptive story.

One of the most substantive replies has come from Daniel Dennett, who argues in Darwin's Dangerous Idea that Gould overstates his case: serious adaptationists such as Maynard Smith and Dawkins already treat adaptive hypotheses as testable and revisable rather than simply assumed, so the 'Panglossian' target is something of a strawman. This sits alongside the older tautology problem that the spandrels paper itself endorses -- the charge that 'survival of the fittest' really just means 'survival of the survivors,' and so cannot be falsified -- and together the two amount to a serious case that natural selection is a considerably less important force in evolutionary change than the Modern Synthesis assumed."
