Working on a newsletter piece about antinatalism and fell down a bit of a rabbit hole with David Benatar's 'asymmetry' argument from Better Never to Have Been -- the one for why coming into existence is always a harm. Someone I talked to for the piece made a point that's stuck with me: the actual novel part of Benatar's argument isn't 'life contains suffering,' its the weirder claim that the absence of pain is good even when there's nobody around to enjoy that good, while the absence of pleasure is only bad if someone actually exists to be deprived of it. And a much more modest version of that same asymmetry -- we've got strong reason not to bring a kid into the world if it's going to be miserable, but no real obligation to bring a happy one into existence -- is something plenty of people already accept even if they think having kids is totally fine and aren't antinatalists in the slightest.

So can you spell out exactly how Benatar's version differs from that more basic, widely-shared one? Specifically: does his stronger conclusion -- that existence is never a net benefit, even for a genuinely happy person -- actually fall out of the same reasoning as the modest asymmetry, or is he sneaking in an extra assumption that's a lot easier to reject than the modest version is?
