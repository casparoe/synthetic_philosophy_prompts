For most of my working life I was a physical chemist, and I spent far more hours than I care to admit calibrating calorimeters and fretting over activation energies. Since retiring, I have been filling my mornings with philosophy of science, rather later in life than is probably advisable, and I recently found myself arguing about it with an old colleague from the lab who has taken up the history of science as a retirement project of her own. She thinks the long parade of abandoned theories -- phlogiston, caloric, the luminiferous ether, and so on -- ought to embarrass anyone who still calls themselves a scientific realist. I was not so sure, but I found I could not put the best realist reply into words nearly as well as I wanted to.

Reading further, I think the crux is this. The pessimistic meta-induction notes that many past theories were empirically successful in their day and yet turned out to be false in their central claims, and infers that our current best theories are probably headed for the same fate. The most serious realist response I have come across is not the bare "no miracles" move (roughly, that realism alone keeps the predictive success of science from looking like an accident) but the retreat to some selective or structural version of realism, of the kind associated with Worrall, Kitcher, and Psillos, which tries to save realism by restricting it to whichever parts of a discarded theory were doing the actual predictive work -- the standard example being Fresnel's equations, which survived essentially unchanged out of the discredited elastic-solid ether theory and into Maxwell's electrodynamics. What I have not seen satisfactorily answered is the obvious rejoinder: that we seem able to pick out the "working" posits only once we already know which later theory vindicated them, which makes the distinction look tailor-made after the fact rather than something that could have guided anyone's commitments in advance.

I would like you to write me a dialogue, of roughly 700 to 900 words, between two philosophers of science of equal skill and conviction: one a selective or structural realist, the other a critic committed to the pessimistic meta-induction. Set the scene as the two of them standing in front of a museum case (imagined, for present purposes) cataloguing great discarded theories of science, complete with a piece of apparatus for demonstrating phlogiston loss during combustion, a diagram of caloric fluid passing between bodies, and a small bottled sample labelled "luminiferous ether." Have the critic open by using the case as ammunition; have the realist go beyond the no-miracles argument and defend the selective view specifically via the Fresnel-to-Maxwell case; and have the critic press the hindsight objection sketched above as hard as it can be pressed, forcing the realist to genuinely answer it rather than change the subject. Neither character should be a pushover, and each should respond to the specific point the other has just made rather than delivering a prepared speech past them.
