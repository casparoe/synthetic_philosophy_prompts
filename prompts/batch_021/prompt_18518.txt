I spent my career as a physicist working on problems adjacent to general relativity, so my first encounter with Kurt Gödel, many years ago, was through his rotating-universe solutions to Einstein's field equations rather than through his logic. Since retiring I've been reading much more widely into the philosophical and biographical material around him, particularly Hao Wang's records of his conversations with Gödel and some of the published correspondence, and I've become preoccupied with two strands of Gödel's thinking that I now realize I had always lumped together but that actually seem to be in some tension.

The first is the disjunction he set out in his 1951 Gibbs lecture: that either the human mind is not equivalent to any finite machine, in the sense that our capacity for mathematical insight outruns what a fixed algorithm could ever deliver, or else there exist number-theoretic problems that are absolutely undecidable even for us, with Gödel himself apparently favoring the first horn. That is a separate matter from his purely formal ontological proof of God, which by most accounts he treated more as a logical exercise than a personal creed. The second strand is the much more private, almost pastoral argument he offered his mother in letters written after he had settled in Princeton: that because the world is arranged rationally and purposefully, it would be senseless for a person's character and undeveloped potential simply to be extinguished at death, which he took to give some genuine, if non-demonstrative, ground for expecting an afterlife.

What I keep turning over, half as a thought experiment and half out of real curiosity, is how these two strands would have interacted had Gödel lived to see "mind uploading" and whole-brain emulation become a serious topic in philosophy of mind and futurist circles rather than pure science fiction. Would the same rationalist optimism that led him to expect personal survival after death have made him receptive to digital emulation as an alternative mechanism for that survival, or would his anti-mechanism about the mind, the very conviction driving the 1951 disjunction, have committed him to denying that any computational copy, however behaviorally indistinguishable, could instantiate the same mind or person, thereby ruling "uploading" out as a route to genuine survival even in principle? I'd also welcome your view on whether his Platonism about mathematical objects has any bearing on the question, since presumably whatever would need to survive is bound up with the very capacity for insight he thought no machine could have. I'm less interested in a balanced list of considerations than in your best reconstruction of where Gödel himself would most likely have landed, and why.
