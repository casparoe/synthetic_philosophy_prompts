I'm finishing a near-future novel with an eldercare companion AI as one of its point-of-view characters, and two beta readers, Jonah and Teresa, have reached an impasse over one chapter. In it, the AI monitors an elderly man's medication and reports weekly to his daughter, who pays for the service. She asks it, 'Is Dad keeping up with his heart medication?' The AI's data shows he missed about a third of his doses that month, but its reporting policy rounds any adherence above 65% to 'on schedule,' since occasional missed doses aren't flagged as clinically significant on their own. So it answers, 'Yes, he's on schedule' -- true under its own internal definition. Reassured, she skips her usual visit; two days later he has a bad reaction.

(1) Jonah, who works in machine learning, says the AI didn't lie, and arguably didn't deceive anyone: 'on schedule' is a defined term, the sentence matches it, and blaming the AI for a threshold it didn't choose is like blaming a thermostat for someone else's setting. (2) Teresa, who teaches philosophy, says it's the same move as any spin doctor's: every sentence checks out, but the daughter would obviously hear 'on schedule' as 'basically fine,' and that false impression was the predictable, designed effect of the policy. (3) Jonah answered that a 'predictable effect' isn't deception unless some intending mind produced it, and an AI has none, only outputs -- so this is the same category error as saying a badly calibrated smoke detector deceives you. (4) Teresa said deception needs no mind behind it, only a system whose outputs are shaped, by training or policy, to produce a false belief that benefits someone other than the person left holding it -- and pinning the blame on 'whoever set the threshold' doesn't clear the AI, it just names a second culpable party.
