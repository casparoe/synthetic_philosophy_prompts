Random context: I'm a phil of bio PhD student TAing this semester for an undergrad seminar thats cross-listed between biology and philosophy, and this week's discussion section covers Gould & Lewontin's spandrels paper plus the follow up piece with Vrba where they coin "exaptation." Every single year when I ask students to come up with their own example of a spandrel/exaptation, I get back the exact same handful verbatim from the readings -- feathers evolving for thermoregulation and later getting co-opted for flight, the panda's "thumb" (the enlarged wrist bone), maybe the human chin or the whole female-orgasm-as-byproduct-of-male-orgasm debate if someone did the extra reading. Makes for a pretty dead discussion since nobody actually has to think, they just pattern match to the syllabus.

So here's what I want from you: give me a genuinely different candidate spandrel/exaptation, i.e. some trait (doesn't have to be human, any organism is fair game) that plausibly arose as a non-adaptive byproduct of some other adaptation, or of a purely developmental/structural constraint -- in the spirit of the actual architectural spandrels the paper is named after -- and was then later co-opted by selection for a new use. Please don't just give me insect wings evolving from thermoregulatory flaps, that's basically the intro-textbook version of the feather story at this point.

And to make it actually useful for discussion section, I want you to also be upfront about the obvious adaptationist comeback: what's the "it was selected for this function all along" story a skeptic could tell about your example instead, and is there any real way, even in principle, to adjudicate between the two stories -- or does it just dissolve into an unfalsifiable just-so-story-off in both directions? Honestly that second part is the part I care about most, since half the point of the unit is getting students to notice that Gould and Lewontin's own move can be exactly as unfalsifiable as the adaptationist stories they're mocking.
