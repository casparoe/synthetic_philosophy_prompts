I recently joined the board of my town's historical society, which is finishing a report on why the Ashford Mill, once the town's largest employer, closed in 1986. At our last meeting, two volunteers central to drafting the report -- Warren, a retired economist, and Nadia, who conducted most of the oral-history interviews -- disagreed sharply about how the closing should be explained, and I haven't stopped turning their exchange over since.

Warren argued that the report should be anchored in a general pattern: mill towns whose transportation and labor costs rose as Ashford's did through the 1970s were bound to lose out to cheaper producers, and citing that regularity is what makes the account an explanation rather than a mere chronicle.

Nadia answered that this gets things backwards. What actually accounts for Ashford closing in 1986, when the mill two towns over survived, is the specific sequence she had documented -- a bitter 1981 strike, the owning family's refusal to modernize, a costly lawsuit -- since a general tendency cannot distinguish the mills that failed from comparable ones that adapted.

Warren replied that this is no real objection: the regularity explains the closing only once the specific facts are added as initial conditions, so Nadia's details are simply the missing conditions his account always required, not a rival to it.

Nadia was unconvinced, arguing that his 'regularity' has too many exceptions among comparable mills to function as a genuine law, and that showing how the closing grew intelligibly out of the particular choices involved is a different, and no less rigorous, way of explaining an outcome than subsuming it under a loose tendency.

The report is still stalled, and both of them have asked me who I think had the better case, which I honestly cannot judge myself. Could you go through their exchange and assess who has the stronger argument at each stage, not just declare an overall winner, and note anything important that both of them seem to be missing?
