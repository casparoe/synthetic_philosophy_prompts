I'm about two-thirds through a novel built around two philosophy professors, Mireille and Tomas, who've apparently been refighting the same argument since grad school, and I've hit a scene I can't write my way out of without actually understanding the philosphy underneath it myself. They've just walked out of a screening of a film adapted from a novel they both love, and Mireille admits, a little embarrassed, that she was crying at the end even though, as she puts it, "nothing happened, there's no one there for it to happen to." This reignites their old fight. Tomas take something like the position I've seen called the "quasi-emotion" or make-believe view -- I think it traces back to a philosopher named Walton and a thought experiment about a guy named Charles watching a horror movie -- where what she felt wasn't actually grief or pity at all, just a pretend version of it, since real grief would require her to believe the person in question exists and is suffering, and she doesn't believe that. Mireille pushes back with what I gather is called "thought theory," insisting the tears were completely real, just directed at a vividly imagined situation instead of a belief that it's actually happening.

My writing group read a draft of this scene last week and said it reads like the two of them are just waiting for their turn to talk instead of actually arguing, which is fair -- I fudged the philosophy because I don't fully have it myself. Could you write out their actual argument as a straight dialogue (just the back and forth, no stage directions or scene-setting, something like 600-900 words)? I want Tomas to land the classic objection to Mireille's view -- something like "if you really believed you pitied her, you'd have reached for your phone, but you just sat there eating popcorn" -- and I want Mireille to come back with something real instead of a dodge, and then Tomas needs an actual comeback to that too, not just a restatement of his opening point. Neither of them should come off as the one who's obviously right here; they've been doing this for fifteen years, so it should read like two people who respect each other's minds and know exactly where the other's argument is weak, not like one side quietly strawmanning the other.
