Two friends of mine, Nora and Theo, got into a drawn-out argument at dinner recently that neither of them clearly won, and I'd like an outside assessment of it.

The background: a mutual acquaintance, Mara, is notorious for cancelling plans at the last minute. Her housemate, Bea, finally called her out over text for it, calling it disrespectful. Nora remarked, almost in passing, that Bea was in a poor position to say so, since she cancels on people just as often herself. Theo objected, and the exchange went roughly as follows.

1. Theo said whether cancelling on people is disrespectful doesn't depend on who points it out, so Bea's own record doesn't touch the substance of the complaint -- that's just a tu quoque.
2. Nora said she wasn't disputing the substance, only whether Bea had any business raising it: blaming someone isn't just asserting a true sentence, it's implicitly holding them to a standard, and you can't do that in good faith for a standard you flout yourself.
3. Theo replied that on this view almost nobody could ever criticize anyone for anything, since virtually everyone fails their own standards somewhere -- which would make ordinary moral criticism nearly impossible.
4. Nora said that was too quick: Bea isn't someone who slipped up once, she is presently and unapologetically just as bad, so this is less "imperfect person criticizes imperfect person" than the pot calling the kettle black while still on the stove.
5. Theo said that even granting the distinction, it shows at most that Bea's complaint is graceless or badly timed, not that she forfeits any standing to make it -- "forfeiting standing" has to mean something stronger than "this is a bit rich coming from you."
6. Nora said Bea's own history arguably cuts the other way: having cancelled on people constantly herself, she understands exactly how it feels and why it's inconsiderate, making her unusually well placed to say so rather than disqualified from saying it.

I couldn't tell who came out ahead, or whether by the end they were even disagreeing about the same thing. Could you go through moves 1 through 6 and say, for each, who has the better of the argument and why, including anything you think both of them are missing? Then say whether 'losing standing to blame' names a real, distinct phenomenon, or whether, as Theo's last point suggests, it collapses into something weaker once you push on it.
