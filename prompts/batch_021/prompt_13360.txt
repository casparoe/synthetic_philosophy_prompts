My aunt Ruth died three weeks ago at sixty-three, which everyone agrees was too soon, and I've been asked to give the eulogy this weekend. She left school at sixteen and spent her whole working life painting houses, but she read philosophy for fun on her own, out of library-sale paperbacks and photocopied essays, and about twenty years ago she pressed one of those essays on me — Moritz Schlick's piece on the meaning of life — telling me it had settled something she'd been turning over since she was a girl. I'm no scholar either, just someone she got started on it, so I follow the argument only in my own rough terms: that a life spent entirely on purposes — doing one thing so as to afford or achieve some further thing — never actually lands anywhere, because each purpose only hands you off to the next, and that whatever meaning a life has must come instead from whatever in it counts as play rather than work: things done the way a child plays, for no reason beyond themselves. Ruth took this more seriously than anyone I've known: she turned down two promotions to keep her afternoons free, spent years teaching herself to identify birds by ear for absolutely no reason, and, from her hospice bed, got quietly furious at a cousin who called her "such a hard worker." She left a note asking whoever spoke not to praise her work ethic, her accomplishments, or any "legacy," and not to console anyone with the thought that a longer life would have amounted to more — she wanted it said plainly that meaning was never a matter of how much got done or how long there'd been to do it in.

I'd like your help writing that eulogy. It has to actually honor what she asked, not just gesture at it before sliding into the usual language once things get emotional, and it has to work for a room of maybe eighty people who mostly knew her as a good painter and a warm, slightly odd woman rather than as somebody with a philosophy of her own — except for one friend of hers from a secondhand-bookshop reading circle, who will notice immediately if the idea gets flattened or misstated. I don't know myself whether it's better to actually name Schlick and the essay or to carry the idea through in plain language and let the philosophy stay invisible. Five or six minutes, please, and something that could actually comfort people rather than just being correct.
