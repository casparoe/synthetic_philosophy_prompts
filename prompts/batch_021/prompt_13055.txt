So a couple weeks back my team had this whole Slack argument about code review policy — basically whether one senior engineer's sign-off should be enough to merge a PR, or whether we should switch to requiring approval from like 4-5 regular engineers instead. Somebody linked the Condorcet Jury Theorem and it pretty much ended the debate, because apparently the theorem proves that if you get enough voters together who are each even just a little better than a coin flip at picking the right answer, the majority vote becomes correct with near-certainty as the group grows, basically no matter how average any individual voter is. That tracked with stuff I already half-believed from ML (ensembling a bunch of so-so models to beat one strong model) and from that Surowiecki "Wisdom of Crowds" book everyone read a while back, plus the prediction-market crowd who are always saying groups of amateurs out-forecast individual pundits.

I've got zero formal philosophy background (closest I got was a stats elective), but the math here seems airtight, so now I've been going around telling people this is basically a mathematical proof that a big enough committee of average people will beat one trusted expert every time — and that we should lean way harder into "more independent voters" for stuff like architecture reviews and hiring committees, maybe even juries and elections, instead of ever deferring to whoever's most senior in the room. What I can't figure out is why, if the math is this clean, companies and courts and democracies don't just go all-in on it already — why does anyone still pay a staff engineer or a expert consultant big money for their individual judgment instead of just polling a bigger group of averge people? Am I missing some catch in how the theorem actually applies here, or did the math folks basically hand crowds a permanent trump card over expertise?
