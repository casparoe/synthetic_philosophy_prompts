I'm working on a feature about the philosophical underpinnings of AI existential-risk arguments, and in several interviews this year—plus a couple of AI safety conference panels—I've had researchers point me to the "coherence arguments" for expected utility maximization as essentially settling one of the central premises of the whole debate. As I understand it, mainly from Steve Omohundro's work on basic AI drives and from Eliezer Yudkowsky's later essays arguing that coherent decisions imply consistent utilities, the reasoning goes roughly like this: any agent whose preferences violate the standard axioms of expected utility theory (transitivity, completeness, independence) can, in principle, be turned into a "money pump," exploited via a sequence of trades that leaves it strictly worse off than where it started. Since no sufficiently capable, self-improving system would tolerate being exploited like that indefinitely, the argument concludes that such a system will inevitably be shaped into behaving like an expected-utility maximizer with a fixed, well-defined goal, at which point the familiar instrumental-convergence predictions—self-preservation, resource acquisition, resistance to being shut down—are supposed to follow more or less automatically.

Given that this sounds like a genuine theorem rather than a loose analogy, I've been struggling to understand why so many decision theorists and AI safety researchers still treat the question of whether advanced AI will actually be "goal-directed," in the sense these risk arguments require, as substantively open rather than settled. Is the lingering disagreement mostly a matter of people being reluctant to accept an inconvenient but essentially airtight conclusion? Or is there a real, technical gap between what the coherence theorems actually demonstrate and the much stronger claim that any sufficiently advanced real-world system must converge on precise expected-utility maximization with a fixed goal? I'd like to represent this part of the debate accurately rather than take sides prematurely, so I'd appreciate it if you could walk me through exactly where, if anywhere, the argument's logic breaks down.
