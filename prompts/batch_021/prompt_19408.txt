Lately I've been feeling pretty worn down by party politics — the sense that almost every argument gets settled by which team you're supposed to be on before anyone actually looks at the issue itself — and that frustration recently sent me down a rabbit hole into Simone Weil, the French philosopher and mystic who, in 1943, spent the last months of her life in London working for the Free French before dying in England later that year. Buried in her writing from that period is a short, oddly radical essay usually translated as "On the Abolition of All Political Parties" (I've also seen it rendered "Note on the General Suppression of Political Parties"), where she argues that parties shouldn't just be reformed or made more civil but abolished outright, even in a free, post-war democracy. From what I've read of it and about it — I'm going on a translation plus some secondary discussion, so I may have a few finer points slightly off — her case treats a party as, in effect, a machine with three features: it exists to manufacture collective passion, it's built to pressure each of its individual members into lining up their opinions with the group's rather than examining them independently, and its real operative goal, whatever its founding cause may have been, quietly becomes its own indefinite growth. On this basis she treats multi-party democracies as running on essentially the same engine as one-party totalitarian states, just with that engine split up and competing against copies of itself rather than holding a monopoly.

Whatever her own reasons for writing this while watching French democracy collapse into Vichy, the argument sits awkwardly next to what a lot of people think we've since learned about mass democracy — that parties are basically how tens of millions of voters manage to hold politicians accountable at all, by giving their choices a stable "brand" to vote for or against, and how legislators manage to form workable coalitions instead of bargaining from scratch every time. There's also the older Madisonian answer, from Federalist No. 10, that factions of this kind are an unavoidable by-product of political liberty itself, so the sane response is to manage their effects rather than legislate them out of existence. I keep going back and forth: something real does seem to be pointed at when Weil describes opinions being adopted pre-packaged rather than examined, but "abolish all parties" looks like a wild overreaction even if the diagnosis is onto something.

What I'd like is an essay that actually takes a side rather than just cataloguing considerations. Reconstruct Weil's argument as strongly as you reasonably can — the collective-passion point, the conformity-pressure point, and the self-perpetuating-growth point — and reconstruct the strongest functional case for parties against it, then weigh the two directly against each other. Tell me, and defend, where you actually land: something like "full abolition is untenable, but Weil correctly isolates one specific mechanism that reformers should target directly" would count as landing somewhere, while "there are good points on both sides" would not.
