I spent thirty-five years as a condensed-matter physicist working mostly on statistical mechanics before retiring, and for the past two years, largely to keep myself occupied, I have served as a non-clinical member of my regional health authority's priority-setting committee — the body that decides, within a fixed annual budget, which treatments get funded and which do not. We use the standard cost-per-QALY framework, with an informal threshold of about $30,000 per quality-adjusted life year, and the arithmetic is usually uncontroversial. This month it is not. We have before us a funding request for an enzyme-replacement therapy for four identified children in our region with a severe, ultimately fatal metabolic disorder: treating all four for a decade would cost roughly $9 million and, given how little the therapy can do against a disease this advanced, works out to about $600,000 per QALY gained — twenty times over threshold. That same $9 million, redirected to a proposed hypertension-and-cholesterol screening-and-treatment programme, would, on the committee's own modelling, generate more total QALYs, spread across some 400,000 people who would each receive a barely perceptible reduction in their lifetime risk of stroke or heart attack. Roughly half the committee wants to fund the screening programme and decline the therapy on exactly these grounds; the other half considers it indefensible to let a diffuse statistical benefit to people who will never know they were helped outweigh the concentrated, visible suffering of four named children, and keeps invoking what I gather bioethicists call the "rule of rescue."

Reading around the problem since the meeting was scheduled, I have discovered that our dilemma is a fairly direct instance of what some moral philosophers call "torture versus dust specks": whether summing enough negligible harms, or foregone benefits, can outweigh one severe and concentrated harm, or whether such sums are illegitimate no matter how large the numbers get. I came across a paper by Alastair Norcross, "Comparing Harms: Headaches and Human Lives," that runs essentially our case using headaches in place of stroke risk and argues the aggregation is perfectly respectable. I confess my own instincts here are contaminated by my training: I spent a career adding microscopic contributions together to obtain macroscopic quantities, and my first reaction is that the committee's QALY sum is doing nothing more exotic than that. But I suspect that instinct may be exactly the mistake, since a person is not a molecule, and I genuinely do not know whether the standard objections to this kind of aggregation — about the separateness of persons, or about thresholds below which a harm should not count at all — are principled or just a dressed-up version of the rule of rescue.

I have been asked, as the member with no personal or clinical stake in either request, to circulate a short written opinion before we vote next Wednesday. Could you draft that opinion for me? It should reach a clear recommendation — fund the therapy, fund the screening programme, or some principled split or threshold rule between the two — with reasoning that our clinicians and lay members alike could follow and actually respond to, rather than a bare restatement of the QALY arithmetic or a bare appeal to intuition. I would especially like it to take the strongest form of whichever side it does not ultimately favour seriously, since I will have to defend it in front of colleagues who hold both views sincerely and will not be satisfied by a memo that simply dismisses one of them.
