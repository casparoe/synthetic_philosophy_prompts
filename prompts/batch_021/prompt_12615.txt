I cover science for a living, and for an unrelated piece I've spent the past couple of weeks reading through the old epistemological argument about testimony: whether believing something purely on someone else's word can be a basic source of knowledge in its own right, on a par with perception and memory (the position associated with Thomas Reid, who held that a natural "principle of credulity" in the hearer answers to a natural "principle of veracity" in the speaker), or whether testimonial belief only becomes knowledge once we have independent grounds for trusting the particular speaker, so that it ultimately reduces to inference from our own experience of witnesses generally being reliable (the more reductionist reading standardly attributed to Hume). I hadn't expected this to be more than background for the piece, but it turns out to map almost exactly onto a live dispute on my outlet's editorial standards committee, which I sit on, and we need to reach a decision before tomorrow's 9 a.m. call.

The case: a small biotech, running its own unblinded, uncontrolled trial of a gene therapy for a rare degenerative childhood disease, reports striking improvement in four of six treated children. The lead scientist has spent hours with our health desk, has shared her raw case notes with us under embargo, and has struck every reporter who's dealt with her as careful, candid, and appropriately worried about overselling her own result; no outside lab or clinician has verified anything, and two independent biostatisticians we consulted flagged the absence of a control arm and the small numbers as reasons the finding could easily be noise, wishful interpretation, or both. Two rival outlets are about to run unhedged "breakthrough" stories based on nothing but the company's own account. Roughly half the committee thinks a sufficiently careful, transparent, first-hand report from a credible scientist is itself evidence we're entitled to report, properly framed; the other half, led by our health-data editor, argues that a researcher's sincere testimony about her own uncontrolled trial can never, by itself, warrant reporting the result as anything but a rumor, however honest she is being.

Please draft the memo I'll bring into that meeting. It should recommend one specific course of action — run the story, hold it, or run it framed in a particular way — with a concrete timetable for revisiting the decision as more information comes in, and it should take seriously whichever side's position it ends up rejecting, rather than treating that side as merely over-cautious or merely credulous.
