I've been down a rabbit hole lately on the Kelly criterion and this "ergodicity economics" stuff (Ole Peters and that crowd), partly because a friend who does quant trading keeps sending me videos about it, and I want you to steelman the strongest version of their claim for me, because every explainer I find just glides past the obvious objection instead of actually answering it.

Background in case it's useful: the pitch is that for repeated multiplicative bets (reinvesting your bankroll each round), maximizing the time-average growth rate of your wealth turns out to be mathematically identical to maximizing expected log wealth, i.e. the Kelly criterion — people sometimes call this "geometric" rationality, since you're optimizing a geometric-mean growth rate instead of an ordinary expected value. Peters and co. insist this isn't just "another utility function smuggled in": their claim is that orthodox expected utility theory illegitimately uses an ensemble average (over parallel copies of you across possible worlds) when what actually matters is the time average of the one single trajectory you actually live through, and because wealth growth is multiplicative (hence non-ergodic) these two averages diverge, so only the time-average one is the objectively "correct" thing to optimize. On this story, log-utility-ish risk aversion isn't a subjective preference at all, its forced on you by the dynamics themselves.

The obvious objection, which I can't find a satisfying answer to anywhere, is: why is "time-average growth of wealth" the priveleged quantity, rather than time-average growth of sqrt(wealth), or of utility(wealth) for whatever utility function you'd have picked anyway? Isn't choosing "raw wealth" as the thing to time-average just as arbitrary as choosing a utility function in the first place — haven't they basically smuggled log-utility back in and relabeled it "objective," when the actual choice (which variable gets the ergodicity treatment) is still exactly as preference-laden as before? Can you give me the strongest possible argument that this objection actually fails — i.e. that there's a principled, non-arbitrary reason wealth itself, and not some transform of it, is the right thing to time-average, so that log-like risk-aversion really is rationally forced on everyone and not just one preference dressed up in fancier math?
