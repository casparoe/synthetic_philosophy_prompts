Two friends of mine, Marcus and Elena, got into this argument at a barbecue last weekend and I haven't been able to stop thinking about who actually won each exchange. It started because Marcus mentioned that his uncle believes a big pharmaceutical company buried it's own data on a common drug's side effects, and (1) Marcus said that's just a conspiracy theory, so it's not even worth engaging with as a hypothesis. Elena wasn't having it: (2) she said you can't dismiss a belief just for fitting the label "conspiracy theory," since Watergate, MKUltra, and the tobacco industry's decades-long cover-up of its own cancer research were all dismissed the exact same way before they turned out to be true — so every case has to be judged on its own specific evidence, not on which bucket it falls into.

Marcus conceded the historical point but came back with (3): "conspiracy theory" doesn't just mean "a belief that people secretly coordinated," it names a specific bad reasoning pattern — treating the lack of confirming evidence as itself proof of a cover-up, and absorbing every piece of disconfirming evidence by just expanding who's supposedly in on it — and his uncle's theory clearly fits that pattern, which is what actually makes it worth dismissing. Elena's reply had two prongs: (4) first, that this is suspiciously convenient, since you only ever seem to notice "the pattern" in theories you've already decided are false, so he's smuggling the verdict in through the back door; and second, that even granting the pattern is real, it can't be doing the epistemic work he wants, because a genuine, ongoing, well-hidden conspiracy would necessarily look exactly the same from the outside — unfalsifiable-seeming, absorbing every leak — right up until the day it collapsed. MKUltra and the tobacco cover-up looked exactly like "bad conspiracy reasoning" to outsiders for years, for the simple reason that they were real and successfully hidden.

Marcus's response was (5): fine, maybe you can't tell in real time which rare theory is going to be the true one, but you can still say a belief was irrational given the evidence actually available at the time, even if it's later vindicated — rationality and truth aren't the same thing. Elena said (6) that's just her original point wearing a different outfit: if all that ultimately matters is whether a belief was well-supported by the specific evidence available at the time, then there's no extra "is this a conspiracy theory" filter doing any independent work, which is exactly what she said from the start. Neither of them will concede, so they've roped me into refereeing, which I am hopeless at. Could you go through it move by move and say who's got the better argument at each numbered step, rather than just declaring an overall winner — and flag it if you think there's some shared assumption of theirs that's actually the root of the disagreement?
