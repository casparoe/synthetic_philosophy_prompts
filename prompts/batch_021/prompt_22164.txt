I've been chewing on the internalism/externalism about reasons debate (Bernard Williams' "Internal and External Reasons" is the classic starting point) for a seminar, and theres one specific move that keeps bugging me. Williams says an internal reason has to connect, via "sound deliberation," to something already in the agent's actual motivational set S -- but S isn't fixed or fully transparent to the agent, and sound deliberation can apparently unearth all kinds of things they didn't realize they wanted or cared about. That starts to feel suspiciously like its doing the same work an external reason would do -- like, if "sound deliberation from S" can always cough up a reason for basically anyone to be less cruel or selfish, in what sense is that meaningfully different from just saying "everyone has reason to be less cruel, full stop, regardless of their psychology"? A classmate of mine thinks this worry is confused and that there's a real difference between the two views; I'm honestly not sure who's right.

Could you write me a dialogue between an internalist and an externalist about reasons where they actually hash this out, using the test case of someone with basically zero relevant motivation anywhere in S toward kindness or fairness (a genuinely cold, self-absorbed person -- not just someone confused or misinformed)? I want the externalist to press hard on the amoralist-reductio ("your view just lets him off the hook, that can't be right") and on the deliberation-collapse worry above, but the internalist needs real, non-strawman comebacks too -- like why "sound deliberation out of an empty-ish S" doesn't secretly smuggle in external reasons, and why "letting the amoralist off the hook" in the internalist's specific sense isn't as costly as it sounds. Neither side should win just because the other forgot their own best move -- I want them actually engaging with each other point by point.
