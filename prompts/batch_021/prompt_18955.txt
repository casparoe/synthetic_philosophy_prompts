I spent thirty-five years running an experimental atomic-physics lab, mostly building optical lattice clocks, before retiring two years ago, and I've since been filling my mornings with a self-directed course in philosophy of physics. Working through Newton's Principia alongside a modern commentary, I finally read the Scholium passages properly rather than in the potted version everyone gets in undergraduate mechanics. The rotating bucket I already knew, more or less, but the companion thought experiment a few pages later gets far less attention: two globes joined by a cord, revolving in an otherwise perfectly empty universe, with no fixed stars or any other matter against which their motion could be judged. Newton's claim is that the cord would still go taut, revealing the rotation regardless. It struck me as the sharper of the two arguments, since it dispenses with water's messy inertia and viscosity and leaves the relationist with no matter at all to point to.

When I mentioned this to a philosopher I've befriended at the local university, she agreed the two-globes case is nastier for a Machian than the bucket is — you cannot say the water is really responding to rotation relative to the fixed stars if, by hypothesis, there are no stars — but she insisted the whole family of arguments has been superseded anyway. The dispute that actually matters now, on her account, is the "hole argument" that Earman and Norton revived out of a problem Einstein ran into around 1913 while developing general relativity: because the field equations are generally covariant, one can take any solution and drag its metric and matter fields around by a smooth map that is the identity everywhere outside some small region but not inside it, producing a second solution that agrees with the first everywhere outside that "hole" yet disagrees inside it. If spacetime points are substantival individuals in their own right, this seems to saddle the theory with a strange indeterminism, since one and the same state of affairs outside the hole turns out to be compatible with two distinct point-by-point futures within it. She says the standard escape, "sophisticated substantivalism," simply denies that the two solutions describe genuinely different possibilities to begin with — what she called "denying haecceitism about spacetime points" — and that this is really relationism smuggled in under a substantivalist label, adopted only to dodge an embarrassing consequence. I don't yet trust my own judgment on whether that is a fair characterization.

I would like an essay from you that goes past the point where most popular treatments stop. Please explain why the Machian appeal to the fixed stars, whatever its intuitive pull, fails to settle anything once a case like the two globes is on the table; lay out the hole argument carefully and assess whether it genuinely refutes substantivalism about spacetime points through the threat of indeterminism, or whether that threat is overstated; and weigh sophisticated substantivalism against relationist and structuralist alternatives on their actual merits rather than dismissing any of them as mere relabeling. Close with an actual verdict on whether spacetime points, or whatever plays their role, should be regarded as substantival, relational, or something else again — I would rather you commit to a position and defend it than tell me that able philosophers disagree.
