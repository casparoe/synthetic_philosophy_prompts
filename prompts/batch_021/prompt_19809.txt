ok this has been stuck in my head since our formal epistemology reading group. the common prior assumption is what makes Aumann's agreement theorem tick -- two Bayesians who start from the exact same prior and only differ in private info literally cannot have posteriors on some event that are common knowledge AND different, they basicaly have to converge. Harsanyi's whole apparatus for games of incomplete info leans on the same move, and the no-trade theorems people derive from it get used to argue that speculative trading on private info shouldn't happen between fully rational agents at all.

meanwhile Keynes spent a good chunk of the Treatise on Probability arguing that probabilities often aren't comparable or even numerically well-defined in the first place, and his "weight of argument" idea is precisely about how two judgements can carry the same probability number while resting on totally different amounts of evidence. and later, obviously, he leans hard on that kind of irreducible uncertainty (plus the beauty-contest picture of markets as agents guessing what other agents are guessing) to explain why speculation and disagreement are basically permanent features of markets, not some info asymmetry that just gets ironed out once everyone's rational.

so, genuine question: if you sat Keynes down and explained the common prior assumption to him, do you think he'd reject the whole Aumann/Harsanyi setup as quietly assuming away the exact thing his probability theory says can't be assumed away, or is there some weakened version of it he'd actually be fine with?
