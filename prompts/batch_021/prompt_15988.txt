So this has been rattling around in my head since our formal epistemology reading group a couple weeks ago, where we went through Nover and Hajek's old paper on the Pasadena game and I don't think I've fully recovered. Quick recap in case its useful: you flip a fair coin repeatedly until it lands heads for the first time, and if that happens on toss n (which has probability 2^-n) you get paid (-1)^(n+1) * 2^n/n dollars, so the payouts go +2, -2, +8/3, -4, +32/5, and so on forever. If you try to compute the expected value the normal way you just get the alternating harmonic series, 1 - 1/2 + 1/3 - 1/4 + ..., which converges to ln(2) but only conditionally, not absolutely. Our prof's whole point was that because its only conditionally convergent, Riemann's rearrangement theorem guarantees you can reorder those exact same infinitely many outcomes to make the sum come out to literally any real number, or to blow up to +infinity or -infinity, or to just oscillate forever without settling anywhere, and there's no principled fact about which ordering counts as the expected value of the game.

What I can't stop thinking about is what this means outside the toy example. I also TA for a seminar on longtermism, and a big chunk of the case for prioritizing stuff like AI safety or biosecurity over nearer-term interventions leans hard on expected-value math involving tiny probabilities and enormous payoffs. If a game this simple already shows expected value can be undefined, or defined only relative to an arbitrary choice of ordering, that seems like it should bother people doing that kind of reasoning way more than it actually seems to. Can you walk me through the most important implications of the Pasadena paradox for how much weight expected-value calculations can bear once you're comparing small-probability, huge-payoff options like that, and whether there's a principled way of blocking the damage (bounding utility, restricting to absolutely convergent cases, whatever) short of just not looking too closely at the rearrangement problem?
