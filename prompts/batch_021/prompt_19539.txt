Among people I follow in the rationalist and effective-altruism blogosphere, Harsanyi's theorem is regularly invoked as something close to a mathematical proof of utilitarianism. What rarely gets spelled out is that "Harsanyi's theorem" actually names two distinct results with different premises: the 1955 Social Aggregation Theorem, derived from Pareto indifference plus von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms on both individual and social preferences, and the 1953 Impartial Observer Theorem, derived from a hypothetical observer with an equal chance of "becoming" each person and evaluating prospects by expected utility. As I understand the Sen-style objection to the first theorem, its additive, weighted-sum conclusion is compatible with treating every individual's utility function as independently rescalable and non-comparable, so that nothing in the proof itself forces the weights to mean anything across persons – the appearance of interpersonal comparison is smuggled in by the utilitarian gloss rather than delivered by the axioms. The Impartial Observer Theorem is supposed to avoid this problem by construction. Could you walk through, with real technical precision, exactly how the first theorem's weights fail to secure interpersonal comparability despite its additive form looking utilitarian, and exactly what work the equiprobability device is doing in the second theorem that lets it succeed where the first one doesn't?
