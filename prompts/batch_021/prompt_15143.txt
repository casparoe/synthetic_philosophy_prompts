I've been putting together a session for my formal epistemology seminar on Aumann's agreement theorem -- the result that if two Bayesians share a common prior, and it becomes common knowledge between them what each one's posterior probability for some event actually is, those posteriors have to be identical. So on Aumann's picture, two rational agents literally cannot end up with it being common knowledge that they disagree. A couple of my students heard this and immediately said "oh, that's basically the equal weight view" -- meaning the idea from the peer disagreement literature (Elga, Christensen, that whole debate) that when you learn a peer disagrees with you, you're supposed to give their credence equal standing with your own and move toward it. I wasn't quick enough on my feet to say whether thats right, or whether its actually a pretty different claim dressed up in similar language, so I'd like to sort it out properly before I see them again.

Could you compare the two for me in some depth? I'm especially interested in what work the common prior assumption and the common knowledge condition are doing inside Aumann's proof that has no real analogue in the informal "equal weight" story, and whether that means the theorem is actually much narrower than it sounds -- a static impossibility result about idealized agents, rather than a rule that tells anyone how to update once they discover an actual disagreement. There's also a follow-up literature, I think from Geanakoplos and Polemarchakis (don't hold me to the exact reference), on what happens if two such agents just keep trading their posteriors back and forth -- does that dynamic version get any closer to a genuine equal-weight-style updating rule, or is it still answering a different question entirely? Basically I want to know exactly where the formal theorem and the epistemic norm actually touch, and where people are just pattern-matching two things that sound alike because they both involve the word "agree."
