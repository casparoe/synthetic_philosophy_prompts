The philosophy club at my high school just spent two meetings working through the section of Saul Kripke's book on Wittgenstein where he lays out his rule-following paradox, and it has been rattling around in my head ever since. The part that really got to me is the "quus" example: since I have only ever added finitely many pairs of numbers in my life, Kripke argues that nothing in my past behavior, and nothing about the state of my brain, settles whether by "+" I have always meant addition rather than some deviant function—call it "quaddition"—that agrees with addition on every sum I have actually computed but diverges once the numbers get large enough (his example has 68+57 coming out to 125 under plus but 5 under a "quus" function that switches over at 57). He calls this a skeptical paradox about meaning, and the response he attributes to Wittgenstein, the "sceptical solution," does not so much refute the skeptic as change the subject: what I mean by "+" turns out to be grounded in my agreement with the community's practice rather than in any fact about me individually. Our advisor mentioned that some people call the resulting position "Kripkenstein," since it is genuinely unclear how much of it is Wittgenstein's own textual view rather than Kripke's own construction, which somehow made the whole thing feel even less stable.

Could you write me a short story, something like 800 to 1500 words, that dramatizes this unease rather than explaining it—closer in spirit to Greg Egan's philosophically-driven science fiction than to a dialogue with a professor? I would like a character whose confidence that they have always meant one particular thing by some rule, word, or procedure they have relied on for years—mathematical, legal, linguistic, whatever you like—starts to come apart under exactly this kind of pressure, without anyone in the story ever using the words "Kripke," "Wittgenstein," or "quus." Someone who has never heard of the rule-following paradox should still feel that something has gone quietly wrong under the character's feet; someone who has heard of it should be able to point to the exact moment a quus-style worry starts doing the work. Set it wherever seems to serve the idea best—I keep picturing a courtroom or an old research lab, but that is just where my own imagination happened to land.
