So in my political philosophy seminar we're spending like three weeks on Nozick's Anarchy, State, and Utopia (mostly for the entitlement theory / Wilt Chamberlain stuff, obviously), but this week I got kind of side-tracked reading ahead and ran into the short bit where he talks about animals, in the chapter on moral side-constraints. My prof only mentioned it in passing so I might be fuzzy on some of the details, but the basic idea I got is: the reason persons get "side-constraints" -- i.e. can't just be used as means to maximize aggregate good, the way strict utilitarianism would allow -- is that they're rational agents who can shape their own lives and give them meaning. Animals presumably don't have that (or have way less of it), so he floats something like "utilitarianism for animals, side-constraints for people" as maybe the right combo view.

Except he seems to immediately get uneasy about his own suggestion, because pure utilitarianism about animals still seems to permit stuff that feels wrong regardless of the aggregate payoff -- like, gratuitous cruelty seems bad even holding fixed everyone's utility -- and I think he also brings up (or maybe this is just the general "logic of the larder" arguement and I'm misremembering it as his) the question of whether breeding-and-killing an animal for food is fine since it wouldn't otherwise have existed at all. Either way he ends up hedging without really landing anywhere firm. And it doesn't seem like he ever squares this with the classic argument from marginal cases: if side-constraints are grounded in some capacity threshold, infants and severely cognitively disabled humans arguably don't clear it either, so on his own logic shouldn't they get bumped into the "utilitarian" bucket too? (I've also heard, tho I haven't actually confirmed this, that he walks the whole thing back somewhat in The Examined Life -- not sure how much weight to put on that.)
