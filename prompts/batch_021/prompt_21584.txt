"The puzzle arises because the statue (call it Goliath) and the lump of clay it's made of (call it Lumpl) seem to occupy exactly the same place at the same time and share all the same material parts, which suggests they're really just one object. But Lumpl and Goliath differ in their modal properties: Lumpl could survive being squashed flat, whereas Goliath could not, since squashing it destroys the statue. Leibniz's Law says that if two things are identical, they must share all of their properties -- so since we've found a property (surviving squashing) that one has and the other lacks, by the identity of indiscernibles this shows that Lumpl and Goliath cannot be one and the same object, even though they are made of the same matter at the same time.

One solution to this puzzle is the constitution view, originally developed by Allan Gibbard in his well-known paper on Lumpl and Goliath. On this view, the statue is constituted by the lump of clay, but constitution is a distinct relation from identity -- weaker than identity but stronger than mere spatial overlap. This lets us hold onto both intuitions at once: that the statue and the lump are intimately connected (via constitution) and that they have different persistence conditions (so are not identical), all without violating Leibniz's Law. I think this is a pretty satisfying solution because it matches how we actually talk about statues -- we say a statue 'is made of' clay, which sounds a lot more like a constitution relation than strict identity."
