I'm taking a seminar this term on the philosophy of probability, and for our unit on the principle of indifference our instructor asked us to write a short critical response (about 800 words) engaging with some aspect of Bertrand's chord paradox. We spent a full session working through Edwin Jaynes's invariance-based resolution, and a friend of mine in the class came away convinced that Jaynes basically rescues objective Bayesianism from the paradox. I wasn't convinced, so I wrote up the following argument against that reading. I'd really appreciate your comments before I hand it in, since this is meant to be my own contribution to the discussion and I don't want to either misread Jaynes or unknowingly just restate someone else's published objection as if it were mine.

Here's what I have so far (working title: "Does Jaynes Save Indifference, or Bury It?"):

Bertrand's original puzzle asks for the probability that a chord drawn "at random" on a circle is longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle. Depending on whether "random" means uniform endpoints on the circumference, a uniform point along a fixed radius, or a uniform midpoint within the disc, one gets 1/3, 1/2, or 1/4 respectively: three equally natural-looking applications of indifference yielding three incompatible answers. The standard moral is that "choosing at random" is not a determinate instruction until a parametrization has been fixed, and indifference with respect to one parametrization is generally not indifference with respect to another.

Jaynes's well-known reply is that the problem becomes well-posed once enough of the physical set-up is specified. If chords are generated by tossing straws onto the circle from far away, in random position and orientation, then whatever distribution governs the outcome must be invariant under translations of the circle, rotations of the circle, and changes of its scale, since none of these should matter to a process that doesn't "know" where the circle is, how it's oriented, or how big it is. Jaynes shows that only the random-radius method satisfies all three invariances at once, and concludes that 1/2 is the uniquely correct answer for that physically specified problem.

My worry is that this doesn't rehabilitate the principle of indifference so much as quietly abandon it. Mere ignorance of which chord will be drawn does not, by itself, entail that the generating process is translation-, rotation-, and scale-invariant; that's substantive information about a physical mechanism, not an expression of knowing nothing. Once we're permitted to import symmetry facts about a specific causal set-up, we're doing statistical mechanics, not epistemology from ignorance. And choosing which invariances to impose is exactly the kind of choice that produced the paradox in the first place: someone could specify a different generating process, invariant under a different symmetry group, and thereby resurrect a different answer with equally good Jaynesian credentials. Van Fraassen's cube-factory case, where "no reason to prefer" can be expressed with respect to edge length or volume with equal apparent innocence, suggests the trouble is general: nothing about ignorance alone tells you which variable to be indifferent over. So it looks like Jaynes hasn't shown that indifference, unaided by further physical premises, ever picks out a determinate distribution. He's shown that once you stop applying indifference and start applying physics, the paradox dissolves - for that physical problem. That looks less like a vindication of the principle of indifference than a demonstration that it was never doing any work to begin with.
