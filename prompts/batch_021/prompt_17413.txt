Since retiring after nearly forty years as an experimental nuclear physicist, I have been attending a small philosophy of science reading group that meets fortnightly at the local university. Over the past month we have been working through the dispute between Humean and anti-Humean accounts of laws of nature, reading selections from Lewis and Armstrong alongside the dispositional essentialism of Ellis and Bird. At our last meeting, two of the regulars, Nora, a retired mathematician, and Piotr, a philosophy graduate student, fell into a genuine disagreement that the rest of us mostly just watched unfold, and I would value a considered outside opinion on it, because I left unable to decide who had actually gotten the better of the exchange.

(1) Nora opened by defending the Humean line: laws, she said, are nothing but the theorems of whichever true deductive system of the world best combines simplicity and strength, and nothing further needs to exist beyond the total pattern of particular fact, since positing necessary connections between distinct events adds machinery we cannot detect and that earns no additional predictive dividend. (2) Piotr objected that this cannot be the whole story: on Nora's view, the half-life of a given isotope is just a summary of every decay event that has ever occurred or will occur, including the ones we might measure tomorrow, so the law cannot explain why any particular nucleus decays when it does, whereas working physicists (he gestured at me) treat the decay constant as prior to, and explanatory of, the individual event, something that grounds objective chances and licenses claims about samples that were never actually observed. (3) Nora replied that no single decay event is what makes the best system best: remove or alter any one such event from the total pattern and the same system still wins on simplicity and strength, so for any particular case the explanation runs cleanly from law to event without circularity, in exactly the way a statistical model fitted to a large data set can still be said to explain an individual point in that data set even though that point was among the data used to fit it. (4) Piotr granted this for any single event but said the worry simply resurfaces one level up: the account still owes us some explanation of why the pattern displays this exceptionless uniformity across every place and time a nucleus of that isotope has ever existed, rather than that being a standing cosmic coincidence that the best system merely redescribes efficiently; this, he said, is why he found dispositional essentialism more satisfying, since if the decay constant flows from the essential nature of the nuclear property itself, the uniformity is guaranteed by what the property is, rather than being a pattern awaiting a summary. (5) Nora answered that Piotr's own training should make him suspicious of that move: physicists treat coupling constants and decay rates as measured, contingent quantities, which is exactly why we run the experiment rather than derive the number from first principles, and why cosmologists take seriously the idea that other regions of the universe, or other universes altogether, might have different values for those very constants. On Piotr's essentialist view, she said, none of that could even be coherently entertained, since a world with a different decay constant would, by his lights, simply fail to contain the same nuclear property at all. A maneuver that forecloses the question that easily, she added, looks less like a metaphysical discovery than a decision about how to individuate properties.

I would be grateful if you could go through the exchange move by move rather than simply declaring an overall winner, telling me at each stage where the speaker is right and where they overreach, and in particular whether Nora's argument in (5) genuinely defeats Piotr's position or merely shows that both sides can help themselves to a parallel maneuver. I would also welcome your view on whether there is some consideration neither of them raised that should have changed the shape of the argument.
