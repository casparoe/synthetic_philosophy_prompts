ok so this might be the 500th time i've seen parfit's hitchhiker come up on a rationalist blog or forum, and it almost always gets flattened into "CDT says dont pay, thats why CDT is bad, FDT fixes it, next topic." the part that usually gets skipped is the actually spicy bit -- parfit's broader point (from reasons and persons, the stuff on self-interest theory and dispositions) that it can be straightforwardly rational to make yourself into someone who will, in the moment, do the thing that's irrational by direct/local lights. people sometimes call this "rational irrationality": being rational as a disposition requires being irrational as an act, and that's not a contradiction so much as two senses of "rational" talking past each other.

can you write me a dialogue set right after the hitchhiker gets dropped off in town, standing at the ATM. one speaker is the hitchhiker themselves, now safe, leaning causal-decision-theorist: the ride already happened, nothing about handing over cash now reaches back and causes that, so paying is just burning money for no reason. the other speaker is a friend who's there when it happens and tries to talk them into paying anyway, on parfit's grounds that the disposition to pay was what was rational, and that calling the payment itself "irrational" doesn't undercut that, it just shows act-rationality and disposition-rationality can point different ways.

don't make the hitchhiker a pushover -- give them a real objection or two (e.g. "why should it matter what some algorithm would've output, when i can just... not do that now," or the complaint that the friend is smuggling in the conclusion by relabeling "the disposition i wish you had" as "the rational action," which begs the question of whether the two should even be judged by the same standard). and don't let the friend win just by repeating "but then you'd never have gotten picked up" on loop -- have them actually engage the objections. maybe 500-700 words of real back and forth.
