I'm taking an upper-level seminar on the metaphysics of persistence this term, and we recently spent two sessions on the endurantism/perdurantism debate, working through Lewis's discussion of the problem of temporary intrinsics in *On the Plurality of Worlds*. The basic setup made sense to me: if I'm bent while sitting and straight while standing, and shape is about as paradigmatic an intrinsic property as there is, how can one and the same persisting thing have both shapes, given that intrinsic properties are supposed to be had absolutely rather than merely relative to something else? What I couldn't settle in my own head is whether either side's standard fix actually earns its keep. The endurantist's move of saying that shapes are really relations to times struck several of us in section as quietly conceding the perdurantist's point about temporary intrinsics while keeping the old vocabulary of "having a shape"; but the perdurantist's temporal-parts story seemed to invite the equally awkward result that no persisting person is ever, strictly speaking, bent or straight -- only some momentary part of them is -- which sits oddly with the ordinary thought that it is one and the same person who stood up, walked over, and sat back down.

Since we never resolved this in discussion, I'd like to see the exchange actually play out rather than have the two positions summarized side by side. Could you write a dialogue between an endurantist and a perdurantist -- perhaps set in a seminar room, with one of them standing up out of her chair partway through just to make the bent/straight case vivid -- who argue through temporary intrinsics in real time? I'd like the endurantist to run the relational-properties response, the perdurantist to press the objection that this trades away genuine intrinsic change for a semantic trick, and then the endurantist to come back with the "no one is ever really bent" charge against temporal parts. If it fits naturally, I'd also love for the perdurantist to bring in the vagueness-based motivation for their view -- the worry that without temporal parts filling in every moment, an object's coming into or going out of existence would need either an arbitrary sharp cutoff or brute vagueness about whether the thing exists at all, and that both options look unacceptable -- and to see how the endurantist handles that in turn. Please don't let either side dodge a point or concede prematurely; I want genuine back-and-forth, and I'd like your own sense, at the end, of whether one position actually comes out ahead here or whether this is a real stalemate.
