For the past few years I have moderated an online forum for amateur geometers, where a recurring feature of the role is corresponding with contributors who are convinced they have squared the circle or trisected the angle with straightedge and compass, and who meet standard refutations with polite incomprehension rather than assent. I recently discovered that Thomas Hobbes belongs squarely in this tradition: from the 1650s until his death he insisted, against Wallis and the Oxford mathematicians, that he had squared the circle, revising his proof repeatedly but never once conceding the underlying error. What I would like to understand is whether this was simply a personal blind spot, unconnected to his philosophy, or whether it followed naturally from his own official account of why geometry alone among the sciences yields certainty — that we can have perfect knowledge of its objects because we construct them ourselves through definitions grounded in motion, a standard that also led him to dismiss Wallis's algebraic and indivisibilist methods as illegitimate mathematics. Write an essay that weighs these two readings of the Hobbes–Wallis dispute against one another, taking the relevant textual and historical evidence seriously on both sides, and that defends a definite verdict rather than a noncommittal split-the-difference conclusion.
