For my philosophy of mind seminar this term, we've spent the last few weeks on panpsychism, and this week's unit is the combination problem: the difficulty of explaining how a multitude of micro-level subjects of experience could combine into a single macro-level subject, such as a human mind. One of the replies we've read treats priority cosmopsychism as a way out of this difficulty, making the entire cosmos the one fundamental subject of experience and treating individual minds like ours as derivative, partial "decompositions" of that cosmic subject rather than as sums built up from many micro-subjects. Please argue that this reply is illusory rather than a genuine solution — that priority cosmopsychism does not avoid the combination problem but merely relocates it into an equally difficult "decombination" (or boundary) problem: explaining how and why a single, unified cosmic experience differentiates into the many distinct, bounded subjects that each of us seems to be. In making your case, address whether there is any principled reason to think decombination is easier to explain than combination, or whether this is simply the same problem viewed from the other direction.
