In the Guide for the Perplexed (III:32 and III:46), Maimonides explains the Torah's institution of animal sacrifice as a concession to the Israelites' deep-seated habituation to the sacrificial cults of the idolaters among whom they had lived: because human nature cannot leap suddenly from one extreme to another, God redirected an already-familiar mode of worship toward Himself, intending it as a stage on the way to the purer worship of prayer. Commenting on Leviticus 1:9, Nahmanides firmly rejects this account: he points out that Abel, Noah, and the patriarchs offered sacrifices that God received favorably long before any idolatry existed in the world at all. Please argue that Maimonides' accommodationist theory can be defended against this objection, addressing directly whether the pre-Sinaitic history of sacrifice actually undermines his explanation of why the Torah legislates sacrifice as a commandment, or whether some principled distinction allows the theory to survive intact.
