So I do a lot of formal verification work at my job (model checking, temporal logic, that kind of thing), and CTL model checkers quantify over a branching tree of all the possible future paths through a system's states. A coworker mentioned that this whole "branching time" framework actually traces back to a philosopher, Arthur Prior, who was trying to fix a 2000 year old headache from Aristotle known as the "sea battle" problem: if the sentence "there will be a sea battle tomorrow," said today, is already either true or false, doesnt that mean the outcome is somehow already fixed before anyone's made a move, which seems to threaten the whole idea that the future is genuinely open?

I fell down a bit of a rabbit hole on this and found out there's a real fight between two camps about how to handle it. One side, "Ockhamism," has this idea of a "thin red line" running through the branching tree that marks which branch is the actual future, so the sentence IS already true or false today, we just can't know which yet. The other side, supervaluationism (which I gather descends from an idea of Peirce's), says the sentence is neither true nor false right now, only becoming settled once the battle happens or doesn't. I saw a paper referenced, I think by Belnap and Green, called something like "Indeterminism and the Thin Red Line," arguing the first view secretly smuggles in a totally undetectable metaphysical fact about which branch we're "really" on. That seems like a fair hit, but I don't get how the second view isn't weird in its own way either, shouldn't "it'll happen or it won't" be automatically true, full stop, even if neither half is?

Could you write me a dialogue between an Ockhamist and a supervaluationist actually hashing this out, staying focused on the sea battle sentence specifically? I want real back and forth where they engage each other's strongest objections instead of just restating their own view past each other, and I'd like each of them to land at least one genuine hit on the other's position by the end, even if neither one fully concedes.
