So I've been in a running argument with a friend from my meetup about the two-envelope paradox for like three weeks now (I know, we need better hobbies). You know the setup: two envelopes, one has twice the money of the other, you open yours and see $X, and there's the classic tempting argument that the other envelope has 0.5(2X) + 0.5(X/2) = 1.25X in expectation, so you should always switch. Everyone who's read the standard writeups (SEP, the wikipedia page, a dozen LW comment threads) gives basically the same resolution: that argument secretly needs a prior over the smaller amount such that P(other = 2x | you see x) = 1/2 for literally every possible x, and no proper prior can satisfy that for every x, so the step is just invalid. Fine, I buy that. But that resolution is explicitly about the version where you peek and see a specific x. There's a second "you don't even have to open the envelope" version of the argument — just call the amount in your envelope A, note the other one is 2A or A/2 with probability 1/2 each by symmetry, and take E[other] = 1.25 E[A] — and that version never mentions a prior at all, so "no proper prior exists" can't be the whole story for it.

Here's my attempt at untangling that second version, and I'd like a sanity check before I write it up properly on my blog. My guess is that the symbolic version smuggles in the assumption that E[A | my envelope happens to be the smaller one] equals the plain unconditional E[A] — i.e., that learning you hold the smaller pile shouldn't move your estimate of your own pile at all. That's false for basically any actual distribution: if L is the smaller amount and H = 2L the larger, then unconditionally E[A] is a 50/50 mix of E[L] and E[H], so E[A] = 1.5 E[L], while E[A | smaller] is just E[L], i.e. (2/3)E[A]. Push that correction through instead of the naive substitution and E[other] comes out to exactly E[A] every time, for any proper prior over L with finite mean — no impropriety required anywhere in the derivation.

What I can't decide is whether that's a genuinely seperate bug from the "no proper prior makes it 1/2 for every x" story, or secretly the same fact wearing a different hat. When I worked a concrete case (L uniform over {1,2,4,...,2^N} for some big but finite N, so definitely a proper prior) the naive "50/50 no matter what you see" claim actually holds EXACTLY at every interior value of x, and only breaks down at the two boundary values (x=1 and x=2^N, where you know for sure which pile you're holding) — which makes me suspect it's really one phenomenon after all and my "second version" framing is just double-counting the same insight. Could you check the algebra above, tell me honestly whether this is a real distinction or whether I'm fooling myself, and say which of the two framings (impropriety of priors, vs. the conditional-expectation-isn't-the-unconditional-one point) you'd actually lead with if you were explaining this to someone who's already heard the standard "it needs an improper prior" line and found it unsatisfying?
