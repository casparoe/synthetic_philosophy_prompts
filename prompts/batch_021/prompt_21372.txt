Over the last year or so, entirely on my own time and with no philosophy background to speak of, I've worked through most of the accessible material on panpsychism I could find — Philip Goff's popular books, a stack of Aeon and IAI essays, a few long podcast interviews. It struck me as a genuinely elegant way to sidestep the hard problem of consciousness, right up until I ran into the combination problem, which I now understand is treated as one of the view's most serious internal difficulties rather than a minor technicality. If fundamental particles carry some primitive flicker of experience, there's no obvious story about how billions of those separate, private points of view could ever add up to the single, unified experience I seem to be having right now. I gather some panpsychists respond by positing a brute, irreducible relation — something like "phenomenal bonding" — that just does the combining, while others think this response barely makes sense, on the grounds that a subject of experience isn't the kind of thing that can be built out of smaller subjects the way a wall is built out of bricks: bricks don't have points of view that would need to vanish, or be overridden, for the wall to acquire one of its own.

What's actually kept me turning this over at night, though, is a further move I only found gestured at in passing: rather than starting from countless micro-subjects and building upward, "priority cosmopsychism" treats the entire cosmos as the one fundamental subject of experience, with individual minds like mine somehow carved out of, or derived from, that single undivided point of view. Whoever holds this view still owes an account of the mirror-image puzzle — how one undivided cosmic experience could ever give rise to bounded, separate-feeling subjects like you and me — which I've seen called the decombination problem, and which is sometimes claimed to be the easier of the two puzzles to solve. I honestly can't tell whether that's true or just the same mystery wearing a new name, and I don't know a single person in my life willing to hash it out with me. Could you write a dialogue between a committed micropsychist, who builds minds bottom-up and is prepared to defend phenomenal bonding, and a committed priority cosmopsychist, who takes the universe itself as the one basic subject — imagine them cornered together at a coffee table after a conference panel, unable to let the disagreement drop? I'd like each of them to press the other on their actual weakest point rather than on a caricature of it, to respond to what the other has just said rather than simply restate their own view, and to end the exchange still disagreeing about something precise: whether decombination is genuinely more tractable than combination, or simply relocates the same unexplained unity somewhere else.
