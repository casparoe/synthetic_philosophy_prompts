I spent thirty-four years as a field physiologist, most of it studying thermoregulation in alpine ungulates, and it is only since retiring that I have had the leisure to read philosophy properly rather than in stolen half-hours before a grant deadline. For the past year I have belonged to a small reading group — two retired academics, a former schoolteacher, and myself — that works through classic papers in the philosophy of biology. Last month we spent two sessions on Gould and Lewontin's 1979 paper, "The Spandrels of San Marco and the Panglossian Paradigm," together with some of the sharper exchanges it set off between Gould and more committed adaptationists in the years that followed. It left two of the group, Miriam and Desmond, still arguing well after the rest of us had moved on to coffee, and I have not been able to settle in my own mind which of them had the better of it.

Miriam's position was that the paper has essentially been vindicated by history: biologists really did have a habit of assuming that any trait must exist because selection built it for its current job, and of inventing a plausible story to that effect instead of testing it against rivals such as developmental constraint or sheer non-functional byproduct — Gould's own favourite illustration being the human chin, which he treated as nothing more than what is left over when two separately growing parts of the jaw fail to keep pace with one another. Desmond answered that the paper's central image tells against it once you look closely: a dome mounted on rounded arches leaves the shape of the corner infill entirely fixed, with no freedom in it whatsoever, whereas nothing remotely that strong has ever been shown for the chin, or for any other proposed biological "spandrel" — so the byproduct label is borrowing a rigor from the architecture that the biology has not actually earned. He added a sharper point: once Gould, via the later notion of "exaptation," allowed that a spandrel could always be picked up later and put to some new use, the whole scheme became unfalsifiable in exactly the way the original paper accused adaptationists of being — any trait that looks designed can be called an adaptation, and any trait that does not can be called a spandrel that got exapted, with no possible observation left to rule out either verdict. Miriam replied that this is a fact about how the concept gets misused, not a defect in the concept itself, since a careless adaptationist story is open to the identical charge, and that Desmond was demanding a fit between metaphor and biology that Gould and Lewontin never claimed and that no illustrative metaphor could possibly survive. Desmond's rejoinder was that this defence saves the paper only by shrinking its claim down to a truism nobody serious ever denied — that a trait is sometimes not for what it is currently used for, and that people sometimes reason sloppily — which is not the claim that made the essay famous or turned "adaptationist" into something close to an accusation.

I would be grateful if you would go through their exchange move by move and say which of them is right about each specific point, rather than simply declaring an overall winner, and note anything you think both of them are overlooking. And since I spent my working life looking for tests that could actually decide a question, I would especially value your view on whether this dispute could be settled empirically even in principle, or whether it is, at bottom, a disagreement about what we are allowed to mean by "adaptation" and "spandrel" in the first place.
