"Ubuntu" is a word I first ran across years ago in Desmond Tutu's writing on reconciliation, tied to the phrase "I am because we are" — the idea that a person's identity is bound up with other people rather than being some free-standing thing that exists prior to those relationships. Since then I've kept reading around the topic on my own, and I recently worked through an essay by the Nigerian philosopher Ifeanyi Menkiti that pushes this much further than I expected. On his view, full personhood isn't simply given to you by being born a human being; it's something the community confers on you gradually, through upbringing, ritual incorporation, and the discharge of moral and social obligations — to the point that a newborn, or (in some of the traditional settings he draws on) an infant who dies before the relevant naming or incorporation rites, doesn't yet count as a person in quite the sense a respected elder does, and an adult who fails badly enough at his duties to the community can be said to have partly forfeited that same standing.

Digging further, I found a rebuttal by the Ghanaian philosopher Kwame Gyekye, who is happy to grant that community shapes and completes a person's character but balks at this "radical" version: on his view there has to be some individual endowment already in place beforehand — a capacity for rational agency, a will, a capacity to evaluate and sometimes push back against the very norms one is raised with — because otherwise there's nothing there for the community to shape or confer personhood onto in the first place. That objection struck me as pretty forceful, but I can imagine the first philosopher having more to say than just restating the original claim, and I haven't been able to work out for myself whether the two of them are really disagreeing about what a person fundamentally is, or more about how differently-situated human beings ought to be regarded and treated in practice.

Could you write a dialogue between two philosophers who each hold one of these positions? They needn't be named after Menkiti and Gyekye specifically, but should be clearly recognizable as, respectively, a "radical" and a "moderate" communitarian about personhood, and they should be evenly matched — genuinely responding to each other's arguments rather than talking past one another. I'd like the exchange to get concrete by working through the two hard cases above (the infant who dies before incorporation rites, and the elder who has lived selfishly) rather than resting at the level of slogans, with the moderate pressing the "nothing there to shape" objection as hard as they can and the radical giving a real answer to it rather than just reasserting the thesis. Ideally, by the end, the two of them will have made some progress on the question that stumped me: whether their dispute is at bottom a metaphysical one about the nature of personhood, or an ethical one about how personhood should be assessed and treated.
