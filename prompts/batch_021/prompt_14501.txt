Now retired after a long career in chemistry, I have taken up philosophy in earnest, and in working through Hacking and Searle on social ontology, I keep encountering the claim that race, gender, and money — unlike the kinds I dealt with professionally — are merely "socially constructed." In my own field, whether a sample counted as gold was always fixed by its atomic number, not by anyone's opinion. A socially constructed kind, as I understand the term, exists instead only because some community collectively believes that it does, from which it would seem to follow that such a kind must be freely revisable by whoever changes the relevant beliefs. A few friends and I, for instance, ought to be able to make our handwritten notes count as money simply by agreeing among ourselves to treat them that way, and the same should presumably hold for any other socially constructed category. Since that plainly is not how the world works, I suspect that "social construction" is a much weaker, almost rhetorical notion than its champions in philosophy and sociology suppose. Is that a fair conclusion, or am I misunderstanding the idea?
