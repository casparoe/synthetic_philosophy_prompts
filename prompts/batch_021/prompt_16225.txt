I'm drafting a feature on the intellectual prehistory of the idea that political and legal authority ultimately rests on force rather than on justice — a lineage that runs from Thrasymachus through Hobbes and into modern debates between legal positivists and natural-law theorists. Rather than working from textbook summaries, I've been reading Pascal's Pensées directly, since he's sometimes invoked as an unusually blunt way station on that route, and I've gotten stuck on a short fragment usually catalogued under the header "Justice, force."

In the translation I've been using, it runs roughly as follows: "It is just that what is just should be followed; it is necessary that what is strongest should be followed. Justice without force is powerless; force without justice is tyrannical... Justice is subject to dispute; force is easily recognized and not disputed. So we have not been able to give force to justice, because force has contradicted justice and declared that justice was unjust, and that it itself was just. And thus, being unable to make what is just strong, we have made what is strong just."

What I can't work out is the shape of the argument as a whole, rather than any single sentence. The opening sounds almost like a reformer's plea — align might with right! — but the closing lines seem to pull the rug out from under that reading, implying instead that what we call "justice" is simply whatever force has managed to get dressed up as. Is Pascal equivocating between a claim about how people come to use the word "just" and a claim about what justice really is, or is that slide the whole point of the fragment? And how should it be read against the rest of his thought: as a piece of cynical political sociology offered more or less in Pascal's own voice, in the spirit of Thrasymachus; as a diagnostic claim that only makes sense inside his Jansenist picture of a fallen world, where human justice is inherently corrupted and force is a stopgap that Providence merely tolerates; or as one move within the larger apologetic argument of the Pensées, meant to soften the reader up for faith by first destroying their confidence in worldly justice and reason — in the same spirit as his remarks elsewhere on custom and what he calls the "mystical foundation" of authority? I'd also be curious how seriously scholars take the resemblance to Hobbes, given that the two were writing within a few years of each other but from very different starting premises. Any help walking through the logic of the fragment and placing it within this wider context would be great.
