"Round 1: R is strictly dominated by C for the column player — 1 > -5 in row T, 2 > -5 in row M, 1 > -5 in row B — so we can drop R. (Side note: it doesn't matter that I did a column first; iterated elimination of dominated strategies always lands on the same surviving set no matter what order you eliminate in, whether you're eliminating by strict or by weak dominance, so I could equally have started by looking for a dominated row.) Round 2: once R is gone we only need to look at the L/C sub-game. B isn't dominated by T on its own (T only gets 1 at C, worse than B's 2 there) and isn't dominated by M on its own (M only gets 1 at L, worse than B's 2 there). But mixing T and M can still dominate B: if the row player plays T with probability q and M with probability 1-q, the mix earns 4q+1(1-q) = 1+3q at L and 1q+3(1-q) = 3-2q at C. Both need to beat B's payoff of 2, so 1+3q>2 and 3-2q>2, giving 1/3 < q < 1/2 — so e.g. q=0.4 gives a mixed strategy that strictly dominates B, and B is eliminated. Round 3: in what's left, T doesn't dominate M (T does better at L, M does better at C) and L doesn't dominate C (L does better against T, C does better against M), pure or mixed, so no further elimination is possible and the game isn't dominance-solvable. Since neither player is left with a dominant strategy, there's no pure-strategy equilibrium here — the equilibrium has to be a mixed one. Setting the row player's payoffs from T and M equal, 1+3q=3-2q, gives q=2/5, so the column player mixes L and C with probability 2/5 and 3/5; setting the column player's payoffs from L and C equal, 1+2p=2-p, gives p=1/3, so the row player mixes T and M with probability 1/3 and 2/3. So the unique Nash equilibrium of the game is row playing (1/3 T, 2/3 M) against column playing (2/5 L, 3/5 C)."
