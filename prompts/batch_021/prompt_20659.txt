The following is a question from an upper-level philosophy of mind exam, together with a student's written answer to it.

Question (15 points). In "Minds, Brains, and Programs" (1980), John Searle presents the Chinese Room argument against what he calls "Strong AI."
(a) [6 points] Reconstruct the core argument, stating its premises and conclusion, and make clear what work the syntax/semantics distinction is doing.
(b) [5 points] Explain the Systems Reply and Searle's rejoinder to it.
(c) [4 points] State precisely what Searle takes the argument to establish, and what it leaves open about the possibility of artificial minds.

Student's answer:

Searle's target is what he calls Strong AI: the thesis that a computer running the right program does not merely simulate a mind but thereby has one — that implementing the correct program is sufficient, all by itself, for genuine understanding. The argument can be put as follows. Programs are purely syntactic: they consist of formal rules for manipulating symbols defined by their shape, not their meaning. Minds, by contrast, have semantic content — a thought or utterance is about something. Syntax is neither constitutive of nor sufficient for semantics, since a system can satisfy every formal condition on symbol manipulation while attaching no meaning to any of the symbols involved. It follows that no program, however sophisticated, is constitutive of or sufficient for a mind. The Chinese Room illustrates the crucial premise: Searle, who knows no Chinese, sits in a room manipulating Chinese symbols according to an English rule book, well enough that native speakers outside are convinced they are conversing with a fluent speaker, and yet he understands nothing of what he is doing. Searle makes essentially the same point with the China Brain thought experiment, in which the entire population of China is enlisted to reproduce the firing pattern of a brain's neurons by relaying signals to one another; since it would be absurd to credit the resulting system with genuine thoughts, this confirms that formal symbol-shuffling, on any scale, is not enough to constitute a mind.

The Systems Reply concedes that the man does not understand Chinese but denies that this refutes Strong AI, since on that hypothesis it is the whole system — man, rule book, symbols, and room together — that is credited with understanding, not the man in isolation. Searle's rejoinder is to have the man internalize the entire system: memorize the rule book and all the symbols, and do the manipulations in his head, with no room and no external apparatus left over. Since nothing is now left outside him, and he still understands no Chinese, there is no separate "system" left for the reply to appeal to, so the reply fails.

Searle takes the argument to show that no computer could ever really think or understand anything, no matter how cleverly it is programmed, and he rules out the possibility that any future machine, however constructed, could achieve genuine understanding. This is why he regards Strong AI as mistaken not merely about the details of some particular program, but about the very idea that a machine could be made to think.
