In a philosophy of biology seminar I'm taking, the units-of-selection debate came up through the standard case of eusocial insects, and it sparked a disagreement between two classmates that we didn't get to resolve before the seminar ended. The shared premise both of them accepted: worker honeybees routinely forgo their own reproduction, and will die defending the hive, in order to help their mother queen produce more offspring.

(1) Renata argued that this is a clean case of selection acting at the level of the colony: colonies with a higher proportion of self-sacrificing workers produce more successful daughter colonies (via swarming) than colonies with more "selfish" workers, so it is colony-level reproductive success, not the individual bee's, that does the explanatory work, and biologists are entitled to treat the hive itself as the relevant unit of selection.

(2) Marcus objected that none of this requires talk of colonies at all. Because of haplodiploidy, a female worker is more closely related to her full sisters (sharing about three-quarters of their genes) than she would be to her own offspring (sharing one-half), so a gene that inclines her to help rear sisters instead of reproducing herself can still increase its own frequency in the population — this is just Hamilton's rule, rB > C, operating on individuals via their genes. Calling the colony "the unit being selected" is loose talk at best, on a par with saying selection favors "the team" rather than the players on it.

(3) Renata replied that this concedes more than Marcus realizes. Hamilton's rule, she pointed out, can itself be derived from statistics that partition variance in fitness into a within-group component and a between-group component — exactly the partition that explicit group-selection models use. If the kin-selection math and the group-selection math are just two ways of carving up the same variance, then the formalism itself doesn't say the individual/gene framing is more correct than the colony framing; preferring one is a matter of bookkeeping convenience, not biological fact, so Marcus's dismissal of colony-level talk as "loose" isn't something the mathematics actually licenses.

(4) Marcus answered that formal convertibility doesn't make the two framings equally good explanations. The kin-selection story tracks the actual causal pathway — relatedness making a sister's offspring a partial substitute for one's own — without requiring colonies to compete with or replace one another the way organisms do. A default habit of saying "the colony is the unit of selection" invites people to assume that kind of between-colony competition is doing the real work even in cases, like this one, where it isn't. So even granting that the statistics can be rewritten either way, he thinks there's a genuine, non-bookkeeping reason to prefer the gene's-eye framing.
