I spent my working life as a solid-state physicist, doing a fair amount of numerical simulation along the way, and I've only recently started reading philosophy in earnest — so forgive me if my framing here is a little naïve. Something in epistemic logic strikes me as odd: the standard possible-worlds treatment of knowledge and belief has the consequence that an agent who believes P thereby believes every logical consequence of P, however remote — the so-called logical omniscience problem. This seems to treat entailment as a kind of free action, indifferent to how hard the derivation actually is to carry out. Could you construct a concrete example built around a specific, genuinely hard computational problem — say, factoring a large semiprime, or an instance of an NP-complete problem — in which it seems clearly wrong to credit an agent with knowing or believing some proposition Q merely because Q follows logically from something they already believe? The wrongness should turn specifically on the fact that deriving Q would take computationally intractable time, not merely on the agent's psychology or limited attention — and I'd like the example to make clear why complexity-theoretic tractability, rather than mere computability, is doing the real philosophical work in diagnosing what has gone wrong.
