In three weeks a few of us are throwing my wife Mara a small dinner that has turned into both a leaving party and a belated birthday celebration, and I've been asked to give the toast. She has spent the last six years at a small alignment research group working almost entirely on corrigibility and what her colleagues call the shutdown problem: the puzzle of designing an agent that won't disable its own off-switch, won't maneuver anyone into pressing it early either, and that otherwise remains genuinely useful rather than collapsing into something that simply does whatever it's told. The group's whole research agenda rested on the discovery that this is far harder than it sounds -- that any sufficiently coherent, capable agent will tend to have a stake, one way or another, in whether the switch gets used, so that real indifference to it has to be built in rather than assumed. Their funding was pulled this spring, and Mara has taken, with a tiredness that is only half a joke, to pointing out that the team devoted to the problem of graceful shutdown has now been shut down itself, on short notice, with none of the off-switch etiquette they spent years trying to design into their own systems, and with no idea yet what she'll do next.

Please write me a five-minute toast built around this. The room will be split down the middle between former colleagues who will catch every reference to the switch, to Armstrong-style utility indifference, and to whatever it was that a decision theorist named Thornley -- I think that's the name; Mara has corrected me on it more than once -- proposed about incomplete preferences, and both our families, who have only ever been told that she "does something with computers." I don't want the idea to sit there as decoration for some generic sentiment about letting go: I want the toast to actually lean on the real distinction her field draws between corrigibility and mere obedience -- the fact that what she spent six years building toward was never a system that just does as it's told -- and use that distinction to say something true and specific about her, and about the difference between leaving a project well and simply being switched off. One good joke about off-switches would help the room breathe, but only if it earns its place rather than being dropped in for its own sake.
