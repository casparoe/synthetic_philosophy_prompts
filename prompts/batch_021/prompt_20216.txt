So a few of us from my local rationalist-adjacent meetup (the kind of group that did a full read-through of Superintelligence a while back and now doesn't know what to do with itself) started doing monthly "dead philosophers who kind of called it early" nights, and this month was Leibniz's Monadology. My friend pulled up the mill passage, section 17, and confidently declared that Leibniz basically invented the Chinese Room argument 250 years before Searle, and everyone just nodded along, but something about that felt too fast to me and I can't quite put my finger on why -- which is bugging me because this whole question of whether an LLM could ever really "understand" anything keeps coming up on the forums, and I'd like to actually understand the historical argument instead of just citing it as a factoid.

Here's the passage (Latta translation): "it must be confessed that perception, and that which depends upon it, is inexplicable by mechanical causes, that is to say, by figures and motions. And, supposing that there were a machine so constructed as to think, feel, and have perception, we could imagine it increased in size while retaining the same proportions, so that we might be able to enter it as into a mill. Now, on going into it, we should find only pieces working upon one another, but never anything by which to explain a perception. This explanation must therefore be sought in the simple substance, and not in the composite or in the machine."

What I can't tell is whether Leibniz is making the same kind of move Searle makes, or a pretty different one that just looks similar if you squint. My hunch is that Leibniz's whole argument is actually leaning on his commitment that only a genuinely simple (partless) substance -- a monad -- can ground the unity of perception, and that no amount of parts pushing on other parts could ever add up to that unity, which is really an argument about unity/simplicity, not about syntax failing to reach semantics the way Searle's is. But I'm loosing confidence in my own read the longer I stare at it, and I don't have the Leibniz background to know if "simple substance" is doing something narrower or weirder than I think it is. Could you walk through what the mill argument actually commits Leibniz to on its own terms, and then give me a real verdict on how much of the "Leibniz basically invented the Chinese Room" claim survives that close reading versus how much is just two arguments that happen to share a vibe?
