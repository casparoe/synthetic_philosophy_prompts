This term I am developing a new unit for my graduate epistemology seminar that moves beyond the classical foundationalist/coherentist debate about the regress problem into the more recent infinitist proposals associated with Peter Klein. While drafting my notes, I keep running up against a worry that I cannot quite resolve to my own satisfaction, and I suspect my students will raise some version of it the moment I present the material. Elsewhere in philosophy, an infinite regress is standardly treated as an automatic mark against a position: we invoke it against cosmological arguments for a First Cause, against the Third Man regress that afflicts Plato's theory of Forms, and against Lewis Carroll's regress of inference in "What the Tortoise Said to Achilles." It therefore strikes me as somewhat ad hoc for epistemologists to turn around and insist that, in the specific case of justificatory regresses, an infinite chain of reasons is perfectly acceptable after all. Infinitism looks less like a genuine solution to the regress problem than a stipulation that the usual strictures against infinite regresses simply do not apply here, dressed up with a more respectable label.

Before I present this unit, I would appreciate help thinking through whether that impression is fair to the view. In particular, I would like to know whether there is some principled, general basis, rather than one invented ad hoc for this purpose, for distinguishing vicious from benign infinite regresses across philosophy, and if so, whether Klein's infinitism actually satisfies whatever standard that basis sets, or whether my suspicion that this amounts to special pleading on the epistemologists' part is correct.
