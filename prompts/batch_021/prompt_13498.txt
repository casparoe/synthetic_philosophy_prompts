I'm taking a philosophy of biology seminar this term, and we've been working through the debate between Robert Cummins's causal-role account of biological function and the etiological (selected-effects) account descending from Larry Wright and later sharpened by Karen Neander and Ruth Millikan. I'd like to test the two views against a case of exaptation: Gould and Vrba's example of feathers, standardly described as selected first for thermoregulation and only later favored for their contribution to flight. Godfrey-Smith's "modern history" theory tries to rescue the etiological account here by privileging a trait's recent selective history over its full history, so that flight comes out as the current function, while Cummins's account seems to dissolve the question by simply relativizing the ascribed function to whichever capacity, insulation or flight, an analysis happens to target. Please compare how the two accounts, in their strongest forms, would actually assign a function to feathers in this case. Which one gives the less ad hoc verdict, and why?
