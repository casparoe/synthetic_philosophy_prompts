I spent almost 40 years as a physicist before retiring, and lately I've been reading philosophy for fun, which has lead to a running argument with my nephew (a semi-pro poker player who figures that makes him the family's resident expert on probabilty). His claim: there's no such thing as a genuine probability for a one-off event -- an election, a specific patient's prognosis, whether it rains tomorrow -- because probability only really means something as a frequency over a long run of repeated trials. So when a forecaster says "70% chance of rain" or a doctor says "30% chance the treatment fails," he thinks that's not a real probability, just somebody's confidence dressed up as a number. It bugs me but I can't quite say why he'd be wrong, if he even is. Is there a respectable way to defend the idea that a single, non-repeatable event can have a genuine probability, and if so does that probability actually tell you something about the world, or is my nephew basically right that it's all just opinion in a tuxedo?
