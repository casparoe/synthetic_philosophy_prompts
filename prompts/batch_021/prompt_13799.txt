I'm a backend engineer at a small startup that builds an in-home companion robot for elderly users who live alone, and thanks to being the person who always asks the inconvenient question in meetings, I've ended up on the five-person committee that has to sign off on any feature with ethical or safety implications before it ships. The feature currently in front of us lets the robot override a standing instruction from the resident — something like "don't ever call my son unless I explicitly ask you to" — once its internal risk model, built from fall-detection sensors, speech patterns, and deviations from daily routine, crosses a threshold suggesting a medical emergency or serious cognitive decline. Product wants the release notes to describe this as the robot "exercising ethical judgment in the resident's best interest," and it's that phrase, more than the engineering choice itself, that's actually splitting the committee.

The committee is roughly split down the middle on how the override should be built, and the split doesn't line up neatly with who's comfortable with the marketing language. About half of us, including our lead ML engineer, are fine with a small hard-coded decision layer sitting on top of the risk model — a handful of human-written rules stating, in effect, that duty of care overrides a standing autonomy instruction once estimated risk crosses a clinician-set threshold — on the grounds that this keeps the actual value judgment visible, auditable, and contestable rather than buried in a model's weights. The other half want to scrap that separate layer and fold the override directly into the same learned preference model that already predicts what the resident would probably want in ambiguous situations, trained on annotated transcripts of what professional caregivers actually did in comparable cases; their argument is that a hand-written "duty of care beats autonomy above X" rule isn't obviously less arbitrary or less opaque than a learned threshold, it's just arbitrary in a way that happens to feel more comfortable to engineers. Cutting across both camps, a few of us, myself included, suspect that "exercising ethical judgment" oversells whichever architecture wins, since either way the robot is just executing a policy that clinicians and engineers already worked out on its behalf, rather than weighing the situation itself in the moment.

I have to circulate a written recommendation before Friday's sign-off meeting. Could you draft that memo? It should come down clearly on one path forward — for instance, approving one architecture with specific conditions, treating both as ethically equivalent and leaving the choice to engineering, or blocking the shipment and/or the marketing language until something changes — justify it in terms the engineers on the losing side of the architecture debate would recognize as a fair account of their actual position, and be candid about the strongest reason someone on the committee might have for rejecting your recommendation.
