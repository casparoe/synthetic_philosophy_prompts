So I went down a rabbit hole this week that started with an old Overcoming Bias post on value-of-information and ended with me reading about I.J. Good's 1967 result that people just call Good's theorem -- the one that says for a Bayesian expected-utility maximizer, the expected value of a cost-free observation before you act is never negative, and is strictly positive as long as there's some chance it'll actually change what you do. It gets invoked a lot, sometimes only implicitly, as basically a proof that the rationalist slogan "more evidence cant hurt, so go gather it" isn't just a vibe but an actual theorem.

Except then I started thinking about all the standard cases where smart people deliberately choose ignorance and call it rational, and it doesnt feel like they're just being irrational or lazy. Like people who decline free genetic testing for things like APOE4/Alzheimer's risk because there's no treatment decision it would change, just decades of dread; the "willful blindness" doctrine in corporate and criminal law, where organizations structure reporting lines precisely so nobody officially knows about a problem; and the classic negotiation move where a principal deliberately keeps their own agent ignorant of the real reservation price, so the agent literally can't leak it under pressure. All of those look like cases of correctly recognizing that finding something out would leave you (or your side) worse off, which seems to be in real tension with "free information is always weakly good."

Can you lay out the most important implications of Good's theorem here? I don't just want "here's the theorem, cool" -- I want a real point by point account of which specific assumption behind the proof (that the info is truly costless, that it's a single decision-maker rather than a strategic setting, that you update and act rationally afterward, whatever) is being quietly violated or sidestepped in each of those ignorance cases, versus any of them that would actually count as a genuine counterexample rather than a change of subject. And if you can, tie it back to something practical: does the theorem give me any real grounds for my longstanding personal mantra that I should always get the extra test done / read the extra report / ask the extra question, or is that mantra secretly resting on idealizations that don't hold for a bounded, non-Bayesian, socially-embedded human like me?
