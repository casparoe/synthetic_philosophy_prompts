Consider a light L wired to two switches, A and B, in parallel, so that L is on iff A is on or B is on (or both). Suppose both A and B are actually flipped on, so L is on.
(a) Does L counterfactually depend on A in the plain "but-for" sense -- i.e., if A had been off, would L have been off?
(b) Given your answer to (a), what verdict does Lewis's 1973 counterfactual-dependence theory deliver about whether A caused L to be on, and why is this considered a problem case for that theory?
(c) Explain how an interventionist analysis (e.g. Woodward's) can still deliver the intuitive verdict that A is a cause of L, despite the failure of the plain test in (a).

And here's the student's answer:

(a) No -- L doesn't counterfactually depend on A in the plain sense, since B is also on, so even if A had been off, B alone would've kept L on. So there's no but-for dependence between A and L.

(b) Given that, Lewis's 1973 theory, which analyzes causation as counterfactual dependence of the effect on the cause, has to say A didn't cause L, since L doesn't depend on it in that sense. This is the classic problem of symmetric overdetermination -- both switches are individually sufficient for L, so neither one passes the but-for test, yet intuitively both are causes of L being on. This is basically why Lewis eventually gave up on plain dependence and moved over to Woodward's interventionist semantics, which was built to handle exactly these cases.

(c) The interventionist move is to bring B in as a way of testing A's contribution against a fixed background, instead of just asking "what if A alone were off." Concretely, we hold B fixed at its actual setting (on) and then intervene on A. Since B is still separately doing its own sufficient work, this isolates A's contribution rather than letting B mask it -- once we control for B this way, we can see that A is doing real causal work in producing L, which the plain test in (a) missed entirely. So the moral is that fixing the values of other causally relevant variables is what lets interventionism recover the right verdict where naive dependence fails.
