So this whole thing got kicked off by an argument in an SSC-diaspora discord about whether "ADHD" or "depression" count as dysfunctions in any principled sense, versus just traits that are a bad fit for modern life. Somebody brought up Wakefield's harmful-dysfunction analysis of mental disorder, which leans heavily on the etiological/evolutionary notion of biological function, and that lead me down a rabbit hole into the actual philosophy-of-biology literature on what "function" even means. I wrote up my current understanding as a doc for the group and would like a second pair of eyes before I embarrass myself by posting it as if I know what I'm talking about. Here's the relevant chunk:

Wright's original 1973 move was to define function etiologically: X's function is whatever effect of X explains why X is there at all, a definition general enough to cover both natural selection and, deliberately, artifacts like thermostats. Neander tightened this up for biology into the "selected effects" version everyone actually uses now: the function is specifically the effect selection favored, which is exactly why a heart's function is pumping blood and not making its thump-thump sound -- the sound never explains why selection kept hearts around, so it doesn't get to count no matter how reliably it happens. Cummins, in the same decade, proposed something totally different: pick a system, pick some capacity of that system you're trying to explain, and a part's function (relative to that analysis) is just whatever causal contribution it makes to the capacity, no history required at all -- which is why Cummins-functions work fine for one-off systems, non-biological systems, or brand new mutations with zero selective history behind them yet. The reason people keep refighting this forty-year-old argument is malfunction: etiological accounts explain for free why a heart with a hole in it still has the function of pumping blood (that's still what it was selected for, it's just bad at it now), whereas on a strict Cummins analysis a damaged heart that no longer actually contributes to circulation seems to just lack that function rather than have-it-but-fail-at-it -- Cummins himself basically conceded the point. My tentative take is that this isn't really a knockdown argument for etiology being "the" one true theory of function, it's a symptom of the two accounts answering different questions -- what explains this trait's historical presence, vs. what does this part contribute right now -- and once you see that, the apparent war between philosophers (mostly selected-effects people) and biologists doing comparative anatomy, who Amundson and Lauder say mostly reason in Cummins-style terms anyway, dissolves into people just wanting different explananda.
