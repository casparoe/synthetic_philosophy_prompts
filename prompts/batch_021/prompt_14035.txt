A friend of mine—she trades exotic derivatives for a living and has a mathematically mischievous streak—recently proposed a new game for our monthly poker night, and a week later I still can't decide whether it's a fair bet, or whether "fair bet" even means anything for a game built like this one. I'm a software engineer, so naturally I couldn't just let the question sit; I went and wrote some code to check the math myself.

Here's the game: flip a fair coin repeatedly until it lands heads for the first time. If that happens on flip number n, the payout is 2^n/n dollars: hers to me if n is odd, mine to her if n is even. So heads on the first flip nets me $2, heads on the second costs me $2, the third nets me about $2.67, the fourth costs me $4, and so on—the amounts escalate quickly, but who pays whom keeps alternating. To work out a fair upfront ante, I did the obvious thing: multiply each outcome's payout by its probability and add everything up in the natural order (n = 1, 2, 3, …), which settled just under $0.70. Two things then bothered me. First, when I grouped the very same set of outcomes differently—say, two of my wins for every one of hers, instead of strictly alternating—the running total converged to a completely different number, and other groupings seemed to let me steer the total toward almost any number I liked, or even make it diverge outright, without touching a single probability or payout, only the order in which I added the terms up. Second, when I simulated the game outright instead of computing it by hand—drawing random coin flips and averaging the payouts over millions of trials—the running average never settled down the way it does for every other betting game I've ever coded up; it kept getting knocked sideways by rare, enormous outcomes, and independent reruns gave noticeably different long-run averages each time.

When I described all this to my friend, she waved it off as an ordinary variance problem that more trials would fix. That answer doesn't sit right with me, mainly because the reordering trick has nothing to do with sampling at all: it's exact arithmetic on the complete, fixed list of possibilities, with no randomness involved anywhere. So I'd like an outside opinion. Is there a genuine, correct fair ante for this game, meaning my friend is right and I simply need a smarter simulation to find it? Or is something more fundamental breaking down in what "expected value" is even supposed to mean here—something that would make "what's the fair price to play?" the wrong question to be asking in the first place?
