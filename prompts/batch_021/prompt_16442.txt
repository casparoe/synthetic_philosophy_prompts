I'm a retired physicist -- forty-odd years in condensed matter, and now, in retirement, hopelessly hooked on philosophy of mind -- and these days I sit on my community hospital's clinical ethics committee. We've got a patient who's been in unresponsive wakefulness syndrome (what use to be called a vegetative state) for over a year, and her neurologist wants to run the TMS-EEG "zap-and-zip" test -- the Perturbational Complexity Index that Massimini and Tononi's group built out of Integrated Information Theory -- before the next family care conference, as one more data point on whether to keep the feeding tube in. That's forced a bigger question onto the committee's agenda: whether to approve PCI testing as a standing part of our prognosis workup generally, not just for her case. Half the committee's keen, since PCI has flagged covert consciousness in behaviorally unresponsive patients before, echoing the old fMRI mental-imagery studies. The other half point out that IIT itself got publicly branded "pseudoscience" by a pile of consciousness researchers a couple years back, partly over the "tiny grid of logic gates could have more phi than a human brain" problem that Aaronson raised and that never really got a clean answer -- so how can a number derived from that theory honestly carry weight in a life-or-death call? Could you draft the memo I'll bring to Thursday's meeting -- an actual yes/no/yes-with-conditions recommendation, with a review point down the line, that takes that objection seriously rather than just waving it off?
