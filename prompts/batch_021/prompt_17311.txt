I sit on my museum's conservation committee. A mid-twentieth-century bronze figure in our collection has a failed internal armature and active bronze disease; the proposed treatment would, over several campaigns, excise and replace roughly two-thirds of the original metal with new sections cast from the sculptor's surviving plaster molds, leaving the piece visually and dimensionally indistinguishable from how it left the studio. The sculptor's estate maintains that the result still counts as 'the original bronze' for cataloguing and insurance purposes, since every replaced section follows the artist's own molds; one trustee argues that once most of the historical metal is gone we would in effect own an authorized replica rather than the object we accessioned, and that continuing to catalogue and insure it as an untouched original would misrepresent what we hold. Draft the committee's decision memo for next month's board meeting: recommend a concrete course of action -- proceed, decline, or proceed under specific cataloguing and disclosure conditions -- and engage seriously with the trustee's objection rather than treating standard conservation practice as though it already settles the question.
