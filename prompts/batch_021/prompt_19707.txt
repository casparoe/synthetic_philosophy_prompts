A friend and I had a long argument last night about coherence arguments for expected-utility maximization, and we left it unresolved. She defended the money-pump argument for completeness: an agent that lacks even a weak preference between two options can be exploited, and—to preempt the obvious reply—you don't need a scenario spread out over time to show this, since a single decision tree, chosen all at once, can be built so that its branching structure alone forces the agent, on pain of a sure loss, to either complete its preferences or select a dominated plan. I argued that this still trades on a substantive assumption about what makes an option "the same" when it recurs at different nodes, and that an agent following a resolute policy—treating earlier commitments as binding rather than re-deciding at each node as if fresh—can avoid the dominated plan without ever ranking the options themselves. She thought resoluteness was just a complete preference over plans wearing a disguise; I wasn't convinced, but couldn't say exactly why not.
