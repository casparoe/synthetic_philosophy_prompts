After forty years running a low-temperature physics laboratory, I retired three years ago and have spent much of the time since auditing philosophy and economics courses at the university where I used to keep an office. Alongside the coursework, I've been pursuing a fairly undisciplined program of reading on my own; at the moment, a secondhand copy of Bentham shares my kitchen table with a rather more technical textbook on consumer theory. This term's seminar on rational choice finally reached revealed preference theory, and I found it one of the most satisfying pieces of theoretical machinery I have encountered outside my own field: rather than asking a consumer to introspect and report how much satisfaction some bundle of goods gives her, one simply observes her choices across a range of budgets, and, provided those choices satisfy the weak and strong axioms of revealed preference, one can construct a utility function such that she behaves, in every relevant respect, as though she were maximizing it. It struck me as exactly the move a physicist would want to make: inferring a hidden scalar quantity from observed behavior, much as we infer temperature from the statistics of molecular collisions rather than from a substance's own testimony about how hot it feels.

What I take this to show is that economics has finally supplied classical utilitarianism with the instrument Bentham's felicific calculus always lacked: an objective, behaviorally grounded measure of a person's pleasure and pain, extracted from choice data rather than from unreliable introspective report. My former colleagues in the economics department seem oddly reluctant to draw this conclusion, but none of the objections they have offered amounts to more than the remark that utility isn't measured that way, which is a strange thing to say about a procedure that is, after all, already standard practice in demand theory. So here is my actual question. Given two neighboring households whose consumption choices have each been used, following the standard procedure, to construct a revealed utility function, what is the correct way to rescale those two functions onto a common footing, so that a town council could sum them and determine which of two competing proposals -- a new playground, say, versus a repaved road -- produces the greater total happiness in the Benthamite sense?
