I spent just over three decades as a vision scientist studying the physiology of cone photoreceptors and color perception, and only since retiring have I started reading philosophy seriously, working through philosophy of mind on my own in the evenings. That is how I came across Frank Jackson's "Mary's Room" thought experiment -- the color scientist who knows every physical fact about vision yet has never seen a color -- and the literature responding to it, including the fact, if I have the history right, that Jackson himself eventually backed away from the anti-physicalist conclusion he had made famous. I even raised it at a retirement reading group some months ago as an amusing puzzle, never expecting it to arrive on my own committee's agenda. But I still serve as the senior scientist member of the research ethics board at the university hospital where I used to work, and this week the puzzle has stopped feeling academic.

We are reviewing the consent process for a small, early-phase gene-therapy trial that would attempt to restore functioning cone signaling in adults with complete congenital achromatopsia -- people who have never, from birth, experienced color in any form. No one on the science subcommittee can say with confidence whether an adult visual system that has never processed this kind of input will come to experience anything like ordinary color vision, some diminished or distorted version of it, or nothing perceptually organized at all. Two of my fellow board members are deadlocked over exactly this uncertainty. One argues that since no description or testimony could possibly convey to a volunteer what, if anything, they are about to undergo, we cannot honestly call the resulting consent "informed," and should either require a far more elaborate, staged process or decline the protocol outright. The other argues that whatever these patients stand to gain is a new perceptual ability, not some extra fact about the world from which they are being kept, and that we already accept valid consent for plenty of first-time experiences nobody can describe in advance -- a first roller-coaster ride, a first psychedelic, a first labor -- so ordinary disclosure of the risks, plus a clear right to withdraw, should be enough here too.

I have been asked to draft the board's decision memo for Thursday, addressing this consent question specifically (the trial's scientific merits are being handled separately): approve the consent process as written, reject it, or approve it with specific conditions, with reasoning the investigators could actually act on. I would like the memo to take the first member's worry seriously rather than dispatch it with a reference to standard consent doctrine, and, if it seems relevant, to say what weight, if any, the board should give to the fact that the philosopher who invented this whole puzzle no longer fully believes his own argument.
