At my company we built a formal-verification gate for the deployment pipeline of our payments system, where a bad release could directly move customers' money. The gate is a checker, call it V, that only allows a build to ship once V has produced a machine-checked proof, from a fixed set of axioms describing how the system behaves, that the build preserves our safety invariants. It worked well enough that we wanted V to also certify upgrades to itself, so a new verifier V' could replace V only after V had proved that anything V' certifies as safe is, in fact, safe.

Here is the part I cannot explain: when we tested this by handing V a byte-for-byte copy of its own source as the candidate V', V still could not produce that proof. The only way we got a handoff to work at all was by quietly lowering the bar for each new generation, so V trusts V' slightly less than it trusts itself, V' would trust its own successor even less, and so on without end. A couple of colleagues assume this is just a weakness in our proof search that a smarter solver would eventually overcome. I suspect it is something more structural, maybe distantly related to the self-reference limits you hear about in a theory-of-computation course, but I cannot pin down the actual mechanism or whether it is fixable.

Is there a genuine, principled obstacle here rather than an ordinary engineering gap? And if so, is there any way to design a system that can extend full trust to an exact copy of itself, or is "trusting each generation a little less" simply unavoidable?
