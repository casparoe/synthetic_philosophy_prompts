I am working on a feature about the philosophical foundations of AI-risk arguments, and the piece keeps circling back to the "coherence arguments" associated with Eliezer Yudkowsky and Stephen Omohundro: the claim that any sufficiently capable, goal-directed agent will end up behaving like an expected utility maximizer, because an agent whose preferences are incomplete, or otherwise violate the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms, can be money-pumped out of its resources. In this literature, incompleteness is generally treated as a defect that optimization pressure will iron out. Please argue instead that an agent can retain genuinely incomplete preferences indefinitely and never be money-pumped, simply by adopting a standing policy of refusing any trade that it can foresee will, in conjunction with its own later choices, cycle it back to an option it has already turned down at a net loss. Your argument should establish that such a policy counts as rational rather than as an ad hoc patch, and should spell out what its availability does to the inference from "coherent under optimization pressure" to "converges on a single, fixed utility function," on which the instrumental-convergence literature depends.
