I'm a novelist, and I've been asked to give the closing toast at the 150th-anniversary dinner of the Symposiacs, the earnestly ridiculous wine-and-ideas dining society I belonged to as an undergraduate at my old Cambridge college. The current members apparently decided that my last novel, which follows a fictional Athenian household through the night of a catastrophic dinner party, makes me the obvious choice this year, despite the fact that my own undergraduate contribution to the society amounted to enthusiastic attendance and essentially no wine-related self-command. The society has always kept one founding rule that everyone treats as a kind of in-joke: there must be a "sober president" for the evening, someone who doesn't drink and calls the toasts, because -- so the story goes, and I've never actually checked how accurately -- its nineteenth-century founders lifted the idea straight out of Plato, on the theory that wine is meant to test a person's character rather than excuse the lack of one.

The dinner draws a genuinely mixed room: a handful of current fellows in classics and ancient philosophy who will know exactly which dialogue is being half-quoted on the society's own notepaper, and will be listening closely to see whether I actually understand the argument behind it, plus a much larger crowd of old members, current students, and family who mainly want a good toast with their pudding. I don't want to reach for the easy, over-quoted material -- Alcibiades crashing the Symposium drunk, "in vino veritas" as a throwaway line -- and I'd rather build the toast around the stranger, more deliberate case Plato makes in the Laws for regulated drunkenness: the idea of wine as a supervised trial of self-command, the specific ages at which a young man is allowed to drink at all and then, much later, permitted to actually get drunk, and the odd, rather touching claim that intoxication is something owed to the old as a kind of medicine against the stiffness that age puts on the soul. I'd like that argument to genuinely organize the toast -- including, ideally, some use of the room's own spread of ages -- rather than turning up as one erudite line before the thank-yous.

Could you draft the toast itself? Something in the region of five minutes, warm and properly funny rather than lecture-shaped, that leaves the classicists satisfied I've actually understood the source rather than just gestured at it, and that still ends on a line the whole room, philosophers and family alike, will enjoy raising a glass to.
