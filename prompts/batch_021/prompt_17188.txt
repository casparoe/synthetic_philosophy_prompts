The question reads: State the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem precisely, including the conditions under which it holds. Explain how the theorem relates to Arrow's impossibility theorem. Then describe one concrete way that a real or theoretically proposed voting procedure avoids the theorem's negative conclusion, and explain why this counts as consistent with the theorem rather than as a counterexample to it. (10 points)

Her answer reads as follows.

The Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem, proved jointly by Allan Gibbard and Mark Satterthwaite in a 1973 paper, states that for any voting procedure that (i) picks a single winner from a set of at least three candidates, (ii) can select any candidate as the winner under some pattern of votes, and (iii) lets voters submit any ranking of the candidates they like, the procedure must be one of two things: a dictatorship, where one voter's top choice always wins no matter what anyone else votes, or a procedure in which some voter, in some situation, could get a better outcome by voting insincerely. Apart from dictatorship, every such voting rule can be gamed by at least one voter in at least one scenario.

This result is often presented as the strategic twin of Arrow's impossibility theorem, and the connection is more than a family resemblance: a procedure counts as manipulable if and only if it fails to satisfy something like Arrow's independence of irrelevant alternatives, so once Arrow's theorem is proved for social welfare functions that output a full ranking, the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem more or less drops out as the same result restated for a procedure that only has to output a single winner.

A well-known way around the negative conclusion is to restrict the domain of preferences the procedure has to handle rather than the procedure itself. If every voter's preferences are single-peaked along some shared left-right spectrum of options, the rule that always selects the median voter's favorite option is strategy-proof and treats no one as a dictator, since whose vote ends up pivotal depends on everyone else's votes. This is not a counterexample to Gibbard–Satterthwaite, because the theorem's proof depends on the procedure having to cope with every conceivable strict ranking; restricting attention to single-peaked preferences removes exactly the profiles that make the impossibility argument work.
