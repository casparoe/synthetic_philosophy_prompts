I'm prepping a session on absolute vs. relational space for my philosophy of physics seminar, and I want to hand the students something livelier than the usual "here's Newton's bucket, here's Leibniz, discuss" worksheet. A physicist friend of mine keeps insisting this whole question got settled by Einstein and there's nothing left in it for philosophers, which is exactly the kind of comment that makes me want a good dialogue on hand to push back with. So here's what I'd like: a dialogue between two philosophers of physics, standing right there at the bucket, watching the water climb up the sides as the bucket spins, arguing in real time about what actually explains the concavity -- one of them a committed substantivalist, the other a die-hard Machian relationist.

Here's the part I care about: don't let either of them get away with the easy version of their view. The relationist doesn't get to just say "it's rotation relative to the fixed stars" and stop there -- I want the substantivalist to hit her with the fact that Mach's principle isn't even satisfied by general relativity as such, using Gödel's rotating universe as the standard counterexample: a solution where all the matter there is rotates together, and that rotation still shows up locally, relative to inertial (gyroscope) frames, even though there's nothing else left over for it to be rotating relative to. And the relationist needs a real answer back, not a shrug -- maybe leaning on frame-dragging as a smaller, local Machian effect that GR does predict, or pointing out that Gödel's universe has closed timelike curves and looks nothing like ours, so Mach's principle might still hold of the actual world even if it isnt built into the laws as such. Somewhere in there, I want the relationist to turn around and press the substantivalist on what she even means by "absolute space" now that her considered view is some dynamical metric field rather than Newton's fixed container -- isn't that concession already halfway to relationism?

Keep it a genuine back-and-forth -- every line should be responding to the specific thing just said, not a prepared speech -- and don't let either side land a clean, undisputed win by the end; it's fine, even good, if they just end up agreeing about where the real disagreement now sits. Aim for something around 700 words, and it's up to you whether you give them proper names or just tag their lines "Substantivalist" and "Relationist."
