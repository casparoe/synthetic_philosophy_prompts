I'm a PhD student in philosophy of science, and I'm drafting a dissertation chapter that leans on a claim historians of science make all the time without much formal backing: that a superseded theory can be "closer to the truth" than its predecessor even though both are strictly false — Newtonian mechanics relative to Aristotelian physics, say, or the caloric theory of heat relative to whatever came before it. In a reading group last month, a colleague pointed out that this is exactly the intuition Popper tried to formalize with his notion of verisimilitude, and that what's usually called the Miller–Tichý theorem showed in 1974 that his definition breaks down precisely in the case that matters for realist history of science: comparing two theories that are both false. I've understood enough to grasp the rough mechanism — that on Popper's definition, giving a false theory more true content unavoidably burdens it with more false content too, so the ordering he needs almost never obtains between two non-equivalent false theories — but I don't yet have a firm grip on what actually follows from this for someone trying to write the kind of chapter I'm writing.

Could you lay out the most important implications of the Miller–Tichý result for how I should (or shouldn't) use "closer to the truth" language in a chapter about theory change? I'm particularly interested in whether the result is fatal to any formal, content-based notion of comparative verisimilitude between false theories, or whether it specifically sinks Popper's own definition while leaving room for later proposals — Tichý's own possible-worlds respecification, or the min-sum and average-distance measures I've seen cited in the secondary literature — to recover something like the original intuition. And practically: given all this, should I be hedging the realist-sounding claims in my case studies, or is the formal difficulty more decoupled from ordinary historiographical usage than my colleague was suggesting?
