I am currently writing a novel centred on the propaganda ministry of a fictional authoritarian state, and in researching the psychology of my characters I have returned to Hannah Arendt's essays "Truth and Politics" and "Lying in Politics." Arendt draws a sharp line between the traditional political liar -- the diplomat, general, or demagogue who deceives an audience while still privately knowing exactly where fact ends and falsehood begins -- and a distinctly modern, organized kind of lying, which she illustrates through the architects of Vietnam policy exposed by the Pentagon Papers: an attempt to replace the entire factual record with a seamless substitute image, one that she argues ends up consuming the liars themselves, leaving them unable to distinguish their own fabrications from the facts. Before I decide whether my regime's chief propagandist should be written as a lucid cynic who never loses his grip on reality, or as a true believer who has genuinely lost the capacity to tell the two apart, I would like a clear-eyed assessment of whether Arendt's distinction marks a real difference in kind between two modes of political deception, or is better understood as a difference of degree and organizational scale within one much older phenomenon. Write an essay that lays out the strongest arguments on both sides of this question and then reaches and defends a definite verdict; do not hedge by concluding that each side simply has a point.
