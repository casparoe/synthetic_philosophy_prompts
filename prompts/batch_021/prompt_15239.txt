My old PhD advisor is retiring after thirty-five years in the philosophy department, and I've somehow ended up on the hook to give the toast at her retirement dinner next month. (I finished my PhD with her a while back and then went off to do alignment-adjacent work instead of staying in academia, but we've stayed close.) Her whole career was basically one long argument with Jaegwon Kim's causal exclusion problem -- the argument that if a mental property supervenes on some physical property, and the physical property is already causally sufficient for whatever effect you care about (the "causal closure of the physical" thing), and your not allowed to believe in systematic overdetermination, then the mental property's contribution looks screened off (my own Bayes-net-brained gloss on it, not hers) and non-reductive physicalism quietly collapses into epiphenomenalism.

She never wanted to go reductive about it -- she cared a lot about multiple realizability and wasn't going to just identify pain with one specific brain state -- so she spent decades instead on the "proportionality" side of that literature, the Yablo-ish move where the mental property gets treated like a determinable (say, "red") and the physical realizer like the determinate (some exact shade), and the claim is that the determinable can be the actual, non-redundant, proportionate cause rather than a shadow cast by the "real" physical cause underneath it. She had this whole bit in her philosophy of mind seminar involving a trained pigeon and different shades of red that I'm now realizing I never fully understood and definately can't reproduce correctly.

Here's my actual problem: the dinner is going to be maybe half current faculty and former students who'll catch every one of these references, and half her actual life -- husband, kids, her book club -- who've heard her say "epiphenomenalism" at Thanksgiving for twenty years with zero idea what it means. I want the toast built around one real bit about mental causation, not just a pun for the philosophers to smirk at, because it's been bugging me that a toast is exactly the genre where you're supposed to say "she changed my life," and her entire research program is basically a technical argument about whether a sentence like that can be true once you've fixed all the microphysical facts underneath it. (Having flashbacks to the reductionism sequence on LessWrong here, the "is the cat really on the mat" stuff, minus the argumentative rigor.) I want the speech to use her own commitments to actually earn the claim that my gratitude toward her isn't epiphenomenal -- something that functions as a real argument, not just decoration -- while staying warm and funny enough that her book club doesn't tune out, and it should end with an actual line we can raise glasses to. Keep it to about five minutes. Can you write it?
