Over dinner last weekend, a friend and I spent nearly two hours arguing about Rawls's original position and never managed to reach any agreement. My position was the fairly standard one: once you strip people of any knowledge of their talents, social position, and conception of the good, it seems reasonable that they would want to protect whoever ends up worst off, which is roughly how Rawls moves from the veil of ignorance to the difference principle. My friend, who has a background in decision theory, was unconvinced. She pointed out that Harsanyi had used what looks like essentially the same device — a chooser ignorant of which member of society they will turn out to be — and had reached what looks like the opposite conclusion, average utilitarianism, simply by treating the choice as an ordinary problem of expected-utility maximization under uncertainty, with equal probability assigned to occupying each position. Neither of us could work out, on the spot, why two thought experiments that look so similar on the surface are supposed to support such different theories of justice.

I would like a more careful treatment of this than we managed over dinner. Could you compare Rawls's route from the veil of ignorance to the difference principle with Harsanyi's route from a structurally similar device to average utilitarianism, focusing specifically on where the two arguments actually part ways? I am especially interested in whether the disagreement is best understood as a dispute over whether probabilities can legitimately be assigned behind the veil at all, a dispute over which decision rule — maximin or expected-utility maximization — counts as rational under uncertainty, or something more fundamental about what the veil of ignorance is meant to represent in the first place. I would also like your assessment of Harsanyi's charge that maximin reasoning is excessively risk-averse and yields absurd prescriptions outside Rawls's rather special case. Please do not simply present both positions symmetrically: tell me which derivation you find more defensible overall, and defend that verdict.
