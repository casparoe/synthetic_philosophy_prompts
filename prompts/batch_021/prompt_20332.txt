A close friend of mine is midway through a PhD studying fear conditioning in mice as a model for human anxiety disorders, and this past weekend a casual dinner turned into a two-hour argument about whether her research tells us anything about suffering at all. She invoked Joseph LeDoux's more recent position, developed together with the philosopher Richard Brown, that a state only counts as consciously felt fear if it is the object of a suitable higher-order representation, so that the freezing behavior and stress hormones her mice display could, in principle, be produced entirely by what LeDoux calls "survival circuits," with nothing it is like to be the mouse at all. I objected that the same reasoning, applied consistently, seems to threaten the consciousness of human beings who plainly can't form sophisticated thoughts about their own mental states either -- advanced-dementia patients, say, or pre-verbal infants -- which struck me as a reductio rather than a discovery. Neither of us could get past an impasse where she would say, in effect, that this is simply what the theory predicts and maybe it's correct, and I would say that it can't be. We eventually gave up, and I would like a more careful adjudication than either of us could manage alone.

Could you set out the most important implications of higher-order theories of consciousness for judging which creatures -- non-human animals, but also humans with limited conceptual or metacognitive sophistication -- are capable of phenomenally conscious states like fear or pain, and for what should follow from that practically: how much evidentiary weight animal-model research of the kind my friend does should carry in debates about animal welfare, and how we should think about the inner lives of patients who cannot demonstrate the relevant higher-order capacities? I would find it especially useful if you distinguished where the actualist version of the view (requiring an occurrent higher-order thought, associated with Rosenthal), the dispositional version (associated with Carruthers), and higher-order-perception versions (associated with Lycan) actually diverge on these questions, and whether a defender of any of them has a principled way to block the uncomfortable implications for infants and severely impaired patients, or whether biting that bullet is simply the cost of holding the view.
