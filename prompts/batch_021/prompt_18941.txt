I've spent a good deal of time in the rationalist and EA-adjacent corners of the internet, where the periodic threads on moral-circle expansion and the criteria for moral patienthood keep resurfacing, and Kant is almost always invoked as the cautionary example: the philosopher whose ethics supposedly has no room for animals at all, except as things we happen to owe it to ourselves not to be cruel to. I pushed back on this a little in a comment thread recently, since that summary seemed too quick once you actually read the "duties regarding animals" material in the Doctrine of Virtue alongside the Formula of Humanity, and things get more interesting once you look at how some contemporary Kantians -- Christine Korsgaard's work on this is the version I know best -- have tried to derive direct duties to animals from inside the Kantian framework itself rather than importing them from utilitarianism. Nobody in the thread wanted to actually reconstruct the argument on either side, so I'd like your help doing that properly.

Please write a dialogue rather than a summary. One speaker should be Kant, defending his considered position: only self-legislating rational beings can be ends in themselves, animals lack the autonomy the Formula of Humanity is tracking, and our only duties "regarding" them are therefore indirect and owed to humanity, since cruelty toward animals coarsens a capacity for sympathy we need in our dealings with other rational beings. The other speaker should be an unnamed contemporary philosopher arguing in a broadly Kantian idiom, close to Korsgaard's line, who tries to show that Kant's own argument for the unconditional value of rational nature already presupposes that having a good of one's own is what matters, since rational agents are always embodied animals legislating on behalf of their own natural needs -- and that consistency in this premise extends the same standing to other animals. I don't want either side to simply restate their view and stop: have Kant press on why a being's good mattering to that being is not the same as that being's being an end in itself, and have the contemporary Kantian press back on why rational agency as such, rather than the mere having of a good, should be doing the moral work Kant wants it to do. Let the exchange run long enough to isolate the actual point on which they stop agreeing about what's at stake, rather than trailing off into restated positions or an easy concession from either side.
