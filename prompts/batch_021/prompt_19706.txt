Question (20 points, roughly 6/8/6 across parts): (a) State the Condorcet Jury Theorem, including the assumptions it depends on. (b) Consider the following claim, of a kind you'll have seen in popular treatments of "the wisdom of crowds": "Since a jury of six people, each more often right than wrong, is more reliable than any one of its members alone, a jury of six hundred would be better still — bigger deliberating bodies are simply more trustworthy than smaller ones." Using the theorem, assess whether this inference is sound. (c) Identify one respect in which real juries or committees tend to depart from the theorem's assumptions, and explain what that departure does to its guarantee.

Student answer:

(a) The Condorcet Jury Theorem describes a group of voters choosing between two options, exactly one of which is correct. If every voter decides independently of the others, and each is more likely than not to pick the correct option — that is, each has a "competence" greater than 0.5 — then as the group grows larger, the probability that a majority vote lands on the correct option approaches 1. This is often presented as a formal vindication of "the wisdom of crowds," and it is essentially the same insight behind Francis Galton's famous demonstration that the median of hundreds of independent guesses at an ox's weight, made at a country fair, came within a pound or two of the true value even though almost no individual guess did: in both cases, pooling many separately fallible judgments cancels out error and leaves only signal.

(b) The inference is only as good as its assumptions. Competence above 0.5 is doing real work: if a body's members are, on the relevant question, worse than a coin flip, then making the body bigger makes it worse, not better, since the theorem runs in reverse and the majority grows increasingly likely to be wrong as the group grows. Even granting competence above 0.5, the claim also needs independence, and that assumption is more fragile. A jury of six hundred who read the same coverage, hear the same arguments, and influence one another in deliberation is not six hundred independent trials; statistically it may behave like a much smaller independent sample, so "bigger juries are more accurate" does not follow from the theorem just by adding heads.

(c) The main departure is exactly this failure of independence, encouraged by deliberation, shared media exposure, and social pressure. A subtler point is that the independence assumption is best secured, not undermined, by voters who reason strategically: a voter who thinks carefully about the circumstances under which her vote would be pivotal, and votes accordingly, is a voter whose ballot tracks her own private evidence rather than simply mirroring everyone else's, which is precisely the kind of independence the theorem needs. Committees swayed by a charismatic member or an early straw poll fall short of this strategic ideal, which is a further way the assumptions can fail outside the classroom.
