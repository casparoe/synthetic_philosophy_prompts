I'm a novelist, asked to give the toast at the wedding of two old friends from graduate school, both historians of early twentieth-century philosophy — one wrote her dissertation on Bertrand Russell. Their courtship began, half in jest, in an argument over Marriage and Morals, and they've built the marriage itself on its least fashionable claim: that sexual exclusivity isn't what makes a marriage a marriage, and that jealousy is a habit to be unlearned rather than a law of nature. They want the toast to reflect this.

I don't want to pretend Russell's own life bears the claim out: his second marriage, to Dora Russell, was arranged on almost exactly these lines, and it came apart amid more jealousy and grief than either of them had bargained for. But I also don't want a toast that quietly drops the idea the moment it becomes inconvenient. About half the room will catch every reference; the rest are aunts and uncles who have never heard of Bertrand Russell. Please write the four-minute toast — built around one detail from Russell's own marriage that can land as a knowing joke for the philosophers and as harmless color for everyone else — honest about where his experiment failed, yet still, in the end, a toast.
