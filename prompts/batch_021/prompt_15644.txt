During a reading group in my program on the demandingness objection to consequentialism, two of my fellow PhD students ended up in a running argument over dinner afterward that I still haven't been able to adjudicate.

(1) Maya opened by claiming that the demandingness objection isn't really a philosophical argument at all, only a report of discomfort: the fact that a theory requires enormous sacrifice doesn't show it's false, since our sense of what counts as a "reasonable" sacrifice might just track self-interest rather than any principled moral limit.

(2) Daniel replied that this trivializes the real worry, which is structural rather than psychological: a theory that requires agents to weigh their own projects, relationships, and commitments no more heavily than a stranger's marginal gain in welfare doesn't merely feel unpleasant, it seems to eliminate the very possibility of having a personal point of view, which is precisely the structural point behind Bernard Williams's integrity objection.

(3) Maya responded by invoking what's sometimes called the compliance condition, associated with Liam Murphy: if everyone did their fair share, no one would be asked to sacrifice much at all, so the extreme burden that falls on conscientious individuals is really a symptom of other people's non-compliance, not evidence that consequentialism itself demands too much.

(4) Daniel countered that this resolves the problem only by changing the subject: an act-consequentialist theory tells you to promote the best outcome given how things actually stand, not how they would stand if everyone cooperated, so capping anyone's obligations at their hypothetical "fair share" just swaps consequentialism for a different, fairness-based principle rather than defending the original view.
