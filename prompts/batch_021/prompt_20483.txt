I write about science for a living, and I've realized I lean on a phrase constantly without ever really interrogating it: theory X is "closer to the truth" than theory Y. General relativity is closer to the truth than Newtonian gravity, the Bohr model of the atom was closer to the truth than the plum-pudding model but the full quantum mechanical picture is closer still, plate tectonics beat out the old geosyncline theories, etc. A philosopher of science I interviewed for a totally unrelated story mentioned, almost in passing, that this idea has a pretty embarassing technical history: Popper coined "verisimilitude" to try to define closeness-to-truth rigorously, something about a theory's "truth content" versus its "falsity content," and then in 1974 a couple of logicians, Tichý and Miller, independently showed his definition entails that one false theory can basically never be more verisimilar than another false theory, which is a problem given that literally every scientific theory we've ever had turns out to be false eventually. She had to run to a meeting before I could ask any follow-up questions, so i've just been left chewing on this ever since.

Could you lay out the most important implications of this whole verisimilitude mess for how science journalists (and scientists themselves, in papers and press releases) should talk about theories getting "closer to the truth" or being "better approximations" of reality? Like, am I quietly saying something incoherent every single time I write that sentence, or is that too strong a conclusion to draw from one formal definition failing? And is there a version of this from Niiniluoto or anyone else that actually rescues ordinary talk of scientific progress, or have philosophers more or less given up on making "closer to the truth" precise?
