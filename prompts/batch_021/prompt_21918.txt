I've been working through Sidgwick's *Methods of Ethics* for an online reading group, and somewhere along the way I went down a biographical rabbit hole that I can't stop thinking about. Everyone else in the group wanted to talk about the "dualism of practical reason," but what actually struck me was learning that this famously scrupulous, self-doubting philosopher spent something like the last quarter-century of his life as a co-founder and first president of the Society for Psychical Research (founded in 1882, with informal investigations among his closest Cambridge collaborators going back further still), personally sitting in on tests of mediums and alleged telepaths — including the notorious Creery sisters case, whose initially striking thought-transference results fell apart once investigators began suspecting signalling. By his own repeated admission in his presidential addresses to the Society, decades of this work never turned up evidence that met the standard he thought a genuinely rigorous, scientifically-minded conscience required. He had also, notably, resigned his Trinity fellowship years earlier rather than keep affirming a religious test he no longer believed in, and more than one historian of the period reads the SPR project, at least in part, as an attempt by Sidgwick and his closest collaborators there (Myers and Gurney especially) to find some empirical, secular substitute for the hope of personal survival that organized religion had once supplied them.

This bothers me because it cuts against a story I like to tell myself about epistemic virtue — something like: if you are careful, self-critical, and willing to publish your own null results, you are basically safe from being led astray by what you want to be true. Sidgwick was clearly all of those things at the level of individual experiments and individual pieces of testimony. But a quarter-century of unresolved, personally costly investigation into a question with such an obvious emotional stake could just as easily be read as itself a symptom of motivated reasoning — just one operating a level up, at the point of "which project do I keep devoting my career to" rather than "how do I interpret this particular sitting with this particular medium." It's the same worry I've seen raised, in a different guise, in rationalist-adjacent writing about why smart, scrupulously honest people can spend years chasing down parapsychology or other low-prior claims without that honesty ever quite translating into knowing when to stop.

Write an essay that weighs these two readings of Sidgwick's psychical research against one another, and then commits to a definite verdict rather than settling for "a bit of both." If your verdict ends up being something like "mainly virtue, except in this specific respect," that's a perfectly acceptable answer, but say precisely what that respect is and why it survives even though the rest of the case doesn't. I'd also be curious, if it fits naturally into your answer, what general lesson, if any, this episode should teach present-day rationalists about how much epistemic credit to extend to rigor and public self-doubt as safeguards against bias, in people who sink large amounts of effort into unfashionable, low-prior research programs.
