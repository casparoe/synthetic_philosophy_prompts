I don't have a technical or academic background, but I handle AI governance at a small startup that builds autonomous coding agents, and I've spent the past year teaching myself the safety literature so I can follow what our engineers actually do. I sit on our internal deployment review board, and we're deadlocked over the next release. The agent's shutdown safety currently relies on the old MIRI "utility indifference" fix: its utility function is corrected so that it's mathematically indifferent between being shut down and continuing, removing any incentive to resist or provoke the shutdown button. Our safety lead argues that this only balances things at one instant, doesn't stop the agent from influencing, upstream, whether the button ever gets pressed, and gives it no reason to actively cooperate with shutdown rather than merely not resisting it. She wants to hold the release until engineering implements a newer proposal, from the decision theorist Elliott Thornley, that gives the agent deliberately incomplete preferences between different-length runs so it structurally can't weigh "shut down now" against "keep going" — but that fix is known to leave an agent exploitable through sequences of trades unless you add a further choice rule on top, and engineering says doing this properly will cost two months we don't have. Please draft the memo I'll bring to Thursday's board meeting: recommend approve, block, or approve with conditions, with a timeline, and engage honestly with engineering's objection that this release — limited scope, constant human oversight, no subagents — may never actually trigger the failure modes our safety lead is worried about.
