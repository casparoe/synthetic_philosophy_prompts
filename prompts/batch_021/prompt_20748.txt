Write a short story of 800–1200 words built around the "quantum suicide" thought experiment popularized by the physicist Max Tegmark. A character convinced that the many-worlds interpretation guarantees her own subjective survival builds a device whose trigger depends on a genuinely random quantum event, and repeatedly tests it on herself. Rather than narrating this from inside the branch where she keeps waking up unharmed — the familiar framing in which the protagonist marvels at her own charmed luck — tell the story through someone who loves her and does not get to follow her into that branch: someone for whom, on any reasonable way of counting outcomes, each trial is overwhelmingly likely to end in an ordinary death. Let this asymmetry between first-person and third-person stakes do real dramatic work. Without pausing to explain the physics, the plot itself should also register at least one further complication that philosophers of physics have raised against taking comfort in quantum immortality: for instance, that Everettian probabilities are still supposed to be weighted by the Born rule, so that indifference to danger is not obviously rational even by the many-worlder's own lights; or that repeated "survival" need not preserve enough psychological continuity, on a Parfit-style view of identity, for a later branch's survivor to be someone worth identifying with at all. No character should simply explain the physics or the philosophy aloud.
