A friend and I got into it after our metaphysics seminar last week and neither of us backed down, so hoping you can settle it for us. As I understand it, the identity of indiscernibles is basically just Leibniz's Law -- if two things share literally every property, they're actually one and the same thing. That seems almost trivially true to me, like its just spelling out what "identical" means rather than asserting anything substantive about the world.

But then our professor spent a good twenty minutes on Max Black's old example with two exactly similar iron spheres, each a mile across, floating alone in an otherwise perfectly symmetrical universe, treating it like a genuinely open problem that metaphysicians still argue about. I don't see how that's possible though: if sharing every property makes two things numerically the same by definition, no hypothetical scenario should be able to overturn that, any more than a thought experiment could overturn "all bachelors are unmarried." Isn't Black basically just stipulating that there are two spheres from the outset, and then acting puzzled that no property distinguishes them, so the whole "paradox" just smuggles in the conclusion it's supposed to be testing? What am I not getting about why philosophers still treat this as a real, substantive principle instead of an analytic one?
