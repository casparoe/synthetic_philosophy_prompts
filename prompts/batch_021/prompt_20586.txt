For the past few months I have been mulling over two ideas I stumbled onto completely independently, and I have started to suspect they are quietly circling the same puzzle, even though nobody I have mentioned them to seems to know both at once. The first is Douglas Hofstadter's old notion of superrationality, which I first read about in a battered secondhand copy of one of his essay collections. As I understand it, the argument goes roughly like this: if two players in a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma are both perfectly and identically rational, then whatever line of reasoning leads one of them to a decision must lead the other to exactly the same decision, so the "rational" choice is not really an individual decision at all, but a matter of asking which joint answer would be best if adopted by both players at once. On Hofstadter's view, that turns out to favor mutual cooperation over mutual defection, contrary to what standard game theory recommends.

The second idea I only ran into much later, while reading some fairly technical material on rationalist-adjacent blogs: the notion of program equilibrium. Here, instead of the two players choosing moves directly, each submits a program that is allowed to inspect the other player's code before deciding what to do. Apparently there are constructions - relying on a self-referential trick tied to something called Löb's theorem, which, I will admit, I only half understand - that let two copies of the same program cooperate with each other in a single round without collapsing into the obvious infinite regress of "I will cooperate if you cooperate if I cooperate if you cooperate...". Some refinements of the idea are even supposed to stop such a program from leaving value on the table when it faces an opponent that cooperates no matter what.

I cannot shake the feeling that program equilibrium is essentially superrationality made rigorous and mechanical, but I worry that this is too quick, since one of these is an informal piece of reasoning about what the single "rational" answer to a shared problem has to be, while the other is a fully specified construction that does not obviously rely on any of Hofstadter's assumptions about symmetry or shared rationality. Could you compare the two ideas in some depth and tell me whether the program equilibrium construction genuinely vindicates the intuition behind superrationality, or whether the two are actually solving quite different problems that merely resemble each other from a distance? I would also like to know which of the two, if either, you think gives the more defensible account of why cooperation in a one-shot dilemma might count as rational, and why.
