"The cluelessness objection, developed by James Lenman, points out that consequentialism assesses acts by their overall consequences, but those consequences ramify outward indefinitely in ways we have no hope of tracing. Lenman's own illustration involves a bandit who, around 100 BCE, spares the life of a man who turns out to be a distant ancestor of Hitler; had the bandit killed him instead, Hitler presumably would never have existed and the Holocaust would never have happened, yet nothing available to the bandit at the time could have flagged this. The same holds for almost anything anyone does, since ordinary choices are constantly shifting the timing of who conceives children with whom, and hence who is ever born; the long-run value of our acts is therefore radically unpredictable, and consequentialism seems unable to tell us whether we are acting rightly.

Hilary Greaves responds by distinguishing 'simple' from 'complex' cluelessness. In simple cases we are ignorant of some of an act's further effects, but have no more reason to expect them to be good than bad, so by a principle of indifference these unknown effects cancel out in expectation, leaving the foreseeable, asymmetric effects to settle the comparison as normal. In complex cases we do have some evidence bearing on the unknown effects, but it is too thin and one-sided to ground any stable estimate, so the indifference move is unavailable. Because simple cluelessness of this sort attaches to literally every choice we ever make, while complex cluelessness only arises in the special cases where suggestive evidence happens to be available, Greaves ultimately treats simple cluelessness as the deeper and more persistent difficulty, with complex cluelessness being the comparatively rare, practical worry that further evidence could in principle dissolve. She also traces this whole puzzle back to the non-identity problem, since both turn on the fact that our acts settle who later exists.

So the simple/complex distinction only partly succeeds: it explains away most everyday unforeseen effects, but on Greaves's own account the harder, simple form of cluelessness remains, so consequentialist expected-value reasoning is not fully rescued."
