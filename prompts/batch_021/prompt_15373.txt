I'm putting together a presentation for a graduate seminar on nineteenth-century abolitionist political thought, and I want to make sure I have Frederick Douglass's constitutional theory straight before I present it. From what I've read, Douglass's position on the U.S. Constitution was essentially the same as William Lloyd Garrison's: because the document was drafted and ratified by men who knowingly built protections for slavery into its text -- the three-fifths clause, the fugitive slave clause, the twenty-year constitutional bar on Congress abolishing the international slave trade -- it was, in its very design, a pro-slavery compact that no person of conscience could vote under, hold office within, or swear an oath to uphold. Since Douglass came up through Garrison's abolitionist circle and never once stopped denouncing the framers' compromises with slaveholders in the harshest terms, this seems to have been his settled view of the document across his entire public life. A friend who knows more political philosophy than history pushed back on this when I mentioned it, but she couldn't quite say what I was getting wrong.

What I can't square is how this fits with the second half of Douglass's career. After the war he campaigned hard for the Fourteenth and Fifteenth Amendments, urged formerly enslaved men to claim the vote as a constitutional entitlement, accepted federal appointments, and generally treated the Constitution as a text whose promises of liberty and equal citizenship simply needed to be enforced rather than escaped. If his foundational position was that the Constitution was irredeemably a slaveholders' document, this later career looks less like principled constitutional advocacy and more like a quiet abandonment of his own earlier critique once emancipation made the question less urgent. Is that the right way to read it -- that Douglass simply set aside an unchanged objection for the sake of political expediency -- or is there a more defensible account of his constitutional thought on which the early repudiation and the later advocacy both express the same underlying position? I'd like to understand this correctly before I try to explain it to my seminar.
