For an independent project through my school's philosophy club, I've been comparing Eliezer Yudkowsky's proposal that a superintelligent AI should be aligned to humanity's "coherent extrapolated volition" (CEV) — roughly, the preferences we would converge on if we were far better informed, thought more clearly, and had more time to grow into the people we'd want to be. I've paired this with older philosophical accounts of a person's good in terms of idealized preferences, especially Richard Brandt's view that our truly rational desires are the ones that survive what he calls "cognitive psychotherapy," and Peter Railton's idea that your good is whatever a fully informed, idealized version of yourself would want for your actual self. Compare CEV with these idealized-preference theories of well-being: beyond the shift from an individual subject to a collective one, where do their underlying assumptions about idealization really agree or diverge? Philosophers have also raised an "alienation" objection against full-information theories like Railton's, worrying that a radically more knowledgeable idealized counterpart might become so unlike the original person that its preferences lose authority over them — does the same objection threaten CEV, or does extrapolating an entire civilization's volition together change the diagnosis? Please defend a definite verdict rather than leaving the question open.
