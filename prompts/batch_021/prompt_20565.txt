A friend and I had a long argument over dinner last week that started, of all things, from her decision not to look at the results of an at-home genetic test until after her wedding. She invoked I. J. Good's 1967 theorem on the value of information to argue that her own reluctance was a mistake: Good showed that a Bayesian decision-maker who can see cost-free evidence before acting is always at least as well off, in expectation, looking as not looking, so refusing to look is never rationally defensible. I pushed back with objections I could only half-articulate at the table -- that the theorem's guarantee concerns the ex ante expectation and says nothing about her particular case, that it assumes the information is free not just to acquire but to carry around afterward, and that I vaguely recalled an economist (Hirshleifer, I think) showing that information can unravel mutually beneficial arrangements even while helping, in expectation, the individual who receives it. We never settled it, and I keep coming back to the disagreement.

As it happens, I host a small decision-theory podcast, and next week's guest is a Bayesian statistician who has built a public-facing case, leaning heavily on Good's theorem, that we are under something like a standing rational obligation to seek out free information whenever it is on offer. I don't want to let her simply re-derive the theorem for the audience; I want the questions to sit exactly where the informal slogan and the formal result pull apart. Could you draft nine or ten interview questions for me? I need at least one question that forces her to explain why the "you can always just ignore the new data" move behind the proof doesn't reduce the theorem to a triviality about having more options rather than a substantive claim about information; one on whether the cost-free assumption leaves the theorem any practical bite once ordinary cognitive and emotional costs of knowing are counted back in; one that puts a Hirshleifer-style case to her, where information that is privately non-negative in expectation nonetheless destroys an insurance or trading arrangement both parties preferred; and one that asks her to explain everyday information-avoidance -- declining a test, refusing to check a balance -- as either flat irrationality or evidence that the theorem's notion of utility is too thin. For each of these, add a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give, and close the list with a question about what sort of formal or empirical case would actually get her to retract the slogan rather than just narrow it further.
