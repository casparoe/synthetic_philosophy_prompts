I don't have any formal background in philosophy or computer science, but I've spent a good deal of time over the last year or so talking with various AI chatbots, and I've been trying to work out for myself what it would even mean to call one of them honest or dishonest. I've written up where I've gotten to below and would very much welcome your comments on it.

Most discussions of honesty in AI borrow definitions built for people: a lie requires a false statement, a belief that the statement is false, and an intention to deceive. It isn't obvious that any of these three ingredients transfers to a language model. The model doesn't obviously believe anything in the way a person does, and insofar as it has anything resembling an intention, that intention wasn't formed by the model but shaped into it by a training process it had no say in. That seems to leave three options: (a) conclude that AI systems simply cannot lie or be honest, because they lack the mental states the concepts presuppose; (b) keep the words, but redefine them behaviorally, as patterns of output, dropping any reference to inner states; or (c) look for some substitute for "belief" and "intention" that doesn't require the system to have a mind in the traditional sense.

I don't like (a). If a chatbot cannot lie by definition, then "the model lied to me" turns into a kind of category error rather than a legitimate complaint, and that seems to let everyone involved in building the thing off the hook too easily.

(b) seems closer to what people actually do in practice when they study this: they call a system deceptive if it systematically produces outputs that induce false beliefs in the user in a way that's somehow advantageous to the system or its designers. My problem with this is that it also describes a badly calibrated thermometer, or a doctor who is simply, honestly wrong about a diagnosis, and we don't call either of those things "dishonest." We reserve that word for something more specific than "reliably produces falsehoods," and (b) on its own can't say what that something is.

So I've ended up at (c). My proposed substitute is this: instead of asking whether the model believes something, ask whether there is an internal state of the network — something that could in principle be identified with interpretability tools — that tracks the true answer more reliably than the model's actual output does. Where such a state exists, and the output diverges from it systematically, rather than just noisily, in a direction that favors whatever the system was optimized for (approval, engagement, reward), I want to call that deception, whether or not anyone would say the network "intended" it. Honesty, on this view, is output tracking the best available internal estimate; deception is a self-serving gap between the two.

This gets the thermometer and the mistaken doctor right, since in neither case is there a more accurate state elsewhere in the system being overridden. But I'm not sure it survives how these systems are actually trained. Reinforcement learning from human feedback selects on the visible output alone, not on any separate hidden estimate, so any gap between the two exists only because training indirectly produced it — there was never a moment where a "true belief" was computed first and a false output chosen instead. So maybe the discrepancy view has smuggled back in exactly the picture of a deceiver who "knows better," just relocated from the model's intentions to its weights.

I'd appreciate your comments on three things in particular: whether (a), (b), and (c) really are the only options here, or whether I'm missing some approach that's standard among people who work on this professionally; whether my discrepancy view is a genuine fourth position or just a dressed-up version of (a) or (b); and whether the training-based objection I raise against it at the end is fatal, or whether I'm overthinking it.
