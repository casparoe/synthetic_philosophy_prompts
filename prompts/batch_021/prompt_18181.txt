So I'm a philosophy PhD student (bioethics concentration) doing a clinical ethics practicum this term at a memory care facility, which mostly means I sit in on the ethics committee meetings and occasionally get handed stuff to draft. Last week's case: a longtime aide told a resident with advanced dementia, who kept anxiously asking where her husband was, that "he's at the shop, he'll be back for supper" -- the husband's actually been dead for six years, but it stopped the resident's distress in about ten seconds flat. Her daughter found out and is furious, wants a hard no-deception rule for the whole unit going forward; most of the nursing staff (and, awkwardly, the resident's other two kids) think that's cruel and unworkable and that this kind of "therapeutic fibbing" is just normal, humane memory care practice at this point.

Here's the thing that's been nagging at me though: I've been reading Augustine on lying for a totally unrelated seminar paper -- he thinks lying is wrong full stop, no exceptions for good motives or bad consequences, and he's oddly precise that only asserting a falsehood counts as lying, so staying silent or steering the conversation elsewhere doesn't. It occured to me that his position is basically the strongest possible version of the daughter's objection, way stronger than anything she's actually argued. So I don't just want a memo that gestures at "honesty matters, but so does compassion" and calls it a day. The chair asked me to draft the actual policy memo for Tuesday's meeting, since apparently doing a philosophy PhD makes me the house expert now -- can you write it? I need a real recommendation (allow the fibbing, ban it, or allow it only under specific conditions), concrete enough that staff could actually follow it starting tomorrow, and it has to take Augustine's absolutism seriously as the best version of the daughter's case rather than just mentioning he existed -- including whether tricks like redirection actually get the committee out of his objection or just quietly relocate the same problem.
