I'm a third-year PhD student in philosophy working in formal epistemology, and this term a reading group I belong to has spent several sessions moving back and forth between the lottery paradox and the preface paradox. The two are constantly presented as close cousins, sometimes almost as two guises of a single underlying puzzle about the closure of justified belief under conjunction, since both involve an agent who is confident in each of many propositions individually while being equally confident that not all of them are true together. What nags at me is that the source of the relevant uncertainty seems importantly different across the two textbook cases: in Kyburg's lottery, my confidence that ticket n will lose rests on nothing but the bare statistics of a fair draw, whereas in Makinson's preface, my confidence that the book contains at least one error rests on an inductive judgment about authors' track records that has nothing essentially to do with statistics and does not single out any particular sentence as the likely culprit.

I'd like to pressure-test that disanalogy against a genuinely new case rather than keep arguing about it in the abstract, so for a short position paper I'm drafting for the group, could you construct an original scenario, drawn from ordinary scientific, academic, or professional practice rather than from lotteries or manuscripts, that clearly has the formal structure of one of the two paradoxes while strongly resisting redescription as an instance of the other? Please state explicitly which of the two paradoxes you take your example to instantiate, and identify precisely what feature of the case is doing the work of blocking the redescription — for instance, whether the evidence behind each individual proposition is statistical or non-statistical in character, whether the doubt about the conjunction is spread symmetrically across every conjunct or concentrated on some unidentified subset, or whether it is really closure under conjunction that generates the tension rather than mere aggregation of independent risks. If, in working the example through, you find that it can actually be redescribed either way without much loss, I would find that negative result just as useful, so long as you diagnose exactly why the two structures refuse to come apart in that particular instance. A few sentences setting up the scenario followed by the analysis should be plenty — I don't need a literature review.
