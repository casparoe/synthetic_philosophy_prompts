At work, a few of us run an informal lunchtime reading group, and we recently made our way through Leibniz's Monadology. The passage that has stuck with me is section 17, the "mill" argument: Leibniz asks us to imagine a machine built so as to produce thought and perception, then imagines it enlarged, keeping the same proportions, until we could walk through it as though it were a mill. His point is that no matter how closely we inspect the gears and levers inside, we will only ever find parts pushing on other parts, never anything that looks like a perception. A colleague with an actual philosophy background mentioned that this passage gets paired constantly with twentieth-century functionalism and its multiple realizability thesis, i.e., the idea that a mental state is defined by its causal or functional role rather than by whatever stuff implements it, so that neurons, silicon chips, or even Ned Block's thought experiment of the entire population of China relaying signals to each other by radio could all, in principle, realize the very same mind.

I don't have any formal training in philosophy, so I can't tell if the following intuition holds up, but it strikes me that Leibniz's move of blowing the machine up to walk-through size while preserving its proportions already looks like it's groping toward something like multiple realizability, even though he draws the opposite conclusion about whether mechanism can ever add up to a mind. What makes me doubt any easy alliance between Leibniz and functionalism, though, is that elsewhere in the Monadology (section 7) he insists that monads "have no windows" through which anything can causally enter or leave, and that whatever looks like interaction between substances is really just pre-established harmony rather than genuine causal influence. Functionalism, as I understand it, defines mental states largely in terms of their causal relations to inputs, outputs, and each other, so it's not obvious to me that a windowless, causally sealed-off monad could even satisfy a functionalist definition in the first place, let alone serve as a precedent for one.

So, supposing Leibniz could be brought fully up to speed on Putnam-style functionalism and the multiple realizability arguments built on it, do you think he would end up embracing some version of the view as a natural extension of his own metaphysics, or would his commitments to windowless monads, pre-established harmony, and the irreducibility of perception to mechanism make him one of functionalism's most fundamental opponents? I'd appreciate an answer that actually works through that tension rather than one that simply observes he predates computers.
