My friend Devi and I got into it after our philosophy of language seminar last week and neither of us backed down. The question was whether Davidson's radical interpretation actually gets you past Quine's radical translation, or whether its just the same indeterminacy problem wearing a nicer suit. Devi's line was that the principle of charity does real, substantive work: by forcing an interpreter to assign a speaker beliefs that are mostly true and inferences that are mostly rational, Davidson solves for meaning and belief at once in a way Quine's gavagai-style behaviorism never could, so radical interpretation isn't just radical translation with a truth theory bolted on, its a genuinely stronger constraint. I pushed back with the stuff from Davidson's "Inscrutability of Reference" -- you can still permute what "rabbit" refers to (undetached rabbit parts, rabbit stages, whatever) and compensate elsewhere so all the same sentences come out true, and charity can't rule that out, since it only cares about whole sentences being held true, not about which objects the sub-sentential bits pick out. Devi's comeback was that Davidson already knows this and isn't bothered by it -- he treats it as harmless in more or less the way a choice of measurement scale is harmless, and that reframing the problem that way IS the philosophical progress, not a concession that charity failed to do its job. We argued about this for like an hour on the walk home and got nowhere, which is annoying because we can usually talk each other into something.

Can you help me actually think this through properly? I don't want a generic "here's Quine, here's Davidson" rundown -- I want a real comparison of the two projects specifically on this point: does the principle of charity change the substance of the indeterminacy/inscrutability result, or only our attitude toward it? And if you had to say which of us, Devi or me, has the more defensible reading of what Davidson is doing in that argument, who's closer and why?
