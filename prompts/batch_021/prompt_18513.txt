So I've been down a Spinoza rabbit hole for a piece I'm writing about how a few early modern political thinkers might size up algorithm-driven politics, and I keep getting stuck on him specifically. Spinoza is one of the most full-throated defenders of democracy among his contemporaries -- in the Theological-Political Treatise he argues its basically the most "absolute" form of sovereignty there is, because it's the only one where people don't hand over most of their natural right to some smaller group, so it stays closer to the equality everyone had in the state of nature than a monarchy or aristocracy ever could. The annoying thing, and a Spinoza scholar I interviewed for an unrelated story pointed this out to me, is that the one book where he was actually going to lay out his mature, systematic theory of democracy, the Political Treatise, just trails off -- he died before finishing the chapter on democracy, partway through the infamous bit where he's trying to justify excluding women from political rights on the grounds that their affects make them naturally subject to men. So all we really have to reconstruct his considered view from is that fragment, his finished chapters on monarchy and aristocracy, the TTP, and his general theory of the passions in the Ethics.

Given all that -- and specifically given what he says in the Ethics about the "imitation of the affects" (his mechanism for how fear, hatred and hope spread from person to person just by watching someone we perceive as similar to us feel them) and his recurring worry in the TTP about ambitious men whipping up superstition in the multitude to control it -- what do you think he'd make of engagement-optimized social media, the kind of feed that's specifically tuned to surface whatever content provokes the strongest collective emotion? I'm genuinely curious whether you think this is basically his worst-case scenario for democracy playing out with better plumbing, or whether the structural machinery he was so keen on -- big councils, staggered terms, splitting power up so no single passion-driven faction can capture it -- actually gives him real resources for defending modern democracies against exactly this. Please reason it out from his actual concepts rather than giving me the greeting-card answer where "Spinoza liked democracy so he'd probably be fine with the internet."
