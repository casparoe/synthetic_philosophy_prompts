I'm about a third of the way into a novel that keeps cutting between our timeline and a "branch" timeline that splits off at the exact moment a senator gets shot (he survives in one strand, dies in the other), and I got sent down a total rabbit hole by my housemate, whos finishing up a linguistics PhD. She brought up that old philosophy chestnut about Kennedy: "If Oswald didn't kill Kennedy, someone else did" sounds obviously true, but "If Oswald hadn't killed Kennedy, someone else would have" sounds obviously false, even though the two sentences are almost identical apart from tense/mood. She said the standard line (something out of Adams and Edgington, I think?) is that this shows indicative and counterfactual "if...then" sentences aren't even the same kind of thing - indicative conditionals supposedly don't have real truth conditions at all, they just track how confident you'd be in the consequent given the antecedent, while counterfactuals are genuinely about which possible world is closest to ours. But then she mentioned, kind of as a throwaway line, that Stalnaker himself didn't think you needed two whole different logics for this - that you could get the same contrast out of a single conditional operator plus a difference in how its "closest world" search gets restricted by context. She didnt really unpack how that's supposed to work though, and honestly the more I sit with it the less sure I am whether that's a genuinely good idea or just a philosopher being stubborn about wanting one elegant theory instead of two messy ones.

This isn't just idle curiosity - I've got people on both sides of my book's split constantly reasoning in these two registers, folks in the "died" timeline piecing together what must actually explain the gaps in the official story (indicative-style) and folks who know about the branch point wondering what would've happened if it split the other way (counterfactual-style), and I'd love the "logic" connecting those two kinds of thinking to be something I could actually defend rather than pure vibes. So: could you make the strongest possible case FOR the one-mechanism, Stalnaker-ish view - that indicative and counterfactual conditionals can run on the same underlying semantics, and that pairs like the Oswald/Kennedy one are best explained by a difference in contextual restriction rather than by two fundamentally different logics? I don't want a balanced survey, I've basically already got that part - I want the actual positive argument, including a real account of how the single-mechanism view is supposed to handle the exact case that usually gets treated as its knock-down objection.
