For an advanced math elective at my school, our teacher covered Zorn's Lemma and then had us swap proofs with a partner to grade using a short rubric before handing everything in. I'd like a careful, independent grading of the proof I was assigned, so I have something to compare my own grading against.

The problem was: "Using Zorn's Lemma, prove that every vector space V over a field F has a basis. Then, in a sentence or two, comment on whether your proof lets us explicitly write down a basis when V is infinite-dimensional, and explain why or why not."

Here is the proof my partner wrote:

"Let 𝒫 be the set of all linearly independent subsets of V, partially ordered by inclusion. 𝒫 is nonempty because the empty set is linearly independent, so ∅ ∈ 𝒫.

Let 𝒞 be a chain in 𝒫, and let U = ⋃_{A∈𝒞} A. Since 𝒞 is totally ordered by inclusion, the sets in the chain increase steadily with no two of them crossing, so their union is just the union of an increasing family of linearly independent sets. An increasing union of linearly independent sets is linearly independent, so U ∈ 𝒫. Clearly A ⊆ U for every A ∈ 𝒞, so U is an upper bound for 𝒞 in 𝒫.

Since every chain in 𝒫 has an upper bound in 𝒫, Zorn's Lemma guarantees that 𝒫 has a maximal element B.

I claim B is a basis for V. Since B ∈ 𝒫, B is linearly independent. Suppose B did not span V. Then there is some v ∈ V with v ∉ span(B), and by the standard fact that adjoining a vector outside the span of a linearly independent set to that set preserves linear independence, B ∪ {v} would be linearly independent. But B ∪ {v} properly contains B, contradicting the maximality of B in 𝒫. Hence B spans V, and since B is also linearly independent, B is a basis for V. ∎

This proof is non-constructive: because we invoke Zorn's Lemma, which is equivalent to the Axiom of Choice, the basis B is not really a specific, well-defined set the way a basis produced by row-reducing a matrix would be. For a general infinite-dimensional space, this method doesn't let us point to any actual basis — we only know that the axioms force one to exist in a formal sense, not that one really exists the way finite bases do."
