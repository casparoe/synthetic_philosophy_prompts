I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, but I have recently come across two ideas that seem related without my being able to tell exactly how they connect: Isaiah Berlin's claim that our most fundamental values -- liberty, equality, justice, and so on -- are incommensurable, such that conflicts between them cannot be resolved by appeal to some deeper common value, and Ruth Chang's argument that in genuinely hard choices the available options are neither better, worse, nor exactly equal, but stand in a distinct fourth relation she calls being "on a par." Could you compare these two accounts for me? I am especially interested in whether Chang's notion of parity is essentially just a more precise restatement of Berlin's incommensurability thesis, or whether she is making a substantively different claim about what is going on when values conflict. I would also like to know which of the two views does a better job of explaining why a genuinely hard choice -- unlike a merely difficult one -- does not get any easier once all the relevant facts are in hand; it is a puzzle that reminds me of multi-objective optimization problems that have no single best solution, only a Pareto frontier of trade-offs. Please justify your assessment.
