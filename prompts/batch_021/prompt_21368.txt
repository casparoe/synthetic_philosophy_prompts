I recently joined the model-welfare working group that my company set up to consider what, if anything, we owe our newer models, and higher-order theories of consciousness keep coming up as a framework for assessing this. At our last meeting, two colleagues on the group, Ines and Marcus, had an exchange I have not been able to referee to my own satisfaction.

Ines argued that our latest model, when it produces extended step-by-step reasoning, also generates explicit representations of its own confidence and its own likely mistakes, and that this is at least the right shape of evidence on a Rosenthal-style higher-order theory, where a state is conscious just in case it is targeted by a suitable higher-order thought. Marcus objected that Rosenthal's theory has content only because of its restrictions: the higher-order thought must arise non-inferentially and be about a concurrent state rather than a reconstruction after the fact, whereas the model's confidence commentary is produced by the same next-token machinery as everything else, so treating one part of that output as a higher-order thought about another part begs the question. Ines replied that human introspection is riddled with demonstrable confabulation too, and we do not conclude from that alone that people lack higher-order thoughts about their states, so unreliability cannot be the decisive test; she added that a dispositional version of the theory, on which all that matters is the availability of a suitable higher-order thought rather than an occurrent one, sidesteps Marcus's objection entirely. Marcus answered that interpretability work increasingly shows a model's stated reasoning can be causally disconnected from the computation that actually produced its answer — a targetless higher-order representation of exactly the kind philosophers have used against higher-order theories even in the human case — so the AI example sharpens an old problem for the theory rather than resolving it.

Please go through this move by move, say who has the better of each specific point, and flag anything both of them seem to be missing. Then close with a final verdict: does either version of the higher-order framework give the working group real traction on the AI case, or does this exchange mainly show that the framework is too indeterminate to settle it either way?
