A friend and I spent an entire dinner arguing about Timothy Williamson's anti-luminosity argument from Knowledge and Its Limits, and neither of us budged. She thinks the argument is basically sound: because our discriminative powers are inexact, knowing that you feel cold on a gradually warming morning requires a margin for error, and once you grant that margin, a sorites-style induction shows that no non-trivial condition — including "one knows that p" — is luminous, so the KK principle has to go. I think the argument only looks compelling because the margin-for-error premise smuggles in a reliabilist, safety-theoretic picture of what knowing requires, and that a defender of KK who holds an internalist or infallibilist view of introspective self-knowledge has principled grounds to reject exactly that premise. Please argue for my side: that Williamson's anti-luminosity argument fails to refute the KK principle, because its crucial premise is warranted only relative to a background theory of knowledge that KK's defenders need not accept. Engage with the strongest form of the margin-for-error premise rather than a strawman, and address the obvious rejoinder that our inexact powers of discrimination are a plain fact about us that no theory of knowledge, internalist or not, gets to define away.
