I spent about 35 years as a behavioral ecologist, a good chunk of it on what the field now calls 'animal personality' -- consistent individual differences in boldness, aggresion, exploration and that sort of thing. Since retiring I've fallen down the philosophy rabbit hole, and right now I'm stuck on the situationist attack on virtue ethics (Milgram, the prison guard study, the whole 'behavior tracks the situation, not some stable character' argument). Funny thing is, the cross-situational correlations the situationists lean on are apparently in the same unimpressive ballpark (something like 0.3) as the repeatability numbers my own field used to get for supposedly consistent animal traits, back before we figured out that a low number like that doesn't actually mean the trait is an illusion. Can you argue that the situationists' jump from 'weak correlation between trait and behavior across situations' to 'therefore there's no such thing as robust character' makes basically the same mistake behavioral ecologists had to unlearn? Please don't just give me the standard 'virtue is really context-sensitive practical wisdom' reply -- I want an argument that actually engages with how you'd detect a stable trait statistically in the first place, and why a modest correlation doesn't settle things either way.
