So a few weeks ago I went down a YouTube rabbit hole about the history of "squaring the circle," and I found out that Thomas Hobbes (the Leviathan guy) spent basically the last 20-odd years of his life in this incredibly petty public feud with a mathematician named John Wallis, because Hobbes was 100% convinced he'd squared the circle using pure geometry, and Wallis just kept publishing pamphlet after pamphlet proving him wrong, and Hobbes never once backed down even though he was, like, definately wrong about it. Kind of hilarious honestly. But what actually caught my attention was WHY Hobbes cared so much about geometry in the first place — apparently he thought it was basically the one area where humans could have totally certain, no-doubt-about-it knowledge, because we make up the definitions ourselves (like, we decide what a triangle even is, so anything we correctly derive from that is just guaranteed true, no guessing needed). And from what I understand, that's also the reason he thought his political philosophy could be just as rock solid, since a "commonwealth" is also something people build on purpose through a social contract, not some natural thing you have to go discover like with physics.

Anyway this has been rattling around in my head since: if you could somehow bring Hobbes back today and show him non-Euclidean geometry (where you toss out the parallel line postulate and still get a perfectly consistent, working geometry) and also Gödel's incompleteness theorem (which as I understand it means any sufficiently complicated formal system is going to have true statements that it can't actually prove using its own rules), do you reckon that would wreck his whole "geometry equals perfect human certainty" idea? And if it did wreck that, would it also take down his idea that politics could be a science every bit as certain as geometry, or is there some clever way he could rework his own logic to survive both of those discoveries? I never studied any of this formally so I might be totally missing some obvious counter-argument, but I'd love an actual real argument for what you think he'd say and why, not just a "guess we'll never really know."
