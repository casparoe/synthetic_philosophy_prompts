In a graduate seminar on free will and moral responsibility, we recently spent a full session on Harry Frankfurt's famous argument against the Principle of Alternative Possibilities (PAP), the thesis that an agent is morally responsible for an action only if she could have done otherwise. In Frankfurt's classic case, Jones decides entirely on his own to perform some action, while a counterfactual intervener, Black, stands ready to manipulate Jones into performing that very action should Jones show any sign of choosing otherwise; because Jones never wavers, Black never actually has to step in, yet Jones seems just as responsible as he would be if Black did not exist at all — even though Jones could not have done otherwise. What most interested me was a technical rejoinder my professor introduced as the "dilemma defense," associated with Widerker and Ginet: Black can only know when to intervene by relying on some sign, occurring prior to Jones's decision, that reliably indicates how Jones is about to choose. But then either that sign is deterministically connected to the ensuing choice, in which case the whole scenario simply presupposes determinism and begs the question against the libertarian about whether alternative possibilities are needed, or the connection is not deterministic, in which case Jones could produce the sign and still fail to go on and choose as predicted — which would mean he had a genuine alternative possibility after all, contrary to what the case was supposed to establish.

Fischer, Pereboom, Mele and Robb, and others have developed or defended more sophisticated "blockage" cases that try to sidestep this dilemma entirely, typically by having the mechanism that would force the outcome operate directly on the neural or psychological process constitutive of the decision itself, rather than on some temporally prior and separately identifiable sign. I would like an essay that reconstructs the dilemma defense carefully, explains how blockage-style cases are meant to escape it, and then reaches an actual verdict rather than a survey: do these more sophisticated cases succeed in rescuing the Frankfurt strategy against PAP, or does a version of the original dilemma resurface even here — for instance, in the form of doubts about whether the indeterministic process that is supposedly "blocked" and the intervener's deterministic back-up can really count as bringing about the very same action? Please weigh the strongest considerations on each side, but commit to a definite answer rather than leaving the matter open.
