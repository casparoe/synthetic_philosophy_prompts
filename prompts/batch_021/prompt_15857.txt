Two classmates in my philosophy of science seminar got into a running argument this week, right after a lecture covering Popper, Quine, and Lakatos, and I haven't been able to settle it for myself.

(1) Elena said dark matter is basically a modern pseudo-science by Popper's own standard: whenever a galaxy's rotation curve or a cluster's dynamics doesn't match what the visible matter predicts, astrophysicists just add invisible matter in whatever amount and distribution is needed, so the hypothesis can absorb any observation and is never at risk.

(2) Marcus said that misdescribes the actual track record: dark matter has produced independent predictions confirmed later rather than retrofitted -- the Bullet Cluster's weak-lensing map shows the inferred mass sitting apart from the visible gas, exactly what you'd expect from a real substance and not a fudge factor.

(3) Elena replied that this dodges her real point: if the lensing map had lined up with the gas instead, would anyone believe astrophysicists would have abandoned dark matter, rather than adjusting the assumed particle properties or distribution yet again? If not, it's unfalsifiable at the core, however lucky particular versions of it have gotten.

(4) Marcus answered that by that standard no theory survives Duhem-Quine: every theory is tested together with a bundle of auxiliary assumptions, and revising them to save the core claim is exactly what happened when Uranus's orbit didn't match Newton's predictions and astronomers posited an unseen planet rather than abandoning Newton -- which led straight to Neptune's discovery. What actually matters, he said, is whether such rescues are progressive (yielding new, confirmed predictions) or merely degenerating patches, and dark matter looks progressive by that test.

(5) Elena answered that this cuts both ways: nineteenth-century astronomers made the identical move to explain Mercury's orbit, positing a planet they called Vulcan that was never found, and only gave it up once general relativity offered a rival account -- so we can't really tell whether dark matter is progressive or degenerating until a genuine rival theory -- which is what MOND is trying to be -- is fully worked out.

Could you go through their exchange move by move, saying who has the better of it at each stage and why -- it needn't be all-or-nothing -- and then say what, if anything, about the demarcation problem they're both missing?
