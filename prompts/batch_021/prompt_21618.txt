Two months ago I was the logistics lead for a small relief team responding to flash flooding. On the worst night, we had fuel to run our only boat to just one of two cut-off villages before the crossing became impassable, and both had radioed in medical emergencies that sounded equally severe. I made the call, one village got help and the other didn't, and I still haven't shaken the feeling that I failed the second village, even though I don't believe I could have chosen otherwise. Reading around afterward, I found the dispute between philosophers in the tradition of Ruth Barcan Marcus and Bernard Williams, who hold that cases like mine can be genuine moral dilemmas — situations in which both "oughts" were real and neither was cancelled, so the guilt I still feel is fitting rather than confused — and philosophers in the tradition of Alan Donagan, who hold that a fully consistent morality can never leave two such obligations standing at once, so that only one of my obligations could have been genuine, however hard it was to tell which at the time. A summary of the debate hasn't been enough for me to see how the two sides actually engage each other. Could you write a dialogue between a philosopher defending the reality of moral dilemmas and one denying it, having them work through the issue using a case modeled closely on mine? I'd want them to get into whether "ought" agglomerates, how each side squares its view with "ought implies can," and whether moral residue like mine is tracking something real or is a natural but mistaken reaction to loss — with neither side written as the obvious winner.
