I have no formal training in philosophy, but I have spent a good part of this year working through the Zhuangzi in translation, and one tension in the passages on death keeps bothering me. When Zhuangzi drums on a basin and sings after his wife's death, his reasoning seems to be that dying is just another turn in a continuous process of change, like the succession of the four seasons, so grief simply reflects a failure to understand fate -- a positive claim about how things really are. But the story of Lady Li, who wept bitterly when first carried off to serve the ruler of Jin and only later regretted her tears once she saw how good her new life had become, seems to make a more modest, skeptical point instead: not that death is actually fine, but that we have no real grounds for assuming it is bad. Are these genuinely different kinds of argument -- one about the nature of things, one about the limits of what we can know -- or do they collapse into the same point on closer inspection? And if they differ, which one is doing the real work in Zhuangzi's case against grieving?
