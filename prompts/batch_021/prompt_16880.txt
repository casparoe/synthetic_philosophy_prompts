I'm part of a small amateur forecasting group -- nobody in it has formal training, we just enjoy putting numbers on real-world questions and arguing about them once a month. At the last meetup, two regulars, Owen and Farah, got into an argument about Aumann's agreement theorem that started over an actual forecast (Owen had it at 65%, Farah at 85%, and forty minutes later neither had moved) and turned into something more abstract that I'd like your outside judgment on.

(1) Owen said that since they both start from the same prior and each trusts the other to reason correctly, once their numbers are fully out in the open they're rationally obligated to converge -- and, he added, they wouldn't even need to explain their reasoning: just repeating their current number back and forth would force them to the same value within a handful of rounds.

(2) Farah called this a shell game: a bare percentage carries no content by itself, so trading "65" and "85" with no justification behind either is just noise, not evidence -- and even a shared number at the end wouldn't mean they'd landed on the right one.

(3) Owen replied that the number isn't empty, since each person's stated posterior is already a function of everything they privately know; hearing it rules out things the other could be thinking, which is why the exchange has to stabilize after finitely many rounds. He granted this doesn't guarantee truth, only that two fully transparent people can't rationally stay in open disagreement.

(4) Farah answered that this all assumes they started from the same prior, which is exactly what's in question between two actual people, not something you get to stipulate; and that real "common knowledge" -- knowing that the other knows that you know, without end -- isn't something one conversation ever reaches, so none of this touched their actual disagreement.

(5) Owen's response was that a common prior isn't arbitrary: if their starting opinions differed for no reason tied to some difference in evidence, that difference would itself be a form of irrationality, so assuming a shared prior is the reasonable default, not a trick -- and finite versions of the theorem show agreement is reached after a small, bounded number of exchanges, so "we didn't talk long enough" isn't the escape hatch Farah wants it to be.

(6) Farah wasn't satisfied: she said the "default" move just relocates the assumption, since now it has to be true that any real difference in prior traces back to a difference in evidence, which is no easier to establish about two actual people than the original claim was -- and a clean, bounded number of rounds in a formal model isn't obviously the same thing as forty minutes of two people talking past each other at a bar.
