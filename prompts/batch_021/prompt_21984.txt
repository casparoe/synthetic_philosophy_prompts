I spent my career as an experimental physicist, and in retirement I've taken up philosophy of physics and find myself stuck on the quantum suicide thought experiment. What strikes me is that two people can accept the same physics -- the full Everettian many-worlds picture -- and still divide sharply over whether it gives anyone a reason to act. Write a dialogue between two such Everettians standing beside the device: a mechanism wired to a quantum trigger that spares the subject with some specified, possibly tiny, weight and otherwise kills them. One holds that since any observer who lives to reflect on the outcome will, by construction, find themselves in a branch where they survived, 'quantum immortality' makes pulling the lever rational, or at least not a mistake, however small that branch's weight. The other, echoing David Lewis's reply to the case, insists rational self-concern has to track the branch's Born-rule measure, not the mere fact that it exists, so the act is exactly as reckless as it looks from outside. Have them argue it out directly, each answering what the other actually said rather than a caricature of it, and let them work out, if they can, whether it matters that whoever wakes up in the surviving branch may not, in any sense that counts, be the one who pulled the lever.
