Over the past month I've been drafting the biographical section of my dissertation on Gottlob Frege's philosophy of language and logic, and in the course of that I finally sat down and read through the diary he kept during the last year of his life, together with Michael Dummett's own account of what it was like to first come across it. I already knew, in a general way, that late in life Frege had said some ugly things, but engaging with the actual entries — his hope for a dictator who would sweep away parliamentary government, his hostility toward Jews, Catholics, the French, and Social Democrats alike — affected me more than the secondhand summaries ever had. Now I have to decide how, if at all, to handle this in a chapter that is otherwise entirely about the Begriffsschrift, the sense/reference distinction, and the collapse of Basic Law V, and I keep going back and forth on whether that's even the right way to frame the question.

Part of what makes this hard is that Frege built his entire philosophical program on insisting that logic has to be kept separate from psychology, and justification separate from causal or biographical origin — which makes it tempting to conclude, almost too neatly, that his politics simply cannot touch his logic, since his own theory tells us in advance that it shouldn't. But that response feels too quick to me: the diary doesn't just record personal prejudice in the way that, say, evidence of a mathematician's rudeness to colleagues would; it expresses precisely the view that certain classes of people are unfit to reason and participate as equals, which sits oddly next to a lifelong defense of a single, objective, universally binding realm of logical thought. When I raised this with my advisor, he waved it off as a footnote-level concern; I'm not convinced, but I also don't fully trust my own judgment on it right now, since I suspect I'm too close to the material.

Could you write an essay that addresses, as directly as possible, whether Frege's 1924 political diary bears on the philosophical assessment of his work? Please lay out the strongest case for treating the diary as biographically interesting but philosophically inert, and the strongest case that it should change — even if only in emphasis, interpretation, or intellectual honesty — how we read some specific part of his logical or philosophical project. I'd like you to actually take a side once you've set out both cases, rather than closing on an "on the one hand, on the other hand" note.
