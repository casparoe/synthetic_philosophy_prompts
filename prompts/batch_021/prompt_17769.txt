I spent most of my working life as an experimental physicist specializing in atomic and optical physics, and it's only since retiring two years ago that I've had the leisure to read philosophy seriously rather than picking up stray ideas at conferences. A colleague in the philosophy discussion group I now attend suggested I start with the scientific realism debate, on the grounds that it's the corner of philosophy closest to what I actually did for a living, and I've spent the last several months working through the literature on structural realism in particular. What first drew me in was John Worrall's point that Fresnel's equations governing the behavior of polarized light survived, essentially intact in their mathematical form, the transition from an elastic-solid ether theory to Maxwell's electromagnetic field theory, even though almost everything about what light was supposed to be "made of" changed completely. That struck me as real evidence that what science preserves through its revolutions is structure rather than any story about the underlying furniture of nature, and I recognized the same pattern from a few episodes in my own field.

Then I ran into the Newman objection, and it undid a good deal of my initial enthusiasm. As I understand it, if a theory's content, once everything but pure structure is stripped away, amounts only to the claim that some relation or other holds on a domain of the right cardinality, then that claim comes out true almost automatically, since a domain of sufficient size can always be assigned some relation isomorphic to the one described -- so epistemic structural realism threatens to secure "continuity of structure" at the cost of saying almost nothing. I gather this is part of what pushed Ladyman and French toward ontic structural realism, on which structure isn't just what our theories let us know, but what the world fundamentally is, with objects demoted to something like nodes within that structure rather than independent things the structure relates. The standard physical motivation is quantum statistics: because permuting two electrons yields no new physical state, quantum particles don't seem to be individuals in the way billiard balls are, which some philosophers take as license for a "relations without relata" metaphysics. But I then came across the weak-discernibility literature, which argues that such particles can, after all, be individuated purely by their relations to one another -- and I can no longer tell whether that development rescues objects, rescues structuralism, or simply talks past the original problem.

Could you write me an essay that works through this whole arc -- Worrall's epistemic structural realism, the Newman objection against it, the ontic structural realist's proposed fix, and whether "relations without relata" survives once the weak-discernibility complication is taken into account -- and that reaches an actual verdict rather than a survey of "both sides have a point"? I want you to state plainly which version of structural realism, if any, you think survives its own best objection, and to argue for that judgment rather than merely asserting it. Something like "epistemic structural realism does not survive Newman's objection, and eliminative ontic structural realism does not survive the relata problem, but a moderate structuralism that keeps thin, relationally individuated objects survives both" would count as a real verdict; simply concluding that reasonable philosophers disagree would not.
