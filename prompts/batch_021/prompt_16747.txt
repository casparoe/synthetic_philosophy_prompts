I'm a novelist, and I've been asked to contribute a personal essay to a small collection on the ethics of fiction-writing, alongside several contributors who are actual philosophers, so I'd rather not embarrass myself with something that falls apart under mild pressure. The essay leans on Rorty, and the section below is the part I trust least. I'd like your comments on it.

Rorty's antirepresentationalism holds that sentences don't mirror an independent reality; they're tools within vocabularies, and a vocabulary earns its keep by what it lets us do, not by how well it corresponds to the world. Because there's no vantage point outside every vocabulary from which to referee between them, he thinks our deepest commitments -- what he calls a final vocabulary, the words we use to justify our beliefs and our lives, which run out of further justification the moment someone asks us to justify them -- can't be defended by argument at all, only altered by redescription. This is why he ranks the "edifying" philosopher who unsettles a vocabulary above the "systematic" philosopher who is still hunting for foundations to place under it.

The move that matters for my purposes: Rorty thinks argument only works on people who already share enough of your final vocabulary to feel its pull. What moves someone who doesn't is redescription -- being shown, in enough irritating particular detail, a person or a species of suffering that their old vocabulary let them not notice. That's his case for why Orwell's 1984 and Nabokov's Lolita did more for the moral imagination than a comparable quantity of moral philosophy: the novel gets past argument's gate by not going through the gate. His "liberal ironist" -- who regards his own final vocabulary as no less contingent than anyone else's, yet remains committed to reducing cruelty -- is supposed to be produced by exactly this kind of exposure, not argued into being.

Here is my worry, and I don't know whether it's fatal or just uninformed. If what does the moral work is redescription rather than getting anything right, I don't see what principled thing stops a skilled piece of propaganda from counting as the same kind of achievement. Rhetoric that redescribes a group of people as vermin also bypasses argument, and has also worked, horribly well. The liberal ironist just happens to prefer redescriptions that widen the circle of "us" rather than shrink it -- but "happens to prefer" is carrying an enormous amount of weight for someone who has already told us there's no further fact of the matter to appeal to instead.

I try to make this concrete with a scene from my own second novel, where a doorman who has been furniture for three hundred pages is given, in one chapter, a memory of his own. I want to say that chapter is doing what Rorty says novels do. I can't quite tell whether I believe that or whether I just enjoy being told my day job matters by a philosopher.

Three things, specifically. First, tell me whether I've gotten Rorty right, or whether I've quietly flattened antirepresentationalism into a relativism he would have resisted -- I suspect I've smoothed over exactly the part I should be most careful with. Second, is the propaganda worry a real problem for his view, or does he already have an answer I'm forgetting? I recall that the split between the private ironist and the public liberal is supposed to be doing some work here, but I don't remember whether it actually blocks the objection or just relocates it. Third, and please be honest: is the doorman scene doing any argumentative work, or is it decoration for a claim I haven't earned?
