I'm several drafts into a novel centered on two former doctoral students of the same philosopher of language, estranged for nearly twenty years, who are thrown back together on a panel at a small conference held in their late advisor's memory. The scene I can't get right is the panel itself, where an old disagreement between them -- one that predates, and partly explains, the estrangement -- resurfaces in front of an audience of former colleagues. Both were trained on their advisor's reading of Wittgenstein on rule-following, but the elder of the two, Innes, has spent his career defending Kripke's skeptical solution, while the younger, Priya, has drifted toward the Baker-and-Hacker camp and now treats the whole "skeptical paradox" as a piece of exegetical sleight of hand: a problem generated by misreading Philosophical Investigations §201, not one Wittgenstein himself thought needed solving.

Could you draft this exchange for me? My editor wants the scene cut as too abstract for a general readership, and I would like to prove her wrong by making the philosophy itself the drama, which means both positions need to be argued at full strength rather than one propped up against a strawman. Please have Innes and Priya get past the "plus/quus" anecdote -- textbook fare to both of them -- and into the harder pressure points: why Kripke thinks a disposition alone can never constitute a fact about meaning, since a disposition only tells you how you would respond, errors included, not how you ought to; what the community view actually buys you once you have given up on individual facts about meaning, given that the same skepticism seems to threaten the community's own practice; and whether Priya's move -- dissolving the paradox by denying that any meaning-constituting fact was ever required in the first place -- is a genuine alternative to Kripke's solution, or simply a refusal to answer the question he posed.

I don't want a clean winner. These two have been having some version of this argument for two decades, and it would ring false if either simply capitulated. But I do want each of them to land at least one point the other cannot easily absorb, and I would like the exchange to end with the rest of the room, and the reader, genuinely unsure who got the better of it. Two or three pages, mostly dialogue with only minimal staging, would suit the chapter best.
