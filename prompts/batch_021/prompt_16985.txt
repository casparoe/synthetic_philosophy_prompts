My uncle was an actuary for almost 40 years — he's actually the reason I ended up in a stats PhD program — and his signature line, the one he said about literally every decison, big or small, from buying a car to whether to get a second opinion on his own hip, was "more information can never make you worse off, only better, so you might as well look." A couple years ago I realized this was basically his own folksy version of a real result, something decision theorists call Good's theorem, about how the expected value of free information is never negative for a Bayesian decision-maker. He loved that there was "real math" behind his favorite line and made me explain it to him twice.

Here's what I'm stuck on. Right at the end, when his oncologist offered him a pretty specific timeline, he said he didn't want to hear the number, and he held to that all the way through. My aunt keeps bringing this up like he chickened out of his own worldview at the last second, and I don't think that's right, but I can't articulate why on my own. I'm giving his eulogy Saturday, maybe 6 minutes tops — a good third of the room is old colleagues from the actuarial world who heard him say that line in a hundred meetings and will clock it immediately if I mention his last decision without addressing it, and the rest is just family who've never heard of Bayesian anything. Could you write the eulogy so it actually uses the real content of the theorem — what it does and doesn't promise — to explain why declining the number wasn't a betrayal of the thing he spent his career saying, instead of just hand-waving it as "he wanted peace at the end"? It just needs to land as a eulogy, not turn into a decision theory lecture.
