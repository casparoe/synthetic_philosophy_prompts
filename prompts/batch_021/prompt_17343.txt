My daughter's getting married in a few weeks and I got asked to do a toast at the reception, which is already nerve-wracking enough without the added wrinkle that her fiancé Theo is finishing up a philosophy PhD where, near as I can tell, his whole dissertation is basically about why a promise binds you at all — like what makes saying "I promise" a heavier, different thing than just telling someone what you plan to do. From dinner table arguments I've picked up that he thinks the popular idea that a promise is basically just a useful social convention (something Hume apparently started, or at least gets blamed for) can't be the whole story, because it doesn't explain why we'd still think you were wrong to break a promise even in a case where literally nobody would ever find out and no one's expectations were even riding on it. He also gets weirdly annoyed when people describe a wedding vow as "basically just a contract," so theres clearly some real distinction in his head that I don't fully get. My own half-baked theory, for what it's worth, is that a promise is less like betting on your own future behavior and more like just deciding, right then, who you're going to be from now on — no idea if that's an actual position or if I just invented it.

Here's my problem: half the room is going to be his PhD cohort and his advisor, who I'm told will absolutely notice if I just wave my hands at "philosophy stuff," and the other half is my side of the family, who mostly think philosophy is what you did in your first year of college before picking a real major. Theo also pulled me aside, half-joking, and asked that I not make him out to be the guy whose research proves marriage is basically a fiction everyone's agreed to play along with — apparently his mom already has some worries along those lines. So what I want is maybe two or three minutes of the toast that actually gets into this question for real, using it to say something true about why THIS promise, the one they're making at the altar in front of everybody, is supposed to hold even on the bad days — without turning into a lecture, and without the philosophers in the room deciding I butchered the actual debate. Can you write me that part of the speech?
