I'm a philosophy PhD student working in formal epistemology, and the second chapter of my dissertation leans heavily on I.J. Good's 1967 paper, fittingly titled "On the Principle of Total Evidence," which proves that a Bayesian agent who conditionalizes on a cost-free signal and then chooses optimally can never end up with lower expected utility than an agent who ignores that signal and acts on the prior alone. I've been treating this "Good's theorem" as close to a formal proof that rational agents should always want more evidence before acting, and I open the chapter by presenting it as a decisive answer to anyone who thinks intellectual curiosity or inquiry needs some further philosophical justification beyond Bayesian rationality itself.

Over dinner last week, a friend finishing her economics PhD brought up Jack Hirshleifer's 1971 paper, "The Private and Social Value of Information and the Reward to Inventive Activity," where he argues that when information about some future contingency becomes public before the parties involved can write insurance contracts against it, the resulting foreknowledge can eliminate the market for mutual insurance altogether, leaving everyone involved worse off in expectation than they would have been under continued ignorance. That struck me as exactly the kind of case Good's theorem says is impossible: free information, correctly processed by fully rational Bayesians, making all of them worse off ex ante. So I've come away thinking Hirshleifer essentially handed us a counterexample to Good's theorem, which would force me to either drop or heavily qualify the opening move of my chapter. Am I right that Hirshleifer's result shows Good's theorem to be false as generally stated, and if so, how does the literature typically restrict the theorem to accommodate cases like his?
