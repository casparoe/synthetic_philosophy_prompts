I cover science and ideas for a living, and I've been down a rabbit hole for a piece that started out as a story about goalkeepers choking on penalty kicks and somehow ended with me reading Timothy Williamson at 1am. The bit that snagged me is this idea of "luminosity" and how it supposedly wrecks something called the KK principle. As far as I can tell, the KK principle says that whenever you know something, you're automatically in a position to know that you know it, no gaps allowed. Williamson's move against this is to argue that basically no interesting mental state is "luminous," meaning a state you're guaranteed to be able to detect from the inside whenever your actually in it. His go-to case is feeling cold: there's no crisp instant when you flip from clearly-warm to clearly-cold, so somewhere in that fade your sense of your own state gets less reliable than the KK principle would require.

That example makes sense on its own terms, but it's the one every intro syllabus reaches for, and I'd rather not just parrot it back in my piece. Could you come up with a different, fairly concrete example — not feeling-cold, not the heap-of-sand/sorites stuff, ideally something a bit less sensory — of a state or condition that fails to be luminous in Williamson's sense? I want you to actually walk through why it fails, not just assert it, and then spell out what that specifically buys you against the KK principle: is the upshot just that we're fallible near borderline cases, or does it actually undercut the bigger idea that knowing = being able to know that you know? Bonus points if you can gesture at why anyone outside an epistemology seminar should care.
