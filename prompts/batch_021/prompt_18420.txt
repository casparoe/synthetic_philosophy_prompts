I recently started a research fellowship at an organization that works on AI alignment, and in my first week I sat in on a lunchtime debate between two of the researchers, Naomi and Felix, over a classic case used to motivate updateless decision theory (UDT): the "Counterfactual Mugging."

The setup under discussion was this. Omega, a perfect predictor, flips a fair coin. If it comes up heads, Omega hands you $10,000 — but only if it predicted in advance that you would pay it $100 in the event the coin came up tails. If it comes up tails, Omega simply asks you for that $100. The coin comes up tails, and Omega asks.

1. Naomi said you should pay. Before the flip, the policy "pay on tails" has a far higher expected value than "never pay" — you lose $100 half the time but gain $10,000 the other half — so refusing once tails actually comes up just means you were never willing to be the kind of agent who could win the bet in the first place.

2. Felix said that's a fact about which policy looks good in advance, not a reason to hand over money now. The coin has already landed tails; the heads-world in which you'd collect $10,000 doesn't exist and never will, whatever you do. Paying in the one situation you're actually in makes you $100 poorer for nothing, and calling that "rational" confuses the average payoff of a rule with what's rational to do in the single case in front of you.

3. Naomi replied that Felix's framing already assumes what's at issue, since it treats "what should I do given that I've observed tails" as the only question that could count as rational. Omega's prediction happened before the coin was flipped, so an agent who pays only when paying benefits its present branch is exactly the kind of agent Omega would have predicted wouldn't pay — meaning that agent was never actually in the running for the $10,000. The mistake, if there is one, isn't made at the moment of paying; it's already baked into the disposition you showed up with.

4. Felix granted that the disposition to pay is good news for your bank account before the flip, but said Naomi was quietly answering a different question than the one on the table: "which disposition is best to have" and "what should this instance of me do right now" aren't the same question, and you can consistently answer "pay" to the first and "don't pay" to the second, since the tails-you who exists and the heads-you who doesn't are not choosing together. He added that if Naomi's argument worked, it would justify handing over money to absolutely anyone you can imagine having precommitted to reward you for it, with no principled place to stop.
