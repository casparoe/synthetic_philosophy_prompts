I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, but I've recently been reading about functionalism in philosophy of mind and the associated idea of multiple realizability — the claim that a mental state like pain is defined by its causal or functional role rather than its physical substrate, so that it could in principle be realized in neurons, silicon, or some entirely different medium. What I'm stuck on is a later objection, apparently raised by Putnam himself against the view he helped found: that the underlying notion of "implementation" is far too permissive, since if a physical system counts as implementing a finite-state automaton whenever some mapping can be found from its physical states onto the automaton's formal states, then almost any sufficiently complex system — even a rock — can be mapped onto essentially any automaton, including one that supposedly describes a mind. If that's right, multiple realizability stops being a substantive fact about minds and becomes trivially true of everything, which seems to undercut functionalism rather than support it. I understand Chalmers tried to block this by constraining what counts as a genuine implementation, but I don't have a solid grasp of how that fix is supposed to work. Could you explain concretely why the naive, mapping-based notion of implementation collapses into triviality like this, and what kind of constraint is supposed to rule out the rock while still letting silicon and neurons count as implementing the same mind?
