Six months ago I took over as the de facto moderator of an online community that has operated for more than a decade, and a recent controversy over one of its oldest unwritten rules — never voted on, never written down, yet enforced for years through simple peer pressure — has left me trying to understand what actually gives such a rule its grip on people. That question led me to David Lewis's *Convention*, where he presents his theory as making good on an idea he explicitly traces back to Hume. Going to the source, I found the following passage in the *Treatise* (Book 3, Part 2, Section 2, "Of the Origin of Justice and Property"), and I've been stuck on it ever since:

"This convention is not of the nature of a promise: for even promises themselves, as we shall see afterwards, arise from human conventions. It is only a general sense of common interest; which sense all the members of society express to one another, and which induces them to regulate their conduct by certain rules. ... When this common sense of interest is mutually express'd, and is known to both, it produces a suitable resolution and behaviour. And this may properly enough be call'd a convention or agreement betwixt us, though without the interposition of a promise ... Two men, who pull the oars of a boat, do it by an agreement or convention, though they have never given promises to each other. Nor is the rule concerning the stability of possession the less deriv'd from human conventions, that it arises gradually, and acquires force by a slow progression ... In like manner are languages gradually establish'd by human conventions without any promise."

What I cannot reconcile is Hume's insistence, in the same breath, that this "convention" is an agreement that is "mutually express'd, and known to both" parties, while also maintaining that it involves no promise and can arise as gradually and anonymously as a language does, with no identifiable moment at which anything is actually communicated. If nothing resembling an ordinary act of communication ever occurs, what work is "mutually express'd" doing here, and how does Hume get from a mere convergence of self-interest to something with enough normative standing to later ground genuine obligation — so that breaking the convention counts as unjust, not merely unwise? Is this a real gap in the argument, and if so, does Lewis's later apparatus of common knowledge and coordination equilibria actually close it, or does it just relocate the same puzzle in more technical dress?
