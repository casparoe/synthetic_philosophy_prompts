I'm a formal epistemology PhD student, and somewhat randomly the philosophy person on my university hospital's ethics committee. We've got a case for Thursday that's been eating at me. Two years ago, while stable, a patient with bipolar I wrote a psychiatric advance directive (a 'Ulysses contract') asking staff to medicate her by force if she ever became manic and refused treatment, and stating explicitly that her future manic self's objections shouldn't count as her real wishes. She's manic now, unnervingly articulate, insisting she's fine and the directive shouldn't apply anymore. Her psychiatrist wants to honor what she signed. Her sister says overriding what a coherent, competent-sounding person is telling you right now, just because paper-her outvoted her, isnt respecting her, its picking whichever version of her is more convenient. Off the clock this is basically van Fraassen's Reflection Principle to me -- when exactly are you licensed to not defer to your 'future' self's stated beliefs? Can you draft the memo I owe the committee before Thursday -- it needs an actual recommendation with a concrete timeframe, not 'gather more input,' and it has to genuinely sit with the sister's objection instead of just pointing at the signed form. Nobody else on the committee has heard of Reflection so it can't read like a seminar handout, but don't smooth the real problem away either.
