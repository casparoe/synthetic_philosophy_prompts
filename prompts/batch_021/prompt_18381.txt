Three months ago my diocese asked me to join a small panel of physicians it consults during causes for beatification, and I said yes without thinking much about what it would actually require of me. I'm an oncologist, and I've just been handed the panel's first real case: a parishioner whose biopsy-confirmed, metastatic pancreatic cancer went into complete remission within three weeks of a novena at a candidate's tomb, after she had stopped chemotherapy and entered hospice care, with clean scans five years on. Our narrow task, before the file goes to Rome, is to say whether the cure has any currently known medical explanation, not whether a miracle occurred. One of the other panelists, a philosopher the bishop added for exactly this reason, keeps pressing Hume's old argument: however carefully we phrase our finding, "no known explanation" functions as testimony for a suspension of natural law, and an unrecognized natural cause -- even a spontaneous remission with no known mechanism -- will always be antecedently more probable than a genuine violation of natural law, however well the case is documented. Another panelist, our chaplain, thinks this proves too much: if no finding could ever meet that bar, we should say so plainly to the tribunal rather than issue another hedge that sounds authoritative but isn't. I need to draft the memo I'll bring to next week's meeting: it should recommend specific language for our finding, or specific next steps if we're not ready to issue one, and it should actually answer the philosopher's argument rather than talk past it.
