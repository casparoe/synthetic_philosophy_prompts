I'm a retired physicist (condensed matter, mostly) who's gotten a bit obsessed with philosophy since retiring. It started because a friend told me David Lewis's modal realism—the idea that all possible worlds are just as real and concrete as ours, merely cut off from us causally—was basically "Everett for philosophers." That's not quite right once you dig into it, but it got me reading more Lewis, which is how I stumbled on a paper of his I never expected: "Do We Believe in Penal Substitution?", where he turns his usual careful analytic style on the Christian doctrine that Christ was punished in the place of sinners. The bit thats stuck with me (I might be mangling some of the details, it's been a few weeks since I read it) is that we're all totally fine with a third party paying off someone else's debt—if my neighbor pays my mortgage, the bank doesn't care whose signature is on the check—but having someone else serve my prison sentence doesn't obviously clear my name the same way. Lewis pokes at why punishment seems to resist that kind of substitution even though debt doesn't, and alot of it seems to turn on what "desert" really means.

Rather than me botching a summary of his actual moves secondhand, I'd love to see the dispute played out properly. Could you write me a dialogue between two philosophers—say, running into each other after a seminar on this very paper—where one defends a carefully qualified version of penal substitution as coherent (something like separating who bears the cost or suffering from who bears the guilt, or leaning on legal notions like vicarious liability), while the other insists punishment just can't be transferred the way debt can, so the whole doctrine trades on an equivocation between "penalty" and "punishment" no matter how it's dressed up. I want them actually responding to each other's specific moves rather than talking past one another, and neither should be the obvious loser going in—make them both genuinely sharp. Bonus points if one of them notices that a lot of this hinges on assuming retributivism in particular, and asks whether a more consequentialist or expressive theory of punishment would make substitution easier, or maybe even harder, to defend.
