"Wittgenstein's target in On Certainty is Moore's attempt to answer external-world skepticism simply by asserting propositions like 'Here is one hand' and claiming to know them with certainty. Wittgenstein's diagnosis is that Moore has misused the verb 'to know.' In ordinary contexts, saying 'I know that p' implies that one could in principle be mistaken, that one has grounds or evidence for p, and that the claim could be checked against something. But propositions like 'I have two hands' or 'The earth has existed for a long time' are not held on the basis of evidence at all: nothing could count as evidence for or against them, because they are presupposed by the very practice of gathering and weighing evidence in the first place. So when Moore says 'I know I have a hand,' he treats a proposition that actually functions as a rule or frame for inquiry as though it were an ordinary empirical hypothesis that had simply been verified particularly well. Since nothing could count as evidence either way, Wittgenstein is really agreeing with the skeptic that we do not, strictly speaking, know these propositions; the dispute between Moore and the skeptic turns out to be a dispute over something that falls outside the scope of 'knowing' altogether, and on that narrow question Wittgenstein comes down on the skeptic's side, even while insisting that the skeptic's doubt is itself confused.

Positively, Wittgenstein calls these presupposed propositions 'hinge propositions,' using the image of a door: the hinges are what let the door, our ordinary practices of doubting, testing, and giving reasons, turn at all, which is exactly why the hinges themselves cannot be among the things that turn. Elsewhere he uses the image of a river-bed, fixed beneath the flow of the ordinary empirical propositions that run over it, and this fixity is what gives hinge propositions their characteristic permanence: once a proposition has hardened into a hinge it stays there, immune to the kind of revision that ordinary empirical beliefs undergo, which is precisely what qualifies it to serve as a foundation for everything else. On this picture, hinges are best understood as the naturalized, Wittgensteinian descendants of Cartesian foundations: propositions we are certain of not because we have proven them, but because doubting them would undermine the very possibility of doubting anything else."
