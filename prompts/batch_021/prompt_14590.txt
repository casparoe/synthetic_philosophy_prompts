I'm writing my IB Extended Essay in Philosophy on Fitch's paradox of knowability, and before I hand my supervisor a full draft next week I'd like an outside opinion on the argument itself. I first ran into this in Theory of Knowledge, during a discussion of Michael Dummett's idea that a statement can only be true if it is at least possible, in principle, for someone to know it; someone in my class asked what would stop that idea from collapsing into "everything true is actually known," which sounded like a silly worry until I found out that this is essentially what Frederic Fitch proved in 1963 - and, stranger still, that the proof had apparently been handed to him anonymously by a referee back in 1945, who nobody identified as Alonzo Church until decades later.

The section I would like feedback on first reconstructs the argument and then compares two replies to it. The reconstruction goes: assume the knowability principle, that every truth is knowable in principle (∀p(p → ◊Kp)); assume also non-omniscience, that some truth is not actually known (∃p(p ∧ ¬Kp)); substituting an instance of the second into the first yields ◊K(p ∧ ¬Kp) for that instance, but a short side-argument, relying only on the assumptions that knowledge distributes over conjunction and that knowledge entails truth, shows that K(p ∧ ¬Kp) is self-contradictory and therefore not even possible - directly contradicting what was just derived. Since giving up the knowability principle would mean giving up the anti-realist position that motivated it in the first place, the anti-realist seems forced to give up non-omniscience instead, landing on the absurd conclusion that every truth is actually known. I then compare Tennant's fix, which restricts the knowability principle to "Cartesian" statements (ones it is not self-contradictory to know), with Dummett's fix, which restricts it to "basic," logically simple statements, and I argue that Tennant's version is strictly more minimal, since it excludes only statements that are unknowable for purely structural reasons - and I go on to suggest that this also makes it the more defensible of the two, though I have a nagging feeling I am moving too fast there. Finally, I bring in a complication that I believe comes from Brogaard and Salerno's reply to both Dummett and Tennant (I am not fully confident I have the attribution right): that even granting the Cartesian restriction, strengthening the knowability principle into a biconditional and adding the independently plausible "KK principle" - that knowing something entails knowing that you know it - lets you rederive essentially the same absurd conclusion without ever substituting a non-Cartesian statement into the principle, which I take to suggest that restricting the principle's syntax is treating a symptom rather than the underlying disease.

Could you go through this with a critical eye and tell me, specifically: whether my reconstruction of the core proof is valid as I've stated it, particularly the step from "K(p ∧ ¬Kp) is self-contradictory" to "so it is not even possible" (that is the move I feel least sure of, since it seems to quietly assume that those two epistemic principles hold of necessity rather than just happening to be true); whether I am representing Tennant's and Dummett's actual positions fairly, or flattening them in a way that would make a real epistemologist cringe, and whether my "more minimal, therefore more defensible" inference is too quick; and whether the closing complication is a genuinely interesting point or a well-known one that I am presenting as though I had thought of it myself. I would much rather hear all of this now than after I've submitted the essay.
