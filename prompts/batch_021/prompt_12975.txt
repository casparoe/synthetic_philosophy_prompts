A friend and I spent most of dinner last night arguing about Aristotle's sea-battle passage in Chapter 9 of the *De Interpretatione* (the Bekker numbering is something like 19a, though I might be misremembering the exact line), and neither of us walked away convinced. His position is that the whole puzzle is really just a scope ambiguity dressed up as metaphysics: once you distinguish "necessarily (p or not-p)" from "(necessarily p) or (necessarily not-p)" — what the scholastics apparently called the composite and divided senses of the claim — the argument dissolves into a fairly mundane logical point, and there's no need to saddle Aristotle with anything as exotic as a truth-value gap for statements about the future. I pushed back that this reading is too tidy, because Aristotle doesn't just say that neither disjunct is necessary; he also seems to say we cannot yet say determinately which one is true, which sounds like a genuinely semantic claim about the present status of future-tensed statements, not merely a remark about modal scope.

For reference, here is the passage we were actually arguing over (this is from the older Oxford translation we were assigned for our ancient philosophy seminar — Edghill's, I believe):

"It is therefore plain that it is not necessary that of an affirmation and a negation one should be true and the other false. For it is not the same with things that are not always existent, or not always non-existent... One of the two propositions in such instances must be true and the other false, but we cannot say determinately that this one or that one is false, and must instead leave the matter undecided. One may indeed be more likely to be true than the other, but it cannot yet be said to be actually true or actually false. [...] Let us take the case of a sea-battle: it is necessary that there either will or will not be a sea-battle tomorrow, but it is not necessary that there should be one tomorrow, nor that there should not be one — though it is necessary that there either should or should not be one."

Could you help me actually work through this rather than giving me the standard textbook summary of "the problem of future contingents"? Specifically: does Aristotle's language here commit him to denying bivalence for future-contingent statements in advance of the fact (so that "there will be a sea-battle tomorrow" is, right now, neither true nor false), or is my friend right that everything he says is compatible with each such statement already being true or false, just not necessarily so? Is the composite/divided-sense distinction actually enough to make the "cannot say determinately" language innocuous, or does that phrase resist being fully absorbed into a scope point? And how do you think this bears on the worry, sharpened later by the Diodorean Master Argument, that accepting ordinary bivalence and excluded middle for future-tensed claims threatens to collapse into fatalism — is Aristotle's maneuver here best read as a deliberate way of blocking exactly that inference?
