I'm working on a piece about how the mechanics of political deception have shifted since the mid-twentieth century, and I keep returning to Hannah Arendt's 1971 essay "Lying in Politics," written in response to the Pentagon Papers. There she distinguishes the ordinary political liar — who still needs to keep the truth in view in order to conceal it — from a newer figure she diagnosed among the war's systems analysts and managers, people fluent in the idiom of problem-solving, who dealt not in isolated falsehoods but in self-consistent models of the war that eventually displaced, even for their own authors, the reality those models were meant to track. As she argues in a related essay, "Truth and Politics," factual truth, unlike the truths of logic or mathematics, is contingent, could always have been otherwise, and survives only so long as witnesses remain willing and able to attest to it — which is exactly what makes it vulnerable to organized, wholesale falsification. Write me a short story that dramatizes this specifically modern danger, centered on a professional maker of narratives — a strategist, analyst, spokesperson, or whatever role you find most compelling — who constructs an alternative account of events so thoroughly that they lose their own bearings inside it. Let the story show, rather than simply state, what makes this kind of lying different in kind from an ordinary lie about a single fact.
