I spent my career as an analytical chemist, and since retiring I've been reading philosophy for the first time, mostly epistemology -- which is probably why a recent dispute at my birdwatching club has been on my mind ever since. Our most experienced member, Gerald, logged a rare warbler on the strength of a two-second glimpse. When another member's recording from that same morning was later flagged by outside experts as showing a common look-alike species instead, Gerald refused to even listen to it, insisting that forty years of fieldwork meant the tape had to be wrong somehow.

That set off a running argument between two other members, Helen and David, that I haven't been able to settle myself. (1) Helen said this was plainly irrational: no one can license themselves in advance to disregard evidence merely because they are confident they're right, since that would make it impossible ever to catch one's own mistakes. (2) David replied that if Gerald genuinely knows what he saw, then anything seeming to contradict it must be misleading somehow -- so it isn't irrational of him to expect the tape is flawed; the real question is only whether Gerald knows, not whether that inference is legitimate. (3) Helen answered that this only proves her point: from the inside, Gerald has no way of telling whether he truly knows or his confidence has, for once, misled him, and refusing to listen is refusing to gather the one piece of evidence that would tell him which. (4) David came back that this proves too much: scientists and birders alike routinely, and rightly, decline to re-examine well-established observations whenever a contrarian appears, and forty years of accurate identifications is exactly the record that earns someone that latitude.

Neither budged, and I confess I keep changing my mind. Could you go through the exchange move by move, saying who has the better of it at each point, and flag whatever you think both of them are missing?
