So I've been going down a metaethics rabbit hole lately -- in my seminar we're doing buck-passing theories of value, basically Scanlon's idea that for something to be valuable just IS for it to have properties that give us reasons to desire it, admire it, promote it, whatever -- value itself doesn't do any independent work, it "passes the buck" to those other reasons. Our TA brought up the standard objection to this, the wrong kind of reasons problem, using (I think) the Rabinowicz/Rønnow-Rasmussen demon case: some demon tells you he'll cause horrific suffering unless you admire a saucer of mud. Now you've technically got a reason to admire the mud, but the mud obviously isn't admirable, it doesn't have any admiration-worthy properties, the "reason" is coming from somewhere totally outside the object. The fix everyone reaches for is to say buck-passing should only count "the right kind" of reasons, ones actually grounded in the object's features, but then the worry is you can't cash out what makes a reason "the right kind" without secretly already appealing to facts about fittingness/value -- which is exactly the thing buck-passing was trying to explain in it's own reductive terms, so the whole thing threatens to be circular.

Can you write me an essay that actually takes a stand on this? Walk through the main candidate ways people try to draw the right-kind/wrong-kind line (e.g., restricting to reasons that are about the object itself rather than about the state of having the attitude, or saying wrong-kind reasons only bear on whether it's good/prudent to have the attitude rather than on whether the attitude is correct/fitting), and be honest about where each one runs into trouble. But please don't just land on "reasonable people disagree" -- I want an actual verdict on whether the wrong-kind-of-reasons problem is fatal to buck-passing/fitting-attitude accounts of value, or whether one of these moves (or some combination) genuinely rescues the view, and I want you to defend that verdict rather than just gesturing at it.
