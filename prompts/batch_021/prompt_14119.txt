I have been TAing an upper-level undergraduate seminar in moral philosophy this term, and I am partway through grading the take-home essays on the situationist critique of virtue ethics — a topic I know mostly through the rationalist-adjacent literature on the fundamental attribution error rather than through the philosophical literature proper, so I would like a careful outside check on my grading before I record final marks.

The essay question was:

"Explain the situationist critique of virtue ethics developed by Gilbert Harman and John Doris, making clear what empirical evidence it appeals to and what conclusion it is meant to establish about the Aristotelian conception of character. Then explain how EITHER Julia Annas OR Rachana Kamtekar responds to this critique, and briefly assess whether the response succeeds."

Here is one student's answer, reproduced in full:

"The situationist critique argues that virtue ethics rests on an empirical assumption that the relevant psychological evidence does not support: that people possess broad, temporally stable character traits — such as honesty, courage, or compassion — that issue in consistent behavior across a wide range of situations. Gilbert Harman and John Doris both draw on decades of social psychology to challenge this assumption. Perhaps the best-known evidence is Darley and Batson's study, in which seminary students crossing campus to deliver a short talk were far more likely to stop and help a man slumped in a doorway if they were not in a hurry, regardless of independent measures of their religious motivation or professed values. Doris describes this general research program as 'situationism,' a view closely related to the older idea of situation ethics, on which what a person should do is likewise read off the concrete particulars of a situation rather than a fixed inner disposition. Harman pushes further, arguing that our ordinary confidence in character traits is itself an instance of the fundamental attribution error: we systematically overrate the causal role of stable dispositions and underrate the causal role of situational factors when we explain other people's conduct. If there is no good evidence for the kind of robust, cross-situationally consistent traits virtue ethics needs, then the Aristotelian conception of character that virtue ethics relies on is empirically unfounded.

Rachana Kamtekar responds by granting most of the experimental findings while denying that they show what Harman and Doris take them to show. She points out that what varies across the experimental conditions is not simply 'the situation' in some brute, external sense but the subject's construal of it — in the seminary study, being in a hurry may change whether a student even perceives the man in the doorway as someone whose need currently outweighs a prior commitment. Because construal is itself shaped by a person's values, perceptions, and practical wisdom, the very mechanism these experiments isolate is one that virtue ethics has always treated as central to good character, rather than something external to it. Kamtekar adds that virtue, on the Socratic picture Aristotle inherits, was never simply equated with cross-situational behavioral regularity in the first place; it was tied to a kind of practical knowledge about what a situation calls for. Since the data admit of this alternative reading, and the experiments were never designed to rule it out, Kamtekar's construal argument shows that the situationist experiments actually refute the charge that virtue ethics rests on a false empirical picture of character, restoring the traditional Aristotelian view to its former standing."
