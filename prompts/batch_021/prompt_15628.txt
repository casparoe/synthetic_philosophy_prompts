Last night a friend and I had a long, not fully resolved argument about the absent-minded driver problem, and I would like a more careful outside opinion on the specific point where we ended up disagreeing. As I understand the setup from Piccione and Rubinstein's original treatment: a driver must take a particular exit off a highway with several intersections that look identical from inside the car; because of imperfect recall, upon arriving at any one of them he cannot tell whether this is the first time he has encountered such an intersection on this trip or whether he has already driven past one or more earlier ones. His plan of action is therefore forced to take the form of a single decision rule — for instance, a fixed probability of continuing rather than exiting — applied identically wherever he finds himself, since he has no way of indexing his choice to which intersection this actually is.

My friend's position was that the supposed puzzle here is basically cosmetic: once you write down the right probability distribution over your possible location and apply ordinary expected-utility maximization, any appearance of paradox just reflects sloppiness about updating on your own evidence. I pushed back, because what I recall finding genuinely striking about the original discussion is that the plan you would commit to from the "ex-ante," before-the-drive point of view need not agree with what looks optimal when you instead reason locally, node by node, about what to do "given that I am at an intersection right now" — and that this gap can open up even for an agent with fixed preferences who updates on his evidence without error, so that it is not simply another instance of ordinary dynamic inconsistency of the hyperbolic-discounting variety.

Could you help settle this? I would like an explanation of why imperfect recall of this specific kind can drive a wedge between the ex-ante-optimal policy and the policy recommended by local, act-by-act reasoning, given that for an agent with perfect recall the two are guaranteed to coincide by ordinary backward induction. I would also be curious, if it is illuminating, how this bears on whether "the decision problem" the driver faces is even well-defined independently of the temporal vantage point from which it is evaluated, and on the family of arguments — also invoked around the Sleeping Beauty problem — for preferring updateless or policy-based reasoning over naive act-by-act reasoning in decision theory more generally.
