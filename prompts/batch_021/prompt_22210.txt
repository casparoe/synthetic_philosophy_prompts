I've spent the last few sessions of my graduate seminar on formal epistemology working through Löb's theorem and the so-called "Löbian obstacle" it poses for self-trusting agents: roughly, the fact that a formal system cannot get P "on the cheap" merely by proving the conditional "if P is provable, then P" — Löb's theorem shows that any system capable of proving that much can already prove P outright, a result usually written □(□P→P)→□P. This is what makes it so hard, in the AI-safety literature on "tiling agents," for a system to certify a successor that reasons the way it does: any scheme by which the successor's mere say-so about its own soundness would license trusting it collapses, via Löb, into either licensing nothing new or licensing far too much.

A few students have tried their hand at jokes about this in office hours, but every one so far has amounted to "ha, the robot doesn't trust itself" — the sort of line you could deliver about any self-referential puzzle without having understood a word of the actual proof. For next week's problem set I'd like to show them a better standard, so I'm hoping you can write a joke whose punchline genuinely turns on the inferential structure Löb's theorem describes, rather than on self-reference or paradox in some generic sense. Set it wherever the comedy works best for you — a parent withholding the car keys until the teenager "proves" he's responsible, an AI's exit interview with the successor system meant to replace it, a courtroom, anything at all — but it should be the kind of joke that a colleague of mine who works in provability logic would immediately recognize as turning on Löb's theorem itself, rather than merely on the idea that computers and self-reference make for easy laughs.
