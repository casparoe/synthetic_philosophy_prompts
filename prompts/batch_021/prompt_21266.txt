I've spent the past few weeks reporting on the ethics behind search-and-rescue triage protocols, and during a training seminar I watched two veteran volunteer coordinators get into an argument over a tabletop exercise that I haven't been able to resolve in my own head. The scenario: a rescue helicopter has fuel and daylight for exactly one more flight before conditions close in for the night. Two distress calls have come in from opposite valleys: one stranded snowboarder, and a group of five stranded hikers. Per the exercise's stipulations, everyone involved faces certain death if not reached by morning, the odds of a successful extraction are identical at both sites, and there is no way to split the flight or return for a second trip.

(1) Dana said the choice was obvious: send the helicopter to the five, since that prevents more deaths than sending it to the one.

(2) Marcus, who has a philosophy background, pushed back. He said this treats five deaths as one big loss that can be added up across different people, but nobody actually suffers "five deaths" — each hiker stands to lose exactly one life, no more than the snowboarder does, and there's no sixth person for whom things are five times as bad. He mentioned a philosopher named Taurek who'd argued exactly this, and suggested that when strangers' claims conflict this way, something like a coin flip, giving every claim equal weight, is fairer than automatically favoring the larger group.

(3) Dana answered: suppose it were just the snowboarder against a single hiker, and a coin flip was fine there, since the claims are equal. Now add hikers to that side one at a time. Each new hiker is someone else with a claim exactly as strong as the snowboarder's. Every addition can only supply a further reason to fly to that valley — it can't subtract a reason from the other one. So by the time there are five, the accumulated reasons have to outweigh the single claim.

(4) Marcus said that's close to the standard reply to Taurek, but it smuggles back in the very aggregation he was denying: "reasons accumulate" only works if separate people's claims can be piled onto one scale, and five people each having an equally strong claim to a single scholarship doesn't add up to a claim five times as strong. Every hiker's claim matches the snowboarder's, one for one — that doesn't yet mean the five together outweigh him.

(5) Dana asked whether Marcus would really flip a coin between the snowboarder and five thousand stranded hikers. At some point, she said, scale has to matter, or he's committed to indifference between saving one life and saving a city.

(6) Marcus said he wasn't committed to indifference: it can be true that a world where the five thousand live is better than one where only the snowboarder does, without its being true that any single one of them has a stronger claim on the helicopter than the snowboarder has — since it's his individual claim, not some collective one, that the obligation runs to.

The seminar moved on before either conceded anything. Could you go through the six moves in order and say, at each step, who has the better of the argument — the verdict is allowed to shift back and forth rather than settle on one side throughout? Please close with your own overall view on whether the numbers should count in a case like this, and flag anything you think both Dana and Marcus are overlooking.
