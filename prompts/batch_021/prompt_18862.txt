I teach a graduate seminar cross-listed with our PPE program on formal approaches to distributive justice, and this term's take-home exam included a question on Harsanyi's aggregation theorem. The question read: "State Harsanyi's Social Aggregation Theorem (1955), being explicit about the role played by the Pareto condition and by the expected-utility axioms governing individual and social preference alike. Harsanyi took the theorem to underwrite a form of utilitarianism; explain the sense in which it does so, and the sense (if any) in which it falls short of vindicating classical, equal-weight utilitarianism. Finally, situate the theorem relative to one standard objection from the secondary literature, and say whether that objection is best understood as targeting the Aggregation Theorem itself or a distinct Harsanyi result."

Below, with identifying information removed, is one student's answer in full. I would like it graded.

"Harsanyi's aggregation theorem starts from a fixed set of social prospects — lotteries over final outcomes — over which every individual, and 'society' as a corporate agent, has preferences. Harsanyi assumes that each of these preference relations, individual and social alike, satisfies the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms (completeness, transitivity, continuity, and independence), so that each can be represented by a cardinal utility function unique up to a positive affine transformation. To this he adds a Pareto condition: if every individual is indifferent between two prospects, so is society. From these assumptions alone — essentially by a linear-algebra argument about functions defined on a convex set — it follows that the social utility function W must be an affine combination of the individual utility functions, W = a_0 + a_1U_1 + ... + a_nU_n.

It is worth being careful about what the Pareto condition is doing here, since Weymark (1991) has shown that this result is routinely run together with a different and better-known Harsanyi argument, the 1953 impartial-observer theorem, and that Sen's objections to Harsanyi's utilitarianism are really objections to the latter. Intuitively, though, the aggregation theorem can be motivated in the same way: we imagine an impartial spectator who is equally likely to end up occupying any one of the n social positions, and because this spectator is herself an expected-utility maximizer, her ranking of prospects averages the individual utility functions together — which is exactly what fixes the coefficients a_i in the formula above.

Since the resulting social utility function is just the sum of the individual utility functions, the theorem gives classical, equal-weight utilitarianism a genuine derivation from comparatively weak premises: nobody's preferences are weighted more heavily than anybody else's, and 'society' is required to track the unweighted total of individual utility. This, I take it, is why the result is so often invoked on utilitarianism's behalf, and it is also the target of Diamond's (1967) comment on Harsanyi. Diamond considers two people and one indivisible good and shows that any social welfare function of Harsanyi's form must rank a fair coin flip for the good as no better than simply awarding it outright to whichever person yields the same expected social utility — despite the fact that the coin flip treats the two claimants symmetrically and the outright award does not. Because Diamond's argument turns entirely on the Pareto condition and the linearity of expected utility, and not on any story about impartial spectators, it is properly read as an objection to the aggregation theorem itself rather than to the impartial-observer construction."
