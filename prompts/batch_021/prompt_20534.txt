"Mill's harm principle says the only reason the state or society can ever use force against someone is to stop them from harming other people - not to make them happier, more moral, or better off, and not even for their own good. That's why Mill splits actions into 'self-regarding' ones, which mainly affect the person doing them (or other people who freely consent, like two adults making a private deal), and 'other-regarding' ones, which affect people who didn't consent. Only the second kind can be legitimately restricted by law.

The slavery example is basically Mill stress-testing his own rule. Selling yourself into slavery looks self-regarding since you're the one choosing it, so you'd expect the harm principle to allow it. But Mill says no, because the whole reason liberty matters is so people can keep making free choices, and selling yourself into slavery permanently destroys your ability to do that. So he lets the principle override itself in this one case, since letting someone use their freedom to permanently get rid of their own freedom defeats the point of protecting freedom at all.

Feinberg thought Mill's harm principle alone was too narrow, since there's stuff that seriously disgusts or embarrasses people, like public sex acts, that doesn't really 'harm' anyone the way Mill means it but that most people still want the law to be able to stop. So Feinberg came up with the 'offense principle' to cover those cases. But Feinberg treated offense as basically just a more precise version of harm that Mill hadn't spelled out all the way, so the offense principle isn't really a new, separate principle sitting next to the harm principle - its more like a patch on the same one."
