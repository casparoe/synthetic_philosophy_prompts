So I've been falling down a bit of a rabbit hole this week for a piece I'm noodling on about ancient logic/paradoxes outside the usual Greek suspects, and I keep getting stuck on this one text: the "White Horse Dialogue" (白馬論), attributed to Gongsun Long, one of the guys from the "School of Names" in Warring States China — basically a small cluster of philosophers that the Confucians and Mohists both dismissed as sophists obsessed with pointless word games. His signature move is arguing, seemingly in dead earnest, that "a white horse is not a horse." I pieced together the gist of the core argument from a couple of translations I found (so apologies if the phrasing's a bit clunky, I'm not a classical Chinese scholar), and it goes roughly like this:

"Horse" is the name for a shape. "White" is the name for a colour. To name a color is not to name a shape. Therefore a white horse is not a horse. Further: if someone asks for a horse, a yellow horse or a black horse will do fine. But if someone asks for a white horse, a yellow horse or a black horse will not do. If "white horse" and "horse" were really the same thing, then whatever satisfies one request should satisfy the other — but it doesn't. Therefore a white horse is not a horse.

I brought this up at dinner with a friend who does linguistics and she just shrugged and said it's "probably a mass-noun thing," which felt like a bit of a cop-out answer to me. What I actually can't figure out is whether there's a real, repairable logical point buried in there, or whether Gongsun Long is just exploiting some ambiguity in how "is/is not" worked in Classical Chinese to make a cheap trick sound profound. Is he basically groping toward something like the difference between identity and class-membership (a white horse is a member of the class of horses without being identical to "horse-in-general"), way before anyone in Europe had the vocabulary for that? Or does the "yellow/black horse won't do" bit actually smuggle in an equivocation between "satisfies the request" and "is a horse"? Could you walk through where exactly this argument would need patching up (or where it secretly already works) if you tried to restate it with modern tools — sets, predicates, intension vs. extension, whatever fits best?
