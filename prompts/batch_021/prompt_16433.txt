I'm a second-year philosophy PhD student working on a dissertation chapter about the prehistory of the liberal harm principle, and I've settled on Jeremy Bentham's unpublished writings on sexual morality as my main case study — chiefly the manuscript now generally known, after Louis Crompton's partial 1978 edition, as "Offences Against One's Self," together with the later, still-obscure "Not Paul, But Jesus." What follows is the argumentative skeleton for the section rather than finished prose, and I'd like some outside comments before I flesh it out properly.

My reconstruction runs roughly as follows. Bentham applies the felicific calculus directly to what he calls "irregularities" of sexual conduct and concludes that, since such acts performed in private between consenting adults harm no one, the penalties English law attached to them — up to and including death — amounted to pure utilitarian waste: certain, severe suffering purchased for no offsetting benefit. He then works through the standard objections in turn: the charge of "unnaturalness," which he treats as smuggling in mere unusualness as though it were an independent vice; the fear of depopulation, dismissed both empirically (no society has ever actually suffered this way) and by noting that plenty of tolerated heterosexual activity is equally non-procreative; and the claim that tolerating such conduct saps martial virtue, which he rebuts by pointing to Sparta's military reputation. In "Not Paul, But Jesus" he goes further still, arguing that Christianity's sexual rigorism traces to Paul's epistles rather than to anything Jesus himself is recorded as having said.

The move I actually want to build the chapter around is smaller than any of this, though. Bentham insists that the disgust, or "antipathy," a practice provokes in third parties is not a legitimate kind of pain for a legislator to weigh — a claim that leans on his separate attack, earlier in the "Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation," on what he calls the "principle of sympathy and antipathy" as a pseudo-principle masquerading as moral reasoning. Without this move the whole case collapses, since almost any minority practice generates antipathy in a majority, which by his own lights would then count as a reason to suppress it. I want to argue that this quietly anticipates the Hart–Devlin debate on legal moralism by about 170 years, with Bentham pre-empting something like Devlin's "disintegration thesis" — but I also suspect it exposes a real tension, since Bentham's hedonism is officially blind to the source of a pain (unlike Mill's later qualitative distinctions between pleasures), and I'm not sure he ever gives a principled rather than ad hoc reason for exempting antipathy-pain specifically. My supervisor thinks this is a footnote at best; I suspect it's the most interesting part of the chapter, which is exactly why I want a second opinion. I'd welcome comments on whether the reconstruction is fair to what Bentham is actually doing, whether the Hart–Devlin comparison illuminates more than it strains, whether the tension I'm flagging is real or one Bentham has the resources to dissolve, and which of these threads seems most worth developing versus cutting for space.
