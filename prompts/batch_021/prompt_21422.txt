I'm a software engineer working mostly on NLP and ML infrastructure, with no formal training in philosophy -- everything I know about this topic comes from following citation trails on weekend evenings. A few months ago I ended up reading Stevan Harnad's original writing on what he calls the symbol grounding problem, and one piece of the argument has been bothering me ever since. Harnad's point, as I understand it, is that a purely symbolic system -- one that only manipulates tokens according to formal rules -- can never derive meaning just by relating those tokens to still more tokens, because that is structurally the same trap as trying to learn Chinese from a Chinese-only dictionary: every lookup just hands you more unfamiliar characters, and you circle forever without ever landing on something you already understand. His proposed way out is that symbols have to be anchored, at the bottom, in non-symbolic representations -- first "iconic" representations that are just raw analog copies of sensory input, then "categorical" representations that a system learns by picking out the invariant features that let it reliably sort inputs into the right categories -- and that this bottom layer can only be built through a system's own sensorimotor interaction with the world, not through more text.

The reason this keeps nagging at me is that it looks like a direct verdict on exactly the systems I work with. Large language models are trained end-to-end on token prediction over text, with no sensorimotor contact with anything their words refer to, so by Harnad's own criterion they should be the purest possible case of the dictionary trap: symbols endlessly cashed out in other symbols, never touching the ground. Yet in practice these models often behave as though they have a working grasp of physical, spatial, and social structure that goes well beyond what you would expect from ungrounded pattern completion. When I raised this with a colleague, she argued that the statistical shape of a large enough text corpus is not arbitrary: words for things that are big, hot, dangerous, or nearby tend to co-occur in patterns that mirror how those properties actually cluster in the world, so perhaps a system could reconstruct a great deal of grounding-like structure secondhand, from the corpus itself, without ever needing senses of its own. I countered with a thought experiment I had come across elsewhere, about two people exchanging messages over a cable that is secretly being learned and imitated by a statistically gifted octopus, which manages fine until it is asked to do something that requires genuine contact with the world, such as using an unfamiliar object to solve a physical problem. Neither of us could agree on whether that thought experiment actually refutes her point or just restates, in vivid form, the very intuition we were arguing about.

I would like you to write me an essay that actually settles this rather than merely surveying it. Please lay out the strongest version of both positions -- that grounding can, in principle, be recovered indirectly from the relational structure of a sufficiently large text corpus, versus that Harnad's sensorimotor bottleneck is a genuine architectural requirement that no amount of text-only scaling can substitute for -- and then commit to a specific, defended verdict rather than concluding that reasonable people disagree. A qualified verdict is fine (for instance, something like "grounding comes in degrees, and text-only training can secure the lower grades of it but not the higher ones," provided you cash that out precisely), but I want a substantive claim that could in principle turn out to be wrong, not a summary of the debate.
