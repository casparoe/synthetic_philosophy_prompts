I'm about two-thirds through a hard sf novel where the pov character for several chapters is a shipboard AI, and I really don't want its internal monologue to be the usual "am I conscious, do I have free will" navel-gazing that shows up in pretty much every novel with an AI narrator at this point - it's been done to death. A friend who does AI safety research keeps telling me the more interesting angle is what he calls "embedded agency" (I think its a MIRI/Garrabrant-Demski framing, from a few years back) - the idea being that something like the classic AIXI setup gets to sit outside the environment it's reasoning about, clean input/output channel and all, whereas a real embedded agent is physically part of the world its modeling, is smaller than that world, and can be copied, edited, predicted, or straight up destroyed by other things in it. That distinction feels like great material for a scene, but I don't want to just wave at it vaguely - I want the actual bind to be load-bearing in the plot, something the character has to reason through on the page.

So could you give me one concrete decision or reasoning problem that a genuinely embedded agent runs into specifically because its embedded - not just generic uncertainty about the world, but something more structural (the self-trust/Lob's theorem stuff people bring up, or the naturalized induction angle, or whatever you think is actually the sharpest case) - and then spell out why a disembodied, Cartesian version of that same agent just wouldn't hit that wall at all? Ideally something precise enough that I could dramatize the actual reasoning instead of having the AI announce "I realized I was part of the world" and leaving it at that.
