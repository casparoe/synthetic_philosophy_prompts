For my philosophy of education seminar we just got through the chunk of Locke's Some Thoughts Concerning Education where he talks about how you're supposed to motivate kids to actually learn, and honestly its way more interesting than the tabula rasa stuff everyone already knows him for. His big worry there is that if you reward a kid with sweets or little treats for learning their lesson well, or punish them by beating them (or taking away playtime) when they don't, you end up teaching the kid that learning itself is some unpleasant chore that has to be bribed or beaten out of them. Instead he wants the kid's motivation to come from esteem and shame -- basically caring what people whose opinion matters to them will think -- rather than rewards and punishments attached directly to the schoolwork.

Which got me thinking about basically the entire modern school system: grades functioning as prizes, gold star charts, standardized test stakes hanging over kids' heads, and now gamified apps that hand out points/streaks/badges for reading or doing math drills (my cousin's school uses one of these and she's WAY more hyped about her streak than the actual book she's supposed to be reading). That seems like exactly the "bribe and threaten them about their books" model Locke was against, just scaled up and turned into software.

So genuinely, if 17th century Locke got to see how todays schools and ed-tech actually motivate kids, what do you think his verdict would actually be? Is a grade or an app badge basically just a fancier, more socially acceptable version of the sweets-and-beatings system he already condemned, or is there a principled Lockean reason he'd treat something abstract/symbolic like a grade totally differently from a literal treat or a caning? I don't want a cop-out "he'd probably have mixed feelings" answer here -- actually commit to a position on how far Locke's argument extends to condemn modern reward-driven schooling, and back it up with the actual logic of his argument, not just the pop "blank slate" version of Locke that gets thrown around in every intro philosophy class.
