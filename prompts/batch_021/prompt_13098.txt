At a dinner party a few weeks ago, two friends of mine, Alicia and Ben -- both of whom studied physics as undergraduates before moving into unrelated careers -- ended up in a long argument about dark matter that started as a joke and got surprisingly heated. (1) Ben opened by saying dark matter isn't really a scientific theory anymore so much as a rescue device: whenever a galaxy's rotation curve doesn't match predictions, cosmologists can always posit a differently shaped halo of invisible matter around that particular galaxy to make the numbers work, and a theory you can always save like that can never really be wrong. He contrasted this with MOND, the rival idea that tweaks the law of gravity itself at very low accelerations, which fixed one constant decades ago and has kept getting galaxy rotation speeds right ever since without further tuning. (2) Alicia said that wasn't fair to dark matter: the same halo-based picture, without being retuned for the occasion, also correctly matched the fine pattern of hot and cold spots in the leftover glow from the Big Bang -- a completely different kind of observation, made decades after the halo idea was first proposed to explain galaxies. MOND, she said, has never managed anything like that without quietly borrowing some invisible mass of its own. (3) Ben replied that this only proves both sides patch their theory with unseen stuff whenever the data misbehaves -- MOND needs extra invisible particles to handle the afterglow, dark matter needs a bespoke halo for every oddly-behaved galaxy -- so by his count the two are equally guilty of the same move, and Alicia can't call one side's patch a discovery and the other's an excuse. (4) Alicia answered that not all patches are equal: when astronomers eventually imaged two colliding galaxy clusters, the dark matter picture implied that the lensing mass should turn up separated from the visible gas cloud, since invisible matter wouldn't be slowed by the collision the way gas is -- and that is exactly what was found. That, she said, is a patch that stuck its neck out and could easily have failed. (5) Ben asked why 'we can always go looking for the invisible mass in some new configuration and count it as confirmed once we find it' is really so different from his original complaint -- is there a principled difference between a fix that happens to also foretell something new and a fix that's just a fix? Without an answer, he said, 'novel prediction' is just a compliment people pay to whichever theory they already preferred. (6) Alicia didn't have a clean answer, but guessed the difference has to be about whether a fix was forced by data that hadn't been collected yet, rather than dreamed up only after the fact to explain away an embarrassment -- though she admitted that with enough hindsight and cleverness, defenders of almost any theory can retell an old fix as though it had predicted something all along.

Neither of them budged, and I don't know enough philosophy of science to say who had the better of it at each stage.
