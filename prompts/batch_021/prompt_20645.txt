The question (worth 10 points) was: "Priya and Tomas are Bayesians who share a common prior over a finite state space Ω. Each has a private information partition and forms a posterior for some event E ⊆ Ω. Suppose that at the true state it becomes common knowledge between them what the other's current posterior for E is (say, because they've been telling each other their number back and forth). (a) [6 pts] State what Aumann's agreement theorem says must then hold, and sketch why it's true. (b) [4 pts] It's sometimes claimed that this shows that if two rational people with the same prior talk to each other long enough, they must end up knowing all the same things. Is that an accurate reading of the theorem? Explain."

And here's what the student handed in: "(a) Aumann's theorem says that once it's common knowledge what Priya's and Tomas's posteriors for E are, those posteriors must actually be equal -- they can't just agree to disagree. This is Aumann's famous 1976 result (also known as the no-trade theorem), and it's a little surprising, since Priya and Tomas can have completely different private partitions and completely different information and still end up giving E the same probability. The reason is basically this: 'it's common knowledge that Priya's posterior is p and Tomas's is q' is itself an event, and for it to be common knowledge it has to be built out of whole cells of both Priya's and Tomas's partitions at once. So you can work out the probability of E given that event two different ways -- averaging over Priya's cells, where the posterior is always p by assumption, or averaging over Tomas's cells, where it's always q -- and since it's the same event and the same prior, both averages have to come out the same, so p = q. (b) I'd say that reading is basically right. If Priya and Tomas keep swapping posteriors for E and updating on whatever the other just said, the only way their numbers can stop moving is once neither of them has any private info left that would still change their own estimate -- otherwise they'd keep revising. So by the time they hit common knowledge and their posteriors match, whatever information was relevant to E has effectively already been folded into that shared number, even if they never told each other their actual signals. So in that sense, talking long enough really does bring two rational people with the same prior to a shared information state, at least about E."
