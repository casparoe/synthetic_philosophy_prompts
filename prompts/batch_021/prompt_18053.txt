I'm working on a feature about how philosophers of language have found themselves drawn into debates about large language models, and I keep returning to a question that Donald Davidson's death in 2003 left permanently unresolved. Davidson's account of radical interpretation has an interpreter construct a Tarski-style truth theory for a speaker under the constraint of the principle of charity, solving for meaning and belief together rather than one at a time; and in his later essays he added a further requirement, triangulation, on which content is only fixed when two creatures jointly respond to a shared object in a shared world, since it's the intersection of their two causal responses to that object, rather than any other link in the causal chain, that determines what their responses are about. None of this was written with anything like a text-trained transformer in mind, and I would like your help reconstructing, as faithfully as you can, what Davidson himself would most plausibly have said about it.

Concretely: would he have treated a large language model as an extreme version of his own Swampman — the creature assembled by chance when lightning struck a dead tree, whose speech is behaviorally indistinguishable from Davidson's own but which he denied has any content, because it lacks the right causal history linking its internal states to the objects those states would need to be about? Or does the triangulation requirement do further, independent work that the Swampman case doesn't quite capture, given that a language model, unlike Swampman, has never shared perceptual access to any object with anyone at all, and has only ever ingested text describing a world it has never itself triangulated on? I'm especially curious whether Davidson would have treated the fact that a model's training corpus is itself the residue of millions of earlier instances of genuine human triangulation as a meaningful loophole, or as beside the point entirely. A philosopher I interviewed for an earlier piece waved the question away as a simple category error, which struck me as too quick; I would like a more careful reconstruction. Please work through this as an argument Davidson could plausibly have constructed himself, rather than as a general essay on machine understanding, and note explicitly where you are extending his framework beyond anything he considered, versus where the verdict seems to follow fairly directly from commitments he already held.
