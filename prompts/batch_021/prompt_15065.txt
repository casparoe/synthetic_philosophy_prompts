I'm a PhD student in philosophy working on formal epistemology and decision theory, and for the past year I've also been an informal member of a weekly interdisciplinary reading group on AI safety, whose other regulars are mostly ML and theoretical-CS PhD students. Two of the regulars, Nora and Theo, got into a real disagreement at last week's session that the discussion leader eventually had to table so the group could move on, and I haven't been able to settle it since. The trigger was a move you see all over the alignment literature: the claim that "coherence arguments" -- the von Neumann-Morgenstern representation theorem together with Dutch-book and money-pump arguments -- license the conclusion that any sufficiently capable agent will end up behaving like an expected-utility maximizer, and that this alone, independent of any facts about how the system was actually trained, grounds the standard worry about instrumental convergence: an expected-utility maximizer with almost any goal will want more resources, more options, and its own continued existence, because these are useful for nearly any end.

(1) Nora defended that inference. (2) Theo objected that mere representability as *some* expected-utility maximizer is nearly empty: as long as an agent's choices are acyclic, a suitable utility function can always be reverse-engineered to fit them -- including a degenerate one that just assigns high utility to whatever the agent in fact does -- so "coherent" in this thin sense doesn't yet imply anything about wanting resources or self-preservation; you need the further, substantive assumption that the fitted utility function has some simple, context-independent shape, and that's a claim about training, not something the representation theorem gives you for free. (3) Nora granted the point but relocated the argument: the representation theorem isn't the real engine, the money pump is -- an agent with genuinely cyclical or incomplete preferences can be walked through a sequence of trades it accepts at each step and ends up strictly worse off than where it started, and since any agent embedded in a training process or a long deployment faces repeated choice situations of exactly that kind, exploitable incoherence should get selected against no matter what the agent's goals are about, and coherence in this dynamic sense already starts to look resource-protective. (4) Theo's rejoinder was that the money-pump argument against incompleteness specifically needs the agent to accept each trade myopically, without foresight; an agent that can instead adopt a standing policy of declining any trade that is part of a pattern doing worse, taken as a whole, than some available alternative can keep genuinely incomplete preferences -- there need be no fact about whether it prefers A to B -- and never actually get pumped, so the dynamic argument forces only "don't be exploitable by naive sequential trades," not the full completeness that instrumental-convergence stories quietly assume. (5) Nora's last word was that whether real trained systems end up with that kind of foresighted, resolute policy, rather than either naive incoherence or full completeness, is an empirical question about optimization dynamics that neither argument settles from the armchair -- and that's where the leader cut them off.

Both of them are sharper on this than I am, which is exactly the problem: I can't tell whether Theo has actually defused the coherence-argument-for-power-seeking move or merely relocated the goalposts, or whether Nora's concession at (3) gave away more than she needed to. Could you go through the exchange move by move, using my numbering, and say who has the better of each specific point rather than only who wins overall? I'd also like your own view on the bottom-line question the group never resolved -- whether coherence arguments, on their own, really support expecting instrumental convergence in advanced systems -- and a note on anything important that you think neither Nora nor Theo raised.
