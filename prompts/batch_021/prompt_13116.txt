I'm putting together a graduate seminar on nineteenth-century diagnoses of modernity, and this year I want to devote a full session to Kierkegaard's *Two Ages*, especially the material on "the public," the press, and "leveling." The more I reread it against the biographical record, though, the more I've come to doubt how this material is usually framed in the secondary literature, namely as an existential or religious diagnosis of selfhood that merely happens to use the press as its illustration. Kierkegaard wrote *Two Ages* in the very months that Goldschmidt's *Corsair* was busy turning him into a laughingstock all over Copenhagen, caricaturing his trousers and his gait, and he was writing against the backdrop of a Danish liberal movement openly agitating for a freer press and a constitution to replace absolute monarchy. Read against that background, his claim that the press manufactures a leveling, irresponsible "public" looks to me less like a diagnosis of the human spirit and more like a political argument, dressed up in existential vocabulary, for why expanding press freedom and popular opinion-formation are actually dangerous and ought to be resisted.

I'd like some help thinking through the implications of this for how I frame the session. Given that Kierkegaard's real target in *Two Ages* is press freedom and the democratization of public opinion rather than some more abstract existential category, how should I handle the fact that he spent the last year of his life, in *The Moment*, flooding Copenhagen with his own signed pamphlets attacking the state Church? Should I present those pamphlets to my students as a practical retraction of his earlier hostility to mass publication, or is there a way of reading them as consistent with a considered political position against a free press?
