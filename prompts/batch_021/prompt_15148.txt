Question: Some philosophers hold that a person can lose the standing to blame another for doing Φ without this in any way excusing or mitigating the other person's Φ-ing. Explain this distinction, illustrating it with G.A. Cohen's discussion of hypocritical condemnation of terrorism. Then say whether, in your view, mere past wrongdoing on the blamer's part is enough to trigger this loss of standing, or whether something further is required.

Student's answer: Cohen's essay asks whether Western governments -- who have historically committed or backed comparable acts of political violence -- have the standing to condemn terrorists. His key move is to pull apart two questions that ordinary "tu quoque" arguments run together: whether the terrorist act is wrong, and whether this particular speaker is the right person to say so. For Cohen these are independent: exposing someone's hypocrisy is a criticism of the person doing the condemning, not a defense of the person being condemned. If I've done X and then blame you for doing X, my hypocrisy doesn't make X okay; it just means I'm not well placed to be the one pointing it out.

It follows that when a hypocritical government condemns terrorism, the charge of hypocrisy actually concedes that the terrorism is exactly as wrong as they say -- the criticism only bites because both sides agree X is wrong, and the government is guilty of X too. So the hypocrisy makes the government's moral position, taken as a whole, incoherent: they're demanding a standard of the terrorists that their own conduct fails to meet, and this failure gives the terrorists grounds to say they're being held to a standard nobody is actually prepared to enforce evenly, which functions as a kind of excuse even if it isn't a full justification. That's why Cohen thinks the honest response to a hypocrite's condemnation isn't to defend the terrorists, but to point out that this particular condemner is poorly placed to be making the accusation.

This generalizes past Cohen's case: on the standard view, whenever a blamer has done the very thing they're condemning, they lose standing to blame -- the same way a smoker loses standing to lecture their kids about smoking. This is usually called the non-hypocrisy condition, and philosophers typically list it alongside other standing conditions, like a privacy/"none of your business" condition, and a non-complicity condition covering blame for a joint wrong you took part in. All of these show that the fittingness of blame can come apart from its accuracy -- an emotion can be tracking something true about its target and still be out of order for this particular person to express.

I think the literature is right that these are separate issues: it would be strange if the wrongdoer's culpability depended on the moral track record of whoever happens to be criticizing them.
