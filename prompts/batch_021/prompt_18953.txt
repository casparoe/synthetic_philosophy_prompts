**(a) [10 pts]** Explain Adam Elga's argument for the "thirder" position in the Sleeping Beauty problem (that upon waking, Beauty's credence that the coin landed heads should be 1/3), and explain David Lewis's central objection to it. Some people additionally point out that betting on tails at even odds wins more money on average than betting on heads, across many repetitions of the experiment, and treat this as further support for the thirder position. Is this betting consideration decisive on its own? Why or why not?

**(b) [10 pts]** Now consider a variant. Instead of waking Beauty twice on separate days if the coin lands tails, suppose the experimenters instead create an exact physical and psychological duplicate of Beauty on Sunday night if (and only if) the coin lands tails. The original and the duplicate both wake up Monday morning in separate, identical rooms, and neither can tell from the inside whether she's the original or the duplicate. If the coin lands heads, only the original wakes (no duplicate is made). Upon waking, what credence should any of the resulting individuals assign to the coin having landed heads? Does this version raise the same philosophical issue as the standard version, or a different one? Explain.

And here's the answer, copied verbatim:

*(a) The thirder position, defended by Elga, says that when Beauty wakes up she should think there's a 1/3 chance the coin landed heads. The argument goes like this: when beauty wakes up, she can't tell whether its Monday or Tuesday, or whether the coin landed heads or tails -- from the inside all the wakings feel exactly the same. So there are three "situations" she might be in: heads+Monday, tails+Monday, tails+Tuesday. Elga argues she should treat these as equally likely. Part of the reasoning is that if she were told "it's Monday," this shouldn't affect her credence in heads at all (the coin's chance of landing heads is 1/2, and finding out it's Monday doesn't give her any information relevant to a fair coin, so P(heads|Monday) = 1/2). And if she's told it's tails, she has no way of favoring Monday over Tuesday, so those get equal credence too. Putting this together, heads+Monday, tails+Monday and tails+Tuesday all get 1/3 each, so P(heads) = 1/3.

Lewis's objection is that Beauty doesn't actually learn anything new when she wakes up. She knew on Sunday, with certainty, that she would wake up at least once no matter what the coin did, so "I am now awake" isn't evidence of anything -- it's true with probability 1 either way. Since ordinary Bayesian updating says you only change your credence in response to genuine evidence, and there's no genuine evidence here, Beauty's credence in heads should just stay at 1/2, same as it was on Sunday before she went to sleep.

As for the betting consideration: I think this actually clinches the argument for the thirder side. If Beauty bets on tails every time she's asked, at even odds, she comes out ahead in the long run, and if rational credence just is the betting price you're willing to accept, then this directly shows her credence must be 1/3 for heads -- otherwise she'd be accepting bets that lose her money on average, which is basically the definition of being irrational. So the betting argument isn't just supporting evidence, it's basically a proof, since credence is nothing more than the odds you're willing to bet at.

(b) In the duplication version, I think the same three-way split still works: heads+original, tails+original, tails+duplicate are the three subjectively indistinguishable possibilities, so by the same indifference reasoning as before, each gets 1/3, and P(heads) = 1/3 again.

But I think this version is actually philosophically different from the original, and simpler. In the original problem, the puzzle is specifically about self-locating belief -- Beauty has to figure out facts about her own location in time (is it Monday or Tuesday?) without any new evidence, which is what makes it a distinctively hard case for Bayesian updating. In the duplication case, though, the original and the duplicate are just two separate, fully distinct people from the moment the duplicate is created -- there's no single continuing subject who has to wonder "where/when am I," just two different individuals who each happen to have identical evidence. So this isn't really an instance of the self-locating belief puzzle at all -- it's closer to an ordinary chance setup, like being handed one of three otherwise identical envelopes where only one contains a "heads" ticket. The interesting philosophical question in this version is more about personal identity (are the original and the duplicate "the same person"?) than about self-location or indexical belief.*
