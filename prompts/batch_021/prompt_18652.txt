I cover science and ideas for a mid-size outlet, and along with the actual reporting I sit on the internal committee that writes our editorial standards -- sourcing rules, corrections policy, that sort of thing. We hit a real snag last month that I think needs an actual standing policy rather than a one-off ruling, so I volunteered to draft something for the committee to argue over, and I'm hoping you can help me think it through before I do.

Here's the case that started it. Our two most experienced health reporters were each given the same pile of recent studies on moderate drinking and heart disease -- same cohort studies, same meta-analyses, same newer genetic studies pushing back on the old "a glass of wine a day is good for you" findings -- and they came back with opposite framings. One wants to lead with "the case for any cardiovascular benefit has basically collapsed," the other thinks that overreads the new evidence and wants to lead with "the picture is still genuinely contested." Neither can point to an actual mistake in the other's reasoning; both are equally careful, equally experienced, and nobody on staff has some known edge in reading this particular literature. They've just weighed the same studies differently. This isn't really a sourcing or legal problem (our lawyers are fine either way), and it's happened on other beats before and will again, so what we actually need is a rule.

About half the committee wants a policy that says: when this happens and there's no real asymmetry between the reporters, the desk pushes them toward a hedged, split-the-difference framing rather than letting either one's confidence carry the day. The other half thinks that's cowardice dressed up as balance, and that an editor should just back whichever framing holds up better under questioning, even if that means overruling a reporter who is, by every measure we have, just as reliable as the one getting sided with. Could you draft the memo I'd bring to committee -- something that actually lands somewhere, with a rule the desk could apply on deadline rather than "use good judgement," and that gives whichever side ends up loosing a fair hearing rather than a strawman? I keep flip-flopping on this myself, so don't just tell me what I want to hear.
