I've been reading LessWrong, the Alignment Forum, and various decision-theory blogs for the better part of a decade, and a few months ago some friends from my local rationalist meetup and I started a small interview podcast covering unusual work in that broad neighborhood. For the next episode, I'm interviewing a decision theorist who has spent much of her career formalizing "surrogate goals" as a proposed defense against acausal blackmail: the idea that an agent who expects to be threatened by a distant, causally disconnected but predictively transparent counterpart can pre-commit, in advance, to treat some cheap and otherwise worthless variable exactly as if it were the thing the threatener actually wants to hurt, so that a rational threatener ends up aiming at the decoy rather than the real target. Everyone in this corner of the internet has already heard the Roko's-basilisk-flavored introduction to acausal threats, so I'd like this conversation to skip past that and get into the parts of the surrogate-goals proposal that strike me as held together by considerably more hope than argument.

Could you draft six to eight interview questions for her? I'd like at least one question on how the target is supposed to establish, with no causal channel available, that the threatener will actually treat the surrogate as an equally valid thing to threaten -- this seems to require a shared convention between two agents who can only reason about each other's dispositions, not negotiate them directly. I'd like one question asking whether adopting a surrogate goal isn't itself exactly the sort of early, hard-to-reverse commitment that the commitment-races literature warns against making before you know who or what you'll end up bargaining with. I'd like one question about whether the whole scheme unravels once it becomes common knowledge, since a threatener could simply make compliance conditional on the target's not having shielded itself this way. And I'd like one question pressing on whether defending a surrogate goal exactly as fiercely as the real one is behaviorally distinguishable, even in principle, from simply ceasing to care about the real goal -- which would seem to undercut the very deterrence the target needs to keep working against threats on other fronts. For each of these four, please add a short follow-up question tailored to the most likely response, so I'm not caught out if she gives the standard answer; and if there's room, close the list with a question about what evidence or argument would actually persuade her to give up the approach, rather than merely complicate how it's implemented.
