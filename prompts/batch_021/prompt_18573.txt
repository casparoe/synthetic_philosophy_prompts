I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and I've been following the debate over Prolonged Grief Disorder, the diagnosis the American Psychiatric Association added to the DSM-5-TR in 2022 for grief that remains intense and impairing well past the point clinicians expect mourning to naturally ease. Supporters say the new diagnosis finally gives the acutely bereaved a route to treatment and insurance coverage that a finding of "normal grief" doesn't provide. Critics, including some grief researchers themselves, worry about something subtler than plain overmedicalization: that handing people a diagnostic category for their mourning changes how they interpret, narrate, and even feel that mourning once they know which side of the line they've been placed on. This resembles what the philosopher Ian Hacking called the "looping effect" of classifying human kinds: unlike rocks or elements, people who are classified can respond to the classification, and that response can loop back and reshape the category itself. Write an essay that takes this looping-effect worry seriously as a real mechanism rather than a rhetorical objection, weighs it against the practical case for the diagnosis, and reaches and defends a definite conclusion about whether the APA was right to add Prolonged Grief Disorder to the DSM-5-TR, rather than concluding merely that reasonable people disagree.
