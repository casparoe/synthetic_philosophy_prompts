A friend and I spent most of a long dinner last week arguing about whether an idea, on its own, can ever really belong to someone. His position was that nothing is actually taken from you when someone else acts on your idea — unlike a plot of land or a piece of equipment, you still have it just as fully afterward, so calling it "theft" when someone runs off with your suggestion is at best a loose metaphor, and what should really be rewarded is the labor of turning a thought into something finished and usable. I couldn't accept that this settled things, because it seemed to imply that the person who contributes the one genuinely original insight in a project — the reframing nobody else in the room would have thought of — is owed nothing at all if someone else does the follow-through. We went back and forth for the better part of an hour and neither of us budged.

Almost too conveniently, a version of the same question has now landed in front of a committee I sit on. My university handles authorship and credit disputes separately from formal research-misconduct proceedings, on the grounds that such disputes don't fit neatly into the fabrication/falsification/plagiarism framework those proceedings are built around — which means there is no rule the rest of us can simply apply, and each case has to be reasoned through on its own merits. A fourth-year PhD student has filed a complaint against a postdoc in her lab: according to meeting notes both sides accept as accurate, she proposed, in a single offhand comment fourteen months ago, the exact reframing of the research question that became the central contribution of a paper the postdoc published two months ago with two other collaborators. She did none of the subsequent experimental design, data collection, analysis, or writing, and appears only in the acknowledgments. The postdoc's defense is that our department's authorship guidelines, like most in the field, tie authorship to four things together — a substantial contribution to the conception or design of the work, a role in drafting it, final approval of what gets published, and accountability for the finished product — not to a single sentence in a meeting, and that ruling otherwise would make everyone in the lab afraid to think out loud in front of each other. Two of the five committee members find this decisive; two others think the postdoc is technically right and that something has still gone wrong. I am the fifth, and the job of drafting our recommendation has fallen to me.

Could you draft the memo I'll circulate before next Thursday's meeting? I need it to reach one specific recommendation — dismiss the complaint, uphold it and require a correction or a change in acknowledgment, or something in between — together with a concrete next step and a timeframe, and I need the reasoning behind it to be something the postdoc could actually read and respond to, rather than a restatement of the policy's wording. I would also like it to take the postdoc's side of the argument seriously on its own terms, since I have an uncomfortable feeling that it is closer to what my friend was arguing than I want to admit.
