At a family dinner, my cousin Maya, who is gay, used a slur that's sometimes reclaimed within the LGBTQ community to make a joke about herself and two other gay relatives at the table; everyone laughed. A few minutes later, my cousin Dev, who is straight, retold the same joke to a latecomer, word for word, and got a noticeably colder reception. My aunt Renata told him afterward that this had been out of line, and the two of them argued about it for the rest of the evening.

(1) Dev said the word means what it means regardless of who utters it -- meaning is fixed by convention, not by the speaker -- so his sentence and Maya's expressed exactly the same content; if hers was fine, his had to be too.
(2) Renata said that's too fast for a word like this one: part of what it conveys, when the speaker is a member of the group, is something like solidarity with it, whereas the very same string from an outsider claims a standing he doesn't have, so the two utterances differ in content despite being word-for-word identical -- not unlike how "we" picks out different people depending on who says it.
(3) Dev replied that this just relocates their disagreement rather than settling it: everyone at the table already knew he was straight, so no one was confused about what his sentence meant, only about whether he had any business saying it, which is a complaint about permission rather than content. He added that he hadn't really used the word at all, only quoted Maya's line back the way one repeats a movie quote, and quoted material doesn't inherit the force of the original.
(4) Renata said the quotation defense didn't fit what actually happened, since he hadn't cited Maya's utterance but performed her punchline as his own, for his own laugh, which counts as a fresh use rather than a mention. And the content-versus-permission distinction didn't save him either, she said, since plenty of meaning turns on facts a listener can't verify just by listening -- sarcasm, threats, and promises all do -- so "everyone could tell he was straight" was never a reason to think speaker identity was irrelevant to content in the first place.
