So I'm about two hundred pages into a novel set at Cirey in the 1740s, with Émilie du Châtelet as my central character—juggling her Newton translation, a marriage that's basically just paperwork at this point, and a new affair with the poet Saint-Lambert while Voltaire looks on with the resigned patience of a man whos been demoted to friend. There's a chapter where she gets cornered by her (invented) niece about what happiness actually requires, and I wanted her answer to be lifted almost straight from the real argument of her Discours sur le bonheur rather than generic Enlightenment uplift, since a couple of people in my writing group already flagged the philosophy in that scene as "kind of vague."

Here's the paragraph as it stands, Émilie speaking:

"You think I praise illusion because I haven't the stomach for truth, Margot, but it's the opposite. I've looked hard enough at things to know that the woman who insists on seeing every fault in her lover, every line in her own glass, every limit the Academy has set on what a woman may learn, is not made happier by her clarity—only more idle, and colder. So I keep certain passions the way a ship keeps ballast: my numbers, because study asks nothing of my face or my birth, and doesn't fail a woman the way love and youth eventually will; the card tables, because you don't learn what your own nerve can bear until you've risked losing something you can't easily replace; and yes, love, though I no longer ask it to promise me constancy in exchange for my illusions about it."

Can you tell me how well this actually tracks the real content of the Discourse—the illusions argument especially, and the bit about study substituting for the "glory" that's denied to women—versus where I've just invented something that sounds plausible but isn't really hers? And seperately from the history: does the "illusion is good for you" move actually hold up philosophically, or is it just a rationalization wearing a nice dress? I'm less interested in notes on the prose than on whether the ideas underneath it are doing real work.
