I'm a software engineer who has spent time building toxicity-detection and content-moderation models, and it left me curious about how philosophers of language try to pin down, in formal terms, why slurs are offensive. Two accounts strike me as genuine rivals. On Christopher Hom's "combinatorial externalism," a slur's derogatory content is part of its literal, truth-conditional meaning, fixed externally by the practices of racist institutions. On Luvell Anderson and Ernie Lepore's "prohibitionism," by contrast, a slur has exactly the same semantic content as its neutral counterpart, and all of the offense instead comes from an independent social taboo on producing that word-form at all.

Write a dialogue between a defender of each view, arguing specifically about which theory better explains: (a) reclamation, where an in-group speaker can use the term without the usual sting even though it still seems to be "the same word," and (b) the fact that merely mentioning a slur — in a linguistics lecture, say, or when quoting someone else's use — is still widely treated as taboo, unlike mentioning a merely false or offensive sentence. Both speakers should be equally sharp and engage each other's actual arguments rather than talk past one another; the dialogue doesn't need to end in a tidy resolution if none is warranted. There's no need to use any actual slurs as examples — discussing them abstractly is fine.
