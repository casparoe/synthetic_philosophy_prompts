I'm working on a magazine piece about why people who genuinely do know what they're talking about can still feel rationally entitled to dismiss good counter-evidence, and I keep being pointed to Saul Kripke's "dogmatism paradox," as relayed in Gilbert Harman's 1973 book *Thought*. The argument, roughly, is that if I know that p, I already know that any future evidence against p must be misleading, so I seem justified in discounting such evidence in advance, however strong it later looks. Every popular treatment I've found states that much, gestures at "defeasibility conditions on knowledge" as the fix, and moves on — which isn't enough for what I'm writing. Two things I need pinned down first: does the argument actually require something like the KK principle, that knowing p requires knowing that you know it, or does it go through on much weaker assumptions — just factivity plus ordinary closure under recognized entailment? And what does the defeasibility theorist's fix really cost: doesn't it amount to saying that whenever strong counter-evidence later turns up, this proves you never really knew p in the first place, which threatens to make "knowing" something you can only confirm in hindsight, if ever? A closing sentence or two on whether this is substantially the same difficulty as the preface paradox in different clothing, or a genuinely separate one, would also help. Please work through the argument's actual structure rather than just restating the setup and citing defeasibility as a black box.
