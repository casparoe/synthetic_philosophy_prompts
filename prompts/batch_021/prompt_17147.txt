For a seminar on social ontology and the philosophy of norms, I have been reading David Lewis's Convention, and I think I have worked out a general way of using his account to predict how resistant a given social practice will be to change. On Lewis's definition, a regularity counts as a convention, rather than as a rule imposed by nature or necessity, only if several conditions hold together: people generally conform to it, it is common knowledge that they do, each person prefers to conform on condition that others do (so that the regularity solves a coordination problem), and, crucially, at least one alternative regularity could in principle have solved that same coordination problem just as well. This last requirement is Lewis's arbitrariness condition, and it is meant to mark off genuine conventions from regularities that hold simply because the situation leaves no real alternative.

It seems to follow fairly directly that arbitrariness, in this specific sense, should track how easy a practice is to change: if nothing about the underlying coordination problem favors the status quo regularity over some alternative, then a population that wants to switch should be able to do so with relatively little friction, just by enough people coordinating on the alternative instead. Practices that persist for some other, non-arbitrary reason should by contrast be comparatively rigid. I mentioned this to a couple of classmates and they found it fairly intuitive as well, so we started testing it on a couple of standard illustrations of his view, such as driving-side conventions and the pairing of words with meanings within a shared language.

Here is where I would like your help: does identifying a practice as "arbitrary" in Lewis's technical sense really support the conclusion that it should be comparatively easy to shift away from, once a population recognizes it as arbitrary? And if so, how should I square that with the fact that practices like driving-side conventions and word meanings are, if anything, notoriously hard to change from within a population, short of some external shock or coordinated edict? I would like to know whether the trouble lies in my reconstruction of Lewis's view, in the further inference from arbitrariness to ease of change, or somewhere else entirely.
