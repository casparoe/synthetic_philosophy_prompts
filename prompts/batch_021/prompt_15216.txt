I've been teaching Aumann's agreement theorem in my formal epistemology seminar and I want to give my students a meatier objection than the usual "real people never have common knowledge of each others posteriors" line. The standard defense of the common prior assumption is the Harsanyi doctrine: two rational agents facing the same evidence shouldve started out with the same prior, so any leftover disagreement has to trace back to an actual difference in information, not to some free-floating difference in priors. Please argue against this defense -- make the case that the common prior assumption isn't actually derived from anything independently compelling about rationality, and that smuggling it in is what's really doing the work in the "you can't agree to disagree" result, rather than some deep fact about Bayesian rationality itself. Bonus points if you can say something about why subjective Bayesianism, on its own, has no resources to insist that two agents *should* share a prior to begin with.
