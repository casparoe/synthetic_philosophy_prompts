A friend of mine — the kind of guy who does quant work for a hedge fund and can't resist "correcting" people's intuitions at parties — cornered me at a dinner a few weekends back with a game. He flips a fair coin, the pot starts at $1, and every time it lands heads the pot doubles and he flips again; the second it lands tails, the game's over and I keep whatever's sitting in the pot. Simple enough. Then he asks: what's the most I'd pay, up front, for the one-time right to play this?

I said something like $20, and he got this insufferable little grin and told me that mathematically there's no number where I should stop — not $1,000, not $50,000 — because once you add up the payout across every possible way the game could go, weighted by how likely each one is, it comes out infinite. When I pushed back with the obvious "but an extra dollar doesn't mean as much to me once we're talking huge sums" objection, he said he'd already baked that into his calculation and it still comes out unbounded, so my refusing to pay serious money was just me being bad at probability, not some deeper reasonable instinct kicking in.

It's been nagging at me ever since, partly becuase I'm currently reporting a piece on how ordinary people size up long-shot, high-payout bets — lottery tickets, seed-stage startup investing, that sort of thing — and this felt weirdly relevant. I genuinely can't tell whether my friend has cornered me into admitting something uncomfortable about my own irrationality, or whether there's an actual problem with treating "expected payout" as the thing you're obligated to maximize here. Is my instinct to cap it at $20 or $200 actually defensible on some principled level, or is he right that I'm just chickening out of a bet I should, by the numbers, be thrilled to take?
