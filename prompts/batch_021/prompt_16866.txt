I'm putting together the powers-and-dispositions week for my grad metaphysics seminar, and I got into it with a colleague over lunch about whether the conditional analysis is actually dead or just dinged up. He thinks Lewis's fix in "Finkish Dispositions" basically neutralizes Martin's fink cases, and that anyone who goes dispositional essentialist (Bird and that crowd) is abandonning a perfectly good reductive project over a solvable technical glitch. I'm less convinced, mainly because of the masking/antidote cases -- the poison-and-antidote scenario, the bubble-wrapped vase -- which seem to me to be doing something genuinely different from finking (the causal basis stays completely intact and something downstream just blocks the process), and I don't think Lewis's move, which is built specifically to handle basis-tampering, actually touches that.

Write me an essay I can hand out to the students as a provocation. Start with the simple conditional analysis of dispositional predicates and show how finkish cases -- in both the "finkish gain" and "finkish loss" directions, e.g. the classic dead-wire-plus-electro-fink case and its mirror image -- refute it. Then explain why masks/antidotes are usually treated as a genuinely seperate species of counterexample rather than just more finks, and actually work through whether Lewis's requirement that the causal basis persist through the stimulus is enough to save the reformed analysis from masking too, or whether that move only ever protected it against finks specifically. I don't want a survey where you present both sides and shrug at the end -- come down on an actual answer: is there some version of the conditional analysis (Lewis's own, or a further patch like Choi's or Fara's habitual analysis) that survives both finks and masks, and if not, say plainly whether that failure should push us toward dispositional essentialism/primitivism about powers, or whether even that "solution" just relocates the mystery rather than solving it.
