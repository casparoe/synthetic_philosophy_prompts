And here's the answer she got:

"Mill's 'conversion' gets overstated a lot. In the Autobiography he says he and Harriet Taylor arrived at an ideal that would put them, in his own words, among 'Socialists' -- but he's explicit that this isn't the revolutionary, property-abolishing kind. It's a long-run aspiration to be reached through voluntary experiment and better education, not legislation imposed on an unwilling society.

He works this out properly in the unfinished Chapters on Socialism, which Harriet helped him draft in his later years before it was published in the Fortnightly Review. There he separates communism from socialism: communism is the strict version, common ownership plus distribution in strict equality regardless of how much each person contributes, while socialism is the broader category, including cooperative schemes -- Fourierism above all -- that keep pay tied to work or skill rather than insisting on equal shares. He prefers the cooperative version because it doesn't ask people to be more selfless than they currently are, and doesn't require abolishing competition altogether.

Importantly, Mill never asks for the state to run industry. His picture is evolutionary: once workers are educated enough to manage their own firms, cooperatives will simply out-produce ordinary capitalist businesses and gradually replace them through ordinary market competition rather than expropriation, which is why some historians now read him as an early market socialist. Some of this was aimed at Marx, whose surplus-value story Mill treats as dressing up a moral complaint about inherited advantage as if it were an economic law. Mill's own remedy is narrower: heavy taxation of large bequests, plus his Land Tenure Reform campaign to tax the 'unearned increment' on rising land values. Through all this, the harm principle and his suspicion of paternalism stay fully intact -- socialism has to earn its place by expanding liberty, not shrinking it."
