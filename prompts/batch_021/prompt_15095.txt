I am plotting a novel set largely within a Cambridge philosophy faculty in the early nineteen-seventies, and one of its central relationships is an antagonism between an elderly logic don, who took tutorials from Russell himself as an undergraduate decades earlier and has spent his career defending and teaching the theory of descriptions, and a brash young lecturer freshly back from a year at Princeton, where he had sat in on Kripke's now-celebrated lectures on naming and necessity, still unpublished at the time. I want the scene in which the two men finally clash, over sherry after a faculty seminar, to turn on a genuine and specific philosophical disagreement rather than a vague generational squabble, so I have been trying to get the underlying logic straight before I trust myself to write it convincingly.

My understanding, from the reading I have done so far, is that Russell's theory of descriptions amounts to the claim that an ordinary proper name such as "Scott" or "Aristotle" is covertly a definite description, a bundle of identifying properties in disguise, and that Kripke's great achievement was to demolish this claim once and for all with his modal argument: that "Aristotle" would still denote the very same man even in a world where he never tutored Alexander or wrote a word of philosophy, which no mere description could do. If that is an accurate picture, it seems as though my young lecturer should be able to walk away from their exchange with a clean and total victory, leaving the old don with nothing left to defend beyond nostalgia and wounded pride. Before I write the scene that way, I would like to know whether that is in fact a fair account of what became of Russell's theory once Kripke was finished with it, or whether the old man would, in reality, still have a substantive rejoinder available to him.
