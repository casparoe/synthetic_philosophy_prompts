Since retiring from a long career in experimental physics, I have taken up philosophy in earnest. Lately I have been engrossed in an episode I had never properly appreciated: Thomas Hobbes's conviction that geometry occupies a uniquely privileged place among the sciences, admitting of a certainty that natural philosophy can never reach, because its objects—points, lines, figures—are generated by our own construction, so that we can know their causes directly rather than merely inferring them from effects, as we must with the bodies of nature. Hobbes staked this epistemological claim on his own repeated, and repeatedly refuted, attempts to square the circle—a construction we now know, via Lindemann's 1882 proof that pi is transcendental, to be strictly impossible—and he waged a public dispute over the matter with the mathematician John Wallis for decades, until his death in 1679. I would like you to write a short story that dramatizes this predicament from the inside: a thinker whose entire theory of certainty depends on geometry being the one domain reason cannot betray him, unknowingly chasing a construction that could never exist. Please let the philosophical stakes of his position, rather than mere stubbornness, be what drives the story.
