Over the past couple of months I have been teaching myself the history of the dispute between Newton and Leibniz over the nature of space, starting from Newton's bucket experiment in the Principia and working forward through the Leibniz-Clarke correspondence, mostly via Wikipedia and a couple of Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy articles. I think I have a decent grip on the shape of Leibniz's case: leaning on the principle of sufficient reason and the identity of indiscernibles, he argued that a "shifted," but otherwise qualitatively identical, universe cannot be a genuinely distinct possibility from the unshifted one, so that absolute space, and more generally any commitment to space as a substance in its own right, is doing no real work. What I cannot work out on my own, since it postdates him by two centuries, is what he would have made of the way this same style of argument resurfaces in twentieth-century physics.

Specifically, I have been reading about the "hole argument" against spacetime substantivalism in general relativity, which several writers present as essentially Leibniz's own shift argument transposed into a dynamical, diffeomorphism-invariant theory: if spacetime points have an identity of their own, independent of the metric field defined on them, then you can smoothly redistribute that field within an otherwise matter-free "hole" region, leaving everything outside untouched, and end up with two mathematically distinct solutions that no observation could ever tell apart. As I understand it, the standard substantivalist response, "sophisticated substantivalism," blocks this by denying that such diffeomorphic models represent different possibilities in the first place, which strikes me as borrowing Leibniz's own indiscernibility move to rescue the very position he was attacking. Separately, I gather that Mach's principle, usually placed in the same broad anti-absolute-space tradition as Leibniz, is only partly borne out in general relativity: rotating matter does drag inertial frames around with it, but the theory also allows entirely empty solutions that still have a perfectly well-defined inertial structure, which a strict relationist about inertia should presumably find uncomfortable.

Given all this, what do you think Leibniz himself would most plausibly have said about the hole argument and about sophisticated substantivalism's reply to it, reasoning from his actual metaphysical commitments rather than from the generic assumption that he was a relationist and so would simply have welcomed it? Would he have treated it as a vindication of his dispute with Clarke, or would the fact that his own logical apparatus ends up being redeployed on the substantivalist side have bothered him? And how do you think he would have reacted to the partial, imperfect way Mach's principle actually plays out in general relativity? I would like your best-supported reconstruction of his likely position on both points, and I would appreciate it if you flagged the places where you are extending his views into territory he never addressed, rather than presenting speculation as settled fact.
