I picked viola back up this year after a solid decade off and joined a community orchestra, and our conductor is a philosophy-PhD-dropout who will NOT stop bringing up Schopenhauer during rehearsal breaks. It's actually gotten me reading chunks of The World as Will and Representation instead of just nodding along politely, and now there's a specific thing bugging me that I can't shake.

As I understand it, Schopenhauer's whole theory of art is built around Platonic Ideas: each art form lets you contemplate the wills objectification at a certain grade, will-lessly, and that contemplation is what's aesthetically pleasurable — architecture shows you the crude Idea of gravity versus rigidity fighting it out, sculpture shows you the Idea of the human body, tragedy shows you the Idea of human suffering up at the top of the ladder, and so on. But then when he gets to music he basically drops all of that. Music isn't supposed to depict any Idea at all — he says it's a direct copy of the will itself, running parallel to the whole world rather then showing us a piece of it, to the point where he even suggests it could still count as music in some sense even if the entire phenomenal world of Ideas didn't exist. Our conductor treats this like the obvious culmination of the theory, but to me it reads like Schopenhauer quietly exempts his favorite art from the very framework he spent the whole book building.

Could you compare his general, Idea-based theory of art with his separate treatment of music — laying out the actual argument for why music supposedly has to work differently — and then tell me whether you think this amounts to a genuine inconsistency in his system, or whether there's a good internal reason it isn't one? Justify your answer either way.
