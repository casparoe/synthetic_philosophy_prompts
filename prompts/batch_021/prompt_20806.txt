And here's the student's answer, word for word: "Sen's paper is basically a response to Rawls's theory of justice, which measures how well off people are using an index of primary goods - income, wealth, basic rights and liberties, opportunities, and the social bases of self-respect. Sen's objection is that this index ignores the fact that people differ enormously in how well they can convert these goods into actual functioning: a disabled person, or someone who is pregnant, needs more income to reach the same level of mobility or health as an able-bodied person, so treating two people with identical primary goods as equally advantaged is misleading.

His objection to welfare-based equality - which Sen treats as basically the utilitarian idea that what we should be equalizing is how satisfied or happy people are - is that welfare, understood as pleasure or desire-satisfaction, adapts too easily to bad circumstances. Someone who has spent their whole life deprived may scale down their desires so much that they report themselves as content even though they're objectively badly off, so a metric that just tracks reported satisfaction will systematically understate long-term deprivation. If we equalized welfare we'd end up saying such a person doesn't need anything, which is clearly the wrong conclusion.

Sen's own proposal is to equalize capabilities instead - the sets of functionings (beings and doings, like being well-nourished or being mobile) that a person is actually able to achieve. This tracks real freedom rather than just resources or reported satisfaction, and it naturally handles the disability case, since it's defined in terms of what people can actually do rather than what they own. Nussbaum later operationalized this by giving the approach a fixed list of about ten central capabilities, since Sen's original version was really too abstract to be used for actual policy."
