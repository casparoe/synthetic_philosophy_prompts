I write about cognitive science and philosophy for a science magazine, and I'm drafting a piece that puts two literatures in conversation. One is the psychological research on confabulation associated with Nisbett and Wilson, which shows people confidently reporting reasons for their own behavior or feelings that demonstrably are not the real causes. The other is Timothy Williamson's argument against the "luminosity" of mental states in Knowledge and Its Limits — the one involving a room that warms so gradually that someone who starts out feeling cold cannot always know, moment to moment, whether they still do, which he uses to argue that the KK principle (that knowing something puts you in a position to know that you know it) fails at the margins. My instinct is that Williamson has essentially supplied, a generation later, a formal, a priori proof of what the psychologists had already shown empirically: that our own reports about our mental states can't be fully trusted. Is that a defensible way to connect the two literatures for readers, or does it flatten a distinction that actually matters?
