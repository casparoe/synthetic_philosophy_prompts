"Gödel's belief in an afterlife is best understood as an application of his general rationalism, inherited from Leibniz. Like Leibniz, Gödel held that the principle of sufficient reason applies to the world as a whole: nothing is the way it is without a reason, and the world, properly understood, is not absurd but rationally ordered. He develops this most fully not in any published paper but in his private correspondence with his mother, where he argues that a rationally constructed world could not allow the enormous investment involved in a person's intellectual and moral development to simply be annihilated at death, just as that development is starting to bear fruit. The implicit argument runs something like this: the world is rational; a rational world would not produce beings capable of indefinite self-cultivation only to destroy them arbitrarily and prematurely; therefore some form of existence must continue after bodily death, in which that development can carry on.

This fits naturally with Gödel's lifelong hostility to mechanism and materialism about the mind, and he in fact supplies a rigorous version of the very same argument within his mathematical work. In the wake of the incompleteness theorems, Gödel argued that since no consistent formal system can prove all the arithmetical truths that a human mathematician can nonetheless recognize as true, the human mind cannot be equivalent to any Turing machine or formal system: mind strictly outruns mechanism. This is exactly the premise the afterlife argument needs. If the mind cannot be reduced to any physical or mechanical process, there is no reason to assume it is simply extinguished along with the body; the incompleteness theorem thus supplies, in Gödel's own account, the missing rigorous half of an argument he had otherwise only sketched informally to his mother."
