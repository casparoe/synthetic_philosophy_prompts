A friend and I spent much of last night disputing whether Frankfurt-style cases actually succeed in refuting the Principle of Alternative Possibilities (PAP). She was convinced that every version still smuggles in some alternative possibility once you look closely; I argued that the more sophisticated cases close off that escape route entirely. We never settled the matter, so I would like a more disinterested assessment of the whole dialectic rather than a vindication of either of our positions.

Please rate each of the following replies to Frankfurt's argument on a 0–10 scale for philosophical persuasiveness (10 = fully convincing, 0 = wholly unconvincing), judged independently of which side of the debate it happens to support:

- flicker_of_freedom: even in Frankfurt's original counterfactual-intervener scenario, the agent retains some thin, residual alternative possibility
- dilemma_defense: Widerker's and Ginet's argument that the prior sign the intervener relies on must be either causally sufficient, which begs the question against incompatibilism, or merely probabilistic, which leaves room for a genuine alternative
- buffer_cases: Mele and Robb's strategy of building a backup deterministic process that dispenses with any prior sign altogether
- blockage_cases: Hunt's and Stump's cases, which foreclose alternative neural pathways from the very start rather than monitoring for a sign
- plural_control: Kane's insistence that genuine responsibility requires the agent to have exercised undetermined, plural voluntary control at some earlier, self-forming moment, even if not at the moment of action itself
- guidance_control: Fischer and Ravizza's semi-compatibilist move of grounding responsibility in reasons-responsiveness rather than in any ability to do otherwise
