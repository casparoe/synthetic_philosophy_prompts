"Q3. David Lewis's 'triviality results' are usually glossed as showing that 'the probability of a conditional is not, in general, the corresponding conditional probability.' (a) State the thesis under attack (call it the Equation): what does it say P(A > B) should equal? (b) Consider a simple probability space with three equiprobable worlds w1, w2, w3, where A is true at w1 and w2, and B is true only at w1. Show what the Equation forces P(A > B) to equal once you condition on A∧B and on A∧¬B respectively, and explain briefly why. (c) What further premise, beyond the Equation itself, does Lewis need in order to get an actual triviality result out of these observations? Explain in your own words what 'triviality' means here. (d) Give one way philosophers have tried to block the triviality result, and say briefly how it avoids the problem."

And here's the answer I have to grade:

"(a) The thesis under attack is what's often called Adams' Thesis or the Equation: for any A, B with P(A) > 0, the probability of the indicative conditional 'if A, B' should equal the conditional probability of B given A, i.e. P(A > B) = P(B|A). This is meant to capture the natural idea that how confident we should be in 'if A, B' just is how confident we'd be in B on the supposition that A.

(b) Let P(w1) = P(w2) = P(w3) = 1/3, with A = {w1, w2} and B = {w1}. So P(A) = 2/3 and P(B|A) = P(A∧B)/P(A) = (1/3)/(2/3) = 1/2, so by the Equation P(A > B) = 1/2. Now suppose we learn A∧B (i.e. w1) -- conditional on that, A and B are both already settled true, so B given A obviously has probability 1, and by the Equation again P(A > B | A∧B) = 1. Symmetrically, learning A∧¬B (w2) makes B given A probability 0, so P(A > B | A∧¬B) = 0.

(c) That pattern is already the triviality result: the very same proposition A > B would have to have probability 1 on one bit of evidence, 0 on another, and 1/2 unconditionally, and no proposition can coherently have three different probabilities floating around like that. 'Triviality' just means the Equation forces every conditional whose antecedent doesn't already settle its consequent into this kind of incoherence, which is absurd given how obviously we do reason with uncertain conditionals all the time.

(d) One well-known way of blocking the result is Stalnaker's: he denies that indicative conditionals express propositions at all. On his view, asserting 'if A, B' isn't putting forward a truth-conditional claim that the probability calculus could even apply to -- it just signals that your conditional probability of B given A is high. Since there's no genuine proposition A > B for P to be defined on, Lewis's proof never gets started; the Equation gets replaced by a pragmatic story about assertability rather than an identity between the probabilities of two real propositions."
