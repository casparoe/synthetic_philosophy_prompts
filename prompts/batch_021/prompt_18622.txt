Okay, this is going to sound super in the weeds but I haven't been able to stop thinking about it since it happened. I'm in a metaphysics seminar this semester and we just spent two weeks reading Naming and Necessity against Lewis's old counterpart theory paper, all about what makes it true that "Humphrey could have won the election." After class on Thursday, two people in my cohort, Jonah and Mira, ended up going at it in the hallway for like 40 minutes, and I wrote down as much as I could on my phone because it was actually a good fight, not the usual talking-past-each-other thing.

Jonah opened with the classic move against Lewis: if "Humphrey could have won" is true because some counterpart of Humphrey wins in another world, then the sentence isn't even about Humphrey anymore, its about some other guy who merely resembles him, and Humphrey himself couldn't care less how his lookalike does elsewhere. So counterpart theory quietly changes the subject of every ordinary de re claim we make about actual people.

Mira's comeback was that Kripke doesn't actually have a rival theory here, he just refuses to theorize. His whole line is that transworld identity isn't something you discover by tracking some essence across worlds, it's stipulated: you just say "here's a possible history where Nixon loses" and there's no further fact left to worry about, because rigid designation already fixed that "Nixon" picks out our guy. So it's not fair to say counterpart theory "loses" to a competing account, since there isn't one on the table.

Jonah's reply: fine, but counterpart theory has the exact same problem, just moved one level down. The counterpart relation is a similarity relation, and similarity is completely interest-relative -- weight "career trajectory" heavily and Obama's counterpart in some world is a senator; weight "exact fingerprints" heavily and its a janitor. Lewis needs some principled way to fix which similarities are the modally relevant ones, and any way of fixing it looks either arbitrary or like it's smuggling in the very facts about sameness it was supposed to explain.

Mira's last move was that this cuts both ways: rigid designation doesn't tell you which features of a counterfactual scenario have to hold either, it just presupposes you already know, in this or that made-up case, that it's Nixon losing rather than some other guy losing. So both of them end up leaning on some unanalyzed sense of "same guy" in the end -- Lewis is just more upfront that his is a similarity relation instead of burying it inside "reference."

Then people started packing up and nothing actually got settled. Can you go through this move by move and say who's actually winning at each stage, not just an overall verdict? And if there's some move either of them should've made but didn't, I want to hear that too.
