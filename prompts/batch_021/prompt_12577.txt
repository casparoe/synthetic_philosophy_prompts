I've spent the last decade writing long-form journalism about science and ideas, and I recently ran into a puzzle that came out of my own reporting process rather than any story I was covering. I'd just finished six weeks of work on an investigative feature — just over nine thousand words, resting on something like two hundred individually checkable claims: dates, quoted figures, statistics pulled from three different agencies, and a chain of causal claims tying them together. Our fact-checking is thorough: every claim gets checked against a primary source or independently confirmed by a second reporter, and by the time the piece went to press I was genuinely confident, claim by claim, that each one was accurate.

And yet, precisely because I've done this long enough to have watched meticulously checked pieces at far better-resourced outlets than mine get quietly corrected months later, I also went to press believing that somewhere among those two hundred claims there's probably at least one error I didn't catch. When I mentioned this to my editor, she told me I was being incoherent: if I genuinely believe claim 1, and claim 2, and so on through claim 200, then I'm logically committed to believing their conjunction — that the piece is error-free — which flatly contradicts my hunch that it isn't. Her advice was either to drop the hunch as unfounded anxiety, or go find the specific claim I don't really trust and fix it before it runs.

Neither option sits right with me. I can't point to any single claim I doubt, and yet I would still wager that there's an error somewhere in the piece, which doesn't feel like the same thing as doubting all two hundred claims equally. A statistician friend I described this to, half-jokingly, said it reminded her of a seminar problem about a weather forecaster who is perfectly well-calibrated about each day's forecast individually while also being confident that not every forecast in a given month will pan out, though she couldn't recall what, if anything, philosophers had made of cases like that. So I'm curious for your take: is my editor right that I'm contradicting myself, or is there a respectable way of holding both the claim-by-claim confidence and the overall doubt at once? And practically speaking, when an editor asks me to vouch for a big piece, is there a more honest way to describe my actual epistemic state than either "I trust this completely" or "I secretly doubt something in it"?
