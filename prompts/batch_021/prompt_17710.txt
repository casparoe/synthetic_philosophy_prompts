I've spent the past several months interviewing ICU nurses and physicians for a longer piece on pandemic-era burnout, and one exchange keeps nagging at me even though it's only a small detail in the larger story. A nurse who worked through the worst of the COVID surges told me that hospital administrators constantly praised staff who kept showing up as going above and beyond, invoking language like heroism and sacrifice in town halls, internal newsletters, and press materials. At the same time, she said, colleagues who called out sick, asked to be reassigned away from COVID units, or refused to work without adequate protective equipment faced real professional consequences, including termination in at least a couple of cases she knew about directly. What has stayed with her, in retrospect, is the mismatch: the hospital wanted credit for her continued presence as though it were an extraordinary, freely chosen act of courage, while simultaneously treating anyone's absence as a plain dereliction of duty deserving punishment.

I haven't been able to decide whether there's a genuine inconsistency in that combination or whether I'm just imagining one. On its face, it seems odd to insist that doing something is exceptional and praiseworthy, beyond what could reasonably be demanded of someone, while also insisting that failing to do it is blameworthy and worth punishing — can the same act, for the same person, really be both at once? Or is there a legitimate distinction here, something about what an ordinary bystander owes as opposed to what someone owes once they've taken on a specific professional role, that would let both halves of the hospital's messaging hold at the same time, at least up to some threshold of risk? I'd like to understand where, if anywhere, the reasoning actually breaks down, rather than just concluding that the messaging was cynical.
