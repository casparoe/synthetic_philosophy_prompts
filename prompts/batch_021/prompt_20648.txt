I sit on my university's small interdisciplinary "Mind & Behavior" seed-grant committee -- we hand out $10-15k pilot grants to psych, philosophy, and neuro faculty for projects too preliminary to take to NIH. This round's headache is a proposal from a clinical psychologist working in the Acceptance and Commitment Therapy / Relational Frame Theory tradition, who wants to pilot a relational-framing intervention for avoidance behavior in chronic pain patients. Per our rules we sent it out to two reviewers from the cognitive/neuro side, and both came back unimpressed for the same reason: the proposal never specifies what mechanism is supposed to change -- no claim about beliefs, appraisals, schemas, anything -- it just predicts certain behavioral outcomes will shift, and one reviewer wrote that this makes the whole thing "unfalsifiable, since if it doesn't work they can always say it functioned differently in that context." The applicant's rebuttal (forwarded to me since im apparently "the philosophy person" on this committee) says this gets things backwards: her tradition's philosophy of science, which she calls functional contextualism, explicitly denies that naming an inner mechanism is the goal of a good psychological explanation, and judges claims instead by whether they let you predict and influence behavior reliably. Grading her proposal on the mechanism criterion, she says, is like refusing to fund a machine-learning paper for not explaining mechanistically why the model's weights produce the right answer, when the whole paradigm is built around not needing that. Half the committee thinks she has a real point about differing standards; the other half suspects "our frameworks are incommensurable" is just what you say when your hypothesis is built to never be pinned down enough to fail. (I vented about this to my reading group last night and someone suggested we just fund whoever writes the better prose, which, no.)

Could you draft the decision memo I'll actually bring to committee -- a real recommendation (fund, decline, or fund with specific conditions) and the reasoning behind it, since we vote a week from Friday. I don't want three paragraphs rehearsing Kuhn and incommensurability and then a shrug; I've got colleagues who'll do that part for free. What I need is something that takes both sides at their strongest: her point that judging a contextualist project by a mechanistic paradigm's home standards isn't neutral, and the reviewers' worry that unfalsifiability doesn't stop being a problem just because you can redescribe it as a paradigm difference. And if there's some rule of thumb our committee could reuse next time a proposal like this comes up -- because it won't be the last -- I'd like the memo to actually propose one rather than just flag that the question is hard.
