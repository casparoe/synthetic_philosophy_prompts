Ok this is gonna sound super random but it's been stuck in my head for like two weeks now. Bit of background: I got kind of obsessed with expected value stuff after watching a bunch of poker/betting videos on youtube, which eventually led me down a rabbit hole to the St. Petersburg paradox (the coin flip game where the payout doubles every time it's tails and you cash out on the first heads - the "expected value" works out to infinity). Weird, but at least infinity is an actual answer you can point to.

Then I found a forum post about a nastier cousin of it called the Pasadena paradox, invented by two philosophers named Nover and Hajek I think? Same coin-flipping setup, except the payouts alternate between you winning money and you owing money, and they keep growing every round: flip 1 pays you $2, flip 2 you owe $2, flip 3 pays $2.67, flip 4 you owe $4, and so on forever. If you multiply each payout by its probability and add the results up in the normal order (flip 1's term, then flip 2's, then flip 3's...) you get 1 - 1/2 + 1/3 - 1/4 + 1/5 - ... which settles down to ln(2), about 0.69. But apparently that series only "conditionally" converges (had to look this up, it basically means it stops converging if you make every term positive first), so you're mathematically allowed to reshuffle that exact same infinite list of numbers into a different order and land on literally any total you want, or make it shoot off to infinity, or never settle down at all. The philosophers who came up with the thing use that fact to argue the expected value of the game isn't just infinite like St Petersburg's - it straight up doesn't exist, and that this is a real problem for decision theory since it can't even tell you whether to play.

Here's the bit that's actually bugging me. From what I can tell, their whole case for "no ordering is privileged" rests on treating the payouts as basically a pure abstract set of numbers, where order was never part of the deal to begin with. But the coin flips don't all happen at once in some shuffled order - flip 1 genuinely happens before flip 2, every single time. So why isn't adding the terms up in that exact order (1, then 2, then 3...) just obviously the right, privileged way to do it, seeing as its the order the uncertainty actually gets resolved in? I want you to argue for that position as hard and rigorously as you can: that there IS a privileged, non-arbitrary ordering here (the natural time-order the flips happen in), that this gives the Pasadena game a genuine, determinate expected value of ln(2), and that the "you can rearrange the terms to get any answer you want" fact, while mathematically true, doesn't actually establish what the philosophers think it establishes about rational choice. Don't give me some wishy-washy "well both sides have a point" answer, I've read enough of those already - actually commit to the position and build the strongest case you can for it. And please deal with the most obvious counterpunch head on, which is basically "the sample space is a set, sets don't have a built-in order, so how the flips happen to unfold in time is irrelevant to what counts as THE expectation" - tell me if there's a real comeback to that, or if it actually sinks the whole thing.
