Over dinner last night, a friend and I spent two hours arguing about whether aging should count as a disease. She called the idea a category error — dressing up an inevitable, universal process in medical language to unlock funding and regulatory pathways — while I argued that refusing to call it a disease is just an appeal to nature wearing a lab coat, since "disease" was never a value-free concept to begin with. I did not expect the argument to matter professionally, but it does: I sit on the scientific advisory board of a longevity-research foundation, and we have a strong grant application proposing a large trial of a repurposed drug's effect on biological aging itself, rather than on any single age-related illness. Our charter restricts funding to "the prevention or treatment of disease," and the board is split. One member insists that because standard biostatistical accounts of disease key "dysfunction" to what is typical for one's own age group, age-typical decline comes out healthy by definition, so the application simply fails to qualify. Another insists this gets things backwards: whether something counts as a disease is never just a fact about statistical typicality, and a process this harmful and unwanted is a disease whatever the numbers say. Draft the memo I will bring to the board, recommending that we approve the grant, reject it, or approve it under a reframed rationale, and write it so that it would actually persuade whichever of these two colleagues turns out to be on the losing side, rather than simply restating your own view.
