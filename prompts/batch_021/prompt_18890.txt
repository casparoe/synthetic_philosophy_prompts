Ok so this is a weirdly specific favor but here goes. The little independent school I went to for K-8 (my mom still teaches art there) basically has its entire mission statement lifted from Whitehead's Aims of Education -- the founders were obsessed with it, every teacher there can recite the "inert ideas" passage from memory, and the curriculum is explicitly structured around his romance-precision-generalization rhythm instead of normal grade-level subjects. They asked me, as the alum currently four years into a philosophy PhD, to give the 8th grade graduation speech this June, and I said yes before I'd actually thought it through.

Here's the problem I've talked myself into. Basically every stock commencement line I can think of -- "you've built the foundation, now go out and apply it," "take what you've learned here out into the world" -- is exactly the move Whitehead spends that whole essay attacking. If the generalization/utilization stage is supposed to be happening continuously the whole way through, cycling with romance and precision, rather than banked up during school and cashed in later on, then "go apply what you learned" quietly assumes the inert-ideas view of knowledge he's arguing against -- you cant just sharpen a mind now and postpone its use for later, thats his whole point. So I can't use that move, or anything that smells like it, without the three teachers in the front row (who own underlined copies of the book) clocking it immediately. But I also obviously can't stand up and give a room of 14 year olds and their grandparents ten minutes of epistemology -- most of that room has never heard of Whitehead and shouldn't have to.

So can you actually write the speech? Something a real person could deliver in about 5-6 minutes -- warm, specific to these kids, totally fine for people with zero philosophy background -- but that also genuinely holds together as Whitehead's actual view if the teachers pick it apart on the drive home, not just his name and "the rhythm of education" dropped in for flavor. If you can find a non-cheesy way to gesture at his line about the essence of education being "religious" (he means duty and reverence, not any actual creed, but the school is pretty secular/granola so even that word alone might make some parents twitchy) that'd be a nice bonus, but it's not the main ask.
