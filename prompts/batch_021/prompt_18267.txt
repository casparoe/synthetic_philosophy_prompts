Over the last couple of years I've been teaching myself the history of logic, mostly from library books, since I never studied philosophy formally and don't really have anyone around me to talk this stuff through with. Frege's project of grounding arithmetic in pure logic is probably the single thing that hooked me the hardest -- there's something almost moving about how ruthlessly impersonal he wanted logic to be, how insistent he was that a thought and its truth have nothing to do with the psychology of whoever happens to be thinking it. So it really threw me when I came across a passing mention, in something I was reading about the early history of analytic philosophy, that Frege kept a private diary in the last year or so of his life full of bitterly antisemitic and anti-democratic political opinions, and that this apparently disturbed even Michael Dummett -- the philosopher who probably did more than anyone to build up Frege's reputation in English, and who spent a good part of his life outside academic philosophy campaigning against racism in Britain -- once he learned about it.

I've been going back and forth about what, if anything, this is supposed to show. Part of me thinks it obviously shouldn't touch how we assess the actual philosophy: a proof doesn't become invalid because of who wrote it down, and Frege of all people would have insisted that dragging in facts about a thinker's character or psychology to judge a thought is exactly the mistake his whole career was fighting against. But another part of me can't quite let it go that easily, because the very thing he was fighting against -- letting anything subjective or "impure" contaminate logic -- sounds uncomfortably close, at least in spirit, to the anxieties about purity and contamination that turn up in the diary, just aimed at people instead of propositions. I can't decide if I'm onto something there or just pattern-matching where there's nothing really to find.

Rather than just tell me what to think, could you write me a dialogue between two people who've both thought hard about Frege -- one who holds that the diary is simply irrelevant to any serious philosophical assessment of his work, and one who holds that it isn't, and can point to something specific in his philosophy of logic to justify that -- and have them really push on each other's strongest arguments rather than just restating their own view? I'd like the two of them to end up putting their finger on exactly what the disagreement is actually about: whether it's a real question about the nature of logic and objectivity, or more a disagreement about something else entirely, like what we owe the dead or how a canon gets built.
