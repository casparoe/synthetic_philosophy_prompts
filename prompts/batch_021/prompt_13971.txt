So I've been going down a rabbit hole reading Anthropic's interpretability posts at work (we were evaluating some open-source models for a product feature and I got curious how any of this stuff actually "works" on the inside) and something's been bugging me since. They talk about how a network can be in "superposition" — basically cramming way more concepts into its neurons than it has actual dimensions for, so single neurons end up firing for a random grab-bag of unrelated stuff — and then they train sparse autoencoders on the activations to pull out cleaner "features," like that famous Golden Gate Bridge one they found in Claude 3 Sonnet. Cool, except it also seems like if you'd trained that autoencoder with a different sparsity penalty, dictionary size, or even just a different random seed, you'd probably get a different set of "features" out of the exact same activations, and both would probably score about the same on reconstruction loss. So in what sense is either one THE structure the network "really" has inside it, rather then just being a lens we laid on top afterward?

I brought this up with my roommate, who actually has a philosophy degree, and she got weirdly excited and started throwing out names like Quine and Dennett that I only half followed, so I gave up on that thread. What I actually want from you is this: make up a concrete, detailed toy example (specific tiny network, specific task, doesn't need to be realistic) where two different, each individually pretty reasonable, ways of analyzing "what's inside" the network end up giving genuinely contradictory answers about whether some specific representation is there — not just two different lossy summaries of the same thing, but an actual head-on disagreement that's awkward for anyone who wants to say the network "really has" that representation in some robust sense. Bonus points (optional) if you can connect it to why basically this same puzzle already showed up in philosophy of mind before neural nets were even a thing.
