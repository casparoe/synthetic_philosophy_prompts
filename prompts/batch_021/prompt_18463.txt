So I've got this half-baked blog post sitting in my drafts folder and I want a sanity check before I embarrass myself by publishing it. Background: my sister's doing a philosophy PhD, and over the holidays she mentioned this concept called "grounding" — apparently a bunch of metaphysicians (she name-dropped Kit Fine and Jonathan Schaffer) have spent the last couple decades arguing that grounding is its own distinct relation, seperate from causation and seperate from mere correlation or supervenience — one thing metaphysically explaining another, not just reliably going along with it. The second she described it my brain went straight to "levels of abstraction," which is like, the one thing every software engineer has strong opinions about, so I started drafting a post explaining grounding to other engineers using that analogy. Here's the core of what I've got so far:

- Your Python for-loop's behavior isn't identical to what's happening down in the bytecode, and its not identical to what's happening in the transistors either — but it's not floating free of them, and it's not merely correlated with them the way, I dunno, ice cream sales correlate with drowning deaths. The high-level behavior is grounded in the low-level behavior: fully determined by it, explained by it, nothing extra added on top.
- "Fundamental" just means: bottom of the stack, no further translation layer underneath. Physics is fundamental the way machine code and transistor-level physics are fundamental relative to your Python program. Chemistry is grounded in physics, biology in chemistry, minds in biology — same idea, just a much longer call stack than any real compiler produces.
- So my actual thesis is: grounding talk is philosophers reinventing "compiles down to," and fundamentality is just "hits bare metal."

I'm pretty sure this is naive in ways I can't see, which is exactly why I want comments before I post it rather than after.
