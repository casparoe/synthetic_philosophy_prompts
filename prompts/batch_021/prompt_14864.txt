I spent nearly forty years as a research chemist before retiring, and over the last two years I have taken up philosophy in earnest for the first time, starting, somewhat by accident, with Jon Elster's Ulysses and the Sirens and then George Ainslie's work on hyperbolic discounting and picoeconomics. Partly on the strength of that reading, and partly because a family member went through residential addiction treatment, I recently accepted a seat on the ethics advisory committee of a treatment center near me, alongside a couple of clinicians, a lawyer, and a chaplain.

A case has come before the committee that seems to me to turn on exactly the puzzle Elster and Ainslie were writing about, and the committee is genuinely split. The center allows residents, at intake, while sober and clinically assessed as fully competent, to sign what staff actually call a "Ulysses agreement": a stipulation that if they later ask to leave the ninety-day program early, citing craving or distress, staff will impose a mandatory seventy-two-hour delay -- including a meeting with their counselor and a call to a support person named at intake -- before any discharge is finalized. One resident, five weeks into treatment for opioid use disorder and by every clinical measure lucid, is now invoking that delay and asking the committee to override it. She says she has simply changed her mind for reasons unrelated to craving, and that honoring a contract over her present, competent objection is just detention with paperwork attached. Some committee members treat this as exactly the reversal her sober self foresaw and tried to bind against, and point out that leaving now, with her tolerance reduced by five weeks of abstinence, would leave her at real risk if she relapses. Others argue that once you grant she is presently competent, overriding her stated wishes is hard to distinguish from ordinary paternalism regardless of what she signed earlier; one member keeps invoking Frankfurt's "unwilling addict," which a couple of us suspect quietly assumes the very thing in dispute -- that her present wish is craving rather than a genuine change of mind. Our written policy says only that staff should exercise "clinical judgment," without saying what should count as evidence either way.

Could you draft the short memo I will bring to Monday's meeting? It should say plainly what should happen with this resident in the next seventy-two hours, and separately, how the committee should rewrite the Ulysses-agreement policy so staff are not improvising the criteria each time this arises. Please commit to an actual recommendation rather than concluding that reasonable people differ, but engage seriously and specifically with the strongest version of whichever side you do not recommend, including the prior question of whether this is really best understood as a case of weakness of will at all, rather than one we are too quick to redescribe that way.
