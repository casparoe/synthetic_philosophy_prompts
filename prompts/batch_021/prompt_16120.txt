Over the past year or so I have gotten pretty absorbed in reading about evolutionary biology as a hobby, starting with the obvious entry point (Dawkins's *The Selfish Gene*) and then following my curiosity into blog posts, old forum threads, and papers I can only half-follow. One episode I keep coming back to is the controversy that broke out after Martin Nowak and Corina Tarnita, together with E. O. Wilson, published a paper arguing that kin selection and inclusive fitness theory should essentially be abandoned as the explanation for how ants, bees, and other eusocial insects evolved, in favor of modelling selection acting directly on the colony using ordinary population genetics. This seemed to hit a real nerve in the field: Wilson had spent decades as one of the most prominent defenders of kin selection, a long list of other biologists signed public rebuttals defending inclusive fitness theory, and I even came across what I believe was a fairly unforgiving Dawkins review of a related book Wilson wrote for a general audience around the same time. It has turned into a genuine preoccupation of mine, even though I can't always follow the technical details.

I don't have any formal training in biology or mathematics, so I can't evaluate the technical claims myself, but from what I've pieced together, part of the case against inclusive fitness theory was that Hamilton's rule quietly depends on assuming weak selection and additive, pairwise fitness effects between individuals, which real social interactions don't always satisfy. Another part was that the classic "haplodiploidy hypothesis" — the idea that ants, bees, and wasps became eusocial so often because their sex-determination system makes sisters unusually closely related — looks much weaker once you notice that termites and naked mole rats are eusocial despite being ordinary diploid animals, and that most haplodiploid species never became eusocial at all. I've also seen people respond that a more general, regression-based version of Hamilton's rule sidesteps those restrictive assumptions entirely, which seems to take a lot of the force out of the original critique, though I have no way of judging for myself how serious that rebuttal really is.

What I'd like from you is the strongest possible case for the Nowak/Tarnita/Wilson side of this dispute: not merely that inclusive fitness theory is an unnecessary alternative bookkeeping method, but that it is a genuinely worse foundation for a general theory of social evolution than standard natural-selection models. Please engage directly with the generalized-Hamilton's-rule rebuttal rather than setting it aside, and tell me honestly which parts of the case still seem shaky to you even once you've made it as strongly as you can.
