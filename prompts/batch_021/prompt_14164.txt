Question: "In the Meno, Socrates claims that knowledge is more valuable than mere true belief, even though someone with only a true belief can act just as successfully as someone with knowledge. (a) Explain Socrates's own answer to this puzzle, using his examples. [6 pts] (b) Contemporary reliabilists analyze knowledge (roughly) as true belief produced by a reliable cognitive process. Explain why this view runs into the 'swamping problem' when it tries to accomodate Socrates's verdict. [7 pts] (c) Explain how virtue epistemology tries to escape the swamping problem, and briefly assess whether it actually succeeds. [7 pts]"

Student's answer:

"(a) Socrates raises this by comparing two people trying to get to Larisa: one who has actually travelled the road and knows it, and one who has just guessed the right road but never been there. Both get the traveler to Larisa equally well, so on a purely practical measure they're equal. His diagnosis is that true beliefs that aren't 'tied down' are like Daedalus's animated statues - liable to wander off, since the believer can be talked out of them or just forget - while a true belief that's been secured by working out the reason why (an aitias logismos, giving an account) becomes stable and can't easily be dislodged. So knowledge beats true belief not because it's more useful for any one action, but because it's more durable across situations where the first might fail you.

(b) Reliabilism says a belief is knowledge when it's true and was produced by a process that reliably produces true beliefs. The problem is that once you already have a true belief in hand, it's hard to see what further value reliability adds to that particular belief. If a reliable process is only good because it's a good way of getting to true beliefs, then its value is merely instrumental, and instrumental value gets swamped once the goal is reached - similar to how, once you're holding a good cup of coffee, it doesn't make the coffee taste any better to learn that the machine that made it reliably makes good coffee every time. So reliabilism can't explain why a reliably-produced true belief should be worth more than a true belief that just got lucky, even though Socrates is sure it is.

(c) Virtue epistemologists respond by relocating the value in the ability instead of the outcome: a true belief produced by exercising a reliable cognitive virtue is an achievement the believer can be credited for, the way sinking a putt through skill is a better achievement than sinking it by fluke, even though the ball ends up in the same hole either way. Since an achievement has value beyond the value of its result, that value can't be swamped - we're crediting the agent, not just re-describing the process. This is really just Plato's own solution restated, since a justified true belief, in the classical sense, is precisely a true belief the thinker can be credited with because they can back it up, which is what giving an 'account' does in the Meno. So virtue epistemology and Plato converge on the same idea, and I think it works, since it explains the Larisa case perfectly: the person who knows the road is drawing on real skill, and the lucky guesser isn't, even though they arrive at the same place."
