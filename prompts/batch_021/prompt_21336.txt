My dad died last Tuesday, pretty suddenly, and I'm giving the eulogy this Saturday. He taught philosophy for 38 years and as far as I can tell his entire intellectual life was one long conversation with P.F. Strawson's "Freedom and Resentment" -- he had this bit he'd trot out at every faculty party and most Thanksgivings about how funny it is that Strawson's own son, Galen, grew up to be a philosopher and build basically the best argument going that nobody is ultimately responsible for anything (the regress thing: you can't be responsible for the way you are without being responsible for the way you got to be that way, and so on back forever), while everyone assumes father and son disagree when actually dad's whole point was that they don't. P.F.'s paper isn't a rebuttal of Galen's kind of argument, dad always said, it's a case that the "are we ultimately responsible" question just doesn't bear on whether resentment and gratitude are the right response to another person. Dad's considered position, after four decades, was that Galen is right and it doesn't matter -- nobody deserves credit or blame in some cosmic ledger-balancing sense, and that shouldn't change one thing about how we actually treat each other.

He was completely explicit about this for as long as I can remember, not some deathbed thing -- he'd been saying it since I was a teenager getting the LW-sequences version of free will secondhand from him before I even knew what LessWrong was -- that he did not want a eulogy that smuggles desert back in through the side door: no "he earned his rest," no "he got the life he deserved for being such a good man." But he was just as clear he didn't want what he called "the objective attitude" taken toward him either: no clinical, this-was-a-brain-that-did-brain-things framing, no eulogy-as-case-study. He wanted people to be actually angry at him for the stuff he did wrong, actually grateful for the stuff he did right, and actually grieving, full stop, with no metaphysical hedge on any of it. Problem is the room's gonna be maybe a third his old grad students and collegues, who will absolutely clock it if I flatten this into generic "he taught us to question everything," and two-thirds family who've never heard of either Strawson and just want to hear that their brother and uncle and dad is missed. Could you write this eulogy for me? Something like 5-6 minutes read aloud, and it needs to actually work as a eulogy -- people crying, not people taking notes.
