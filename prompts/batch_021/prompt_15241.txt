Six weeks ago I had emergency surgery and spent nine days in the hospital. A friend, Owen, visited almost every afternoon, even though he had told me before that hospitals leave him shaken for days afterward -- his mother died in one, after a long illness. At a dinner that two other friends, Elena and Marcus, held to mark my discharge, the two of them ended up arguing about whether Owen's visits had any real moral worth, and I would like an outside, move-by-move judgment on their exchange, since I was too close to referee it myself.

(1) Elena said that when she asked Owen why he kept coming despite dreading it, he told her, "you don't leave someone alone in there, it isn't right" -- and that this deflated the visits for her: it showed he was following a general rule about decent conduct, not responding to anything about me in particular.

(2) Marcus said this has it backwards: overriding a strong, specific aversion in order to do what one takes to be right is what makes an act admirable, not what cheapens it. If finding the visits easy or pleasant were required for them to count, the friend who found them effortless would deserve more credit than the one for whom they were genuinely hard, which seems exactly backwards.

(3) Elena granted that this shows the visits were dependable, but said dependability is not the same as their being a response to me: if Owen would have shown up with identical persistence for any friend in my position, the visits were not really about me at all, just about a rule I happened to instantiate.

(4) Marcus said that charge is either trivial or unfalsifiable: a principle worth having is general enough to cover other friends too, since that is what makes someone loyal rather than merely fond of one person, and nothing about being moved by such a principle stopped Owen from attending to my actual situation, which he plainly did.
