I'm working on a longer feature about the second life Wittgenstein's On Certainty is having outside philosophy departments: it keeps being invoked, usually in slightly garbled form, by columnists and even a few cognitive scientists trying to explain why confronting someone with facts so rarely changes their mind about anything they care about. Before committing my own gloss on the argument to print, I have been reading the text itself alongside some of the secondary literature -- mainly the entitlement-and-hinge-commitments line associated with Crispin Wright and Duncan Pritchard -- because I want to be sure I have the underlying philosophy right. My editor has already raised an objection to my summary that I have not been able to answer, so I am hoping you can help before the piece goes any further.

Here is where I have landed. On Certainty grew out of arguments Wittgenstein had with Norman Malcolm at Cornell over G. E. Moore's "proof" of an external world, delivered by holding up first one hand and then the other, and the notebooks Wittgenstein kept filling until within days of his death in the spring of 1951 amount, on my reading, to a vindication of Moore's instinct through a better argument: propositions such as "the earth has existed for a long time" or "I have two hands" turn out to be the ones we are most certain of, certain enough that no skeptical argument could ever outweigh them, which is why they sit immune to doubt as the hinges on which all our other doubting and reasoning turn.

If that is right, then my editor's objection seems fair, and I still cannot answer it: why is "hinge epistemology" presented in the literature as a rival to foundationalism rather than a homelier version of it, when it appears to ground everything else we know in a privileged set of maximally certain basic beliefs, exactly as Descartes' cogito or classical sense-datum theories were supposed to? And, more urgently for the piece itself: if Wittgenstein really did identify propositions more certain than any argument that could be brought against them, shouldn't I tell readers plainly that he thereby closed off radical skepticism as a live philosophical problem, rather than reaching for the standard "philosophers still argue about this" hedge?
