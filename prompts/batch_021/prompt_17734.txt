Six months ago, at thirty-four, I was diagnosed as autistic, after a colleague's own recent diagnosis prompted me to look more carefully at traits I had always chalked up to being introverted or "bad with people." Like a lot of women my age, I seem to have been missed for years because the diagnostic criteria, and clinicians' intuitions about what autism "looks like," were built mostly around studies of young boys. Trying to make sense of the diagnosis, I've been reading well outside my usual lane, in the philosophy of psychiatric classification, and I kept running into Ian Hacking's concept of "looping effects" -- developed mainly through his studies of categories like multiple personality disorder, but routinely extended by later writers to cases like mine: the idea that, unlike a natural kind such as gold, a classification such as "autistic" changes the people it is applied to, who then organize, self-describe, and advocate in ways that feed back into how clinicians revise the category itself. The Asperger's diagnosis I might have received as a child no longer even exists as a separate category, since DSM-5 folded it into the broader spectrum, and autistic-led ideas -- like the claim that so-called social deficits are really a two-way mismatch between autistic and non-autistic people, rather than a failure located only in the autistic person -- have visibly reshaped how researchers now frame the condition. All of this looks like a textbook case of looping, and it has left me stuck on a question that two separate conversations with friends have failed to resolve.

The question is this: does the fact that autism is so obviously a "moving target," in Hacking's phrase, mean that the category doesn't track anything about me that exists independently of the classifying -- so that talk of being "really" autistic, in some robust sense, is quietly confused -- or is the looping compatible with the category still picking out something real, if imperfectly and revisably, in the way a medical concept can be corrected without the underlying condition having been invented by the correction? I would like to see this argued out properly rather than settled by assertion. Could you write a dialogue between two philosophers of psychiatry who both fully accept Hacking's description of the looping around autism as accurate, but draw opposite morals from it -- one taking it to support eliminativism, or at least a thoroughgoing anti-realism, about autism as a kind; the other defending a modest, revisable realism that they think looping does not actually threaten? I want the two of them evenly matched and genuinely engaging each other's specific points, including the examples above, rather than restating their starting positions past one another, and I'd like the dialogue to end with them pinning down as precisely as they can exactly where their disagreement still lies, rather than trailing off into vague mutual concession.
