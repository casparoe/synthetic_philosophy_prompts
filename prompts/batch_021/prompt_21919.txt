I am preparing a seminar on the Hart–Fuller debate for next term and would like a dialogue that moves past the usual summary of 'positivism separates law and morals; natural law denies this.' Write a dialogue between a Hartian legal positivist and a Fullerian natural lawyer, set in a public park where a veterans' association has just mounted a decommissioned military vehicle on a plinth as a war memorial, apparently in violation of an old ordinance banning vehicles from the park — a scenario closely modeled on the example Fuller himself raised against Hart in their 1958 Harvard Law Review exchange. Have the positivist open with the core/penumbra distinction, treating the memorial as a hard case at the edge of the rule's meaning that a judge may resolve through discretion without threatening the separability of law and morals in general. Have the natural lawyer answer with Fuller's actual rejoinder: that identifying even the clear 'core' cases already requires reasoning about the ordinance's purpose, so the positivist has not really cordoned evaluative reasoning off into the margins after all. Each character should take up the specific move the other has just made rather than restate a general position, and the dialogue should end with each side conceding one point that genuinely costs it something, while still disagreeing about which theory the park case actually vindicates.
