Since retiring after four decades in analytical chemistry, I have taken up philosophy in earnest, mostly through independent reading and a small discussion group of former colleagues who meet monthly at the university library. A puzzle came up recently that I have not been able to shake, and it grows out of an episode from my own professional life rather than from any of the books we have been discussing.

Toward the end of my career I assembled a handbook collecting several thousand physical and chemical constants that I had personally measured or re-verified over the years — melting points, reaction rates, spectroscopic line positions, and the like. Of every entry in it, I was, and remain, fully confident that it is correct, since each one passed whatever checks I would apply to any result before publishing it. Yet in the preface I felt honor-bound to add a sentence to the effect that, given how many thousands of independent determinations the book contains, it would be naive to suppose that not one single error had crept in somewhere. A former student of mine, now a professor of statistics, read this and told me, half in jest, that I had committed a quiet sort of self-contradiction: I affirm of each entry that it is true, yet I also affirm that the collection as a whole almost certainly contains a falsehood. He compared it to a man who buys a thousand tickets in a large raffle and is perfectly confident, of any ticket he cares to name, that it will lose, while being equally confident that some ticket among the thousand will win. I do not feel that either the handbook-maker or the raffle-buyer is being unreasonable, yet I cannot say clearly why not, nor whether my book and his raffle are really two instances of one and the same difficulty.

I would be grateful for your help in thinking this through: was my former student right that I hold beliefs that cannot all be true together, and if so, does that amount to a genuine lapse of rationality on my part, or does it instead suggest that the ordinary picture of rational belief — on which believing each of several things commits you to believing everything they jointly entail — is too simple to be right? And is there any real difference between the predicament of the handbook and that of the raffle, or are they, at bottom, one and the same puzzle wearing different dress?
