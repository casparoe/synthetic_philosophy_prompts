So I'm about four drafts into a novel and there's one scene I keep chickening out of writing properly, so I want to nail the substance before I try it in my own voice. Quick context: one of my main characters, Elena, is a mathematician who spent her whole career as a serious, old-school intuitionist in the Brouwer-through-Bishop tradition -- not the soft "I just like concrete examples" version, but someone who genuinely thought that a classical proof by contradiction which asserts something exists without ever constructing it isn't just inelegant -- it hasn't actually proved existence at all -- and she said so in print and lost atleast one long friendship over it. She dies about two-thirds through the book, and among her papers her family finds a short, half-joking instruction for whoever ends up giving the eulogy: "no existence proofs without witnesses, please, and mind what you do with excluded middle." The job falls to her favorite former student, now a professor herself, who has to actually stand up and deliver it at the memorial in front of maybe eighty people -- a third of them mathematicians who knew Elena for decades and would definately clock it if the eulogy cheated.

What I need is the eulogy itself: the actual words the student says, roughly five minutes read aloud, grieving and warm and a little funny in the way people are when they're eulogizing someone difficult and beloved. The whole book is partly about how this department fights over foundations more than over anything that actually touches their lives, so I don't want this scene to just gesture at "logic people are quirky" -- the constraint has to really bite. So: no "she's in a better place now" (that's asserting existence with no witness in sight), no "either she made peace with her brother before the end or she didn't, and I like to think she did" (that's excluded middle doing emotional work it hasn't earned), and nothing else that only works if you're quietly allowed classical moves Elena spent her life refusing. It still has to land as comfort for the non-mathematicians in the room, who shouldn't have to notice the logic operating under the hood to feel it.

I did a philosophy minor a hundred years ago and I've been re-reading around BHK semantics and the rejection of double-negation elimination for this chapter, so you don't need to explain the theory to me -- I just need the speech itself, ideally with a stage direction or two if it helps, like where she pauses or where the mathematicians in row two react.
