I am a retired evolutionary ecologist who has spent much of retirement working through David Lewis's *Convention*, alongside some later attempts to naturalize his account in evolutionary terms. At a small reading group of old colleagues last month, two of them fell into a disagreement I haven't been able to settle myself, and I would value your independent judgment on it.

The trigger was the classic double-bridge experiments with Argentine ants, in which a colony offered two equally short routes between nest and food reliably ends up committed to just one of them, through nothing but positive-feedback pheromone reinforcement of whichever branch happened to get a slight early edge. Fenwick, the philosopher among us, called this a textbook Lewis convention: an arbitrary, self-perpetuating solution to a recurring coordination problem, sustained purely by precedent, structurally identical to Lewis's own driving-side and meeting-place cases. Odalys, a fellow biologist turned evolutionary game theorist, agreed, adding that this is exactly why Skyrms treats common knowledge and mutual belief as dispensable idealizations: reinforcement dynamics alone, in ants or in us, do all the work of selecting one equilibrium over its rivals.

Fenwick objected that Lewis's official definition requires the regularity to be sustained by agents' expectations about one another, underwritten by something playing the role of common knowledge; an ant tracking a gradient represents nothing about what other ants believe, so at best this is an analogue of convention, not the thing itself. Odalys replied that this proves too much even for humans, since Lewis, unwilling to saddle ordinary speakers with an actually infinite chain of beliefs about beliefs, defined common knowledge dispositionally, as whatever would produce the relevant beliefs in anyone who considered the matter; ordinary speakers can't state the grammatical conventions Lewis says they share, yet their tacit grasp still counts, so once dispositional knowledge is allowed at all, the gap between a speaker's tacit competence and an ant's tacit sensitivity looks like one of degree rather than kind. Fenwick's rejoinder was that the disposition in question has to be a competence capable of feeding into inference and being revised or reported once pointed out, not just any causal sensitivity, and no amount of reflection could ever upgrade an ant's gradient-following into an explicit belief, because there is no believing apparatus there to upgrade.

Could you go through this move by move -- Fenwick's opening claim, Odalys's Skyrms-flavored agreement, Fenwick's expectations-based objection, Odalys's dispositional-knowledge reply, and Fenwick's competence-based rejoinder -- and say who has the better of each exchange, including anything both of them seem to be missing? Please close with your own verdict on whether the ant case deserves to be called a convention in Lewis's sense, or something importantly weaker.
