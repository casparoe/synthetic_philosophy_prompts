Been teaching du Chatelets Discourse on Happiness this week and theres a puzzle in it I can't shake. She spends most of the essay arguing that illusions, not truth, are what actually make us happy: you need illusions about the people you love, about your own talents, about whether that last bet was really worth it, and reason has to basically step back for any of that to do its work on you. Which is a little rich coming from the same woman who spent years getting the calculus in her Principia commentary exactly right and had zero patience for sloppy reasoning in physics. Could you write me a dialogue between two du Chatelet scholars hashing this out, maybe set at her worktable at Cirey with the Newton papers on one side and her gambling debts on the other? One of them should think the illusion doctrine is a perfectly consistent extension of her empiricism (reason noticing illusion works and endorsing it on exactly that basis), the other that its a real fault line in her thought she never actually confronts. I want genuine back and forth where they answer each other's actual points, not just restate their own -- and neither should come off as the obvious strawman.
