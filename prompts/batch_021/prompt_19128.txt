Over the past several months I have become seriously involved in trading on prediction markets, on platforms such as Polymarket and Kalshi, where one buys and sells shares tied to whether some future event will occur. A recurring dispute among the people I trade with concerns what a market's current price actually represents before the event resolves. One camp insists that a contract such as "Event E will occur by date D" is, right now, already determinately true or determinately false; we simply do not yet know which, and the price only reflects our ignorance of an already-settled fact. Other traders find this unsettling: if a claim about a genuinely open, contingent future event is already true or false today, does that not mean the outcome is already fixed — fatalism dressed up in the language of markets?

A friend with a philosophy background pointed out that this is essentially Aristotle's "sea-battle" argument from De Interpretatione 9, and that logicians have been fighting over the right diagnosis ever since. I have since started reading around the topic — Prior's tense logic, Thomason's supervaluationist treatment of branching time, MacFarlane's relativist semantics — and as far as I can tell there are at least three broad positions on offer: a "Peircean" line that denies bivalence for future contingents outright, so that such propositions are neither true nor false until the matter is settled; an "Ockhamist" line that keeps bivalence intact while denying that a truth today can necessitate an event tomorrow; and a supervaluationist compromise on which a future-tensed claim is true only if it holds on every way history could still unfold from now, and is otherwise neither true nor false. MacFarlane's relativism tries to do something more subtle still, making truth relative to a context of assessment rather than only a context of utterance.

I would like an essay that takes this dispute seriously on its own philosophical merits, rather than treating it purely as ancient-text exegesis. Please explain clearly why bivalence seems to threaten the openness of the future in the first place, then weigh the Peircean, Ockhamist, and supervaluationist responses (and the relativist one, if you think it is doing something genuinely distinct) against one another. Do not settle for concluding that reasonable people disagree: commit to whichever position you think best survives scrutiny, state explicitly what tips the balance against its rivals, and spell out what your answer implies about the metaphysical status of a prediction-market question that has not yet resolved.
