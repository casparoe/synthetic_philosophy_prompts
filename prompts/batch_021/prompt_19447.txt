My doctoral mentor, a physicist who worked on nuclear weapons risk in the 1980s and, in the final decade of her career, on risks from advanced AI, died unexpectedly last week at seventy-four. For as long as her students can remember, she kept a copy of von Neumann's 1955 Fortune essay "Can We Survive Technology?" pinned above her desk. She invoked its argument against anyone -- students very much included -- who assumed that a technology's dangers could simply be regulated or negotiated away for good: that no such permanent settlement is available, that technological change has grown to match the scale of the earth itself, leaving no safe distance or fresh frontier left to retreat to, and that the only real defense is a long sequence of small, correct, day-to-day decisions, sustained by patience and flexibility, rather than any single fix. She told us more than once that she wanted no eulogy promising that things would work out, and none that simply despaired either: she wanted her actual view taken seriously, not smoothed over. I now work in AI safety myself, and since her death that essay has stopped feeling like a mentor's private eccentricity and started to feel like an inheritance I have to answer for. I am one of several former students speaking at her memorial this weekend, in a room divided between her scientific colleagues, who will recognize every reference, and family who have never heard of von Neumann. Please write the eulogy I should give.
