Over the past several months I have become fairly active on an online prediction-market platform, trading contracts on things like election results, sports outcomes, and whether particular companies will hit product-launch deadlines they've announced publicly. A close friend of mine, who is finishing a PhD in decision theory, looked over my trade history a few weeks ago and told me, half-seriously, that I had produced a textbook money pump: at several points I sold a contract to buy a slightly different one that struck me as strictly better at the time, and a handful of trades later I ended up back in something close to my original position, poorer by the transaction fee paid at every step, even though each individual trade had looked like an improvement when I made it.

In the same conversation she raised a second worry, about what she called a diachronic Dutch Book — not the familiar argument that your odds at any single moment must be probabilistically coherent, but the more specific claim that if you don't plan to revise your odds strictly by conditionalizing on new information, a bookmaker who knows your updating policy in advance can offer you one bet before certain evidence comes in and a second bet after, structured so that the pair guarantees you a loss no matter how things turn out. Until she spelled this out, I had filed both remarks under the same rough heading — agents whose preferences or beliefs are badly patterned can be made to pay for it — but I am no longer sure the two arguments are doing the same philosophical work, or that they are equally persuasive.

I would like a careful comparison of the money-pump argument against intransitive preferences and the diachronic Dutch Book argument for conditionalization. Both seem vulnerable to the same style of reply: an agent who can foresee the entire exploitative sequence in advance can simply decline whichever single trade or bet would complete the loss, without ever having to give up the intransitive preferences or the non-conditionalizing update rule that supposedly caused the trouble in the first place — a response sometimes discussed under headings like "sophisticated" or "resolute" choice. Does this foresight-based reply damage both arguments equally, or is one of the two better equipped to survive it, and why? And stepping back, do these two arguments really answer to one unified notion of diachronic rationality, or is the resemblance between them mostly superficial — two different technical tricks that happen to both unfold over time?
