I spent my career running experiments in evolutionary biology, and since retiring I have been reading systematically in moral philosophy for the first time. One thing keeps striking me: the debate over 'moral worth' — what makes an action not just right but creditable to the agent — has exactly the structure of a confounded-variables problem. In the standard case of helping a friend, an agent's sense of duty and her direct sympathy for the friend are perfectly correlated, so nothing about that case alone can tell us which of the two is doing the explanatory work. This is essentially Michael Smith's 'moral fetishism' worry about the Kantian requirement that only action from duty has moral worth, and much of the subsequent literature amounts to designing cases — analogous to natural experiments — in which duty and inclination come apart.
