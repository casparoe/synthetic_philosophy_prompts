I'm a retired experimental physicist (spent about 35 years building particle detectors before I hung up my lab coat), and for the last year or so I've been tagging along with a little reading group of mostly other retirees, plus a couple of grad students who put up with us, working through decision theory and formal epistemology. Two sessions back we covered Aumann's agreement theorem, and it set off a real argument between two of the regulars, Gordon and Renata, that's been rattling around in my head ever since.

Gordon's opening move was the standard gloss: if two Bayesians share a common prior, and their posteriors on some event become common knowledge (each knows what the other's is, and knows that the other knows theirs, and so on), those posteriors have to converge -- so two honest, well-informed people who've actually talked things through can't rationally just agree to disagree. Renata shot back that the whole result leans on the "common prior assumption," which she said is doing all the real work and almost never holds: two people can run identical evidence through different but equally defensible priors and land in permanently different places without either one being irrational. Gordon's comeback was that a common prior isn't some extra, optional add-on -- if you trace things back far enough, to before either of them had recieved any evidence at all, there's nothing that could have made there epistemic starting points differ, so "start from a common prior" just is what starting from ignorance means. Renata answered that this quietly assumes there's one privileged "state of ignorance" to start from, when really people's priors differ partly because of built-in dispositions and modeling choices that were never based on evidence and never chosen -- and that picking a "canonical" ignorance prior (uniform over which variables, exactly?) is itself a substantive, contestable choice, not a neutral default. Gordon pushed back once more: fine, but if two priors differ for reasons that have nothing to do with evidence, then at least one of them isn't tracking anything real, so Renata can't keep insisting both sides are fully rational. Renata's last word was that this just smuggles back in an objective, view-from-nowhere notion of a uniquely correct prior -- exactly what subjective Bayesianism was invented to let us avoid -- so Gordon doesn't get to help himself to it for free.

Neither one budged, and my own Bayesian stats is rusty enough that I kept flip-flopping on who was actually winning. Could you go through the exchange move by move and say who has the stronger case at each step, rather than just handing out an overall winner? And if there's some standard resolution to this in the epistemology or game theory literature that both of them are overlooking, I'd love to hear it -- I'd like to bring back something better than "huh, I dunno" to the group next week.
