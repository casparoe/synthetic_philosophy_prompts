Last week, in the graduate proseminar I teach on philosophy of mathematics, we worked through Gödel's second incompleteness theorem and its consequences for Hilbert's consistency program, and one of my sharper students posed a question that ended up occupying the rest of the session: given that no theory strong enough to interpret arithmetic can prove its own consistency, on what basis do working mathematicians treat the consistency of Peano Arithmetic, let alone ZFC, as something close to a foregone conclusion? I brought up Gentzen's 1936 proof, which secures PA's consistency only by relocating our trust onto transfinite induction up to ε₀ rather than dispensing with the need for trust altogether, and I mentioned Edward Nelson's 2011 attempt to show outright that PA is inconsistent, a claim serious enough that logicians spent real effort locating the error before Nelson withdrew it. The class wanted an actual position, not just a tour of the relevant history, and I promised to bring a draft to our next meeting.

I would like your help with that draft. Write an essay that takes a definite stand on two connected questions. First: Peano Arithmetic and ZFC cannot be proved consistent except relative to some further system that could, in principle, be doubted at least as much as they are, and the history of foundational mathematics already contains one case, Frege's Grundgesetze, undone by Russell's paradox, in which a rigorously constructed system built by a first-rate logician turned out to be flatly inconsistent. Given both facts, how much confidence are we actually entitled to that Peano Arithmetic and ZFC are consistent? Second, and independently of how likely you think it is: how catastrophic would it really be for mathematics if an outright inconsistency in one of these systems were discovered tomorrow? Weigh whatever considerations you find most compelling on each side, but don't let the essay end in a shrug: I want a specific verdict on both questions, defended rather than merely gestured at.
