A friend and I spent an entire evening arguing about Kripke's rule-following paradox, often nicknamed "Kripkenstein": the claim that no fact about a person's past behavior, dispositions, or introspectible mental life can fix which rule they have been following, since any finite history of applications is equally compatible with rules that agree on every case tried so far but diverge on some case not yet tried. My friend insisted that idealizing dispositions—imagining a speaker with unlimited memory and computational power—removes the indeterminacy; I argued that idealization only postpones the problem, since a disposition still just describes what someone would do, not what they ought to do, and so can never supply the missing normativity. We never settled it. Write a short story—set wherever you find most illuminating—whose plot turns on a moment when a rule that a character or institution has always applied "in the same way" turns out to admit two genuinely incompatible continuations, with nothing in the history of past applications able to settle which one had really been followed. It should also give dramatic weight to Kripke's "skeptical solution": the idea that whatever ultimately fixes the correct continuation does so only because a community is willing to endorse and enforce it, not because of any fact about the rule-follower considered in isolation.
