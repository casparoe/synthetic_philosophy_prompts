I'm outlining a novel about two former colleagues from the same proprietary trading desk who haven't spoken in a decade and who have drifted, in the meantime, into what feel like rival religions of risk. Priya stayed in the industry and made partner; she still thinks in the classical expected-utility idiom she was trained in. Dana left to build a small, fanatically disciplined fund run on what she calls "geometric rationality" — sizing every position with a strict Kelly rule and treating long-run growth, rather than expected value, as the only sane target — and has become convinced along the way that mainstream finance rests on a genuine mathematical mistake, not just a difference in temperament. I want to open a chapter at a reunion dinner where a mutual friend, now a wealthy and slightly mischievous hedge-fund manager, offers to back both of them that same night in a standing proposition: a fair coin will be flipped again and again, and before every flip each of them may choose what fraction of their current winnings to risk — heads, and the amount at risk grows by sixty percent; tails, and it shrinks by half. Betting the whole pile on every flip gives a comfortably positive arithmetic expected value round to round, and yet a player who does this is ruined with near certainty in the long run, since wealth compounds multiplicatively instead of adding up. Priya and Dana now have to decide, out loud and in front of their old friends, how much of the stake each of them is willing to risk, and I want their argument about this to be the spine of the chapter.

Please write the scene as a dialogue of somewhere between 700 and 1100 words. Dana should make the strongest available version of the ergodicity argument: that an ordinary expected-value calculation silently averages the outcome across a population of counterfactual versions of the gambler that don't exist, that the only average with any real claim to describe what happens to the single gambler who keeps playing is the time-average growth rate, and that this is a fact about the dynamics of compounding wealth, not an expression of Dana's personal appetite for risk. Priya should answer with the strongest orthodox reply available to her: that von Neumann–Morgenstern rationality is a constraint on the consistency of one's preferences, not on which utility function represents them, so that log utility has no more claim to being "correct" than any other concave utility function; that Dana's appeal to what happens "almost surely" as the number of flips goes to infinity is itself a substantive and contestable choice about which horizon matters; and that a real trader is never facing a single isolated repeated bet but a portfolio of many simultaneous, imperfectly correlated ones, where ordinary variance-based reasoning already does what Dana thinks only geometric growth can do. I'd like each of them to actually engage with the other's specific move rather than restate their own — Dana, for instance, should have a real answer to the diversification point — and I don't want the scene to resolve into either character conceding. I've had trouble keeping both voices equally sharp rather than letting one of them slide into being the story's designated straw man, so competence on both sides matters to me here more than a tidy ending.
