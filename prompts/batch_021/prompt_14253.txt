I first ran into epistemic logic a few months ago through some work-related reading on the Two Generals and Byzantine agreement problems, where distributed systems need a notion of "common knowledge" before coordinated action is safe. That led me to the treatment of knowledge and belief in Fagin, Halpern, Moses, and Vardi's classic book on the subject, and from there to what they candidly present as an unsolved embarrassment for the field: in the standard possible-worlds semantics, an agent counts as knowing a proposition just when it's true in every world they consider epistemically possible, which has the odd side effect that every agent automatically knows every logical and mathematical truth outright, and knows instantly every logical consequence of anything they know, no matter how many steps the derivation would take a real reasoner to find. This is the "problem of logical omniscience," and from what I can tell, several decades of proposed fixes -- restricting what an agent is "aware" of, admitting logically impossible worlds, treating belief as an unclosed set of sentences, charging a cost per inference step -- still haven't converged on an accepted solution.

Chasing that history further back, I ended up reading about Leibniz, who keeps turning up as a proto-computer-scientist for wanting to build a "characteristica universalis," a universal symbolic alphabet for concepts, together with a "calculus ratiocinator" that would let any dispute be settled the way a disagreement about arithmetic is settled -- by two people sitting down and, in the line he's remembered for, simply saying "let us calculate." What caught my attention is that Leibniz also has a whole doctrine, in his 1684 essay on knowledge, truth, and ideas, about how finite minds mostly think "blindly," or symbolically, manipulating characters that stand in for concepts too complex to hold in view all at once -- his example involves a polygon with so many sides that we can't run through them one by one -- as against the fully "intuitive" grasp of everything simultaneously that he reserves for God. Separately, he holds that necessary truths can always be reduced to an identity by a finite chain of reasoning, while contingent truths about the actual world require an infinite analysis that only God's intellect could ever complete.

Given all of that, I'd like your best reconstruction of what Leibniz would actually say if someone explained the logical omniscience problem to him. Would he treat the logically omniscient idealized agent of modern epistemic logic as a legitimate stand-in for the limit his calculus ratiocinator was reaching toward, or would he insist that any honest model of a real finite reasoner, human or mechanical, has to give up logical omniscience in favor of something closer to his own step-by-step symbolic calculus? And does his distinction between necessary and contingent truths give him a principled basis for holding that logical omniscience is more defensible for some kinds of knowledge than others? Please commit to a specific reconstruction of his likely view rather than simply listing considerations on both sides.
