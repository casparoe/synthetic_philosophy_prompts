I'm taking a philosophy of science seminar this term that has spent the last few weeks on competing interpretations of probability, and a conversation with my family over the summer has been nagging at me in light of it. My uncle was recently told by his cardiologist that he faces a 15 percent five-year risk of a major cardiac event, a figure drawn from a large study of patients who share his diagnosis. My cousin, who is finishing a biostatistics PhD, got curious and looked into the literature herself; she found that once you condition on his specific age bracket, cholesterol level, and family history, comparable cohort studies put the five-year risk closer to 24 percent, and that narrowing further, by his activity level and current medication, yields a smaller subgroup analysis with a lower number still. When my uncle asked, half-jokingly, which number was actually his risk, nobody at the table had a good answer.

What strikes me is that this doesn't seem specific to medicine at all: it seems to come up wherever anyone tries to assign a probability to a single case rather than to a type of case, whether that's insurance underwriting, weather forecasting, or a jury weighing statistical evidence about one particular defendant. In every version, there is no obvious principled place to stop narrowing, or widening, the class of "similar" cases before computing a frequency, and different choices don't just add precision, they yield substantively different numbers. My roommate, who works with statistics far more than I do, thinks this is a non-issue in practice: you use the narrowest class for which the data are still numerous enough to give a stable estimate, and that number just is the answer. That feels to me like a practical compromise rather than a principled resolution, and I increasingly suspect there may be no fact of the matter about a single individual's "true" probability at all, independent of whatever reference group you happen to pick. I would be grateful for help thinking through whether my roommate's stopping rule genuinely solves the underlying problem or merely papers over it, and, more broadly, whether a single-case probability claim like the one about my uncle's risk can be made sense of at all.
