In a reading group on classical Indian epistemology, we've been working through the opening sūtras of Gautama's Nyāya Sūtra, and I've gotten stuck on the definition of perception (pratyakṣa) at NS 1.1.4 -- the first and, for Naiyāyikas, most fundamental of the four pramāṇas (pratyakṣa, anumāna, upamāna, śabda). In transliteration the sūtra reads: "indriyārthasannikarṣotpannaṃ jñānam avyapadeśyam avyabhicāri vyavasāyātmakaṃ pratyakṣam," standardly rendered along the lines of "perception is that cognition which arises from the contact of a sense organ with its object, which is non-verbal, non-deviating, and of the nature of ascertainment."

What I can't get to hang together is how the second and fourth qualifiers are supposed to sit with each other. "Avyapadeśya" (non-verbal, not nameable, inexpressible-in-words) seems to push perception toward something pre-conceptual -- a bare sensory given, prior to any classification -- while "vyavasāyātmaka" (determinate, of the nature of settled ascertainment) seems to require exactly the qualificative, judgment-like structure ("this is a cow," "that is white") that Naiyāyikas elsewhere treat as already conceptualized and arguably word-involving. Vātsyāyana's Bhāṣya glosses "avyapadeśya" as excluding cognition that is really testimonial or name-derived rather than excluding conceptual content as such, and later authors typically reach for the nirvikalpaka/savikalpaka (indeterminate/determinate) distinction to relieve the pressure. But I can't tell whether that distinction is genuinely doing work already implicit in the sūtra, or whether it's a later graft onto a problem Gautama didn't yet have the apparatus to pose.

Could you help me work through the sūtra: what job each of the three qualifiers (avyapadeśya, avyabhicāri, vyavasāyātmaka) is doing and which defect in cognition each is meant to rule out; why avyapadeśya and vyavasāyātmaka look like they're pulling in different directions; and how much weight the standard nirvikalpaka/savikalpaka move can really bear as a resolution, rather than just relabeling the problem? I'd also be interested in your own view of whether the tension points to something left genuinely unresolved in Nyāya's account of how unconceptualized sensory contact with an object is supposed to yield the fully determinate, qualified cognitions that perception, on this school's realist picture, is meant to deliver.
