So this actually happened in my building and it turned into a whole thing at a bbq last weekend. Our HOA bylaws have a rule that goes something like this (paraphrasing from memory but its pretty close): "No unit may be leased or rented for a period of less than thirty (30) consecutive days. This restriction does not apply to a family member of the owner staying without charge." One of my neighbors, lets call her Nadia, has been hosting people through Airbnb for like 3-5 night stays whenever she travels, but instead of calling it rent she charges a $50-a-night "cleaning and utility fee." Someone complained to the board about it, which kicked off a whole argument between two other neighbors of mine, Teresa and Mike, that I just sat there listening to (they weren't even mad at each other, just really dug in).

Teresa's take: obviously she's breaking the rule. "Leased or rented" doesn't require the word "rent" to show up anywhere - if money changes hands for exclusive use of the unit for a few nights, that's renting in plain English, so the 30-day minimum kicks in no matter what she calls the fee. Mike said hold on: the bylaw itself contrasts "leased or rented" (for charge) with family "staying without charge," so what the board actually cares about is money for occupancy - and a cleaning/utility fee is reimbursement for costs, not payment for occupancy, otherwise charging a regular houseguest to split the water bill would count as "renting" your guest room, which is clearly not what the rule means. Teresa's comeback: if a "$50-a-night cleaning fee" just happens to scale exactly with the length of stay and roughly matches market rent for a room, calling it a fee instead of rent is exactly the kind of relabeling that would let anyone dodge the rule - and the whole reason buildings have 30-day minimums in the first place is to stop the turnover/strangers/parking headaches of short-term rentals, which is exactly what Nadia's setup causes no matter what the invoice says. Mike's response was that "it causes the problem the rule was probably about" is a risky standard, because it means whoever enforces the rule gets to punish anything that smells rental-ish even without the specific thing the rule is actually about - someone agreeing to pay for occupancy - and if the board wanted to ban all paid hosting they could've just written that instead of picking a specific 30-day/lease-or-rent trigger. Teresa's last word was that a clear-cut rule which can be beaten just by cleverly wording your invoice isn't really clear-cut at all, it just moves the discretion to whoever's better at labeling things, which seems worse for people trying to follow the rule in good faith, not better.

I keep thinking this is basically the same kind of argument as that "read the exact words of a law vs. read what it was supposed to accomplish" thing I've seen mentioned in articles about judges, but I don't actually know the real terms for any of this and would love an actual breakdown.
