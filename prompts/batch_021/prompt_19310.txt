I'm a PhD student in philosophy currently TAing an advanced seminar built around Jaegwon Kim's *Mind in a Physical World*; we've just spent two sessions working through the causal exclusion argument, and I'm putting together a handout for next week's discussion section on the standard non-reductivist replies. My worry is that several students have started treating "multiple realizability" as a kind of magic word that refutes Kim outright, without seeing why a defender of the exclusion argument would find that reply too quick.

Could you write a dialogue, set at the tail end of the reading group after everyone else has left, between two philosophers of mind who've just finished the same two chapters? One should voice something close to Kim's own considered position: that the conjunction of causal closure and the exclusion principle forces a non-reductive physicalist to choose between local/functional reduction (which Kim is willing to grant for causally individuated properties like belief and pain) and epiphenomenalism (which he accepts for qualia, on the grounds that phenomenal properties resist functionalization). The other should be a committed non-reductive physicalist trying to block that dilemma — but rather than resting on multiple realizability by itself, have her run the proportionality/determinable-determinate strategy associated with Stephen Yablo, on which a mental property can be a genuine, "proportional" cause of a physical effect precisely because it stands to its physical realizer as a determinable stands to a determinate. I want the Kim-defender to have a real comeback to that move rather than simply restating closure and exclusion — in particular, he should press on whether the determinable/determinate maneuver actually dissolves the overdetermination worry or just redescribes it one level up. Let the dialogue end without a manufactured winner, with both characters instead converging on the deeper question the disagreement has been tracking all along: whether causation is fundamentally a matter of producing or generating an effect, or simply a matter of making a systematic difference to it.

Since this is for readers who've already sat through the primary text, please skip any textbook throat-clearing — no need to define "supervenience" or spell out the exclusion principle for the reader's benefit; I'd rather their word choice and the moves they reach for do the work of showing they know the material cold.
