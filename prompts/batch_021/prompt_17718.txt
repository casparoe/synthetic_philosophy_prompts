Question: "Reconstruct Gödel's modal ontological argument in Dana Scott's formulation, stating its key axioms and definitions and showing how they yield the conclusion that a God-like being necessarily exists. Then explain the modal collapse objection to the argument, and briefly assess whether it is fatal to Gödel's proof."

Student's answer: "Gödel's argument rests on the primitive notion of a positive property. Axiom 1 says that for any property, exactly one of it and its negation is positive; Axiom 2 says positive properties are closed under strict entailment; Axiom 3 says that being God-like — having all and only the positive properties — is itself a positive property; Axiom 4 says positive properties are positive of necessity, not just as a matter of fact; and Axiom 5 says that necessary existence, understood via the notion of an essence, is itself a positive property. From Axioms 1 and 2 one shows that the positive properties are jointly compossible, so it is possible that something is God-like. Using the essence definition, one then shows that anything God-like has God-likeness as its essence, and Axiom 5 delivers necessary existence for any God-like thing. Since it is possible that something is God-like, and being God-like necessitates necessary existence, it follows that it's possible that necessarily something is God-like; and by the Barcan formula this collapses to its being necessary, and hence actual, that something is God-like.

The chief worry is the modal collapse objection pressed by Alvin Plantinga: the very same axioms that secure God's necessary existence also entail that any true proposition is necessarily true, erasing the distinction between contingent and necessary truth. Since almost everyone thinks some truths — about the weather, say, or who won a given election — are merely contingent, this is a serious cost, and it is usually taken to show that at least one of the axioms, most likely Axiom 2 or the definition of essence, must be rejected or revised, as Anderson later tried to do. So while the argument is valid given its premises, I don't think it succeeds in proving that God exists."
