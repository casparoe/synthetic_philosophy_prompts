I teach this one-night-a-week elective at the university extension school -- half literature seminar, half "why is illness represented the way it is" philosophy class -- and before we get into the actual novels (Ivan Ilyich, The Magic Mountain, that kind of thing) I always make everyone read a chunk of philosophy of medicine first, so they stop treating "disease" like some neutral, pre-theoretical fact of nature. This term's short-response question was: "Explain the difference between naturalist and normativist accounts of disease, using Christopher Boorse's biostatistical theory and Lennart Nordenfelt's welfare theory as your main examples. Then briefly assess whether Boorse's account can be defended against the objection that his 'statistical normality' standard smuggles in a value judgment about what counts as normal."

Here's one student's answer, copied exactly (so any typos are their's, not mine): "Naturalists about disease, like Boorse, think you can define disease without bringing in values at all. On his biostatistical theory, something counts as diseased when a part or process is functioning at a statistically subnormal level for members of the organism's reference class (grouped by age and sex), where 'function' means the part's typical contribution to survival and reproduction. Since this is just a statistical, biological matter, Boorse treats 'disease' and 'illness' as interchangeable labels for the same underlying fact -- whether you're diseased isn't about how you feel or whether you can work, it's simply a fact about your statistics relative to your reference class. Normativists, like Nordenfelt, reject this picture. On Nordenfelt's welfare theory, health is the ability, in standard circumstances, to realize one's vital goals, so whether someone counts as healthy depends on their own goals and welfare, not just species-wide averages -- which is why a normativist would say biological facts don't really matter to whether something counts as a disease, what matters is purely whether we, or the person themselves, disvalue the condition. As for the objection that Boorse's statistical-normality standard smuggles in a value judgment, I don't think it succeeds, because picking a reference class by age and sex is a straightforward empirical matter, the same kind of grouping any biological study would use, so there's no hidden value judgment doing real work there."
