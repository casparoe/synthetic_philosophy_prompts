Ok, this has been bugging me for ages. I get how Garrabrant et al's logical induction paper works mechanically -- the market of computable traders betting on logical sentences, prices updating as more theorems get proven, the whole 'no trader can exploit the market forever' criterion -- but I don't think I actually have gut-level intuition for why that counts as 'solving' the logical omniscience problem, rather than just being a clever way to approximate omniscience faster. Old-school epistemic logic ppl (Hintikka etc) handled bounded agents by giving them 'impossible possible worlds' to consider -- worlds where the normal laws of logical consequence can fail -- so an agent's ignorance of some entailment just means they haven't ruled out one of those world yet. Can you lay out what's actually different about what logical induction is doing? Like, is it a genuine dissolution of the philosophical puzzle (how a bounded agent can have reasonable, non-omniscient credences about math/logic), or does it just quietly swap the question for 'what's approximable in the limit by efficient traders'? Not after a textbook definition of logical omniscience, more curious where the real disagreement actually is.
