Two friends of mine, Derek and Naomi, got into a disagreement at dinner last week that I haven't been able to settle, and I'd be curious what you make of it. Years ago in college, Derek promised his then-close friend Felix, seriously and not as a joke, that whenever Felix eventually got married, Derek would be his best man; Felix has brought it up fondly more than once since. The two have since drifted apart — they haven't spoken in over a year — and Derek says the friendship that motivated the promise is simply gone. With Felix's wedding two months away, Derek announced over dinner that he plans to email Felix and back out. Naomi pushed back right away, and their exchange, as best I can reconstruct it, went as follows. (It reminded me of a distinction I once came across in a philosophy class, between reasons that depend on what a person already cares about and reasons that are supposed to hold no matter what a person cares about — though I never got clear on which side is correct, if either.)

(1) Derek said a reason to do something has to connect to what you actually care about, and since he feels nothing for Felix anymore — no warmth, no loyalty, no residual pull — there is no reason left for him to go through with it. The promise cannot manufacture a reason out of nothing once the thing that would have made good on it, his caring, is gone.

(2) Naomi answered that this gets promising backwards: the whole point of a promise is to bind you regardless of how you later feel, since people mostly need promises for exactly the situations where their motivation might not last. On her view, the commitment itself is the reason, and it doesn't evaporate just because Derek's feelings did.

(3) Derek replied that this proves too much: you can say he should still care, or that he's a worse friend for having let things die, without any of that showing that some reason was sitting there all along, independent of anything in him, ready to move him. Blaming him for the kind of person he's become is one thing; claiming he already has a reason to fly out and give a speech is another.

(4) Naomi said that distinction is suspicious, because we don't actually treat reminders addressed to indifferent promise-breakers as pointless in the way Derek's picture implies — we think reminding someone what they promised can be exactly the right way to move them, which only makes sense if there is already a reason there for them to be reminded of. And if simply letting a commitment die inside you is enough to cancel it, that rewards exactly the wrong thing: the more thoroughly someone manages to stop caring, the less they end up owing anyone.

Could you go through the exchange move by move, saying who has the better of it at each step and why, and flag anywhere the two of them are talking past each other or missing a consideration that would actually help settle things? Please close with an overall verdict on whether Derek's or Naomi's underlying view of reasons is more defensible.
