I've been part of a small reading group that meets every other week to work through papers and posts on decision theory and longtermist ethics — the sort of material that circulates on the EA Forum and LessWrong. Two meetings ago someone presented a summary of evidential cooperation in large worlds (ECL): the idea, traceable to Caspar Oesterheld's work on what he called "multiverse-wide superrationality" and developed further at the Center on Long-Term Risk, that if the universe (or multiverse) is large or infinite, it contains vast numbers of agents who are causally cut off from us but whose decision procedures nonetheless correlate with ours, and that we should therefore cooperate with the ones whose values differ from ours — helping their causes when doing so is cheap for us — because our choosing to cooperate is evidence that they are simultaneously reciprocating on our values, elsewhere, with no communication or causal contact involved at any point. Two regulars, Marcus and Elena, kept arguing about this long after the rest of us had moved on to refills, and I still can't decide who came out ahead.

(1) Marcus opened by insisting this is a genuine, free-standing decision-theoretic reason to cooperate, not just a moral exhortation dressed up in jargon: structurally it's the same move as cooperating with your psychological twin in a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma, just scaled up to however many correlated-but-differently-valued agents a large world contains. (2) Elena objected that the whole argument only pays out for an agent whose utility function already ranges over parts of the universe it can never observe or affect; an ordinary, indexical agent who just wants good outcomes in its own causal future gets nothing from ECL, so the "new" argument is available only to people who've already accepted a fairly exotic premise — and once you've accepted that premise, you've already granted the agent-neutral, cosmos-spanning kind of caring that's doing the real work, with the correlated-decision-making machinery just riding along for free. (3) Marcus answered by distinguishing non-indexical caring from full-blown impartiality across persons: a consequentialist obsessed with maximizing paperclips throughout the universe, including regions it will never see, still benefits from ECL despite caring nothing for anyone's welfare, so ECL's real requirement is just that your utility function be well-defined over a big universe, not that you be any kind of utilitarian. (4) Elena granted the correction but pressed that this only narrows the target: actual human values, including most self-identified effective altruists' revealed priorities, are far more bounded than that, so ECL is a conditional theorem almost nobody actually satisfies the antecedent of, and she suspected people find the antecedent congenial mainly because they already like the conclusion. (5) Marcus called that an ad hominem and argued that brute indexical caring — treating your own causal future as special just because it's yours — is itself hard to defend once you take seriously that a large or infinite universe (or a many-worlds cosmology) already contains causally disconnected regions physically identical to your own; rejecting indexicality, he said, isn't a strange add-on but close to where anthropic humility leads anyone who follows it through. (6) Elena's last word was that if that anti-indexical argument works on its own, it already delivers most of what ECL gets credit for — a reason to care about distant, causally disconnected value — without needing any correlated-decision-making story at all, which left her wondering whether ECL names a mechanism for gains from trade that's genuinely distinct from ordinary expected-value reasoning under moral uncertainty, or just relabels it. Neither budged, and the group dispersed before it got resolved.

Could you go through this turn by turn and adjudicate it — for each of the six numbered moves, say who has the better of it, or whether it's a wash, and why? I'd especially like you to flag any point where one of them seems to be resting on a premise the other never actually got the chance to challenge, and then close with your own answer to the question Elena leaves hanging at the end: is ECL, properly understood, a genuinely distinct source of gains from trade, or does it collapse into ordinary hedging under moral uncertainty once you grant the non-indexical utility function it needs in order to run at all?
