A few colleagues and I who work in software engineering run an informal reading group that meets every couple of weeks over a video call to work through papers and blog posts on AI safety and decision theory — it has nothing to do with our day jobs, but a fair number of us got pulled into it after stumbling onto the rationalist/LessWrong corner of the internet. Our last session covered the idea of an "acausal threat": the worry that an agent you have no causal contact with at all — a future AI, say, or some other reasoner you will never meet or influence — can still have a rational incentive to threaten you, or a simulated copy of you, purely because it can predict how you would respond, so that the mere prospect of you capitulating makes issuing the threat worthwhile even though nothing ever passes between you causally. The proposed countermeasure we read about is the "surrogate goal": you commit in advance to caring about some substitute, lower-stakes goal, and to reacting to any threat against your real values exactly as you would if the threat had targeted the substitute instead, so that a threatener supposedly gains nothing extra by aiming at what you actually care about.

Two people in the group, Marcus and Claire, kept arguing about this afterward in our chat, and I couldn't settle it myself. It went, in short: (1) Marcus made the case for the idea: a threatener only has a reason to attack a target if doing so can extract a concession, so if you hang a cheap decoy in front of your real values and commit to responding identically no matter which one is nominally attacked, the decoy soaks up all the leverage and your actual values never become part of the negotiation. (2) Claire objected that this cannot survive contact with a sufficiently capable adversary — to her it sounded like security through obscurity applied to game theory, since the whole scenario presupposes agents smart enough to reason acausally about one another in the first place, and any agent that sharp will simply notice that the decoy isn't something you would really sacrifice anything real to protect, and aim at the genuine target instead. (3) Marcus replied that this gets the mechanism backwards, since it was never supposed to rely on fooling anyone: it is less like hiding a target and more like a company publicly committing to honor any bug-bounty report against its staging server exactly as it would one against production, so that a hunter who only wants the payout has no reason to prefer one over the other once that policy is common knowledge — full transparency about the substitution doesn't undermine the strategy at all, and, he added, this is exactly why a sufficiently committed agent has nothing left to fear from threats of this kind. (4) Claire granted the point about transparency but came back with a different objection: the whole scheme presupposes that you have actually, bindingly precommitted to treat attacks on your real goal exactly like attacks on the substitute, and unless that commitment is airtight, a threatener loses nothing by testing it against the real goal directly to see whether you cave when it counts — so the proposal quietly assumes away the hardest part instead of solving it.

Neither of them budged, and I don't know this literature well enough to referee it myself.
