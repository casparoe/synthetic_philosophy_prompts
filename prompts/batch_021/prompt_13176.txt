I'm on the incident-review board at the software company I work for, and we just finished writing up a nasty one: a multi-hour outage that kicked off when an on-call engineer got paged about weird traffic at 2am and snoozed the alert for 45 minutes, because that particular alert had cried wolf all week. Except once you actually dig into it, that's not the only place things could've gone differently -- a seperate team had a ticket open for three months flagging that the service in question had no rate limiting, and it just never got prioritized; the reason it never got prioritized is that the roadmap review last quarter explicitly deprioritized "hardening" work in favor of a launch deadline; and the reason the on-call engineer was covering three services solo that night instead of splitting the load is a hiring freeze from earlier this year. For literally every one of those, it's true that if that person or team had done the thing they didn't do, the outage probably doesn't happen, or is a lot smaller -- so by a plain "but for this, it wouldn't have happened" test, they're all causes.

The postmortem doc is supposed to name actual root causes / contributing factors, not just list everyone who touched the situation, so it can drive real follow-up work -- and the board is stuck on where to stop. Some people want to stop at the on-call engineer's snooze since that's the proximate thing; others think that's just scapegoating someone for decisions made way above their pay grade, and want the roadmap call, or even the hiring freeze, named as the real root cause -- but then someone reasonably points out that if we're willing to go back that far, why stop there and not blame whoever approved the budget behind the freeze, and so on forever. My partner, who's a lawyer, says this is basically the same fight they have over proximate cause, which did not make me feel better. A couple of us are also uneasy that wherever we end up drawing the line is going to suspiciously match whoever we already felt comfortable holding responsible before we started, which makes me wonder whether we're tracking causes at all at that point, versus just dressing up our blame calls in causal-sounding language after the fact.

Could you draft the actual memo I'd bring to the board before we meet again next week -- one that proposes a real method for sorting which of these omissions count as causes for the writeup versus mere background conditions, applies it to say something concrete about this specific incident, and directly answers that last worry instead of dodging it. We run these reviews often enough that I want an actual method out of this, not just a one-off verdict we'll forget about by the next incident.
