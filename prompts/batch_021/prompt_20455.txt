A couple of weekends ago I went down a Wikipedia rabbit hole that started at the history of pi and ended, somewhat to my surprise, at Thomas Hobbes's decades-long feud with the mathematician John Wallis over Hobbes's claimed proof that he had squared the circle. I'm a software engineer with no real philosophy background, so what I knew about Hobbes going in was pretty much limited to "nasty, brutish, and short," and I had no idea he'd spent roughly the last quarter-century of his life insisting, against the repeated and increasingly exasperated objections of Oxford's Savilian Professor of Geometry, that he'd solved a problem that later turned out to be provably unsolvable with compass and straightedge.

What I can't stop thinking about is how badly this fits with Hobbes's own political philosophy. The whole point of the sovereign in Leviathan is that people can't be trusted to adjudicate their own disputes: left alone, everyone keeps insisting they're the reasonable one, so peace requires handing final say to a sovereign whose ruling settles the matter simply because it is the sovereign's, not because it has been shown to be correct. But when actual mathematicians kept demonstrating that his construction was wrong, Hobbes just kept republishing it and doubling down, as though sheer force of insistence ought to work on a circle the same way it works on a commonwealth. It doesn't, and it very visibly didn't.

Could you write me a short joke built on exactly that mismatch — between Hobbes's argument that only an authority with the final word can ever end a dispute, and his own refusal to accept that no such authority existed for him in geometry? I'd like the punchline to depend on actually knowing both pieces of this (the sovereignty argument and the circle-squaring saga) rather than being a generic "philosopher who turned out to be bad at math" joke.
