I'm taking a seminar this term on epistemic democracy, where several of our readings (Estlund, List and Goodin) treat the Condorcet Jury Theorem as a formal backbone for the claim that collective, majoritarian decision-making has a genuine epistemic advantage over reliance on a single expert. Our more recent sessions, though, have focused on just how fragile that backbone turns out to be once you look closely: what happens once you allow voter competence to vary rather than assuming a uniform threshold across the panel (Grofman, Owen and Feld's work on unequal competence), and Austen-Smith and Banks's result that rational, strategic jurors generally have no incentive to vote "sincerely" on their own private information in the first place, which undercuts the theorem's use as a justification for majority rule among real decision-making bodies.

Outside of class, I am the student representative on my university's Academic Integrity Board, a three-member panel (two faculty, one student) that deliberates together on each case before reaching a verdict of "violation" or "no violation" by majority vote. At this Wednesday's meeting, the Dean of Students will propose expanding every panel to nine members; her one-page rationale rests entirely on an informal statement of the theorem: since each panelist is more likely than not to reach the correct verdict, she argues, a larger panel must — in her words, "by the Condorcet Jury Theorem" — be more accurate. Having just spent weeks on exactly this theorem, I think the argument skips over a great deal: our panels deliberate before voting rather than each member forming an independent judgment from a private signal, no one has measured whether panelist competence actually exceeds one-half or how much it might vary between, say, a first-time student panelist and a faculty member who has heard dozens of cases, and there is no consideration of whether panelists might start voting strategically once they know they are one of nine rather than one of three.

Could you draft the short memo I plan to circulate to the Board before that meeting? It should give a clear, specific recommendation on the Dean's proposal — adopt it, reject it, or amend it, and if the latter, how — explained in terms my fellow panelists, most of whom have no background in formal theory, can actually follow, and it should still take seriously the genuine insight behind her proposal: that pooling more independent judgment really should, all else equal, make the Board more reliable.
