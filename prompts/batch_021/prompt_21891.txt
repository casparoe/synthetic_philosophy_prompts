I've been mulling over the puzzle philosophers refer to as "aesthetic testimony," and I'm not convinced it deserves to be treated as a hard problem. As I understand it, the worry is that one cannot gain genuine aesthetic knowledge merely by taking someone else's word that, say, a poem is beautifully crafted or a symphony is deeply moving. But the explanation for this seems straightforward to me: aesthetic verdicts are ultimately just subjective reactions rather than facts about the object itself, so there is no fact there for anyone's testimony to transmit in the first place. On this view, believing a film is a masterpiece purely because a friend told me so should be no different, epistemically, from believing that pistachio is their favorite flavor of ice cream because they told me so. Given this, why do philosophers keep writing about aesthetic testimony as though it were a live and difficult problem, rather than something that simply dissolves once you accept that taste is subjective?
