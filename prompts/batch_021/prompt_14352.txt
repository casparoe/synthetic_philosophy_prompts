I'm taking a graduate seminar on formal epistemology this term, and this week's readings paired Aumann's 1976 "Agreeing to Disagree" paper with the later Geanakoplos–Polemarchakis result on repeated exchange of opinions. I can recite the headline conclusion without trouble—two Bayesians who share a common prior cannot have it be common knowledge that their posteriors on some event differ—but a classmate and I spent an hour after class arguing about why the underlying machinery forces exact equality rather than mere convergence, and neither of us came away satisfied.

The first thing I can't pin down is why common knowledge specifically is doing the work in the static theorem, rather than something weaker like mutual knowledge (I know your posterior, you know mine, and maybe we each know that the other knows). I understand that common knowledge lets you locate an event on which both people's posteriors are constant, and that summing the shared prior over that event in two different ways is what ultimately equates the two numbers, but I can't reconstruct why that summing trick breaks down if you only have finitely many levels of "I know that you know that I know," rather than the full infinite hierarchy. Second, and relatedly, I'm stuck on the dynamic story: if two people just alternate announcing a single real number—their current posterior—without ever revealing the private evidence behind it, why must this process terminate, in finitely many rounds over a finite state space, in the two numbers becoming exactly equal, rather than trailing off into ever-smaller but permanent disagreement? It feels like each bare announcement is somehow carrying far more information than the number itself.

Could you give a mechanism-level account of both points—what is actually happening to the two agents' information as the common-knowledge hierarchy is built, or as the announcements accumulate—rather than just restating the theorems in their usual abstract form? I'd also be curious whether, on your account, the dynamic result is basically the static one unfolding over time, or whether it depends on some genuinely separate idea.
