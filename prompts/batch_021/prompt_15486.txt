For a dissertation chapter on the foundations of Bayesian epistemology, I've been reconstructing the classic Dutch book argument for probabilism, and one feature of its logical structure keeps bothering me in a way I haven't seen fully resolved. The argument shows that if my credences violate the probability axioms, a bookie who knows those credences can offer me a set of bets — each one fair or favorable by my own lights — that jointly guarantee a loss no matter what happens. But it's a long way from "exploitable by a suitably positioned bookie" to "irrational as a state of belief": the vulnerability looks like a fact about my practical exposure to a certain kind of adversary, not obviously a defect in the epistemic standing of my beliefs. There's also something uncomfortably close to question-begging in identifying degrees of belief with fair betting quotients in the first place, since that identification already imports the additive, expectation-like structure the argument is supposed to be establishing. Could you lay out the most serious attempts in the literature to bridge this pragmatic/epistemic gap — to "depragmatize" the argument so that it bears on the rationality of belief rather than merely on prudence — and give me your own assessment of whether any of them actually succeed, or whether Dutch book arguments are best understood as doing something else entirely, such as expressing a structural coherence constraint dressed up in the language of betting rather than a genuine bridge from prudence to rationality?
