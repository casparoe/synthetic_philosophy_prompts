In a graduate reading group on comparative epistemology, we recently spent a session on Jain anekantavada: the doctrine that any finite object has infinitely many aspects, so that no single standpoint, or naya, can ever capture more than part of the truth about it. The companion discipline, syadvada, relativizes any claim about the object to a particular substance, place, time, and mode, prefixing it with "syat" ("in some respect") and running it through the traditional sevenfold scheme — syat asti, syat nasti, syat asti-nasti (predicating both, but successively), syat avaktavyam (indescribable, when both are meant at once), and the remaining combinations — rather than asserting anything about it unconditionally. The session went well, but it left two participants, Daniel and Sofia, in a disagreement that has continued by email ever since and that neither will concede.

(1) Daniel opened by arguing that anekantavada refutes itself: the claim that no standpoint captures the whole truth is itself put forward as the whole, unconditional truth about standpoints, so the doctrine instantiates exactly the one-sidedness (ekantavada) it condemns — a modern-dress version of an old charge that Vedantin and Buddhist critics pressed against the Jains. (2) Sofia replied that this conflates two different things: anekanta is a thesis about objects — that they have infinitely many aspects — not a semantic ban on unconditional assertion, so there is nothing self-defeating about stating it flatly. (3) Daniel came back that this dodges the sharper case, which is syadvada itself: is the instruction to qualify every claim with "syat" issued unconditionally, or only "in some respect"? If unconditionally, the Jains have exactly one dogma exempted from their own rule; if only conditionally, the rule is optional and the whole apparatus loses its motivation. (4) Sofia answered that the syat-rule is a second-order rule about what first-order, naya-bound claims owe us, not one more naya on a par with "the jar exists," and framing rules of that kind needn't apply to themselves the way object-level claims do — "every generalization is revisable" is not itself the sort of generalization it is talking about. (5) Daniel closed by saying that a rule which wants an exemption owes an argument for it, not just a label, and that until Sofia can explain why syadvada specifically earns one, he sees no principled reason to grant it.
