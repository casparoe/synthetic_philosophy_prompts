I am drafting a novel built around a multiverse conceit, and before committing to its internal metaphysics I would welcome comments on the reasoning below, which I have set down as notes to myself rather than as finished prose.

The novel follows a woman, Elena, across several branching timelines, and turns on a scene in which two of her variants, drawn from worlds that diverged decades earlier, meet face to face. My instinct has been to borrow David Lewis's counterpart theory rather than treat the variants as strictly one woman distributed across worlds: it lets me say that the Elena who became a violinist and the Elena who became a surgeon are distinct individuals who merely resemble one another and share a common past, which is close to the slightly uncanny, family-resemblance quality I want that scene to have. Three worries have surfaced, though, as I have tried to make the book's rules consistent.

First, there is something like Kripke's objection to Lewis by way of the Humphrey case: when my Elena laments the surgeon's life "she" might have had, both the grammar and the feeling suggest she is talking about a possibility for herself, not the fate of a merely similar stranger. Counterpart theory seems to get the logic of that lament exactly backwards.

Second, I want the degree of divergence between variants to be continuous and gradable, so that some share every scar and habit while others share almost nothing after the first year, and this feels like it walks straight into Chisholm's paradox: if cross-world sameness, or counterparthood, is fixed by a chain of small resemblances, I do not see what stops that chain from linking Elena, one small step at a time, to someone the book otherwise wants to treat as an entirely different character.

Third, I briefly considered asserting strict transworld identity instead, until I recalled Lewis's own worry about accidental intrinsics: if it is truly one woman, numerically speaking, then her having straight hair in one branch and cropped, dyed hair in another seems to force a single person to bear incompatible intrinsic properties outright, unless I quietly relativize her properties to worlds, which looks like counterpart theory smuggled back in under another name.

Have I represented these three problems fairly, and is my unease about them warranted? Given that I need graded, variable divergence and at least one scene of variants comparing notes on a shared life, which framework, or some other option I have not considered, such as stage theory or Lewis's own move to context-dependent counterpart relations, would actually give the novel a defensible and interesting metaphysics, and where, if anywhere, do you think I am over-engineering a puzzle that the reader will never consciously notice?
