I'm a lay member of the policy committee that oversees the predictive-testing program at our regional clinical genetics service. I ended up in this role after my own family went through counseling and testing for Huntington's disease, and because I don't sit clearly in either of the camps the committee has split into, the chair has asked me to draft the recommendation myself rather than let either side write it. For as long as the program has existed, people have been allowed to have their blood drawn and analyzed without ever finding out the result, provided they say so in advance; this opt-out has only ever applied to conditions like Huntington's, where nothing currently changes the course of the illness, since we already return results by default whenever there is a preventive step to take, as with the hereditary cancer syndromes we also test for. A couple of the geneticists on the committee now want to abolish the opt-out entirely. Their case rests on something I had never heard of until I looked it up out of curiosity: apparently there is a genuine result in decision theory, called Good's theorem, to the effect that a rational person, reasoning in advance, can never expect to do worse on average by consulting a piece of free evidence before acting than by declining to look at it. On their reading, honoring a request not to know is simply indulging a preference that nobody could defend once they saw the mathematics clearly. Our patient advocate on the committee thinks this whole line of reasoning is beside the point for a disease nothing can be done about, and that no theorem gets to tell a person she is making a mistake by choosing not to know.

Could you draft the short written opinion I will circulate before next month's meeting? It should recommend, concretely, whether we keep, narrow, or drop the opt-out for conditions like Huntington's where no treatment or preventive action is available, together with how any change would actually be introduced and by when. I have a nagging sense that the geneticists are asking the theorem to settle a question it was never built to answer, but I cannot yet say exactly where their argument stops working, and I would like that traced through properly rather than waved away by simply asserting that autonomy outranks decision theory. Please also give their position its strongest form, since they are not being careless people, and I do not want to hand the committee an opinion that only wins because it never met their real argument.
