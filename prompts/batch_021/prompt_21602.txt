I'm a novelist, and I've been asked to give the toast at the wedding of two close friends from my MFA years: Marcus, who spent those same years finishing a philosophy PhD at the same university, and Priya, a high-school biology teacher with no formal training in decision theory who has nonetheless absorbed an alarming amount of it by osmosis. Marcus's standing party trick, the one our old cohort still asks him to perform, is walking people through Kavka's toxin puzzle -- whether you can sincerely intend tonight to drink a foul but harmless toxin tomorrow, for a reward you will already have banked by the time drinking it would cost you anything. Priya can lay out the setup as cleanly as any of his old students. The wedding will have exactly the audience you'd predict from that: half philosophy department, listening for whether I get the puzzle right, half family and childhood friends who have never heard of it and don't need to.

I want to build the toast around the puzzle, but the obvious route in is precisely the thing I can't say. Its easy reading -- that a rational person can't really commit now to something they will have no reason to carry out once the reward is already secured -- is exactly the wrong implication to leave hanging over two people getting married, and I don't want a single line that anyone could later replay as "he compared their vows to a bribe for a promise nobody expects you to keep." What I'm after is the harder and truer thing underneath that reading: the actual philosophical difficulty the puzzle raises about intention and follow-through, and what the more serious responses to it suggest about the kind of person whose commitments hold even once the original reason for making them has stopped mattering from one moment to the next.

Could you write me that toast? Aim for something that runs three to four minutes read aloud, gives the philosophers in the room the satisfaction of watching me handle the puzzle correctly, and never needs the phrase "decision theory" to land for Priya's side of the family.
