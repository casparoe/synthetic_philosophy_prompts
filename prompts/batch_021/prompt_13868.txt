My friend Theo has spent the last several months betting on a pretty obscure corner of the sports betting market, using a model he built himself, which — going by the results so far — wins around 60% of the time whenever he can get even odds on it. Deciding how much of their shared "betting fund" to stake on each one has become a running argument between him and his housemate Naomi, who studied engineering and apparently remembers more of her stats than he does. I keep getting pulled in as the tie-breaker even though I don't really follow the math myself, so I'm hoping you can actually settle it.

Their argument goes something like this. (1) Theo's position is that since each bet is positive expected value, they should be staking a big chunk of the fund each time — his rule of thumb is at least half — because a bet that makes you money on average makes you more money on average the bigger you size it, so playing it "safe" is just voluntarily giving up winnings. (2) Naomi says this is backwards for something they're going to repeat over and over out of the same pot: stake too high a fraction, and the loosing streaks that are bound to happen eventually (this bet loses 40% of the time, remember) will crater the fund, and after that they're betting a percentage of a much smaller pile, so a later winning streak doesn't make up for it the way Theo imagines. She says there's a specific, much smaller fraction — something like a fifth of the pot, by her math — that actually grows the fund fastest over time, and going well above that, the way Theo wants, makes them poorer in the long run even though every individual bet is still a good one. (3) Theo's comeback is that this can't be right, because expected value is, by definition, the thing you converge to on average over many repeats — so if bigger stakes mean higher expected value per bet, betting bigger has to mean more money in the long run too, not less. Either Naomi's "fifth of the pot" number is the fraction that maximizes what they'll actually end up with, in which case it should be telling them to go bigger rather than smaller, or it's maximizing something other than their money, in which case why should he care what it says? (4) Naomi's response is that his dilemma is a false one, because the stake that maximizes the expected size of the fund and the stake that maximizes what they'll typically, actually end up with are different questions with different answers here — almost all of that "expected value" comes from a thin slice of extremely lucky runs where they basically never lose, and once you're re-betting a fraction of a fund that can shrink as well as grow, those two things can point in opposite directions. Theo isn't sold even by this: his last word, so far, is that a sportsbook itself is taking on millions of similar bets across millions of bettors, and for an operation of that size the average outcome and the expected outcome basically are the same thing — which makes him suspect Naomi's "different kind of average" isn't some objective fact about the bet at all, just a technical-sounding way of describing how much risk she personally happens to be comfortable with.

Could you go through this move by move and say who's actually right at each stage — including the last exchange, which is honestly the one I'm least sure about myself? And if there's something important that both of them are missing entirely, I'd like to hear about that too.
