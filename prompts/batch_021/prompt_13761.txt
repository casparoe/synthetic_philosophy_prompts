I have been asked to give the toast at the wedding of two former doctoral students of mine, now colleagues at other institutions, who have been partners for nearly fifteen years and are marrying only now, a few months after the birth of their first child. Both wrote on Russell's moral philosophy under my supervision, and they have specifically asked that the toast engage honestly with *Marriage and Morals* (1929) rather than fall back on the usual one-line caricature of Russell as a free-love provocateur. They mean this quite precisely: several years into the relationship, on grounds they trace explicitly to Russell's argument that sexual jealousy is substantially conventional rather than a fixed biological given, and to his claim that the true social rationale of marriage lies in the rearing of children rather than in romantic or sexual exclusivity, they went through a long, openly non-monogamous period, before settling back into something more conventional once they decided to have a child. Most of the philosophers at the wedding know this history; almost none of the family does, and the couple wants it acknowledged rather than airbrushed, though they have also made very clear that they do not want a seminar.

The complication, which I mentioned at lunch to a colleague who found it much funnier than I did, is that Russell's own comparable real-time experiment -- conducted with his second wife, Dora, on principles close to the ones he was defending in the book even as he wrote it -- did not end well: the two children Dora had by another man during the marriage, and a jealousy that neither of them could quite argue away in practice however conventional it may have been in theory, were a large part of what ended the marriage a few years later. I do not want the toast to misrepresent Russell in order to flatter the couple, but I also do not want it to land, even obliquely, as an omen. Could you draft it for me? It should run to about five minutes read aloud; it needs to work for two audiences at once -- philosophers who will recognize exactly which parts of the book are being drawn on, and relatives who have never heard of it -- and it should close with the couple rather than with Russell.
