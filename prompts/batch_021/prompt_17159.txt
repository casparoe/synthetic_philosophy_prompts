In my philosophy of mind seminar, we've traced the mind-body problem from Descartes through the occasionalists before turning to contemporary physicalism, but one comparison the syllabus never quite draws out has been nagging at me. Jaegwon Kim's causal exclusion argument holds that non-reductive physicalism collapses into epiphenomenalism: given the causal closure of the physical domain and the rejection of systematic overdetermination, a mental property that merely supervenes on (rather than is identical to) the physical cause of some effect ends up excluded as a genuine cause by that physical base. Malebranche denied causal efficacy to every finite substance, on the grounds that a genuine cause must necessitate its effect through an intelligible connection that only an infinite will can supply — and, importantly, this denial was fully general, covering body-body interaction as much as mind-body interaction. Suppose Malebranche were confronted with Kim's argument today. Would he treat it as an unwitting, secularized rediscovery of the case for occasionalism, or would he object that Kim's closure premise — which treats physical-to-physical causation as unproblematic — is exactly the assumption his own arguments were meant to undercut? Please work through this using Malebranche's actual grounds for occasionalism, rather than resting on the surface-level resemblance between epiphenomenalism and occasionalism.
