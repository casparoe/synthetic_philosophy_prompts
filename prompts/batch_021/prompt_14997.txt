In my world history class, we spent only a single lesson on the Islamic Golden Age, but my teacher mentioned in passing that the theologian Al-Ghazālī accused the Aristotelian philosophers of incoherence, and that the Andalusian philosopher Averroes (Ibn Rushd) wrote an entire book responding to him. That offhand comment sent me looking for the actual texts, and I found translated excerpts of Al-Ghazālī's Tahafut al-Falasifa and of Averroes's reply, the Tahafut al-Tahafut. One disagreement between them especially caught my attention: in the famous seventeenth discussion, they debate whether fire burns cotton because of some necessary power built into fire itself, or whether the constant pairing of contact with fire and burning is really just God's habitual way of doing things, a pattern God could suspend, as supposedly happened with Abraham and the fire. I think I follow the basic shape of Al-Ghazālī's occasionalism and of Averroes's defense of real causal natures, but the secondary sources I have read do not even agree on how radical Al-Ghazālī's position is meant to be: some treat him as denying that fire has any causal power whatsoever, while others read him as making the narrower claim that the connection is merely not logically necessary, which would leave room for genuine, if contingent, natures after all. I am actually considering this as the topic for my IB Extended Essay, so I would like to be sure I understand the strongest version of each side before I commit to it.

Given all that, could you write a dialogue in which Al-Ghazālī and Averroes actually argue this out face to face? I know the real rebuttal was written decades after Al-Ghazālī died, so just imagine that the two of them are somehow able to sit down together. I would like both sides to be equally sharp: Averroes should press the worry that denying necessary connections makes any real scientific knowledge of causes impossible, while Al-Ghazālī should press the worry that granting fire, or anything other than God, genuine causal power compromises God's omnipotence and rules out miracles from the outset. Each of them should have to respond to the other's specific objections rather than simply restating his own position, and I would like the cotton-and-fire example and the Abraham miracle worked into the actual exchange rather than left as background color. Please end with the two of them at least clarifying exactly what they still disagree about once any purely verbal confusions have been cleared away, whether that turns out to be a dispute about what exists in nature, about what we can know, or about something else entirely.
