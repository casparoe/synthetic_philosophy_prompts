I spent about thirty-five years doing observational astrophysics -- mostly hunting for exoplanets in noisy radial-velocity data -- where Bayesian inference was just the water we swam in: you condition on the data, you update your posterior, and nobody in the lab ever really asked *why* credences have to obey the probability calculus in the first place. Now that I'm retired I've finally got time to sit down and read the philosophical foundations properly, and I've been stuck for a couple weeks now on the old Ramsey/de Finetti Dutch book argument. I get the basic move: if your degrees of belief don't satisfy the axioms of probability, a clever bookie can offer you a set of bets, each one fair by your own lights, that guarantees you loose money no matter what happens -- and apparently there's a converse proof too, that if your credences ARE coherent, no such guaranteed-loss book can ever be built against you, so the two properties are supposed to line up exactly.

Here's the bit that's been nagging me though: is this actually an argument about whats epistemically required of a rational believer, or is it just a pragmatic point about not losing your shirt, dressed up to look like something deeper? A retired colleague of mine (a statistician, very stubborn) insists the betting story is just a dramatic illustration of a purely logical inconsistency among your credences, on a par with believing P and not-P at the same time, and that the money is really beside the point. But I keep getting hung up on two things that push the other way. First, nobody is actually going to walk into my office with a book of bets, so why should the mere hypothetical possibility of a guaranteed loss tell me anything about whether my beliefs are rational. And second, actually noticing that some set of bets is a sure loser seems to require knowing every relevant logical and mathematical entailment among the propositions involved, which is a wildly unrealistic amount of logical omniscience to demand of an ordinary reasoner (or an ordinary bookie, for that matter).

Could you write me a dialogue between two equally sharp characters who disagree about exactly this? One thinks the Dutch book argument, all on its own, is enough to ground probabilism as a genuine norm of rational belief; the other thinks it only ever shows something pragmatic, or else it's quietly smuggling in the very notion of epistemic incoherence it's supposed to be deriving from scratch. I'd like the skeptic to press both of the worries above and actually force the defender to answer them instead of just re-running the textbook version of the argument, and I'd like the defender to land some real counterpunches back too, so it takes a few rounds before we see where they still genuinely disagree, rather than one side just folding in the first exchange.
