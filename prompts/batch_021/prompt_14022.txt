During a meeting of my high school's AI safety reading group, two members got into a disagreement about inverse reinforcement learning that the rest of us couldn't settle, and I'd like a neutral adjudication. Nora argued that IRL is a workable route to value alignment in principle: watch enough of a person's behavior, assume they are approximately rational, and the reward function that best rationalizes what they do is a reasonable proxy for what they actually want. Theo objected that this is underdetermined even granting approximate rationality, since any odd-looking behavior can be explained either as a rational agent pursuing an unusual reward or as an ordinary agent pursuing a normal reward badly, through bias or error, and behavior alone can't tell you which. Nora replied that this is just the underdetermination every empirical inference faces, and that we handle it the way science handles it generally: by preferring the simplest reward function consistent with the data, just as we prefer simple physical laws over needlessly complicated ones that fit the same observations. Theo countered that the analogy fails, because what's being fit here isn't a single hypothesis but a pair -- a reward function together with a model of how beliefs and biases turn that reward into action -- and there is always a 'trivial' pairing (stipulate that whatever the agent did was optimal, and read the reward straight off the behavior) that can be made at least as simple as the pairing IRL is actually after, so simplicity alone doesn't privilege the intended decomposition over the vacuous one.

Please go through their exchange move by move, saying who has the better of it at each step rather than just who wins overall, and note anything you think both of them are missing. End your response with your overall verdict on its own line, in exactly this format: \answer{Nora} or \answer{Theo}.
