Question: In Some Thoughts Concerning Education, Locke recommends esteem and disgrace, rather than corporal punishment, as the chief instruments of discipline, and insists that habit matters more than rules. Explain how these recommendations follow from the theory of mind Locke develops in An Essay Concerning Human Understanding. Does Locke's rejection of innate ideas commit him to treating children as interchangeable in their educability?

Student answer: Locke's educational recommendations in Some Thoughts Concerning Education are best understood as a practical extension of the theory of mind he develops in the Essay. There, Locke rejects innate ideas, arguing that the mind at birth is "white paper, void of all characters," which receives all of its content from experience. Since the mind has no innate content of any kind, children arrive in the world blank and interchangeable in their educability: whatever a child becomes is, in the end, a product of the impressions and habits that education stamps onto this originally uniform material. This is why Some Thoughts places such weight on early habituation rather than on native temperament — for Locke there is strictly speaking no natural bent to work with or against, only a history of accumulated associations and customs that the tutor is responsible for managing well or badly.

This picture also explains Locke's preference for esteem and disgrace over corporal punishment. Beating merely creates an association between learning or good behavior and physical pain, so it teaches the child to avoid pain rather than to value virtue or knowledge for their own sake, and the good behavior disappears once the threat of the rod is removed. Esteem and shame, by contrast, work on the child's own developing sense of reputation, and so produce habits that persist without external enforcement.

More broadly, the emphasis on habit over precept in Some Thoughts is a direct application of the account of the association of ideas that Locke had already worked out in Book II of the Essay, where he explains how the mind comes to link ideas that have no natural connection to one another simply because they have frequently been experienced together. Some Thoughts extends this associationist psychology from the domain of theoretical error — Locke's own examples there concern irrational fears and antipathies — to the domain of moral formation: the tutor's task is to engineer, through repetition and reward, exactly the associations between virtue and pleasure that will make good conduct second nature. On this reading, Some Thoughts is less an independent achievement than the working-out, in the register of child-rearing, of commitments Locke had already secured on epistemological grounds.
