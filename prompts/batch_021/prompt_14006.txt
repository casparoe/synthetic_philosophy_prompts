I work as a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, but I spend a fair amount of my free time reading rationalist-adjacent blogs and hanging around prediction-market and forecasting forums, where Aumann's agreement theorem gets invoked constantly as a kind of mathematical proof that two honest, rational people who trust each other's reasoning cannot simply "agree to disagree." I understand the rough shape of the result: if two Bayesian agents share a common prior and their posterior probabilities for some event become common knowledge between them (not just each stating a number once, but common knowledge in the full, infinite-regress sense), those posteriors are forced to coincide. What I hadn't appreciated, until a friend of mine who is finishing a philosophy PhD pushed back on me about it, is that there is a whole separate literature in mainstream epistemology on "peer disagreement," and that people on the same forums (and even some pop-philosophy explainers) treat Aumann's result as though it were essentially a formal vindication of the "equal weight view" associated with philosophers like Adam Elga: the view that on discovering you disagree with an epistemic peer, you should move your credence toward theirs, roughly splitting the difference, absent some specific reason to trust yourself more on that occasion.

My friend's objection was that this equivalence gets asserted far too quickly, since the theorem and the philosophical view are doing different jobs: the theorem describes idealized Bayesian agents under quite specific conditions (a literally shared prior, strict common knowledge, and an unlimited willingness to update), while the equal weight view is offered as practical advice for actual people, who almost certainly don't share priors, can never quite achieve genuine common knowledge, and typically disagree about far messier things than a single well-defined event. She also mentioned that Robin Hanson's "pre-rationality" idea is sometimes brought in to patch the common-prior problem, though she wasn't convinced it actually closes the gap. I'd like help sorting this out properly. Could you compare Aumann's agreement theorem with the equal weight view on peer disagreement, spelling out exactly which of the theorem's idealizing assumptions would need to carry over to real human disagreement for it to genuinely underwrite the equal weight view? On balance, do you think the theorem lends real formal support to conciliationism about peer disagreement, or is the connection more of a family resemblance than anything resembling a proof? Please justify whichever verdict you reach, and feel free to note briefly whether something like pre-rationality does the patching work my friend was skeptical of.
