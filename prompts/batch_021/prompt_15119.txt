Quick bit of context: I'm about two-thirds through a novel with a subplot about a woman born blind who gets an experimental procedure in her thirties that restores her sight, and I really want the scene where she first tries to match up what she's touching with what she's seeing to feel intellectually earned rather than just vaguely inspirational. So I went down a rabbit hole into the actual philosophical history of this question and dug up my old copy of Locke's Essay Concerning Human Understanding, which has this famous letter from a guy named Molyneux embedded in it. I've read the passage about six times now and think I mostly get it, but the syntax is so knotted up that I keep second-guessing myself, and there's a wrinkle involving Leibniz that I can't fully untangle either.

Here's the bit from Locke (Book II, ch. 9), more or less as it appears in my edition:

"I shall here insert a problem of that very ingenious and studious promoter of real knowledge, the learned and worthy Mr. Molyneux, which he was pleased to send me in a letter some months since; and it is this:—Suppose a man born blind, and now adult, and taught by his touch to distinguish between a cube and a sphere of the same metal, and nighly of the same bigness, so as to tell, when he felt one and the other, which is the cube, which the sphere. Suppose then the cube and sphere placed on a table, and the blind man be made to see: quaere, whether by his sight, before he touched them, he could now distinguish and tell which is the globe, which the cube? To which the acute and judicious proposer answers: Not. For, though he has obtained the experience of how a globe, how a cube affects his touch, yet he has not yet attained the experience that what affects his touch so or so must affect his sight so or so... I agree with this thinking gentleman in his answer to this problem, and am of opinion that the blind man, at first sight, would not be able with certainty to say which was the globe, which the cube, whilst he only saw them, though he could unerringly name them by his touch."

What's bugging me is that a page or so earlier in that same chapter, Locke's already made a big deal out of how, when you look at a uniformly colored ball, what actually lands on your eye is basically just a flat circle with graded shading, and it's only becuase you've learned over years to read that shading as roundness that you experience a solid globe instead of a painted disc - his whole point being that "judgment" quietly does work we mistake for raw sensation. Given that he already thinks ordinary sight is riddled with this kind of learned, automatic inference, I don't get why he's so confident the newly-sighted man in Molyneux's case couldn't lean on some analogous inference from his years of tactile experience and get it right, or at least a lot faster than "not at all, full stop." And to make it messier, I vaguely remember reading (in the New Essays, maybe?) that Leibniz pushed back on this exact passage, saying the man actually could work it out, just through explicit reasoning rather than immediate recognition - which sounds like it's threading some needle between Locke's answer and the objection I just made. Could you help me sort out whether there's a real tension between the globe bit and Locke's answer to Molyneux, and how Leibniz's move is actually supposed to resolve it, if it does?
