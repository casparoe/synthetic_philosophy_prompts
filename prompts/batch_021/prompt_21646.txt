I've spent a long time reading LessWrong and Astral Codex Ten, and more recently I've been following some of the effective-accelerationist writing that has migrated from Twitter into longer-form essays. One piece of intellectual history keeps getting invoked in that latter scene as though it were settled background: that John von Neumann, in his 1955 essay "Can We Survive Technology?," was the first to describe the technological singularity, meaning the point at which self-improving machines would surpass human intelligence, and that he concluded the only rational response to this trajectory was to accelerate toward it rather than attempt to slow or contain it. A few of the more polemical e/acc posts I've come across treat this as a kind of founding endorsement: even von Neumann, one of the most rigorous strategic minds of the Cold War, is said to have looked squarely at where technology was heading and decided that acceleration, not caution, was the only intellectually honest position.

What I'd like help with is reconstructing the actual argument in that essay for why acceleration was the conclusion he reached. It strikes me as slightly in tension with the rest of his career, given how much of his other work involved managing existential risks through careful calculation rather than embracing them, so I assume there is a specific chain of reasoning that gets him from observing the accelerating pace of technological change to endorsing acceleration as the rational policy. Could you walk me through that reasoning, and flag whether the accelerationists citing him are representing his actual position faithfully?
