So I'm a philosophy PhD student working on formal epistemology, and I've been sitting in on an econ department game theory seminar this term mostly because I got sucked into the Aumann backward-induction stuff for my dissertation. You probably know the basic result: in his 1995 *Games and Economic Behavior* paper, Aumann argues that common knowledge of rationality among the players (CKR, as everyone in the seminar just calls it now) all by itself forces the backward induction outcome in any generic game of perfect information — three- or four-leg centipede game, Selten's chain-store paradox, whatever your favorite example is. Every single week someone brings up the "obvious" objection, the Binmore/Reny/Ben-Porath/Stalnaker line, that if player 2 ever actually reaches a node she "shouldn't" reach under CKR, then by that same common knowledge she should already know something's gone wrong, so its totally unclear what she's supposed to believe about player 1's future play from there on — meaning CKR can't really be pinning down backward induction after all, since it's silent (or worse, inconsistent) about what happens once you're off the path. Half the room treats this as basically fatal to Aumann's claim, and honestly most of the secondary lit I've skimmed seems to side with the critics too.

What I can't shake is that I vaguely remember Aumann's own reply to Binmore turns on insisting that the relevant conditionals (something like "if node X were reached, player 1 would still play rationally from then on") should be read as material conditionals rather than the counterfactual/subjunctive conditionals his critics have in mind, and that this is supposed to dissolve the whole "but what happens off-path" worry rather than just dodge it. I don't fully get why that move is supposed to work, or whether it's even a fair response to what Reny and Stalnaker are actually doing with their belief-revision models. Could you write me an actual defense of Aumann's position here — i.e., really argue that common knowledge of rationality does force backward induction and that the standard off-path objection fails — instead of just summarizing the back-and-forth for me like my advisor keeps doing? I'd especially like you to engage with why the material-conditional move is supposed to work, or if you don't buy that it does, find a better way to rescue the result, since that's the part that's been bugging me for weeks now.
