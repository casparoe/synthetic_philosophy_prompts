This came up in a philosophy of religion reading group: we had been working through the fine-tuning argument and its most common naturalistic rebuttal, according to which the apparent fine-tuning of our universe's physical constants for life is unsurprising once you posit a sufficiently vast ensemble of universes with independently, randomly varying constants -- some of them are bound to be life-permitting, and since observers could only ever find themselves in a life-permitting universe, our own existence in one is no evidence against pure chance. Ian Hacking objected that this move has a suspicious structure: he compared it to someone who walks into a casino, watches a stranger roll double sixes on the very first throw he happens to witness, and concludes that the stranger must already have been rolling for a while -- when in fact that single observed roll tells him nothing about how many other rolls occurred, since the chance of double sixes on any fair roll is fixed regardless of how many rolls came before or after it. Hacking called this the inverse gambler's fallacy, and argued that physicists who invoke unseen universes to explain the fine-tuning of this one commit exactly the same error: the fine-tuned constants of our universe cannot be evidence that other universes exist, because how many trials occur elsewhere has no bearing on the outcome of this trial.

The standard multiverse-side rebuttal is that Hacking's casino case is disanalogous in the respect that matters: an ordinary patron could have walked in and witnessed any roll, favorable or not, whereas we could not possibly have existed to observe anything other than a life-permitting universe, however many universes there turn out to be. On this view, it is exactly this observation-selection effect, absent from the casino case, that licenses treating our own existence as evidence favoring a large ensemble over a lone universe, so that Hacking's charge misfires.

I'd like you to defend Hacking's side of this exchange. Argue that the observation-selection defense does not fully succeed, and that some recognizable descendant of the inverse gambler's fallacy still infects the inference from 'we exist in a fine-tuned universe' to 'therefore, probably, many universes exist,' even once selection effects are properly built in. Engage concretely with why the disanalogy is supposed to do the work its defenders think it does, and locate precisely where that defense goes wrong, rather than simply declaring self-selection reasoning illegitimate in general; please also state the strongest form of the observation-selection defense in your own words before arguing against it, so that your conclusion is earned rather than won by default. To keep the question sharp, I'm asking only about the cogency of the multiverse rebuttal itself, not about whether fine-tuning ultimately favors theism once that rebuttal is set aside.
