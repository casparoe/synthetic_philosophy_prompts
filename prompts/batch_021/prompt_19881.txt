Two days before closing on my first house, I got wiring instructions for the down payment by email, apparently from my title company; everything checked out, and in a lower-stakes situation I'd have said flatly that I knew these were the right instructions. But real-estate wire fraud is common enough that, with six figures on the line, I found I genuinely couldn't say I knew the instructions were legitimate, even though my evidence hadn't changed at all from the low-stakes case I was imagining. (I confirmed in person before wiring anything; it was fine.)

I've since learned that this pattern is exactly what motivates two rival views in epistemology: contextualism (DeRose, Cohen), on which "knows" shifts its truth conditions with the speaker's context, and pragmatic encroachment, or subject-sensitive invariantism (Stanley, Fantl and McGrath), on which what shifts is whether the subject actually knows. I can't tell if these are substantive rivals or just two vocabularies for the same insight.

Could you write a dialogue between a committed defender of each view in which they use my case to press their own diagnosis, take on each other's hardest objections (contextualism's alleged "semantic blindness" problem; encroachment's trouble with subjects who are high-stakes without realizing it), and then seriously try to settle whether their dispute is substantive or merely verbal? Neither side should get an easy win.
