I have been following the recurring debates about algorithmic fairness on LessWrong, the EA Forum, and assorted rationalist-adjacent Substacks for years now, and I keep noticing the same dialectical pattern whenever the COMPAS recidivism tool comes up. Someone cites ProPublica's finding that the tool flagged Black defendants who never went on to reoffend as high-risk far more often than it did white defendants who never reoffended; someone else cites Northpointe's rebuttal that the tool was equally well-calibrated across race groups, in the sense that the same fraction of people actually reoffended within each risk decile regardless of race; and then someone invokes the Kleinberg–Mullainathan–Raghavan and Chouldechova impossibility results to point out that both sides were reporting true facts about the same tool, since calibration and equalized error rates provably cannot both hold once the two groups have different base rates of rearrest. That move usually ends the thread, as though showing that the trade-off is mathematically forced also tells you how to make it. It does not, and that is precisely the part everyone conveniently skips.

This is the question I would like you to actually answer. Suppose you are advising a jurisdiction that is redesigning its pretrial risk-assessment tool and has already accepted that it cannot satisfy every fairness criterion on the table at once. I want your considered, committed ranking of which criteria should still get priority in that design, not another restatement of the impossibility theorem. Please sort the following eight criteria into a tier list running from S (should dominate the design even at real cost to the others) down to F (actively the wrong thing to optimize for in this setting): independence / demographic parity; separation / equalized odds; equal opportunity (the true-positive-rate-only relaxation of separation); sufficiency / calibration within groups; predictive parity; treatment equality (equal false-negative-to-false-positive ratios across groups); individual fairness in the Dwork et al. sense; and counterfactual fairness in the Kusner et al. sense. Give the ranking as a JSON object whose keys are the tiers (S, A, B, C, F) and whose values are lists of criteria, for example:

```json
{
  "S": ["..."],
  "A": ["..."],
  "B": ["..."],
  "C": ["..."],
  "F": ["..."]
}
```

Follow the JSON with at most one sentence per tier stating the normative principle that groups those criteria together, so that the placements read as reasoned rather than arbitrary.
