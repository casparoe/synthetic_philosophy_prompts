I'm working on an article about the philosophical scaffolding beneath Bayesian reasoning, and one detail keeps nagging at me. The "synchronic" Dutch book argument for the probability axioms is generally treated as close to decisive, but its "diachronic" counterpart — an argument, traced to an unpublished proof by David Lewis, that rational agents must revise their credences by strict conditionalization on pain of a guaranteed loss — is considered far shakier by specialists. As I understand it, the proof shows only that an agent who has already committed to some fixed, evidence-indexed rule for revising their credence in a hypothesis will avoid a sure loss only by using conditionalization; it doesn't obviously show that rational agents must commit to any such fixed rule in the first place, rather than revising their views in ways that are not simply a function of the evidence received. Please construct the strongest version of this objection: that the diachronic Dutch book argument establishes at most conditionalization's optimality among precommitted updating policies, not the rational necessity of precommitment — and hence of conditionalization — itself.
