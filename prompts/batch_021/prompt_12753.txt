I'm reporting a piece on the recent surge of popular interest in the Kelly criterion — the formula, growing out of Claude Shannon's information theory and later popularized by Ed Thorp, that tells a gambler or investor what fraction of a bankroll to wager on a favorable bet in order to maximize long-run compound growth. In researching the piece, I've run into a cluster of writers and podcasters who lump Kelly betting together with what they call "geometric rationality": the claim that because wealth compounds multiplicatively over time, the geometric mean of your wealth (equivalently, its expected logarithm) is the only mathematically defensible quantity to maximize, rather than ordinary arithmetic expected value. Several go further and frame this as a genuine refutation of mainstream expected-utility theory — arguing that economists' long-standing habit of explaining risk aversion through diminishing marginal utility was really just a clumsy workaround for an error in the underlying math, an error that Kelly's formula, and later the physicist Ole Peters's work on "ergodicity economics," finally exposed.

That's a dramatic claim to put in front of a general readership, so before I run with it I'd like to pressure-test the framing. Is it accurate to say that the Kelly/geometric-growth argument has shown ordinary expected-value reasoning to be simply wrong, such that any genuinely rational bettor or investor should now size every wager by the Kelly fraction rather than by an ordinary expected-value or risk-adjusted calculation? Or is there a more careful way of stating what the Kelly criterion and the ergodicity-economics literature actually establish, and where the popular version of the story overreaches?
