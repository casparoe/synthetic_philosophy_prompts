I spent about thirty-five years as a research chemist before retiring, and since then I've been slowly working my way through a lot of classical philosophy I never had the time for on the job. Right now a couple of old colleagues of mine (also newly retired, ha) and I are reading through Cicero's De Senectute together -- seemed like the appropriate choice given our current life stage.

Somethings been bugging me about the ending though. Cato spends a good chunk of the dialogue building up what sound like genuinely confident arguments for why the soul has to be immortal -- stuff about its simplicity, its kinship with the divine, echoes of what I gather is basically Plato's playbook (Socrates in the Phaedo, that bit about Cyrus's dying speech, if I'm remembering right). But then right at the close he more or less shrugs and says that even if he's wrong about all that, he's happy to be wrong, and no one is going to talk him out of a belief that keeps him comfortable while he's alive. To me that read like a cop-out the first time through -- imagine a chemist spending thirty pages deriving a reaction mechanism and then adding, in the last footnote, "but if this mechanism turns out to be completely wrong, I don't mind, I like believing it anyway." Except then I found out Cicero considered himself an Academic Skeptic, someone in principle committed to not asserting dogmatic conclusions -- so now I'm wondering if this isn't sloppyness at all but some deliberate and recognized philosophical move.

So could you help me understand what's actually going on at the end of the dialogue? Is presenting an argument for a conclusion you don't fully endorse -- especially about something like death, where the "cost" of being wrong might not be symmetrical either way -- a legitimate philosophical strategy with some real pedigree to it, or is Cicero just having his cake and eating it too? And does this move square with how he describes his own skepticism elsewhere, or is De Senectute more of an outlier for him on that front?
