Two people in my PhD cohort got into a genuine disagreement after a seminar on Augustine's theory of lying (mainly De Mendacio), and I'd like an outside verdict on the actual back-and-forth rather than just an overall winner.

Background from the seminar: Augustine defines a lie not by whether the sentence uttered is true or false in the world, but by the relation between the speaker's assertion and what she actually believes, together with the intention to deceive -- a lie is an assertion at odds with one's own mind, made in order to mislead. To press on this, Marcus proposed a test case: a hiker wants to sabotage a rival, so she tells him to take the right-hand fork, believing (wrongly, as it turns out) that this trail dead-ends before the summit. The right-hand fork is in fact the correct route, and he summits without incident.

(1) Marcus: She didn't lie to him. Lying requires saying something false, and what she said was true; at most she tried to lie and failed, the way someone who shoots at someone and misses hasn't committed a killing.

(2) Elena: That's importing a condition Augustine's definition doesn't include -- nothing in "assertion contrary to the mind, meant to deceive" mentions whether the sentence turns out true. You're treating "lying" as an achievement verb like "killing," which needs the world to cooperate. On Augustine's definition it works more like "firing at someone": complete once you aim and pull the trigger with the relevant intent, whatever the bullet does afterward. Whether the sentence happens to come out true is downstream of the act, not part of what makes it the act it is.

(3) Marcus: Granted -- she's exactly as blameworthy as if she'd succeeded, and I'm not disputing that. But your reading has a cost: it makes whether an utterance counts as a lie depend entirely on a fact nobody but the speaker can ever access, namely her belief at the moment of utterance. That seems like the wrong kind of fact for a concept to rest on when we use "lying" to justify blame, explain betrayals of trust, and ground liability for fraud.

(4) Elena: Every intention-based category has that feature. Whether a killing is murder, manslaughter, or a tragic accident already turns entirely on belief and intent that we only ever infer and never observe directly, and that doesn't make those categories unusable -- it's why we hold trials. It isn't even a special cost of Augustine's account of lying: putting the moral weight on the interior will rather than the external result is how his ethics works generally, not a fix invented for hard cases.

Please go through moves (1)-(4) and assess how much force each one has and why, rather than simply picking an overall winner. I'd also like your view on whether Marcus and Elena are, by the end, actually disagreeing about two different questions -- what the word "lying" picks out, versus when someone is morally blameworthy -- without either of them noticing it.
