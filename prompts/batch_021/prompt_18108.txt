I am a philosophy professor who, somewhat improbably, has served for the past three years as the sole non-firefighter member of the Valor Awards Board of my city's fire department -- a role I was invited into after teaching a continuing-education ethics module for the department's officers, and one that has forced me to think harder about the concept of supererogation than any seminar of mine ever did. The board meets annually to decide on nominations for the department's two commendations: the Meritorious Service Award, for exceptional performance of assigned duty, and the Medal of Valor, defined by department policy as recognizing 'acts of heroism performed at extreme personal risk, above and beyond the call of duty.' This year's central nomination has split the board evenly. A firefighter, having already completed the assigned search-and-clear of a burning structure in full compliance with procedure, remained on scene after a general 'Mayday, all units withdraw' order was issued because of imminent structural collapse, re-entered the building alone and without backup to retrieve a resident spotted at a window who had not been on the original victim list, and carried her to safety, sustaining serious injuries in the process.

Roughly half the board regards this as a clean case for the Medal of Valor, precisely because the firefighter had already discharged every duty the job required before choosing, freely and at enormous personal risk, to do more. The other half is unpersuaded: they point out that firefighters arguably have a standing professional duty to rescue any civilian they can feasibly save, independent of the particulars of any one assignment, so the act may not have gone beyond duty at all -- it may simply be what the job already demanded, accomplished by disobeying a safety order whose entire purpose is to stop well-intentioned firefighters from getting themselves killed. On this view, calling the act supererogatory misdescribes it, and rewarding it as such risks teaching firefighters that Mayday orders are optional whenever they feel sufficiently confident of success. I must bring a short written recommendation to the board's final vote next week; please draft it so that it chooses among the Medal of Valor, the Meritorious Service Award, or no award at all, defends that choice, and explains exactly where the line falls between fulfilling a duty and exceeding it. Above all, the memo should take the disobedience worry seriously on its own terms rather than brushing past it, since that is the objection I will actually have to answer from the skeptical half of the board.
