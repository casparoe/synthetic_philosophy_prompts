A few weeks ago, a group of friends and I spent most of a dinner arguing about a dilemma one of us was actually facing at work: whether to report a colleague for cutting corners in a way that put people at minor risk. At one point, the friend facing the dilemma said, "Honestly, I haven't worked through the arguments myself — I've just decided to report him because my old manager, whom I trust completely, told me it was clearly the right thing to do." Everyone at the table nodded along as though this were a perfectly ordinary way to arrive at a moral verdict. But it kept nagging at me on the drive home: if that same friend had instead said, "I've decided the colleague really was cutting corners, because my old manager told me so, and I trust her completely," nobody would have thought twice about it — that's just ordinary testimony, and doubting it would seem paranoid. Yet outsourcing the moral verdict in the same way struck me, and apparently only me, as somehow less legitimate, even though structurally it looks like the same move in both cases: believing something purely because someone you trust told you so.

I've since done a little reading and gathered that this contrast — people online seem to call it the puzzle of "moral testimony" — is a recognized issue in its own right, and not just some private hang-up of mine. But every discussion I've come across illustrates it with the same handful of well-worn cases, things like whether eating meat is wrong or whether abortion is permissible, and I don't think those really isolate what's bothering me, partly because most people already walk in with a settled opinion on those particular questions, which muddies any comparison.

What I would like from you is a pair of scenarios, built from scratch, that match each other as closely as possible on everything except the moral versus non-moral difference itself: the same degree of trust in the source, the same total lack of independent access to the underlying reasons or evidence, the same practical stakes, and ideally a subject obscure enough that I wouldn't already have a settled opinion pulling me one way or another. The pair should be constructed so that most people would find deferring completely unremarkable in the factual case, yet would sense something off, incomplete, or second-rate about deferring in the moral case. Once you've laid out the pair, I'd also appreciate it if you could say a little about what you think is actually doing the work in generating that discomfort in the moral case, since, from what little I've read, even people who study this for a living don't fully agree on the diagnosis.
