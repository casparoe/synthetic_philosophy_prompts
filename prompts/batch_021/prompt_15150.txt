"Presentism and eternalism disagree about the ontological status of the past and future. The presentist holds that only present events and objects are real: the past no longer exists, the future does not yet exist, and reality is exhausted by whatever is happening right now. The eternalist denies this, holding that past, present, and future are all equally real and exist together as a single four-dimensional 'block universe'; on this view, an event's being 'present' is rather like a place's being 'here' — it just marks where the speaker is located within the block, not a special status that the event has and others lack.

The growing block theory, associated with C. D. Broad, is best seen as a version of eternalism that adds a story about time's passage. Like the eternalist, the growing-block theorist holds that once an event has occurred it is just as real as it ever was, so the past is not annihilated the way presentism claims; the passage of time is then explained as new slices of the block continually being added at its 'growing edge,' which we experience as the present. Since presentism is the only one of the three views that denies reality to the past, growing block theory and eternalism end up on the same side of that dispute, against presentism.

Physicists sometimes bring special relativity into this debate, since it denies that there is any observer-independent fact about which distant events are simultaneous with a given event 'here and now.' This is usually taken to be a problem for presentism, because presentism seems to require a well-defined, universe-wide present moment, and relativity implies that there is no privileged way of slicing spacetime into such moments — observers moving at different velocities disagree about which events count as simultaneous with 'now,' and physics gives no grounds for preferring one such observer's slicing over another's. Eternalism sits more comfortably with this, since it can simply identify reality with the four-dimensional spacetime manifold that physics already posits, without needing to privilege any single cross-section as 'the' present."
