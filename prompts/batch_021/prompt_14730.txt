Here is the question as it was set:

"Non-cognitivist expressivism holds that to assert 'Lying is wrong' is to express a conative or attitudinal state (e.g., disapproval) rather than to state a belief with descriptive truth-conditions. Consider the inference:
(1) If lying is wrong, then getting your little brother to lie is wrong.
(2) Lying is wrong.
(3) Therefore, getting your little brother to lie is wrong.
Explain why embedded occurrences of moral vocabulary, as in (1), pose a problem for expressivism (the Frege-Geach problem), and critically evaluate whether the responses developed by Blackburn and/or Gibbard succeed in resolving it." (20 points)

And here is the student's answer, reproduced verbatim:

"Expressivists such as Ayer and Hare deny that moral sentences like 'Lying is wrong' state facts; uttering the sentence is instead supposed to express a non-cognitive attitude, roughly 'Lying: boo!' The difficulty, which Geach pressed against non-cognitivists of this kind by drawing on a point of Frege's, is that moral predicates also occur unasserted, as in the antecedent of a conditional. Asserting premise (1) does not thereby express any disapproval of lying; one might accept the conditional while thinking lying is sometimes permissible. So whatever 'lying is wrong' contributes to (1) cannot simply be the act of disapproving that it supposedly performs when asserted on its own in (2).

Yet the inference from (1) and (2) to (3) is intuitively valid, and its validity seems to depend on 'lying is wrong' meaning the same thing in both premises, on pain of equivocation, as in 'Every bank has a vault; I sat on the river bank; so I sat on a vault.' Since expressivism denies that moral sentences have truth-conditional content that could stay constant across asserted and unasserted contexts, it owes us an explanation of why the inference is valid at all. This is the Frege-Geach problem.

Blackburn's response in Spreading the Word tries to reconstruct logical relations directly among attitudes rather than among truth-conditional contents: accepting (1) is glossed as holding a higher-order attitude against certain combinations, such as disapproving of approving of lying while not disapproving of getting your brother to lie. Someone who accepts (1) and (2) but rejects (3) is then in an unstable, self-condemning state analogous to inconsistent belief, which is meant to explain why the inference strikes us as valid. But this concedes the substance of Geach's challenge rather than meeting it: since there is, on Blackburn's own view, no single proposition that stays fixed across the two premises, the most he can offer is that the argument is valid in a loose, practical sense — that holding the premises together while rejecting the conclusion is psychologically unstable — and not that (1)-(2)-(3) is valid in the strict logical sense in which 'All men are mortal, Socrates is a man, so Socrates is mortal' is valid.

Gibbard's plan-expressivism, developed most fully in Thinking How to Live, does better by treating normative judgment as expressing acceptance of a plan for living, including for contingencies one will never actually face, and building a space of 'hyperplans' — maximally specific, fully opinionated plans — paired with ordinary possible worlds. Normative sentences get assigned the sets of plan-world pairs they rule out, exactly as descriptive sentences are assigned the worlds they rule out, so ordinary possible-worlds semantics for the connectives can be recycled wholesale. This is a real compositional semantics, not merely a story about psychological pressure, which is why Gibbard's proposal, unlike Blackburn's, is usually credited with engaging seriously with the technical demands of the problem rather than dissolving them.

At bottom, both projects are wrestling with a descendant of Hume's observation that no 'ought' can be validly derived from an 'is': expressivism wants logical relations among 'oughts' to hold without any 'is' available to underwrite them, and it is this absence that Geach's puzzle exploits."
