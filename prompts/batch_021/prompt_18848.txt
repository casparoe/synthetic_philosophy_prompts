A friend and I spent most of a long train journey arguing about Dutch book arguments, a topic I had recently run into in a philosophy of science elective, in a debate that began, innocuously enough, over a set of odds I had offered him on which of three horses would win a race we were watching: odds that, converted into probabilities, came to roughly forty-five, thirty-five, and thirty percent. He pointed out that these summed to more than one hundred percent and insisted that this alone showed my beliefs about the race were irrational, since he could, in principle, construct a short combination of bets against me — each of which I would have to accept as fair by my own lights — that would guarantee I lost money no matter which horse won. I pushed back, since it struck me as odd that a fact about exploitable betting behavior should tell us something about the rationality of the underlying beliefs themselves, especially given that I had no intention of ever placing those bets. By the time we reached our stop, we had wandered into further disagreements about whether the argument keeps any force once we allow that an agent's beliefs can change over time, rather than being fixed at the single moment when all the bets are placed, and about whether the whole framework secretly depends on there being a real bookmaker sitting across the table.

We never really settled any of this, and I would like a clearer sense of what is actually at stake. Please describe the most important implications of Dutch book arguments for how I should treat my own everyday probability judgments and risk assessments — for instance, when sizing up a business risk, swapping informal odds with friends, or simply noticing that two of my own beliefs about related uncertain events do not quite cohere — in the ordinary run of cases where there is no literal bookmaker waiting to exploit any inconsistency.
