So I'm giving the toast at my old college roommate Nadia's wedding in a couple weeks, and I've been kind of thrown off ever since an argument with my friend Owen last weekend that I can't quite shake. I was running the toast by him — the gist was going to be that Nadia's just got the best character of anyone I know, always has, like the time she found a wallet stuffed with cash on the subway and spent her whole lunch break tracking down the owner instead of just dropping it at lost and found — and Owen, who's finishing up a philosophy MA, went off on me about the situationist critique of virtue ethics. His point: Milgram, the seminary-student "Good Samaritan" study, decades of this kind of research, apparently show that whether people act well is predicted way better by the situation (are they in a rush, is anyone watching) than by any stable trait, so talking about someone's "character" the way I was is basically astrology dressed up as psychology. I brought up the Stanford Prison Experiment to back myself up and he just laughed — turns out that one's been mostly debunked at this point, something about Zimbardo's team coaching the guards to be cruel, theres a whole expose about it — and said if anything that makes Milgram and the Good Samaritan stuff look sturdier by comparison, not weaker. I came back with: real virtue ethics never claimed people act identically no matter the situation, the whole point of Aristotle's practical wisdom is that a virtuous person reads situations correctly and adjusts, so acting differently in different contexts isn't evidence against having a virtue, it's what having one looks like. Owen's reply is the part that's actually stuck with me: if literally any inconsistency can be waved away as "contextually appropriate," what would ever count as evidence that someone ISN'T virtuous? Doesn't that make the whole idea unfalsifiable? We never got anywhere, just ended up ordering more fries.

Anyway, now I actually have to write this toast, and I don't want to just deliver the line I originally planned like the argument never happened, but I also don't think Owen's right that I should just drop it — I still believe it's true about her. Could you write the toast for me — 4 to 5 minutes out loud — one that still lets me sincerely say Nadia has real character, but actually earns that claim against Owen's argument instead of quietly ignoring it?
