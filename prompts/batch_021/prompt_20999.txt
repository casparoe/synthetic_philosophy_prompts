So I've been falling down a rabbit hole researching a piece around the anniversary of Paul Cohen's independence proof, and theres a "what if" I can't shake, so let me throw it at you. Hilbert put Cantor's continuum problem right at the top of his famous 1900 list of 23 problems, and thirty years later he's the guy standing up in Königsberg declaring there's no such thing as an unsolvable problem in mathematics -- "wir müssen wissen, wir werden wissen," aimed squarely at stamping out the old "ignorabimus" pessimism. If I've got the history right, Gödel had basically just floated his incompleteness result around that same conference, which quietly undercut the whole premise before Hilbert even finished the sentence. And CH itself is maybe the starkest casualty of all this: it turned out to be neither provable nor refutable from the ZFC axioms, once Cohen finished in '63 what Gödel had started with his consistency proof back in the 40s.

So, playing historian for a sec: if Hilbert had somehow lived to see Cohen's result, do you think he'd have counted "it's independent" as a genuine solution to his first problem -- full stop, case closed -- or would he have treated it as a kind of defeat for everything his program was betting on? And would he have thrown his weight behind the later hunt among set theorists for new axioms (large cardinals and the like) to settle CH one way or the other, treating that as just more "knowing," or would that whole enterprise have struck him as a betrayal of the rigor he cared about? Im not looking for a "nobody can really know what a dead mathematician would think" cop-out -- I want you to actually reconstruct his reasoning from what we know of his formalism and his mathematical temperament.
