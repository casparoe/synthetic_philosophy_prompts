For a piece I'm reporting on how different philosophical traditions handle trust in other people's word, I interviewed a scholar of classical Indian philosophy who walked me through the Nyāya school's theory of the pramāṇas, the recognized instruments of knowledge. What stuck with me is that Nyāya treats verbal testimony (śabda) as a basic pramāṇa in its own right, standing alongside perception and inference rather than reducible to either — a position Buddhist epistemologists such as Dharmakīrti rejected, since they preferred to fold testimony into inference, on the grounds that trusting a speaker just is inferring that they are being truthful from signs of their reliability. The disagreement over how many basic pramāṇas there really are ran on for centuries and split along more than two lines: the Cārvāka materialists recognized only perception, while the Mīmāṃsakas eventually arrived at six.

None of this made Nyāya's position a blank check for hearsay. My source was emphatic that testimony only rises to the level of a pramāṇa when it comes from an āpta — someone competent and non-deceptive about the matter at hand — and that the sentence itself has to satisfy further conditions before it yields knowledge rather than mere noise: its words have to connect grammatically, their meanings can't contradict one another, they have to be uttered close enough together to register as a single utterance, and the hearer has to grasp what the speaker actually intended to convey.

I can't shake the feeling that this maps onto my own work more precisely than most philosophy I run into: deciding, most days, whether a source's account can stand on its own as reportable fact or needs independent corroboration first. So rather than having the theory simply explained back to me, I would like to know what you take to be its most important implications: given the Nyāya picture of testimony as an independent pramāṇa with its own built-in safeguards, what should someone in my line of work draw from it about when another person's word is, by itself, a route to knowledge, and when it is not?
