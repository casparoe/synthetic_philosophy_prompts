okay this is going to sound like an odd question for a novel problem but it's actually a logic snag and its bugging me more than the plot itself.

I'm working on a murder mystery where it's established from page one that someone deliberately spiked the tea urn at a reception -- that part's not in question. My detective, Rosalind, says while going through the guest list, "if Callum didn't do it, someone else did," just narrowing things down out loud. Later, once she's worked out that Callum was literally the only guest with both a motive and a way to get near that urn, she says "if Callum hadn't done it, no one would have." My writing group insists these two lines flatly contradict each other -- first she says somebody else could be the culprit, then that nobody else could be -- and wants me to cut one.

But my gut says they're not actually claiming the same kind of thing, even though on the page they read like the same sentence with the verb tense swapped. Is my writing group right that this is just sloppy writing on my part, or is there an actual, principled reason both lines could be true at once without Rosalind contradicting herself? If there's real daylight between these two kinds of "if" statements, I want the actual mechanics of why, not just "one's more hypothetical" -- I need to be able to defend the choice if my editor flags it too.
