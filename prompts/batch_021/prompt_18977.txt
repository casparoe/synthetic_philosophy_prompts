This semester I'm taking a seminar cross-listed between the philosophy and computer science departments on formal theories of agency, and we recently paired two topics I hadn't previously thought to connect: Donald Davidson's account of radical interpretation, with its reliance on the principle of charity, and inverse reinforcement learning (IRL), the machine learning framework in which an observer infers an agent's reward function from observed behavior under some assumption of rationality. Our professor mentioned, almost in passing, that both projects run into a strikingly similar underdetermination problem: on Davidson's view, indefinitely many pairings of beliefs and meanings can rationalize the very same verbal behavior, while in IRL, indefinitely many reward functions -- including the trivial all-zero reward, under which every possible policy counts as optimal -- can rationalize the very same observed behavior. I'd like to pursue this parallel more rigorously for my term paper, and would appreciate your help thinking it through carefully.

Could you compare Davidson's radical interpretation with the reward-identifiability problem in IRL? I'm especially interested in three things: first, a precise account of how each framework's proposed remedy for underdetermination actually works -- the principle of charity on Davidson's side, versus assumptions such as Boltzmann-rationality, entropy regularization, or an explicit prior over reward functions on the IRL side -- and whether these remedies are doing structurally the same kind of work or are only superficially similar; second, whether Davidson's charity is best understood as playing a role analogous to a Bayesian prior in modern IRL, or whether that comparison breaks down on closer inspection, given that charity is supposed to be constitutive of what beliefs and meanings an agent has, whereas an IRL prior is typically just a modeling convenience; and third, your own assessment of which framework offers the more defensible response to the indeterminacy it faces, and why. Please don't stop at the observation that both projects "infer mental states from behavior" -- I already have that much, and what I'm really trying to work out is whether the analogy holds up under real scrutiny or collapses once you look closely.
