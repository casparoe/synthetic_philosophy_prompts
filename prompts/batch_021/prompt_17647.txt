Question (worth 20 points): Explain how Worrall's epistemic structural realism is meant to reconcile the no-miracles argument with the pessimistic meta-induction, using the transition from Fresnel's theory of light to Maxwell's electromagnetic theory as your example. Then explain how the Newman objection threatens this position, and briefly discuss why some philosophers have thought that moving to an ontic structural realism escapes the objection.

Student's answer: "The no-miracles argument holds that the predictive and explanatory success of mature science would be an inexplicable coincidence unless our best theories are at least approximately true, while the pessimistic meta-induction observes that many past theories were similarly successful and yet are now considered false in their central theoretical claims, so a track record of success alone cannot license belief in a theory's full content. Worrall's structural realism is designed to honor both thoughts at once: it grants the pessimistic induction's premise that theoretical ontology is routinely overturned, but denies that everything about a discarded theory is thereby lost, since the mathematical structure of a theory—the pattern of relations among the magnitudes it postulates—can survive even radical changes in ontology, and it is this structure, not the ontology, to which realists should commit. Worrall's illustration is the move from Fresnel's theory of light, on which light consists in vibrations of a mechanical, elastic-solid ether, to Maxwell's electromagnetic theory. On Worrall's telling, the apparent ontological break here is not as sharp as it first looks: since the electromagnetic field plays the same theoretical role the ether used to play, it is best seen as a reconceived descendant of the ether rather than a wholesale replacement for it, so that Fresnel's ontology survives in modified form alongside his mathematical relations—which is exactly the sort of continuity epistemic structural realism needs. The Newman objection, which formalizes a worry going back to Newman's 1928 critique of Russell and was later revived by Demopoulos and Friedman, threatens this picture: if 'knowing the structure' of the world just means knowing that it has some set of relations isomorphic to the relations in our theory, this is nearly trivial, since any domain of objects of sufficient cardinality can be assigned relations realizing any structure whatsoever. Worrall himself took this worry seriously enough to conclude that epistemic structural realism should give way to Ladyman's ontic structural realism, on which there are no individuals with hidden natures at all, only structure, so that there is nothing left for a Newman-style worry to be about."
