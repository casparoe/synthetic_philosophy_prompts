I brought it up with a friend who's finishing his stats PhD, half expecting sympathy, and instead he brought up something called Good's theorem -- I.J. Good, apparently the same guy who worked with Turing at Bletchley Park -- who supposedly proved back in the 60s that for a Bayesian agent, the expected value of a free piece of evidence can never be negative, because you can always just act like you never saw it, so looking can only help you or leave you exactly where you'd have been anyway. That's been bugging me more than I expected. Some part of me thinks he's missing something, but every time I try to say what, it collapses into "well, I just don't want to know," which isn't a counterargument, its just restating my preference. What I actually want from you is to get into the guts of how that proof works -- the "you can always fall back on the action you'd have taken anyway" move specifically -- and use that to build the strongest case you can that Good's theorem doesn't actually show it would be irrational for someone in my position to decline the test, even granting that I really am the fully Bayesian expected-utility maximizer he's assuming I am.
