Over the past two months I've become the primary caretaker for my father's dog, Baxter, a thirteen-year-old retriever who can no longer manage the stairs and has started having accidents in the house that clearly distress him as much as they distress the rest of us. Sitting in the vet's waiting room last week, trying to work out how much further to go with treatment versus letting him go, I found myself thinking harder than I expected about what exactly we owe animals like him, and on what grounds.

That sent me back to some philosophy I half-remembered from an undergraduate ethics course: Kant's notorious claim that direct duties can only be owed to beings capable of setting their own ends through reason, which on his view rules out animals, so that anything resembling a duty toward an animal is really an indirect duty to ourselves, since cruelty toward animals tends to erode the humane dispositions we need in order to fulfil our duties to other people. Rereading it, I was struck to find that Kant actually illustrates the point with something close to my own situation, a dog grown too old to be of further use to its master. It is a tidy argument, but it left me cold, so a friend finishing her philosophy doctorate pointed me toward Christine Korsgaard's Fellow Creatures, where she tries to stay within a broadly Kantian framework while arguing that animals really are owed direct moral consideration after all, by grounding moral status in something like a being's having a good of its own that can be furthered or damaged, rather than in rational agency as such.

I would like your help working out whether Korsgaard's move actually succeeds on Kantian terms. Could you reconstruct, premise by premise, roughly where her argument departs from Kant's own reasoning for restricting direct moral status to rational beings, and then assess whether the resulting view is a genuine, internally consistent extension of Kantian ethics, or whether it has to quietly trade away something essential to what makes an ethical theory distinctively Kantian, such as the tie between moral status and autonomy, in order to reach its conclusion? I would rather you defend a real verdict here than simply present both sides neutrally.
