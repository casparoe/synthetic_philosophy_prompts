I've been reading Spinoza on my own for about a year now (no formal philosophy background, I just fell into it) and lately I can't stop thinking about his politics. His argument for democracy being the most "natural" kind of state basically comes down to: a state's right just is the combined power of everyone in it, and democracy is what you get when that power isn't handed over to a king or a small group, so decisions stay closest to reason. Made total sense to me. Then I found out that the Political Treatise, the book he was still writing when he died, breaks off in its final chapter right in the middle of him arguing that women shouldn't count as full citizens - using that exact same power logic, pointing to men ruling over women almost everywhere as proof it's a natural gap in power and not just custom. And then the manuscript just stops.

I keep going back and forth on whether that's his own system breaking it's own rules, or the ugly thing that was hiding inside "right = power" the whole time. Could you write me a dialogue between two serious Spinoza defenders who land on opposite sides of that question, really engaging with each other's reasoning instead of scoring easy points off each other? I don't want either one to obviously be the strawman.
