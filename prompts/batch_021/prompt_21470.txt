"Consider a two-person bargaining problem with disagreement point d = (0,0) and feasible set S equal to the comprehensive hull of (0,2), (1,1), and (3,0).
(a) [5 pts] Find the ideal (utopia) point and the Kalai-Smorodinsky solution. State the Nash solution too (no need to re-derive it).
(b) [4 pts] State the individual monotonicity axiom, and explain why the Nash solution can satisfy IIA and still violate it.
(c) [6 pts] Gauthier's Morals by Agreement treats 'minimax relative concession' as the outcome rational bargainers converge on from the state of nature. Explain what the principle says, how it relates formally to the Kalai-Smorodinsky solution, and how Gauthier's use of it differs in motivation from Rawls's use of maximin in A Theory of Justice."

And here's the student's answer:

"(a) The ideal point comes from each player's best payoff anywhere in S: player 1 tops out at 3 (at (3,0)), player 2 at 2 (at (0,2)), so a = (3,2). The KS solution is where the ray from d=(0,0) to a, i.e. u2=(2/3)u1, crosses the frontier - on the segment through (1,1) and (3,0), u2=1.5-0.5u1, that gives u1=9/7≈1.29, u2=6/7≈0.86. Nash instead maximizes u1u2 along the frontier, which gives u1=1.5, u2=0.75. So on the exact same feasible set, KS is a bit kinder to player 2 and a bit tougher on player 1 than Nash is.

(b) Individual monotonicity says that when the feasible set expands, neither player should end up worse off than before - growing the pie shouldn't hurt anybody. Nash can violate this because it's pinned down by independence of irrelevant alternatives instead, which only checks whether the old chosen point is still around, not how the rest of the set reshaped, so an expansion that's lopsided toward one player can still drag the product-maximizing point toward the other, leaving the first player worse off than before the set grew. That's the gap Kalai and Smorodinsky's axiom is designed to close.

(c) Minimax relative concession has each side give up the same proportion of their maximal claim, which is just the Kalai-Smorodinsky solution applied to the move from the state of nature into cooperation. That's really the same egalitarian impulse driving Rawls's maximin - both are ways of finding the agreement nobody could reasonably reject from a fair, neutral vantage point, protecting the worse-off party from too lopsided a deal. The difference is mostly technical rather than motivational: Rawls compares absolute payoffs at the worst-off position, Gauthier compares proportional concessions from the ideal point."
