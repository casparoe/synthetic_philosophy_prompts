I spent my working life as an atmospheric scientist working on weather and climate prediction, and one fact from that work has stayed with me since I took up philosophy in retirement. A weather forecast is essentially worthless beyond about two weeks, because small errors in the initial state double roughly every five days under the dynamics Lorenz identified in 1963. Yet the climate — the statistical envelope within which that unpredictable day-to-day trajectory wanders — can be projected with real confidence decades ahead, because we have an independent, quantitative theory of radiative and thermodynamic balance explaining why that envelope sits where it does and how it responds to a given perturbation.

I was struck, then, to find Christian Tarsney, in his paper on the epistemic challenge to longtermism, citing Lorenz's result as one reason to suspect that human societies might behave similarly, and then formalizing that worry as a model built around a rate r of what he calls "exogenous nullifying events." This rate discounts, at an exponential pace, the expected value of steering civilization into a more durable "persistent state" (Greaves and MacAskill's term; Tarsney's own label for the underlying worry — "Weak Attractors" — makes the dynamical-systems allusion explicit).

Write an essay assessing whether this borrowed vocabulary does real argumentative work or merely lends Tarsney's model an unearned aura of rigor. Is his rate r — formally a constant hazard of an exogenous shock — actually playing the role of a Lyapunov exponent, the rate at which nearby trajectories of a chaotic system lose correlation with their starting point, or is that a structurally different kind of uncertainty dressed in the language of chaos theory? And does the weather/climate distinction — fine-grained unpredictability compatible with coarse-grained predictability, but only given an independent macro-theory — make it more or less reasonable to expect r to be small for interventions aimed specifically at reducing extinction risk? Weigh the strongest considerations on each side, and close with a definite verdict on whether the epistemic challenge defeats strong longtermism outright, fails outright, or succeeds only against a specifiable class of interventions.
