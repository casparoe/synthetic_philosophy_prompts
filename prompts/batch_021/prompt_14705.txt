I spent most of my working life as a condensed-matter physicist, and only in retirement have I begun reading systematically in analytic metaphysics. One thread that keeps drawing me back in is the literature on metaphysical grounding, since the basic idea — that some facts obtain in virtue of, or are made true by, other, more fundamental facts — sounded at first like a philosopher's tidy formalization of the reductive picture I grew up with as a scientist, in which chemistry answers to physics, biology to chemistry, and so on. The longer I sit with it, though, the less sure I am that grounding is earning its keep as a genuinely new theoretical posit, rather than just relabeling something philosophers already had.

Here is specifically what I can't resolve on my own. Long before grounding became fashionable, philosophers of mind already had supervenience: mental facts supervene on physical facts just in case there can be no mental difference without some physical difference. That relation already seems to deliver an asymmetric dependence of the "higher" on the "lower," which is most of what grounding talk seems to want. So why posit, on top of it, some further primitive "in virtue of" relation? I recall a paper — by Jessica Wilson, if I have the name right — arguing that everything a unified "Big-G" grounding relation is supposed to do is already done, case by case, by specific "small-g" relations such as functional realization, the determinable/determinate relation, or mereological composition, making Big-G grounding explanatorily idle. Yet defenders such as Schaffer, Fine, and Rosen clearly think something real is lost without a single unified relation. Could you walk me through, in some technical depth, what exactly grounding is meant to supply that supervenience and the various small-g relations do not individually provide, what the strongest deflationary objections are, and how a committed grounding theorist would try to answer them?
