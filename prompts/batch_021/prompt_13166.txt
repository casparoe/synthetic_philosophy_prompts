I am drafting a novel whose protagonist, Rosalind, discovers a way to cross into nearby branches of her own life -- worlds that split off at three or four decisions she made differently -- where she can meet the women those decisions produced. I want to open the first crossing with an argument between the two people who accompany her: her old mentor Iris, a modal logician, and her partner Dev, a metaphysician. They disagree about what Rosalind is actually about to meet. Iris holds a version of David Lewis's counterpart theory: nothing exists in more than one branch, ever, so the woman waiting on the other side of the threshold is at most Rosalind's closest counterpart, tied to her only by resemblance and causal continuity -- and whatever happens to that woman never literally happens to Rosalind, however useful the relation is for making sense of claims about what Rosalind herself could have done. Dev defends the rival view that Rosalind can have a genuine identity across branches -- one and the same person twice over, not a stand-in -- and he presses something like Kripke's "Humphrey objection": that on Iris's view, Rosalind should feel no more invested in this woman's fate than in any stranger's, however uncannily alike, which seems flatly at odds with how Rosalind is about to react. I want Iris to have a real answer to that, not a shrug, and to push back by asking Dev what, if not resemblance and continuity, is even supposed to fix which woman on the other side counts as a candidate for "the same person" in the first place, and why identity should track some of Rosalind's divergences (a different job, a different marriage) but presumably not others (a Rosalind who was never born). Write the exchange itself -- a dozen or so turns, with real back-and-forth, so that neither Iris nor Dev is a strawman the other simply dispatches.
