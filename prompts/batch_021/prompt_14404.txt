I spent almost forty years as a condensed-matter physicist before retiring, and now I'm finally getting around to all the philosophy I used to wave off as "not even wrong." A while back I got curious about claims that relations might be more fundamental than the things they relate, something that comes up in a few readings of quantum entanglement and in "structural realism" debates, and that curiosity send me down a rabbit hole of Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy articles into something much older and weirder: Bradley's regress, the century old puzzle about what makes "aRb," say "the cat is on the mat," a genuinely unified fact rather than just three items (the cat, the on-relation, the mat) sitting next to each other like a grocery list. I brought it up at the little philosophy discussion group I joined at the rec center, and two of the regulars, Marcus and Priya, spent a good chunk of the evening going at each other over it in a way I honestly can't referee myself.

Marcus's opening move was that the regress is genuinely vicious: if a relation R is going to unite a and b, you need a further relation to tie R to a and b, and another to tie that one in turn, forever, so relations can never supply unity on their own, and somewhere you just have to posit, as a brute unexplained fact, that a and b stand in R, full stop. He said this is basically what Armstrong does with his "states of affairs," and there's nothing shameful about a theory that bottoms out in a primitive. Priya said thats not a theory, it's a surrender with better branding, exactly the "dormitive virtue" move, just relabeling the very unity you can't explain as a technical sounding posit. She brought up Frege's idea that concepts and relations are "unsaturated," with a built in gap that objects fill, so that unity isn't some extra ingredient bolted on afterward, it just falls out of what a relation *is*. Marcus's comeback was that "unsaturated" is a metaphor doing zero extra work: swap it for "fact-forming" and the explanatory content is identical, it just feels less like cheating because you get a vivid picture of gaps and things slotting into them. Priya pushed back that the metaphor isn't idle, because it explains something Marcus's brute posit can't: why "a loves b" is a different unity than "b loves a," since direction is built into which slot each object fills, whereas an unstructured "state of affairs" needs an ordering bolted on from outside to get that same asymmetry. Marcus's last word was that Russell tried to get exactly that kind of direction out of a relation based account with his multiple relation theory of judgment, and it collapsed under Wittgenstein's objection that the theory couldn't rule out judging plain nonsense without quietly smuggling back in the very idea of logical form it was supposed to explain, so he's not convinced "unsaturated" is any better off once somebody actually leans on it.

Could you go through this move by move and say who's got the better of it at each stage, rather than just declaring one of them the overall winner? I'd also be curious whether you think they're both dancing around some third option that neither one mentioned.
