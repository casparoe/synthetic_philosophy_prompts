At work last week, two people on my team got into a long argument over whether it even makes sense to grade a machine-translation model's output against one "correct" reference translation, the way BLEU-style metrics do, and it turned into a philosophy debate I'm not equipped to referee. It started when Marcus brought up Quine's "gavagai" thought experiment about the indeterminacy of translation: a field linguist hears a native speaker say "Gavagai" whenever a rabbit runs by, and, Quine argues, has no way even in principle to tell whether the word means "rabbit," "undetached rabbit part," or "rabbit-stage," since every translation manual built on any of these guesses fits the very same pattern of assent and dissent to the sentence. Roughly how it went:

1. Marcus said this shows there's no fact of the matter about which translation is correct below the level of whole-sentence meaning, so scoring our model against one "ground truth" translation is confused: there's no privileged answer sitting out there for either the model or a human translator to get right.
2. Elena answered that the puzzle dissolves under ordinary follow-up questions: show the informant a detached rabbit's foot and ask if "gavagai" still applies; a "no" refutes "undetached rabbit part." The indeterminacy, she said, is an artifact of Quine freezing the evidence at assent or dissent to one sentence under one stimulus — let the linguist probe the way any real translator would, and the ambiguity resolves like any other hypothesis that was merely underdetermined by too little data.
3. Marcus replied that Quine built the experiment to survive exactly that move: any single guess like "undetached rabbit part" can be tripped up by a clever question, but for any finite amount of questioning there is always some other systematic reassignment of meanings across the whole language that matches every answer given so far and diverges only on questions that could never actually be put to the informant — which, he said, is why Quine calls this indeterminate rather than merely hard to discover.
4. Elena said that move relocates the problem rather than solves it: physicists live with rival theories that are empirically equivalent under every possible test, and nobody concludes from that there's no fact of the matter about the physics, only that some facts are inaccessible to us. So Marcus, she said, is smuggling in a further premise — that wherever no possible evidence could settle a question there is nothing there to settle — and it's that premise, not the rabbit, doing all the real work.

Neither of them backed down, and I don't know this material well enough to say who actually had the better of it at each step.
