And here, more or less verbatim, is the student's answer: "According to Aquinas, rule by one man is the best form of government when it is good, but by the same logic tyranny — kingship corrupted into rule for the ruler's own advantage rather than the common good — is the worst, since a single bad ruler with concentrated power can inflict more harm than a corrupt group could. Addressing the practical question of what subjects may do about a tyrant, Aquinas concludes in Book I of De Regimine Principum that a private individual acting from genuine zeal for the common good, rather than personal ambition, may licitly kill him, since a ruler who governs for his own advantage rather than his subjects' has already forfeited the claim to obedience that legitimate authority rests on. He adds that where the community itself has the right to install its ruler, it is preferable for the community to depose him corporately rather than leave the matter to any one person, and that where no such recourse exists, the people should bear with him patiently and commit the matter to prayer, since converting or removing a tyrant's heart lies within God's power. Aquinas returns to the question in the Summa Theologiae's treatment of sedition (II-II, q. 42), arguing that resisting a tyrannical regime is not itself the sin of sedition, since a genuine tyranny already lacks the unity that true government requires. Drawing further on Roman historical examples in the later books of De Regimine Principum, Aquinas ultimately stakes out a considerably more permissive position on tyrannicide than most of his scholastic successors, who tended to restrict it to cases of outright usurpation."
