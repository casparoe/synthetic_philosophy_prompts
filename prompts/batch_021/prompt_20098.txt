I spent thirty-five years leading experimental collaborations in high-energy physics, and in retirement I have been working through the philosophical literature on peer disagreement. There I came across Aumann's agreement theorem, together with the common prior assumption on which it depends, and it seemed to supply, at last, a rigorous foundation for something I had long suspected: that two fully rational scientists confronted with identical data are guaranteed to reach the same conclusion, since rationality itself requires that they start out with the same prior probabilities. On that reading, every protracted dispute I watched play out between excellent colleagues — over whether some bump in a spectrum was signal or background, say — must ultimately be put down to a lapse in somebody's rationality, given that two rational Bayesians cannot coherently hold different priors in the first place. Have I got the common prior assumption right, or am I too quick to conclude that every one of those old disputes must trace back to someone's failure of reason?
