Quick one for you — I've been down a rabbit hole on Dutch book arguments for a piece on Bayesian epistemology, and I want to make sure I've got the actual objections straight, not just the elevator-pitch version. The standard story is: if your credences don't obey the probability axioms, a bookie can offer you a set of bets that you'd each accept as fair by your own lights, but which together guarantee you loose money no matter what happens — so incoherent credences must be irrational. Fair enough, but I keep running into people who say this only proves you're exploitable by a hypothetical bookie, not that you're irrational in some deeper epistemic sense, and that the "diachronic" version of the argument (the one that's supposed to justify updating your beliefs by conditionalization specifically) has an even bigger hole in it, since a sufficiently sharp agent could just refuse the second bet once they see the trap coming, and dodge the sure loss without conditionalizing at all. Can you walk me through the strongest version of that skeptical take, and how defenders of Dutch book arguments try to patch it up?
