I'm a PhD student in economics. My own research is in industrial organization, but I've been TAing an undergraduate course this semester that touches on choice under uncertainty, which is probably why a story my aunt told me over the holidays has been nagging at me since. She runs a small property-management company, and years ago she had a dispute with a contractor whose faulty plumbing work had caused water damage in one of her buildings. Before the case went to arbitration, she was offered a guaranteed settlement of $2,000; her lawyer at the time estimated that going to arbitration instead would give her a 75% chance of being awarded $3,000 in damages, against a 25% chance of getting nothing if the arbitrator decided the damage had predated the contractor's work. She took the guaranteed $2,000, which struck me as a perfectly sensible, moderately cautious decision.

More recently, a different contractor dispute came up, and this time there was no settlement on offer at all: she had to choose between two possible claims to bring to arbitration. A modest claim covering just the repair costs had, according to her new lawyer, a 40% chance of winning $2,000 and a 60% chance of winning nothing; a more ambitious claim that added consequential damages had only a 30% chance of winning $3,000, with a 70% chance of getting nothing. This time, she went with the more ambitious, longer-shot claim.

When I laid the two decisions out side by side, something about them felt hard to square, though I couldn't immediately say what. My aunt isn't an especially impulsive or inconsistent person, and each choice seemed reasonable enough on its own when she described it to me. Is there something genuinely inconsistent about holding both of these preferences at once — some sense in which they couldn't both be the expression of a single, coherent attitude toward risk — or am I overthinking two decisions that were made years apart, under different advice, and don't really need to line up with each other?
