I'm a retired condensed-matter physicist, and for the past year or so I've been teaching myself philosophy of mind, mostly so I have something better to say than "don't be silly" when my granddaughter (she's finishing a CS degree) asks whether the chatbot she uses for her homework might be a little bit concious. Rather than just read the pop-science coverage, I actually got a copy of Christof Koch's book on Integrated Information Theory, so I do understand the headline point, that consciousness is supposed to depend on a system's actual physical cause-and-effect structure and not on the function it computes -- meaning you can't just simulate your way to it on any old hardware.

Even so, it still seems to me that scale ought to count for something. A modern large language model has hundreds of billions of parameters, all densely interconnected, jointly shaping every output it produces, which sounds like about as much integration as any system could have. So granting that phi isn't just "amount of processing," shouldn't something that interconnected still come out with a pretty hefty phi under IIT -- more than a bee, say, even if nowhere near a human? When I brought this up with an old colleague of mine, an electrical engineer, he muttered something about feedforward architectures being "unfoldable" into independant parts, which I didn't really follow, and it's been bugging me ever since. Is there something specific about how these models are actually wired that would tank phi regardless of size, or is my instinct that sheer scale has to count for a lot basically right?
