I'm a retired physical chemist (spent thirty-odd years doing calorimetry and reaction kinetics before I hung up the lab coat), and over the last year I've fallen into the philosophy of science rabbit hole -- mostly Kuhn and Duhem, plus a stack of secondary commentary an old officemate keeps mailing me now that we're both retired. The idea that's really gotten under my skin is the underdetermination of theory by evidence: the Duhem-Quine point that no finite pile of evidence ever logically forces you into one theory over an empirically equivalent rival, because you can always patch the losing theory with some auxiliary hypothesis. I sat down and wrote a few paragraphs trying to think it through using an episode from my own field's history, and I'd like your comments before I inflict it on my little reading group next month.

Here's what I've got:

"Chemists my age were taught that the reality of atoms was settled science by 1900, but it plainly wasn't. Ostwald -- one of the great physical chemists of the era, and no crank -- spent the 1890s arguing that atoms were a convenient bookkeeping fiction, that energetics was the real physics and unobservable little billiard balls were metaphysics in a lab coat. He and Boltzmann went at it in public at Lübeck in 1895, and by most accounts Boltzmann didn't exactly carry the room. Boltzmann was dead by his own hand in 1906, two years before Perrin's Brownian motion work (riding on Einstein's 1905 paper) pinned down Avogadro's number so many independent ways at once -- sedimentation, diffusion, viscosity -- that Ostwald recanted in print. Mach, famously, never really did come around.

So here's my question: was the atom debate ever actually resolved the way Duhem's logic says these debates get resolved -- which is to say, not really? You can always save the 'convenient fiction' story with one more patch; Perrin's convergence across a dozen unrelated methods is just a spectacular coincidence, if your determined enough to read it that way. What actually moved people, Ostwald included, wasn't a refutation, it was that holding onto the fiction got so contrived that almost nobody but Mach could stomach it anymore. If that's right, then underdetermination is real in the strict logical sense but almost never decisive in practice, because what actually breaks ties in real science is something more like triangulation -- independent, structurally unrelated methods converging on one number -- and that bar is met only rarely. Which makes me wonder about two live cases: dark matter versus MOND, and the rival interpretations of quantum mechanics. Does either one have anything like Perrin's convergence available to it even in principle, or are they stuck being underdetermined in a way the atom debate, in the end, wasn't?"

Thats as far as I got. Three things I'd love your take on: first, whether I'm garbling the history anywhere -- I'm working from memory of a couple of popular history-of-science books, not the primary sources, so tell me if I've got Lübeck or the Ostwald recantation wrong; second, whether "underdetermination is real but rarely decisive, because triangulation is rare" is a position that already has a name, because I have a nagging feeling I'm re-deriving something Salmon or Hacking (one of the two -- I forget which) already worked out properly using the actual Perrin case; and third, whether the dark-matter/MOND and quantum-interpretation comparisons actually hold up, or whether there's a disanalogy I'm missing -- I'm a chemist, not a cosmologist or a quantum-foundations person, so I could easily be out of my depth there.
