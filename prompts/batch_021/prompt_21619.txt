I'm a graduate student in a formal epistemology seminar taught by my advisor, who is retiring at the end of this term after nearly four decades of work on common knowledge and the theory of disagreement. Most of this semester's syllabus has effectively been a tour of her own career: Aumann's original short paper on agreeing to disagree, her own extensions of it, and the Geanakoplos–Polemarchakis result showing that two people who simply keep exchanging their posterior probabilities back and forth, without ever stating their reasons, are forced to converge on the same number after only finitely many exchanges. The department is holding a dinner for her next month, and, as the last student to pass through her seminar before she retires, I've been asked to give the toast.

The difficulty is the guest list. Roughly half the room will be economists, statisticians, and philosophers, including the colleague with whom she has spent almost thirty years in a genial, entirely unresolved dispute over whether the common prior assumption is quietly doing all the interesting work in her own theorem; people like that will notice immediately if I say something sloppy about what the theorem does and does not actually establish, and will bring it up at brunch for years afterward. The other half will be her husband, her sister, and a dozen or so relatives whose only prior exposure to any of this is having been told, gently but repeatedly at family dinners, that "let's just agree to disagree" is not, strictly speaking, available to two honest people whose beliefs about the matter have become common knowledge to each other.

I tried drafting this myself, and it came out sounding like the preamble to a problem set, which is not what a room full of people who love her needs to sit through. Could you write the toast instead -- about five minutes long, built around one genuine joke about agreeing to disagree, the kind that a specialist would not silently take issue with, because it actually respects the theorem's assumptions, but that still works as a joke -- and as an act of real affection -- for the people at the table who have never once heard the phrase "common prior"?
