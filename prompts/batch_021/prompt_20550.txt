A few of us in my department run an informal formal-epistemology reading group -- more grad students and leftover pizza than anything official -- and next week we've got someone joining over Zoom for an hour of Q&A, which I should probably be spending on studying for quals instead. She's built her whole research career around defending impossible-worlds semantics as the fix for the problem of logical omniscience: the standard worry that if you model belief or knowledge as truth-in-every-world-you-can't-rule-out, you get the absurd result that believing the Peano axioms commits you to believing every theorem that follows from them, proved or not, and that believing any proposition commits you to believing everything logically equivalent to it. Her move is to let some of the worlds in the model be logically impossible, so an agent can be doxastically stuck in a world where P and "P implies Q" both hold while Q fails -- which is more or less what Hintikka himself ended up doing to patch his own semantics once people started pointing this out to him. When someone objects that this doesn't actually explain the failure of inference, just relocates it into an unmotivated restriction on which impossible worlds count as accesible, she has a stock line -- "you're asking the model to do psychology's job for it" -- that tends to end the conversation rather than continue it.

I want 8 to 10 questions that don't let her just restate the view, and that don't let that line do the same trick twice. At minimum I need: one that goes straight at the "relocates rather than explains" objection and specifically doesn't let the psychology line dodge it a second time; one that asks her to actually distinguish forgivable logical ignorance (missing a hard theorem) from unforgivable logical ignorance (not seeing that P follows from P-and-Q) within her own framework, rather than just hand-fitting the accessibility relation case by case; and one that makes her defend impossible worlds head to head against an approach that has an actual mechanism behind it, like awareness logic or logical induction, instead of a static semantic stipulation. Give me a follow-up for each of those three keyed to whatever answer she's likely to give. Close the list with one question about what result or counterexample would actually get her to abandon impossible-worlds semantics, since in three years I've never heard her name one.
