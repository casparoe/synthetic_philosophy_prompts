I have no formal background in mathematics or philosophy, but I've spent much of the last year reading about decision theory on my own, and the St. Petersburg paradox is the puzzle that has stuck with me longest. Bernoulli's classic fix — pricing the gamble by its expected utility rather than its expected value, since extra money satisfies you less the more of it you already have — seemed to close the case, until I worked out that it doesn't quite: whatever curve you pick for how satisfaction grows with wealth, so long as it keeps climbing without limit, someone can design a new version of the game with prizes tuned to that exact curve, and the expected utility diverges all over again. The only real escape seems to be a utility function with a hard ceiling on satisfaction, and that comes with its own cost, since past that ceiling no odds could make certain gambles worth taking. I'd like a short story built around this second, less familiar layer of the paradox: someone who believes they've found a principled, finite price for an infinite bet, and a counterpart who keeps re-engineering the offer to defeat exactly that reasoning, until the character must either declare a hard ceiling on their own well-being or keep raising the price forever. Keep each escalation mathematically honest rather than merely hand-wavy, and don't let the ending resolve that discomfort too neatly.
