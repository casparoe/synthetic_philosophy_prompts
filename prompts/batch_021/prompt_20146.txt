I am a novelist currently drafting a book set in the late Warring States period, one of whose central characters is an aging recluse modeled closely on Zhuangzi. In the chapter I am working on now, this character's wife dies, and rather than mourning in the conventional manner, he is discovered sitting with his legs sprawled out, drumming on an upturned basin and singing — a scene I have lifted almost directly from the "Perfect Happiness" chapter of the Zhuangzi, where Hui Shi arrives to console his old friend and instead finds him in this state. I have been working mostly from Burton Watson's translation, and I have always taken that episode as the classic proof-text for a fairly strong claim: that the fully awakened Daoist sage does not grieve at all. Once a person truly absorbs the idea that a human being is only a temporary knot of vital breath that must eventually come undone, in the same impersonal way that summer gives way to autumn, grief simply loses its grip on him — much as a thoroughly trained Stoic is said to be untouched by the death of someone he loves. I had planned to underline this later in the book with a companion scene, drawn from Zhuangzi's own reported deathbed remarks, in which he waves off an elaborate burial by observing that he will be eaten either way, by crows and kites above ground or by mole crickets and ants below it, so that the two scenes would bookend the character's life with the same unbroken serenity.

My difficulty with this is a practical one, though I suspect it may not only be practical. Two readers in my writing group who saw the draft said the widower scene left them cold rather than moved, and one went so far as to call the character "sociopathic" rather than wise. Given that this total absence of grief is what Zhuangzi's story is actually showing us, I would like help thinking through how to fix the scene rather than the philosophy behind it: what might I add, cut, or borrow from elsewhere in the same episode, or from the deathbed passage, so that readers experience the character's composure as hard-won rather than as a simple failure to feel anything?
