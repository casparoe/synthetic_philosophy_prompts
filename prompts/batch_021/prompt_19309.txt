The problem: start with the classic two-envelope setup — one envelope has twice as much money as the other, you're handed one, and the (fallacious) argument for always switching says that if A is the amount in your envelope, the other one has either 2A or A/2 "with probability 1/2 each," making its expected value 5A/4. To pin the puzzle down precisely, we define the smaller amount as follows: flip a fair coin repeatedly until it first comes up heads; if that takes n tails, the smaller envelope contains $10×2^n. The larger envelope then contains twice that. The two envelopes are shuffled and you're handed one uniformly at random. Given this fully specified model: (a) suppose you open your envelope and find $80 — using Bayes' rule, find the probability that yours is the smaller envelope of the pair, and use it to compute the expected value of switching; should you switch? (b) In light of (a), explain exactly where the classic "always switch" argument goes wrong. (c) Does the conclusion in (a) hold no matter what amount you find in your envelope under this coin-flipping model, or are there amounts where the answer comes out differently? And more generally, does simply insisting on a legitimate, properly normalized prior (as opposed to an impossible flat one) guarantee that switching is always a matter of indifference? Defend your answer rather than just asserting it.

Priya's answer: (a) Call the smaller amount S. By construction, P(S = $10×2^n) = (1/2)^(n+1) for n = 0, 1, 2, .... If my envelope (showing $80) is the smaller one, then S = $80, so n = 3, and I must also have "won" the 50/50 toss for being handed the smaller envelope: probability (1/2)^4 × (1/2) = (1/2)^5 = 1/32. If instead my envelope is the larger one, then S = $40, so n = 2, and I must have been handed the larger envelope: probability (1/2)^3 × (1/2) = (1/2)^4 = 1/16 = 2/32. Normalizing, P(mine is smaller | I see $80) = 1/3 and P(mine is larger | I see $80) = 2/3. So E[switch] = (1/3)(160) + (2/3)(40) = 160/3 + 80/3 = 240/3 = $80 — exactly what I already have. I'm indifferent; there's no reason to switch. (b) The classic argument treats P(other = 2A) and P(other = A/2) as both equal to 1/2 no matter what A turns out to be. What (a) shows is that this is false: given $80, it's actually twice as likely that I'm holding the larger envelope as the smaller one. Once you replace the naive "50/50" step with the real, value-dependent posterior odds that come from an actual prior, the skew in those odds (1/3 vs. 2/3 here) exactly cancels the asymmetry in the payoffs (doubling vs. halving), which is why switching and staying come out equal instead of switching coming out ahead. (c) Nothing about this depended on the number 80 in particular. For a general n, being handed the smaller envelope of the pair ($10×2^n, $10×2^(n+1)) has probability (1/2)^(n+1) × (1/2) = (1/2)^(n+2), and being handed the larger envelope of the pair ($10×2^(n-1), $10×2^n) has probability (1/2)^n × (1/2) = (1/2)^(n+1) — always a 1:2 ratio, giving the same 1/3–2/3 split and the same exact cancellation for every n. So the indifference conclusion holds no matter what amount you find in the envelope. More generally, I think this is the real resolution of the whole paradox: as long as your prior is an honest, properly normalized distribution over the possible amounts, rather than the impossible "uniform over all positive numbers" distribution the naive argument secretly relies on, the posterior odds will always shift by exactly enough to cancel the doubling/halving payoffs. The feeling that you should switch is never anything more than a symptom of having smuggled in a prior that doesn't actually exist.
