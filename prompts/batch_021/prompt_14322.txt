I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums for long enough that I came to David Lewis's paper "The Paradoxes of Time Travel" rather late, and mostly by way of the comparisons people draw between it and the Novikov self-consistency principle, which gets invoked rather breezily in the Time-Turner arc of Harry Potter and the Methods of Rationality. A friend from my reading group and I have spent the last couple of weeks arguing about how seriously that comparison should be taken, and I would like an outside opinion before either of us has to concede anything.

My position, which he disputes, is roughly this. The physics of self-consistency, as illustrated by the wormhole billiard-ball papers that Kip Thorne and his collaborators wrote in the early nineties, seems to require some extra constraint layered on top of ordinary local dynamics: something that reaches into the ball's trajectory, or into the time traveler's gun, or his nerve, and quietly guarantees that no locally permissible chain of events is ever completed if doing so would produce a paradox. When I finally read Lewis's paper properly, it struck me that his celebrated distinction between two senses of "can" -- Tim can kill Grandfather, relative to his skill and opportunity, but cannot, relative to the wider facts of the case -- is really the same guarantee wearing philosophical dress: however he slices up the word "can," Lewis still needs it to be a settled fact, rather than a mere coincidence, that Tim never succeeds. So the physicists' consistency principle and Lewis's ambiguity in our ordinary concept of ability strike me as the same substantive commitment, stated twice over, once in the vocabulary of differential equations and once in the vocabulary of modal logic. My friend keeps insisting these are doing entirely different kinds of work, and that "he can't, because it would violate self-consistency" and "he can't, because it isn't compossible with the facts we are holding fixed" are not the same move in different clothing. I remain unconvinced. If the two really do come to the same thing, I don't understand why the philosophy of time travel keeps treating Lewis's paper as though it dissolved something that the physics literature still has to labor to earn through explicit calculation -- shouldn't we say instead that Lewis got the easy version of the same result for free, just by talking about "can" instead of solving equations?
