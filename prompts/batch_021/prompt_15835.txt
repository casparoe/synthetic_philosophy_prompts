I'm a physics PhD student, and this term I've been sitting in on a joint physics-and-philosophy reading group, largely because my own work touches on permutation invariance and gauge symmetry, and my advisor thought it would be useful to see how philosophers of science handle cases where a theory's equations survive intact while its underlying picture of "what there is" changes completely. A couple of sessions ago we worked through John Worrall's classic argument for structural realism, built around the transition from Fresnel's theory of light to Maxwell's electromagnetism, meant to let realists keep the "no miracles" intuition about science's success without falling to the pessimistic meta-induction from theory change. I found the basic move genuinely attractive: be a realist about the mathematical structure a theory posits, agnostic about the nature of whatever stands in the relevant relations.

Afterward, though, a philosophy postdoc in the group told me the position is widely held to founder on "Newman's objection": if a theory's content is exhausted by the abstract structure it describes, then, provided the domain merely has the right cardinality, some relation among its objects will always realize exactly that structure, so specifying "the structure" ends up conveying little more than how many objects there are. She added that the more radical, "ontic" version of structural realism (associated with writers like Ladyman and French) — which denies there are individual objects with hidden natures at all, partly on the strength of the same quantum-statistics worries about particle individuality I'd been reading about for my own thesis — only dodges the objection by quietly trading plain, set-theoretic structure for modal structure, and that this trade is itself contested. I came away unable to properly evaluate either side, and would like to.

Could you write a dialogue between two structural realists who take each other's views seriously: one an epistemic structural realist who wants to answer Newman's objection by restricting the relevant relations to natural, physically privileged ones rather than arbitrary ones; the other an ontic structural realist who thinks the only real fix is to deny that there are relata with natures over and above the structure, and to understand structure modally rather than extensionally. Have them work through the cardinality/triviality worry in enough technical detail that the disagreement is concrete, and have each press the other on whether their preferred fix actually defuses the objection or just relocates it — in particular, whether leaning on modal structure secretly reintroduces the sort of "nature talk" that ontic structural realism was supposed to eliminate. I'd like each side to concede at least one genuine cost of their own position in the course of the exchange, and I don't want an artificially tidy resolution at the end, since my sense is that the literature itself hasn't reached one.
