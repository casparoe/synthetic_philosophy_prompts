I am working on a column about Gottlob Frege and would value a second opinion before I file it. Below is my draft. I am not looking for a line edit — I would like your reaction to the substance, especially the three questions at the end.

Gottlob Frege invented modern quantificational logic, gave analytic philosophy its founding distinction between sense and reference, and spent two decades trying to show that arithmetic is nothing but logic in disguise, an effort Russell's paradox eventually wrecked. By general agreement he belongs on any short list of the most important philosophers of the last two centuries, and his prose is a model of austere, impersonal argument.

He was also the author, in the last full year of his life, of a private diary that reads like a minor manifesto of the Weimar far right: contempt for parliamentary democracy and the postwar constitution, a wish to see Jews stripped of political rights, and a longing for a single strong will that could do away with party politics altogether. None of this was public knowledge until the diary turned up among his papers decades after his death. Michael Dummett — a lifelong anti-racism campaigner, and the scholar who did more than anyone to place Frege at the center of the analytic canon — wrote candidly about how badly the discovery shook him.

The interesting question is not whether Frege held repugnant views; he plainly did. It is whether the diary tells us anything about the philosophy, or only about the man. Dummett's own answer was, in effect, quarantine: a valid proof stays valid regardless of who proves it, so Frege's logic and philosophy of mathematics can be judged on grounds entirely separate from his politics.

I want to press on one place where I do not think quarantine quite holds. Frege's great and permanent target was psychologism, the idea that logical laws merely describe how humans happen to think and might therefore vary between people or cultures. Against this he insisted that thoughts inhabit a "third realm," objective and answerable to no one's perspective, so that apparent disagreement about logic can never reflect two reasonable views, only someone's error. That same reflex — one correct view, and only confusion or corruption elsewhere — reappears, in an uglier register, in the diary's scorn for a parliament of competing "particular interests" and its yearning for a will above faction. I am not claiming the logic entails the politics, or that either caused the other. I am suggesting they are two expressions of one intolerance for legitimate plurality, and that noticing this should make quarantine harder to hold onto than Dummett wanted.

Before I file this, I would like your reaction to three things. First, is the parallel between Frege's logical objectivism and his political absolutism doing real philosophical work, or is it just a pleasing pattern-match with no content behind it? Second, plenty of convinced logical objectivists are committed political pluralists, so am I being unfair to the position by implying any elective affinity with authoritarianism? Third, is there a stronger version of "the diary matters philosophically" than the temperamental one I have sketched — Hans Sluga's earlier attempt to read Frege's logical Platonism as continuous with a broader current of anti-naturalist, conservative thought in Wilhelmine academic culture seems like it might supply one. Also flag anything I have gotten wrong about the history.
