I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and I'm currently working on a feature about how arguments from philosophy of science get invoked, and sometimes stretched past what they can actually support, in public disputes over scientific authority. While reporting it, I kept running into a line of reasoning that I want to represent accurately, since it anchors a section of the piece. It runs roughly like this: Duhem observed that no single hypothesis in physics is ever tested in isolation, because deriving a testable prediction always requires a raft of auxiliary assumptions about instruments, background conditions, and the like; consequently, when a prediction fails, logic alone never tells us whether to blame the theory or one of those auxiliary assumptions. Quine later generalized this into the claim that, given sufficiently drastic revisions elsewhere in our web of belief, virtually any statement can be held onto no matter what evidence comes in. Several people I've interviewed for the piece take the moral of this Duhem-Quine thesis to be that the Ptolemaic astronomers who kept bolting new epicycles onto geocentrism to accommodate each new observation were not being any less rational than the astronomers who eventually adopted the Copernican and then Keplerian picture: both groups were just making internally consistent adjustments to fit the data, and the field's ultimate preference for heliocentrism reflects little more than a taste for simplicity that, on this view, underdetermination shows has no special claim to tracking truth.

For the piece, I'd like to walk readers through why this really does follow from Duhem's and Quine's arguments: why underdetermination puts epicycle-patching and theory-switching on equal rational footing, and why the field's eventual preference for simplicity can never be more than an aesthetic tiebreaker dressed up as evidence. Could you help me spell that reasoning out clearly, with the relevant concepts and names attached, so that I represent the philosophy accurately?
