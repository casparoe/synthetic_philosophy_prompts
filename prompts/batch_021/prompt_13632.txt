I'm a PhD student in conservation biology, and this term I'm taking a philosophy of science seminar that spent two sessions on the species problem: the difficulty of pinning down "species" to any single biological reality, given how often reproductive-isolation criteria, phylogenetic diagnosability, ecological niche, and plain morphology come apart in practice. I didn't expect that seminar to bear on my actual work, but this semester I've been placed as a graduate fellow with my state's Wildlife Commission, and its Scientific Advisory Panel is now facing exactly this problem in a live case I've been asked to help write up.

The Panel is reviewing a petition to delist the Pineland wolf, which has held state endangered status since the 1980s and survives today mainly through a captive-breeding and reintroduction program centered on a coastal refuge. A coalition of landowners is petitioning for delisting on the strength of a new whole-genome study of the wild, reintroduced population, which finds average coyote ancestry upwards of 50%, with only a handful of surviving animals scoring above 90% wolf ancestry on the same genetic panel; they argue that an admixed population is not the "distinct species" that the original 1980s listing certified on morphological grounds, so the original listing rests on a taxonomic mistake that better evidence can now correct. Commission biologists counter that the hybridization is itself downstream of the very near-extirpation the listing was meant to fix — coyotes moved into the range only once wolves had been trapped and poisoned to the edge of extinction — that the reintroduced animals remain morphologically and ecologically distinct from the coyotes around them even now, and that several of the wolf's uncontested close relatives elsewhere hybridize just as freely at their own range edges without anyone proposing to strike those species from the books. Our statute, like its federal counterpart, protects "species" and allows a "distinct population segment" to be listed as though it were one, but defines neither term beyond that, and the Panel chair — who has no interest in refereeing the metaphysics of species between geneticists and lawyers — has asked me to draft the options memo going to the full Commission next month.

Could you write that memo? It should give the Commission one clear recommendation — uphold the existing listing, grant the delisting petition, or continue protection under some modified designation (for instance, covering only certain individuals or subpopulations) — together with a concrete timetable for acting on it or revisiting it, rather than an open-ended mandate. It should say explicitly which conception, or combination, of species is doing the real work in that recommendation, rather than invoking "the science" as though the science alone could settle which concept applies here. And it should take the landowners' argument seriously on its own terms: if the Panel is going to recommend continued protection for a population this admixed, it owes them a real answer to "this isn't the species you originally listed," not just a restatement of the conservation stakes.
