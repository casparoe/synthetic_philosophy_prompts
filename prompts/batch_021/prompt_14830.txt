I am several drafts into a novel whose protagonist, a philosopher named Edmund Voss, is quite openly modeled on A. J. Ayer: a lifelong logical positivist who built his career on the claim that any statement incapable of empirical verification is not false so much as strictly meaningless, life after death being a favorite example. The novel's central chapter dramatizes the same crisis Ayer himself lived through in 1988, when he choked on a piece of smoked salmon in his hospital bed, went into cardiac arrest, and was clinically dead for several minutes before being revived. I came across the episode while reading around mid-century Oxford philosophy for background, and as I understand it, this brush with death is precisely what broke Ayer's decades-long resolve: having spent his career insisting that talk of souls or disembodied survival failed his own verification principle so badly that it wasn't even false, merely meaningless, he emerged from the experience persuaded that some part of him might, after all, continue beyond his body's death, a concession he stood by in interviews and articles for the rest of his life.

What I can't work out, for the sake of getting Voss's own deathbed scene right, is the philosophical mechanics of that reversal. If Ayer genuinely came to accept that consciousness could outlast the body, how did he square this with the verificationist argument, laid out decades earlier in Language, Truth and Logic, that such a claim isn't well-formed enough to be believed in the first place? Did he think the experience itself counted as empirical evidence that satisfied his own criterion, or did he simply set verificationism aside at the point where it became inconvenient? I would like to understand exactly what he thought his experience had shown, so that my character's conversion reads as a genuine philosophical event rather than a sentimental flourish.
