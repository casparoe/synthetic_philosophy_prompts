I sit as the lay member on a three-person mental health tribunal panel — the other two are a consultant psychiatrist and a lawyer who chairs our hearings — and next week we're reviewing whether to continue a compulsory treatment order on a patient I'll call Mrs. R. About three weeks ago she stopped sleeping, started talking rapidly and almost without pause, and told her family that God had spoken to her audibly and instructed her to give away her savings before a specific date because the Rapture was coming. She belongs to a small Pentecostal congregation, and her pastor and two fellow congregants have given statements that hearing God's voice directly and expecting an imminent end-time are ordinary, orthodox parts of their shared faith, not evidence that something has gone wrong with her. The treating psychiatrist's report calls the same statements grandiose religious delusions and auditory hallucinations consistent with a manic episode, leaning heavily on how suddenly this started, the total loss of sleep, and the fact that she tried to hand cash to strangers on the street — which her daughter says is wildly out of character.

I keep coming back to something I picked up secondhand, from a philosophy podcast rather than anything I've read properly myself: apparently Wittgenstein argued, in some lectures on religious belief, that a person's faith commitments aren't held or contested the way ordinary factual beliefs are — that believing in the Last Judgement isn't like believing there's a train at platform four, answerable to the same sort of evidence, and that someone who denies it isn't contradicting the believer in quite the way you'd contradict someone about the train. If there's something to that, it seems to cut against treating Mrs. R's statements as delusional simply because they wouldn't survive ordinary fact-checking. But our psychiatrist member has already said, informally, that if "her church says this is normal" is allowed to settle the matter, we'd never be able to intervene on anyone whose beliefs happen to be shared by a large enough congregation, no matter how acute their crisis looks otherwise — and I don't think that objection is a stupid one either.

Could you draft the short memo I'd circulate to the other two members before the hearing? It should recommend one specific outcome — continue the order, discharge her outright, or discharge with particular conditions and a review date — and it should actually explain, in terms the psychiatrist member could engage with rather than wave away, how one would tell a case like this apart from a straightforward, uncomplicated religious conviction, without either letting "it's her faith" become a blanket veto on clinical judgment or quietly treating unusual religious content as illness by default.
