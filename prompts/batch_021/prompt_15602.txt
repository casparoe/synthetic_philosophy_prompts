I recently joined the clinical ethics advisory committee at the hospital network where I work as a genetic counselor, and next month we have to deliver a recommendation on a case that has split the committee more than anything I've seen in three years there. A partner lab wants to pilot an "opportunistic" expanded panel: patients coming in for routine pre-surgical bloodwork can opt in to have the same sample screened for adult-onset conditions we can't currently prevent or treat, including APOE4 status for Alzheimer's risk and confirmatory testing for Huntington's in patients with a family history who have so far chosen not to find out. The lab's bioethicist, arguing for approval, kept invoking something she called "Good's theorem" — I gather it's an actual result in Bayesian decision theory showing that a rational agent's expected utility from acquiring information before a decision is never lower than from declining it, cost aside — to argue that since results are opt-in and can simply be left unopened, adding friction like mandatory pre-test counseling only lowers uptake of something that can only help patients in expectation. Several of us are unconvinced this settles anything, both because plenty of our patients respond to a probabilistic, untreatable diagnosis in ways that look nothing like tidy Bayesian updating, and because "opt-in" hasn't stopped results from shaping insurance and employment decisions elsewhere in the system. The pilot is scheduled to start intake in about six weeks regardless of what we decide.

My own mother's APOE4 result three years ago is a fair part of why I find it hard to think about this case on my own steam, which is why I'd rather work from someone else's reasoning here. Could you draft the memo I'll circulate to the committee before the vote? I need it to land on one concrete recommendation — approve the pilot as proposed, reject it, or approve it with specific conditions — attached to a definite timeline, and I need it to actually engage with the bioethicist's argument on its own terms, including where the theorem does and doesn't reach, rather than just asserting a "right not to know" against it without addressing her point.
