I'm reporting a longer feature on the "coherence arguments" that show up constantly in AI-doom writing -- the idea, going back to money-pump and Dutch-book style reasoning, that any agent whose preferences aren't representable as maximizing some expected utility function can be exploited by a sequence of trades that leave it strictly worse off by its own lights, so a sufficiently capable, self-correcting system will end up behaving like an expected-utility maximizer, which is supposedly part of why we should expect advanced AI to end up with stable goals that resist being changed. I got two sources arguing about this over drinks after a workshop last month and honestly haven't been able to stop turning it over since.

Mara, who does technical alignment work, laid out that argument pretty much as above. Dev, who left the same lab a while back for a philosophy PhD, pushed back that "behaves like an EU-maximizer for some utility function" is nearly empty, since literally any sequence of choices, no matter how erratic, can be rationalized after the fact by a sufficiently gerrymandered, history-indexed utility function -- one that basically just says "whatever you were about to do was the right thing" -- so immunity to money-pumping tells you nothing about whether a system has the kind of simple, stable, world-directed goals that would actually make it dangerous. Mara answered that this gerrymandering is a cheat that only works if the agent is willing to lug around a needlessly complicated utility function, and that any pressure toward efficiency in a self-improving system should push it back toward simpler, more "object-level" goals -- at which point the danger she started with comes right back. Dev's comeback was that this concedes his point: now the argument needs an extra premise about simplicity being privileged, which is a substantive claim about what agents will want, not just about how they choose, and that extra premise was never supposed to be needed for a "coherence" argument to do it's work on its own. Neither of them budged.

Could you go through the four moves -- Mara's opening case, Dev's rationalization objection, Mara's simplicity reply, and Dev's rejoinder -- and say who's got the better of it at each stage, not just who wins overall? And if there's some consideration you think both of them are skating past, I'd like to hear about that too.
