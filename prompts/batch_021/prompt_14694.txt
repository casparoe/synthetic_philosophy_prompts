I spent most of my working life as an atmospheric physicist, and since retiring I've been spending my mornings on philosophy instead of committee meetings -- lately the literature on imprecise, or "indeterminate," probabilities. Two old colleagues of mine, Hendrik (a statistician) and Marta (a physicist who now consults on catastrophe risk for an insurer), still argue by email the way we all used to argue over coffee, and they cc'd me on a thread last week that started from something ordinary -- how to state uncertainty about a poorly constrained physical parameter, the sort where good groups looking at the same evidence have long settled on stably different central estimates rather than converging (equilibrium climate sensitivity was the textbook example of this for decades, before the most recent IPCC assessment finally narrowed things down) -- and turned into a genuine philosophical argument between them.

(1) Hendrik opened by insisting that in cases of genuinely stable expert disagreement like that, honesty requires representing your uncertainty as an imprecise probability -- a whole set of credence functions, not a single distribution -- because picking one sharp number there isn't decisiveness, it's manufacturing precision nobody has. (2) Marta answered that this sounds principled but doesn't survive contact with actually deciding anything: once your credence is a set rather than a number, expected-utility comparisons between rival policies stop returning a single answer, and every proposed fix -- Gamma-maximin (maximize your worst-case expected utility across the set), E-admissibility (permit any option that maximizes expected utility for at least one distribution in the set), whatever -- either smuggles a sharp probability back in, or, across a sequence of two decisions, can be shown to recommend a pair of choices that's jointly dominated by some other pair you could have picked instead. An agent who has to act at all, she said, thereby reveals sharp probabilities whether she admits it or not. (3) Hendrik replied that the domination worry only bites if each decision has to be evaluated in isolation, with no chance to reconsider once the next one comes into view, and nothing in decision theory actually requires that; refusing to fake precision now and revisiting the question as more evidence arrives is the rational response to real ambiguity, not a failure of nerve. (4) Marta then brought up dilation -- cases where conditioning an imprecise credal set on new evidence widens it, for every possible outcome, rather than narrowing it -- and said that any theory of rational belief on which learning something can leave you less informed than you started is in serious trouble. (5) Hendrik countered that dilation only shows up under a fairly specific correlation structure between the new evidence and the parameter at issue, that it's very much the exception rather than the rule, and that one counterintuitive result in a contrived edge case is a poor reason to abandon a framework that gets the ordinary case -- honestly reporting real ambiguity -- right.

Neither of them budged, and my own sympathies keep flipping depending on which argument I last reread. Could you adjudicate the exchange move by move?
