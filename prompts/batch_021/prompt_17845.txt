Here's the answer I have to grade:

"Turing takes ESP more seriously than you'd expect from a mathematician. He runs through the four standard phenomena - telepathy, clairvoyance, precognition and psychokinesis - and says the statistical case for telepathy specifically is strong enough that it can't just be dismissed, which is a strange concession from someone otherwise so committed to a tidy scientific picture of the world. This matters for the imitation game because if the human player happens to be a telepathic 'sensitive' and the interrogator asks the kind of question a sensitive could answer better than chance, the man could give the game away simply by being too good at a task the machine has no way of performing, so the test would stop tracking intelligence and start tracking an unrelated, paranormal knack.

Turing's way of handling this is really the same move he makes everywhere else on his list of things critics say machines will never do - eat strawberries and cream, fall in love, and so on - he bites the bullet. He accepts that if telepathy is real then there is at least one channel of information a digital computer could never reproduce even in principle, so a perfectly ordinary computer could lose the game to a sufficiently gifted human every time. What he actually proposes, practically, is to run the test inside a 'telepathy-proof room,' which stops this particular problem from arising in any given sitting of the game, but it only does that by admitting that the game's verdict has nothing to say about whether the computer can think in the way the sensitive human plainly can."
