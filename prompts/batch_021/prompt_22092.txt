I'm a bio/philosophy double major, and this week's philosophy of biology seminar was all about the etiological vs. causal-role debate on biological function -- the idea that a trait's function is whatever it was selected for by natural selection (Wright's original version, sharpened later by Neander), versus Cummins' view that a trait's function is just whatever it currently contributes to some capacity of the containing system you happen to be analyzing, no evolutionary history required. Our professor kept saying the two views cash out differently on "vestigial" structures, and I didn't really get why that mattered until literally this week, when almost the exact same argument broke out on a committee I'm on.

I volunteer at my school's natural history museum, and I just got added to the small exhibits-text committee that signs off on placard copy before it goes to the fabricator. We're redoing the human evolution display, and the draft panel (written by an outside contractor) says flatly that the appendix, the tailbone, and impacted wisdom teeth are "vestigial structures that no longer serve any function." One of our oldest volunteers, a retired anatomy professor, wants to keep that line exactly as written because its punchy and drives home the point that evolution doesn't design things for our convenience -- good ammo against "everything in the body has a purpose" type arguments. But the newest member, an immunology grad student, is annoyed, since the appendix is now widely thought to house beneficial gut bacteria and immune tissue, and the tailbone anchors several pelvic-floor and gluteal muscles -- so on her view "no function" isn't a simplification, it's just false, depending on which notion of function you're using.

The exhibit reopens in three weeks and copy is due to the fabricator by Friday, so the chair asked me, as "the philosophy person now," to write up a short recommendation memo before then. Can you draft that memo for me? I want it to actually settle on specific wording (or at least a specific process for settling on the wording), give a real deadline for getting it done, and take the anatomy professor's pedagogical worry seriously -- I don't think it's crazy to worry that hedged, careful language will blunt the exhibit's whole point for a bunch of eighth graders on a field trip -- rather than just siding with whoever happens to have the more up to date biology.
