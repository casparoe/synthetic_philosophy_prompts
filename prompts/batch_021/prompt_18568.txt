Earlier this year, a close friend and I had agreed that she would co-sign the lease on a two-bedroom apartment with me; a few weeks before we were due to sign, she told me she had changed her mind, having found a cheaper studio for herself in the meantime. I don't think she was ever lying to me about her intentions when she first agreed to it -- as far as I can tell, she genuinely meant to go through with it at the time -- but I was left scrambling to find a new co-signer, and I still feel that she wronged me somehow, even though no dishonesty seems to have been involved at any point. When I mentioned this to a friend from my undergraduate philosophy days, she pointed me toward Hume's discussion of promising in the Treatise, and from there I went on to read some of Scanlon's more recent work on the same topic.

Reading the two side by side, it seems to me that both accounts -- and really the debate on promising more generally -- ultimately trace the wrongness of breaking a promise back to the wrongness of lying: since a promise just is the sincere declaration of a present intention, going back on one is only a distinct moral failing to the extent that doing so exposes that original declaration as having been dishonest, whether because you never intended to follow through in the first place or because you were careless in overestimating your own resolve. On that reading, since my friend seems to have been sincere throughout -- both when she promised and when she later changed her mind -- none of the theories I've read actually support the judgment that she wronged me; my sense of having been betrayed would just be an intuition that outruns anything the philosophy underwrites. Is that a fair summary of where Hume, Scanlon, and this literature more broadly come down, or am I flattening something important that these accounts are actually trying to capture?
