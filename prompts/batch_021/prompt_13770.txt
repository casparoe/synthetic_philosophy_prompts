Consider a society that must choose between two long-run resource policies. Under "Depletion," resources are used quickly: the next two generations enjoy a markedly higher standard of living, but every generation after that has a lower standard of living than it otherwise would — still clearly worth living, but well below what could have been sustained. Under "Conservation," resources are used more slowly, so the standard of living is a little lower now but remains constant, and higher than the long-run Depletion level, forever after. Assume that on either policy the same number of people would ever live; they would simply be different people, since the two policies lead to different economic conditions, and hence different marriages and conceptions, within a couple of generations. By the time the long-run effects of either policy are felt, essentially no one who exists under Depletion would ever have existed under Conservation, and vice versa.

This is the question a student was asked, together with the answer submitted:

Question:
(a) Explain why this is a case of the Non-Identity Problem, and why a person-affecting principle — "a policy is wrong only if it is worse for some particular person than the alternative would have been for that person" — has difficulty condemning Depletion.
(b) State and explain the principle that Parfit proposes for handling cases of this kind, and its intended scope.
(c) Does Parfit conclude, on the strength of the Non-Identity Problem, that Depletion is not after all wrong? Explain.

Student's answer:

(a) This is a Non-Identity Problem case because the identities of the people who will be affected are not independent of which policy is chosen. Who is conceived, by whom, and when, depends on prevailing economic and social circumstances, and those circumstances diverge increasingly between Depletion and Conservation; within a couple of generations, none of the people who exist under one policy would ever have existed under the other. This creates trouble for the person-affecting principle because that principle condemns a policy only by comparing how a particular person fares under it to how that same person would have fared otherwise. But the people who live under Depletion owe their very existence to it: had Conservation been chosen instead, they would not have existed at all, so there is no alternative history in which they exist and fare better. As long as their lives remain worth living, none of them is worse off than he or she would otherwise have been, so the person-affecting principle cannot locate anyone for whom Depletion is bad, even though most of us are inclined to think Depletion is the worse policy.

(b) Parfit's response is to propose what he calls the Same Number Quality Claim, or Q: when the same number of people would ever live under either of two policies, it is a worse outcome if the people who do live are, on average, worse off than the people who would have lived instead. Q allows us to say that Depletion is worse than Conservation without identifying any individual for whom it is worse; the comparison is made directly between the two possible populations' quality of life, not between the fortunes of any one person across the two scenarios. Because the comparison is impersonal in this way, Parfit treats Q as a general solution to the Non-Identity Problem, applicable whether the two policies would leave the same number of people in existence or different numbers — what matters for Q is only the well-being of whoever exists, not how many of them there are.

(c) Given this, the upshot of the Non-Identity Problem, on Parfit's view, is that our first impression that Depletion is wrong does not survive scrutiny in its original form. Since no particular person is made worse off by Depletion, and since Parfit takes wrongness to ultimately require someone who is wronged, he treats Q as showing that Depletion produces a worse outcome in an impersonal sense without its being a case of wrongdoing to anyone in particular. On this view, Q vindicates the thought that Depletion is regrettable while correcting the further thought that it is wrong: strictly, no one has a complaint against it, so the language of wrongness, though natural, overstates the case.
