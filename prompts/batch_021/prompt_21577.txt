I'm a philosophy PhD student working in bioethics, and as part of a clinical ethics fellowship this year I sit as the trainee representative on my hospital's ethics committee. We've been asked to decide on a pilot proposal that I think turns on a much deeper problem than most of the committee has noticed, and I've been tasked with drafting the recommendation memo we'll circulate before the vote in three weeks. The proposal comes from a startup called Anchor Health, which wants to run a year-long pilot of an AI "advance care planning companion" with patients who carry serious diagnoses that put their future decision-making capacity at risk, mostly early-stage dementia and ALS. While a patient still has capacity, they work with the system over several sessions, which also ingests, with consent, their letters, messages, and recorded conversations with family, to build a rich model of what they value. The pitch, a more ambitious version of what bioethicists call a "patient preference predictor," is explicit that Anchor does not just record what a patient says: it flags apparent contradictions in their stated wishes, discounts what the vendor's documentation calls "framing noise and in-the-moment anxiety," and extrapolates to situations the patient never actually discussed, so as to output one coherent preference profile. Later, once the patient has lost capacity and their written directive turns out to be silent or ambiguous about the actual situation on the ward, which is the usual case in practice, clinicians and family can query Anchor for a recommendation that goes well beyond anything the patient is on record as ever having said.

The committee's disagreement keeps reminding me of the debates around Yudkowsky's "coherent extrapolated volition" proposal for AI alignment; a friend of mine in the CS department who works on alignment spotted the resemblance before I did, and now I can't unsee it. One faction argues that Anchor is just a more rigorous version of the substituted judgment surrogates already attempt, badly (family surrogates are notoriously inaccurate at guessing patient wishes), so a more consistent, better-informed extrapolation serves patient autonomy rather than threatening it. The other faction argues that the very operations being marketed as improvements — resolving "contradictions," discounting "noise" — are exactly where someone's assumptions about what a more rational or more informed version of you would want get substituted for what you actually, presently want, and that there is no neutral standpoint from which to say which strand of an ambivalent person counts as the real, extrapolated one. Dementia adds a further complication: by the time a hard case reaches the ward, the disease may have spent months reshaping the patient's values, so it is genuinely unclear whose preferences — the earlier self Anchor was trained on, or whoever the patient now is — are even the ones being extrapolated. Our advance-directive policy currently says nothing about any of this.

Could you draft the short written opinion I'm responsible for circulating? It should recommend a specific course of action — approve the pilot, reject it, or approve it with stated conditions — attached to a concrete timeframe, with reasoning that Anchor's team and our own clinicians could actually act on, and it should take the strongest version of whichever side's worry you end up setting aside seriously, rather than dispatching it in a sentence.
