This term I have been sitting in on a seminar on classical Buddhist philosophy, partly out of a broader interest in theories of personal identity, and a recent session was devoted to the Abhidharma-era controversy surrounding the Pudgalavādins, the so-called "Personalist" school. Almost alone among the Buddhist schools, they held that the person (pudgala) is real, yet neither identical to nor entirely distinct from the five aggregates that compose it — a relation they illustrated by comparing the person to fire, which is designated in dependence on its fuel without simply being identical to it. Discussing this afterward with my friend Elena, we ended up on opposite sides and neither of us could move the other. I argued that "neither the same as nor different from" is not a genuine third option at all: for any two things, they are either identical or distinct, so the formula just dresses up an incoherence, and the Personalists only reached for it because they wanted the explanatory conveniences of a self — continuity of memory, responsibility for past deeds, the mechanics of rebirth — while still officially subscribing to anatta. Elena thought this let the orthodox reductionists off too easily: their own preferred tools, such as Nāgasena's chariot simile from the Milindapañha, quietly help themselves to talk of unified persons and continuous causal series that do exactly the explanatory work the Personalists stand accused of smuggling in, so if the charge of incoherence sticks, it should stick to both sides or neither.

Neither of us was arguing at the top of our ability, and I would like to see the dispute conducted properly. Could you write a dialogue between a Pudgalavādin and an orthodox Buddhist reductionist (feel free to draw on Vasubandhu's actual case against the Personalists) in which they work through this directly? I would like the reductionist to press both the charge that "neither same nor different" is incoherent and the demand that any theory of persons earn its keep by explaining memory, moral responsibility, and rebirth without relapsing into either a disguised self or an unexplained brute continuity, and I would like the Personalist to defend the fire-and-fuel analogy on its own terms and turn the same pressure back onto the reductionist's use of causal series and conventional designation. Please have the two of them genuinely respond to each other's strongest points rather than talk past one another, and let the exchange end wherever it honestly ends rather than manufacturing a tidy resolution.
