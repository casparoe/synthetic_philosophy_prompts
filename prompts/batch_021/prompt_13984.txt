Question: "Reconstruct the Lucas–Penrose argument against mechanism about the mind. Explain the standard objection to the argument, and assess whether the objection is fatal to it."

Classmate's answer:

"Gödel's first incompleteness theorem tells us that for any consistent, sufficiently expressive, recursively axiomatized formal system F (one that can represent elementary arithmetic), there is a sentence G(F), the 'Gödel sentence' for F, which is true but cannot be proved within F. Lucas, and later Penrose, argue that this refutes mechanism about the mind. Suppose, for reductio, that a given person's mathematical reasoning is exactly captured by some such F: whatever F can prove, the person can prove, and vice versa. Since F is consistent, F cannot prove G(F); but a mathematician reasoning about F from the outside can see that G(F) must be true, precisely because F cannot prove it. So the person can do something F cannot do, and mechanism about that person's mind is false. Since the argument places no restriction on which F we started with, no Turing machine can capture human mathematical reasoning in general.

The standard reply is that the crucial step — 'the person can see that G(F) is true' — covertly assumes that the person knows, or can establish, that F is consistent. If F were inconsistent, it would go on to prove G(F) as well as its negation, so the inference from 'F won't prove G(F)' to 'G(F) is true' needs F's consistency as a premise. And the difficulty is that if the person really is equivalent to F, then F's own consistency is exactly the sort of thing F cannot prove, by Gödel's second incompleteness theorem; so the person has no privileged, non-question-begging way of certifying their own consistency from inside their own reasoning powers. At best they can produce a conditional — 'if F is consistent, G(F) is true' — and F itself can typically prove that conditional; it's the unconditional assertion of G(F) that is out of reach. This isn't a new worry, either: Turing had already made essentially this point in 1950, in direct response to Lucas, observing that we have no more grounds for assuming a human mathematician is consistent than for assuming a suitably designed machine is.

Even Gödel himself accepted the anti-mechanist moral: in his 1951 Gibbs lecture, he proved that the mathematical powers of the human mind can never be reproduced by any Turing machine, a claim he took to follow from his own incompleteness results.

On balance, I think the reply succeeds against Lucas's original argument: it shows that 'seeing that G(F) is true' cannot be had for free, since it smuggles in a consistency assumption the arguer isn't entitled to. Penrose's version in Shadows of the Mind tries to close this gap by arguing that mathematicians can in some sense recognize the soundness of their own methods, but this move has been criticized, e.g. by Feferman, as resting on an unclear notion of what it is to 'know' a system is sound."
