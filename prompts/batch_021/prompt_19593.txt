A while back, a friend on my school's debate team and I got into an argument about whether you should trust your own careful reasoning on a question or defer instead to what a poll of your peers (or of people generally more reliable than you) would conclude. That argument sent me, a junior who's been getting really into philosophy on the side, down a rabbit hole into some pretty technical epistemology I hadn't encountered before, built around what philosophers call "higher-order evidence" and its relationship to something called "epistemic akrasia." I've read enough now to see there's a genuine puzzle here, but not enough to know how it's supposed to be resolved, so I'd like you to actually work through it in essay form rather than just define the terms for me.

Here's my best reconstruction of the problem. Almost everyone accepts some version of an "Enkratic Principle" for belief: it's irrational to believe some claim P while also believing that your total evidence doesn't actually support P (this is supposed to be the belief-analogue of the more familiar idea that a rational person intends to do whatever they believe they ought to do). The trouble is there seem to be cases where you work through a first-order argument and it looks completely airtight to you, but you also have excellent independent evidence that your reasoning isn't to be trusted on occasions just like this one — say, you later learn you'd unknowingly been given a drug known to make people overconfident in exactly this kind of inference, or that people who feel as sure as you do about this type of problem turn out to be wrong close to half the time. In cases like that, it seems rational to lower your confidence in the conclusion because of this "higher-order" evidence about your own reliability, even though, taken purely on its own terms, the first-order argument still seems to you to fully support the conclusion. But holding both attitudes at once is exactly the state the Enkratic Principle is supposed to forbid — which is presumably why philosophers who work on this (I keep running into the names Adam Elga, David Christensen, Sophie Horowitz, and Maria Lasonen-Aarnio, among others) disagree so sharply about whether such "rational akrasia" is genuinely possible, or whether these cases are secretly being misdescribed.

What I'd like is an essay, not just a survey of positions. Start by explaining why the Enkratic Principle is so plausible in the first place, then present the strongest version of a higher-order evidence case that seems to force a violation of it. Lay out, as fairly as you can, at least the main rival responses — something like a "steadfast" or "level-splitting" view that just accepts that akrasia can sometimes be rational, a view that insists the higher-order evidence must always fully defeat the original justification (so the apparent conflict never really arises), and any view you know of that tries to dissolve the puzzle by distinguishing different senses of "rational" (for instance, separating whether a belief fits the evidence from whether your overall set of attitudes hangs together coherently). Then actually take a side: tell me which resolution you find most defensible, or, if you genuinely think none of them work, defend the claim that this is a real, unresolved antinomy — but please don't just hand me a list of options with no answer at the end.
