I'm part of an angel-investing syndicate of about a dozen members that meets monthly to evaluate early-stage pitches. After each founder's Q&A, we go around the table in a fixed seating order, and every member states, in turn, whether they are "in" or "out" on the deal, together with a one-line reason. Looking back over the past year of meetings, I've noticed that the first two or three verdicts almost always predict the eventual unanimous or near-unanimous outcome, even on deals where, in private conversation afterward, several members admit they were not especially confident and had largely been swayed by how the earlier votes went.

A friend who studies behavioral economics suggested that this looks like a textbook information cascade of the kind formalized by economists such as Bikhchandani, Hirshleifer, and Welch, rather than a case of the group genuinely converging on the right answer through independent judgment. That diagnosis struck me as plausible, but also somewhat unsettling, since our entire informal governance structure assumes that a unanimous "in" or "out" reflects the pooled wisdom of everyone in the room rather than the opinion of whoever happened to speak first.

Assuming my friend is broadly right that our sequential, public voting procedure generates rational herding rather than an honest aggregation of private information, please describe the most important implications of information-cascade and rational-herding theory for a small, recurring decision-making group like ours. I would especially like to understand how much epistemic weight a unanimous vote should actually carry once a cascade has plausibly started, and whether attaching a one-line reason to each vote, rather than a bare in/out signal, meaningfully changes the standard cascade dynamics. I'd also welcome any concrete implications the theory has for how we might sequence, time, or otherwise elicit votes so that the group actually pools its members' private information instead of converging on whichever view was voiced first.
