I've been going to the same rationalist-adjacent meetup for about six years now -- we started with a read-through of the Sequences and have long since moved on to arguing about things like Aumann's agreement theorem and calibration training over beer. Last month someone brought up "epistemic akrasia," a term a few of us had picked up from epistemology papers cited in passing on a couple of blogs, and it turned into the kind of two-hour argument that never quite resolves, which is why I'm finally bringing it somewhere I might get a straight answer.

As far as I can piece together, epistemic akrasia is just the belief-side version of ordinary akrasia, and the fix should be the same in kind. The akratic dieter knows the cookie is bad for them and eats it anyway; the epistemically akratic reasoner gets higher-order evidence -- a calibration test, say, showing that people who feel as confident as they do about this sort of claim are only right about half the time -- and knows, in some sense, that this should pull their confidence down, but keeps believing at the old, unadjusted level anyway. If that's the whole story, "epistemic akrasia" sounds like exactly the kind of bias CFAR-style tools were built to patch: trigger-action plans, premortems, deliberate calibration drills, the works. You notice the gap between what your evidence supports and what you actually believe, and you train until the gap closes. So I don't understand why philosophers still write about this as a live, structurally puzzling problem rather than a solved case of "here's a predictable failure to update, here's the debiasing technique for it." Is there something about the epistemic version that makes the willpower-and-habits fix not actually apply, or is the philosophical literature just dressing up the ordinary willpower problem in fancier vocabulary?
