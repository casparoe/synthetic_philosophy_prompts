Q: Explain David Chalmers's conceivability argument against physicalism using the notion of philosophical zombies. Lay out the argument's premises and conclusion, and describe one influential physicalist strategy for resisting it.

Student answer: A philosophical zombie is a being that is physically and behaviorally identical to a conscious person in every way but has no phenomenal experience whatsoever -- there's nothing it is like to be a zombie. Chalmers's argument goes: (1) zombies are conceivable, since we can coherently imagine a full physical duplicate of a person with "the lights off" inside; (2) whatever is conceivable is logically possible, and logical possibility just is metaphysical possibility here, since both simply mean there's no contradiction in the scenario; (3) so zombies are metaphysically possible; (4) but if physicalism were true, fixing all the physical facts about a person would automatically fix every fact about them, including whether they're conscious, so a zombie world would be impossible; (5) therefore physicalism is false, and some further, non-physical fact is needed to account for consciousness.

The most influential physicalist reply is the phenomenal concept strategy, which Chalmers himself introduced to blunt the force of his own argument: our concepts of experience are cognitively special and disconnected from our physical or functional concepts, which is why a zombie world seems conceivable even though there is no extra non-physical property at the level of the actual facts -- consciousness just is a physical property, picked out in a distinctive way. This lets physicalists concede that the entailment from physical to phenomenal facts isn't knowable a priori while still holding that the entailment is there.
