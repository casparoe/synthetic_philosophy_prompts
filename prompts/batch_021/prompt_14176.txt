Question (10 pts): Explain Nelson Goodman's "new riddle of induction." Define the predicate "grue," explain why it's a problem for simple theories of confirmation like Hempel's instance confirmation, and explain how Goodman proposed to resolve the problem.

Student's answer:
Goodman's new riddle is meant to show that Hume's problem of induction isn't the only problem we face in justifying inductive inference. Even granting that induction is justified in general, we still need some way of picking out which predicates are the "right" ones to project onto unobserved cases.

He illustrates this with the predicate "grue": an object is grue if it's green and examined before some future time t (say the year 2050), or blue and examined after t. Every emerald examined so far is green, but since all of them were also examined before 2050, they're all grue too. So our evidence confirms "all emeralds are green" and "all emeralds are grue" equally well under Hempel's instance confirmation, even though the two hypotheses make opposite predictions about emeralds examined after 2050.

This matters because it shows a purely syntactic theory of confirmation can't distinguish a real law from an accidental generalization just by looking at the evidence. Goodman's own answer to the riddle is that grue is simply a more gerrymandered, less natural predicate than green, since defining it requires referencing both a specific time and two different colors, so on general grounds of simplicity we're justified in projecting "all emeralds are green" instead.
