Random thought that's been distracting me from my actual dissertation chapter on 19th-century stat mech: when Zermelo hit Boltzmann with the recurrence objection in the 1890s, Boltzmann's comeback was basically that the universe as a whole spends almost all of its existance in equilibrium, and we just happen to be sitting inside one of the rare, enormous downward fluctuations in entropy, since only inside such a fluctuation could creatures like us exist to notice anything at all. That's already a pretty anthropic move to be making in the 1890s. Given he was willing to go there, what do you think he'd have made of the modern "Boltzmann brain" problem, where cosmologists run that exact same fluctuation logic on an eternally accelerating, de Sitter-ish universe and conclude that a "typical" observer should really be a single freak brain that randomly fluctuates into existence with false memories, vastly outnumbering observers who actually have a genuine evolutionary history behind them? Would his own commitments in statistical mechanics basically force him to bite that bullet, or is there something in how he thought about probability and typicality that gives him a principled way out?
