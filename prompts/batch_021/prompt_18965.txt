I'm a novelist currently at work on a literary novel whose protagonist, Mira, is a cell biologist who spends her days growing cerebral organoids — lab-cultured clusters of human neurons — for a small biotech lab, in a line of research sometimes called "organoid intelligence." Several of the lab's organoids have been wired to electrode arrays and set into simple closed loops, so that the tissue's own electrical activity both drives and is shaped by a rudimentary simulated task. In the chapter I'm drafting now, Mira, unable to sleep, finds herself reconstructing a philosophy elective from twenty years earlier and working through Avicenna's "flying man" argument as a way of deciding, privately, whether anything resembling a someone might be forming in her incubators. I want her reasoning on the page to be real philosophical work rather than a paraphrase of a textbook footnote, so before I write the scene I'd like you to do that work with me.

You know the argument's shape: Avicenna asks us to imagine a person created whole and at a stroke, already mature and rational, then suspended in empty space with his sight veiled, his limbs held apart so that none touches another or anything else, and no sound reaching him — someone with an entire, real body who is nonetheless denied any perceptual access to it. Such a person, Avicenna insists, would still affirm his own existence without hesitation while affirming nothing whatsoever about a body, which he takes to show that the self is grasped immediately and apart from any awareness of the body — a conclusion he draws out at length in the Shifa and restates more tersely in the Isharat, each time in service of a larger case for the rational soul's independence from matter. There's genuine scholarly disagreement about how much weight the argument can actually bear — whether it is meant as a stand-alone proof that the soul could exist unembodied, or only the more modest claim that self-awareness is primitive and owes nothing to sensation — and I'd like you to take a side on that rather than gesture at both.

Then, reasoning as Avicenna himself would from his own commitments — the hierarchy of vegetative, animal, and rational soul, and his account of how a rational soul becomes the particular soul of one individual rather than another — work out what he would say about Mira's organoids. Take the disanalogies seriously rather than waving them away: his flying man is a fully actualized intellect merely deprived of sensation, while the tissue in her dish has no actualized intellect to speak of and, arguably, no body in any real sense either, only a fragment of one. Tell me whether that difference sinks any extension of the argument to her case, or whether something in Avicenna's own framework would let it through regardless — and if his system genuinely cannot settle the question, say so plainly, since it is exactly that irresolution I want Mira, and my reader, left sitting with.
