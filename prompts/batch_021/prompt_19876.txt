As a retired evolutionary biologist who has only in the last few years taken up philosophy of science, I was unsettled to learn that Karl Popper once judged natural selection to be, in his own phrase, a "metaphysical research programme" rather than a genuine scientific theory, on the grounds that "survival of the fittest" threatens to collapse into the empty claim that survivors survive. He later recanted, admitting he had changed his mind about the theory's testability. What I have not been able to work out on my own is whether that recantation rescues the general principle of natural selection itself, or only the specific, independently testable hypotheses biologists build on top of it. Please write a dialogue between two philosophers of biology who fall into this exact disagreement in the corridor after a symposium session on Popper's legacy: one still convinced that the tautology worry bites the general schema no matter how testable its applications are, the other convinced that the distinction between "schema" and "application" is spurious and that the recantation was fully earned. Let them press each other on the hardest version of the opposing view rather than settle for a polite truce.
