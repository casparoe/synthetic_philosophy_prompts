A friend of mine, who is finishing a PhD in economics, and I spent most of a long train ride arguing about the common prior assumption (CPA), after her department's game theory seminar had covered Aumann's agreement theorem the same week my formal epistemology course got to Bayesian models of disagreement. The seminar presents the theorem, roughly, as showing that if two Bayesians share a prior and it is common knowledge what each of their posteriors on some event is, those posteriors must coincide - and treats this as evidence that persistent, well-informed disagreement between rational people is impossible. My friend defended a fairly strong Harsanyi-style line: if two agents are in the same position with respect to a decision problem before any distinguishing evidence arrives, there is no principled way for their priors to differ, so an ideally rational agent's beliefs are best modeled as a common prior conditioned on private information, and any residual disagreement must reduce to a difference in information (or a failure of rationality) rather than a legitimate difference in starting point.

I pushed back along a few lines: that priors have to come from somewhere, and there is no agent-neutral fact about which prior is "correct" even for two agents facing type-identical problems; that the assumption starts to look unfalsifiable once you notice you can always redescribe any two belief states as a common prior plus sufficiently gerrymandered private information partitions; and that the no-trade theorems that fall out of the CPA (if a trade's mutual agreeability is common knowledge among common-prior traders, no purely speculative trade should occur) sit awkwardly with how much speculative trading actually happens in real markets. My friend's response was that the trading-volume "puzzle" is evidence against common knowledge of rationality, or against the idealization generally, not a reason to single out the common prior assumption specifically - and from there we mostly just went in circles.

I'd like an essay that adjudicates this properly rather than just cataloguing both sides. Present the strongest case that the CPA is a genuine requirement on ideally rational agents rather than a mere modeling convenience, the strongest objections to it, and a verdict on what the tension between agreement/no-trade results and the empirical ubiquity of stable disagreement and speculative trade actually shows. Don't end on a "reasonable people disagree" shrug - take a real, defensible position, even a conditional one (something like "the CPA is warranted only for agents with a shared evidential and causal history, not as a general constraint on rational belief" would count as a genuine verdict), and defend it against the strongest objections you can construct.
