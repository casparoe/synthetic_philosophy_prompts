Here's the question from the sheet: "Newton's Scholium doesn't just give us the rotating bucket -- it also gives us two globes connected by a cord, revolving in a universe otherwise empty of any other body. (a) Explain both thought experiments and what each is meant to establish. (b) Several commentators (Sklar, for instance) hold that the globes case is a strictly harder case for the relationist than the bucket case is. Explain why. (c) Sketch how a Machian relationist would try to answer the globes case, and note what, if anything, General Relativity actually vindicates of Mach's proposal."

And here's Denny's answer, copied exactly as he sent it to me:

"Newton wants to show that space itself is a real, substantial thing (substantivalism) and not just a system of relations between bodies (relationism), and he gives two arguments for this in the Scholium.

The bucket: a bucket of water hangs from a twisted cord and is released. At first the bucket spins while the water lags behind, so there's plenty of relative motion between bucket and water, but the surface stays flat. Eventually friction drags the water around until it's spinning together with the bucket -- so the relative motion between them drops to zero -- and yet the surface is now concave, climbing up the sides. Since the concavity doesn't track the relative motion between the water and the bucket, Newton concludes it must be tracking something else: rotation relative to absolute space.

The globes: two globes joined by a cord, revolving around their common centre of gravity, with no other body anywhere in existence to be related to. The faster they spin, the more the globes themselves bulge outward where they meet the cord, and from the degree of bulging alone you could work out the axis and rate of rotation. Because there's no other matter in this universe for the globes to be rotating 'relative to,' Newton takes this to be an even cleaner case than the bucket: there's no relation left for the relationist to appeal to, so whatever the rotation is relative to, it isn't a body.

This is exactly why the globes case is worse for the relationist than the bucket: with the bucket you can always say 'relative to the fixed stars,' i.e. relative to the rest of the matter in the universe, which is basically Mach's move -- but the globes thought experiment stipulates there's no other matter around, so that move is unavailable from the outset.

Mach's response was that inertial effects like these are always really rotation relative to the total mass of the universe rather than relative to empty space, and Einstein took the idea seriously enough that it directly motivated General Relativity, which ended up confirming Mach's principle and showing Newton's absolute space was never actually needed."
