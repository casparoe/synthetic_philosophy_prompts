So I'm taking an astrobiology gen-ed this semester (mostly grabbed it for the lab science credit, not gonna lie) and it's turned out to be way more philosophical than I expected. Last week's lecture was on the Fermi paradox and the "Great Filter," and it kicked off an argument between two people in my discussion section's group chat that spiraled for like two days and never actually got resolved, so I wanted to lay it out for you and get an actual verdict, since I couldn't referee it myself.

One guy started it by bringing up an argument our professor called "Carter's argument," or something close to that: Earth is about 4.5 billion years old, and the sun's only got maybe another billion years or so before its bright enough to boil the oceans and end habitability here, so life basically had a fixed window to work with. Intelligent, technological life took nearly the whole window to show up, not just the early part of it. He argued that if each of the "hard steps" along the way (life starting at all, complex cells, sex, big brains, whatever) were individually likely to happen fast, you'd expect intelligent life to usually show up early in a planet's habitable window rather than squeezing in right before the deadline the way we did -- so the fact that we cut it so close is itself evidence each step is a rare fluke, meaning most habitable planets never get all the way there, which is why we don't see anyone out there. A girl arguing with him said this is a selection-effect fallacy wearing a lab coat: any species capable of having this conversation necessarily exists in a timeline where every hard step happened to go through, no matter how likely or unlikely each one really was, so of course we'll look back and find a story where they all worked out -- that's guaranteed by the fact that we're here to notice, and it tells us nothing about the true odds.

He came back and said fine, existing at all is guaranteed and uninformative, but the timing isn't: we weren't guaranteed to finish so close to the wire instead of, say, a billion years into a much longer window, and if the steps were individually fast, most successful civilizations across the galaxy should typically show up early in thier windows too, so being a "late" case is still surprising evidence even after you account for needing to exist to notice it at all. She granted that timing could in principle carry some leftover information beyond bare existence, but said he hasn't actually earned that conclusion -- it depends on assumptions about how many independent hard steps there really are, whether they're actually comparable/independent, and how you even define the reference class of "possible observers," and that different but equally reasonable assumptions could flip the conclusion entirely, so at minimum he owes a defense of why his particular way of slicing up the evidence is the one to trust. Neither of them backed down.
