At a dinner party last week, a debate broke out that I haven't been able to fully resolve, and I would like to think it through with you. One friend has recently become devoted to the world of fragrance "dupes" - budget perfumes reverse-engineered through chemical analysis to smell as close as possible to expensive designer scents, and marketed openly on that basis. She brought along a fifteen-dollar dupe of a perfume that normally retails for close to three hundred dollars, and in a blind comparison around the table, none of us - including two friends who own and regularly wear the original - could reliably tell the dupe from the genuine article. Delighted, she declared herself vindicated and swore she would never buy "the real thing" again. Another friend, who owns and treasures the original, insisted she would keep buying it regardless, on the grounds that something is lost in the dupe that no amount of chemical matching could supply, though when pressed she could not say clearly what that something was, beyond maintaining that the real thing simply matters because it is real. The table split roughly down the middle between the convert and the purist, and despite having taught on related questions before, I could not offer either side a fully satisfying answer on the spot.

I teach a course that touches on the philosophy of art, so everyone naturally turned to me, but this case felt trickier than the paintings and sculptures I usually rely on to illustrate these debates. Most arguments for why an original outranks an indistinguishable copy seem to presuppose a unique, historically situated object to begin with - the very canvas the artist's own hand touched. Perfume does not obviously have any such thing: even an "authentic" bottle is only one mass-produced token of a formula, no more touched by its creator's hand than the dupe is. So here is what I would like your help with: is there a principled basis for preferring the original once a copy becomes genuinely indistinguishable from it, or is the convert right that the purist's preference amounts to nothing more than brand loyalty dressed up as principle? And does it matter to your answer that the object in dispute here was never a singular original to begin with, only ever a repeatable formula - or does that detail dissolve the debate rather than settle it?
