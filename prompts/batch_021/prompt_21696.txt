I've spent a long time reading LessWrong and its offshoots, where the Dutch book argument gets treated as more or less the founding myth of Bayesian rationality: if your credences don't satisfy the probability axioms, a sufficiently clever counterparty can offer you a series of bets, each individually acceptable by your own lights, that combine into a guaranteed loss no matter how the world turns out. I've always found this compelling as a story, but a friend who is doing graduate work in formal epistemology recently pointed out that it's actually a somewhat contested foundation, since the argument only ever seems to show that incoherent credences are pragmatically exploitable, not that they are mistaken in some deeper epistemic sense — after all, no one is obligated to accept any bet at all. She mentioned that this is exactly the gap that the philosopher Jim Joyce tried to close with what she called an "accuracy-dominance" argument for probabilism, which drops betting altogether and instead tries to show, using a measure of how close a credence is to the truth, that any credence function violating the axioms is guaranteed to be less accurate than some coherent alternative in every possible world, independent of any wager.

I'd like a careful comparison of these two arguments rather than a mere restatement of each. What exactly does each one have to assume in order to get off the ground — for instance, about what counts as a legitimate way of measuring accuracy, or about why a guaranteed loss (of money, or of accuracy) is a mark of irrationality at all — and what precisely does each one succeed in showing about an agent whose credences are incoherent? More importantly, does Joyce's accuracy-based argument actually deliver the purely epistemic vindication of probabilism that Dutch book arguments are usually said to lack, or does it just relocate the same pragmatic or decision-theoretic assumptions somewhere less visible, for instance in its choice of scoring rule? Please defend a definite answer rather than simply laying out considerations on both sides.
