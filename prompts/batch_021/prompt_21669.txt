A friend and I spent much of a long dinner arguing about interpersonal utility comparisons, and I still have not managed to settle the question in my own mind. She insists that Arrow's impossibility theorem closes the matter: since Arrow proved that no way of aggregating individual preferences into a social ranking can satisfy even a short list of minimal, seemingly uncontroversial conditions, she takes this as a mathematical demonstration that comparing utility across different people is incoherent, and that policy arguments such as "a hundred dollars means more to a poor person than to a rich one" rest on a category mistake rather than a genuine insight. When I objected that economists rely on such comparisons constantly, she replied that this only shows how sloppy most economists are about their own foundations. I could not locate the flaw on the spot, but it struck me as suspiciously convenient that a theorem about ranking preferences would settle a much older dispute about utility. Is she right that Arrow's theorem establishes the impossibility of interpersonal utility comparisons, and if her argument does not actually work, where precisely does it go wrong?
