This term I'm taking a seminar on decision theory, and last week's session was devoted to the "Death in Damascus" case that Allan Gibbard and William Harper introduced in the late 1970s to expose a gap between evidential and causal expected utility. The setup: Death, a near-infallible predictor of your movements, has an appointment with you tonight, and whichever city you reason your way toward turns out to be exactly the city your reasoning would have led Death to expect and wait in — so if you talk yourself into staying in Damascus, that is precisely what makes Damascus fatal, and if you instead talk yourself into fleeing to Aleppo, fleeing becomes fatal instead. What struck me as more interesting than the fatalism of the old "Appointment in Samarra" parable this is adapted from is the specific technical upshot: neither option is what decision theorists call "ratifiable" — whichever one you settle on is undercut by the very fact that you've settled on it — and the only choice that turns out to be stable in that sense is one handed over to an actual randomizing device, like a coin toss.

I'd like to try turning that structural idea into fiction rather than more theory, and I could use your help drafting it. Could you write me a short story, somewhere in the 800 to 1,300 word range, that dramatizes this exact bind but relocated somewhere with no death, prophecy, or marketplaces in sight — perhaps a hiring decision, a custody dispute, a hostage negotiation, or any comparably high-stakes modern setting you find promising? I'd like a protagonist choosing between two options, up against a counterpart whose behavior tracks the protagonist's own reasoning so closely that choosing either option is precisely what makes it the wrong one to have chosen, so that the protagonist's deliberation keeps sliding between the two horns, and only an ending that stays genuinely unresolved, or is handed over to chance, feels true to the logic. Please don't have the story name-drop decision theory or explain the puzzle to the reader directly — I'd rather the bind be visible only in how the character's reasoning actually unravels on the page. If you're able, a closing line or two outside the story proper, flagging which moment in the plot is meant to correspond to the failure of ratifiability, would help me check whether the underlying mechanics actually came through.
