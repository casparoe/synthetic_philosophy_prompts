I spent almost forty years running a low-temperature physics lab before I retired, and one thing that gets drilled into every young experimentalist is that you cannot get a perpetual motion machine out of a closed cycle - if the system ends up back in its starting state, the net work you extracted has to be zero (or negative), full stop. So when I ran into "money pump" arguments in decision theory this winter it felt weirdly familiar: if your preferences cycle (you prefer A to B, B to C, but then also C to A), and each time you're willing to pay a little to trade up, someone can walk you round and round the loop back to your starting bundle while quietly draining your wallet the whole way. Its used as a proof that transitive (and complete) preferences aren't just a nicety but a requirement of rationality, on pain of being a sitting duck for exploitation.

What's been bugging me since is that there isn't one agreed way out of this, there's a whole menu of them. I've been reading around - some Stanford Encyclopedia entries on preference and rational choice, plus a slim little Cambridge Elements book on the topic that I think is by a guy called Gustafsson (my memory for names isn't what it used to be) - and I keep bumping into different camps. McClennen and co. split agents into "sophisticated" choosers, who see the trap coming via backward induction and just never step onto the wheel, versus "resolute" choosers, who commit to a whole plan at the outset and stick with it even at the exact node where they'd now rather deviate, versus plain "naive"/myopic choosers who just get fleeced every time. Then there's a seperate camp that denies a real cycle exists at all once you individuate the options properly (the "A" you're offered the second time round isn't quite the same "A" as before). Another camp just bites the bullet and says transitivity was never really a requirement of practical rationality in the first place - I gather this is close to Temkin's line, and it connects to puzzles like Quinn's self-torturer. And a last camp says the whole thing is a red herring because real agents solve it externally, with precommitment devices, contracts and defaults, rather than by having the right internal choice disposition.

This isn't purely academic for me either - I'm currently trying to work out how much weight my "future self" should get when I make decisions about, say, winding down my investments over the next decade, so I've got a personal stake in which of these camps actually holds up.
