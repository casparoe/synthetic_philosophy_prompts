I retired three years ago after a career in theoretical cosmology, and I have been auditing a graduate seminar on philosophy of mind ever since, mostly for the pleasure of being confused by something new after four decades of being merely confused by the usual things. I knew Roger Penrose for years only as half of the Hawking–Penrose singularity theorems, so it came as a genuine surprise to work through the case he builds, out of J. R. Lucas's 1961 argument, that Gödel's incompleteness theorems show a human mind cannot be equivalent to any Turing machine or formal system. The seminar's instructor knows I spent a career doing proof-adjacent mathematical physics and, being buried under two other courses this term, asked whether I would grade the problem set covering this material for his roughly thirty students. I agreed rashly, before remembering that grading someone else's philosophical reasoning is not at all the same skill as having opinions about it myself. Before I commit any numbers to the gradebook, I would like an independent pass at one representative answer; I have a provisional score already pencilled in, but I would rather not tell you what it is.

Here is the question as I set it: "Q3 (20 points). State the Lucas–Penrose argument against mechanism as precisely as you can, making clear exactly which of Gödel's two incompleteness theorems is doing the essential work, and why the argument needs a premise about the consistency of the relevant formal system. Then give what you take to be the single strongest objection to the argument — not simply that the formal system in question might happen to be inconsistent — and assess whether Penrose's development of the argument in Shadows of the Mind succeeds in meeting that objection, or merely relocates it."

And here, verbatim, is the student's answer:

"Mechanism holds that a human mind just is, or is perfectly simulated by, some formal system F — equivalently, some Turing machine — that is consistent and strong enough to express elementary arithmetic. Lucas's argument tries to show no such F can capture a human mathematician's powers. For any such F, Gödel's diagonal construction produces a sentence G(F) such that F itself proves the biconditional 'G(F) is true if and only if G(F) is not provable in F.' The crucial result here is Gödel's second incompleteness theorem, which tells us that if F is consistent, this self-referential sentence is true but cannot be proved inside F. A mathematician reasoning about F from the outside, and granting that F is consistent, can therefore assert G(F) — something F itself is provably unable to do. Since F was an arbitrary mechanist model of the mind, Lucas concludes that no formal system can be the whole story about a mind.

The weakest link is not that F might simply be inconsistent; Lucas can restrict the argument to consistent systems and the conclusion remains a perfectly good conditional one. The sharper worry is that Lucas is not entitled to his own premise: he needs to know that F, whatever F actually captures his own reasoning, is consistent, and that is exactly the kind of fact that the mind's own resources cannot certify if the mind just is F. So there is no clear sense in which the mathematician has done something the machine could not; both are equally in the dark about F's consistency, and the mathematician's 'seeing' that G(F) is true is only as good as an unproven assumption.

Penrose is alert to this, and in Shadows of the Mind he escapes it by refusing to commit in advance to a particular F. Instead he argues for a disjunction: either human mathematical understanding cannot be captured by the output of any knowably sound formal system, or there are mathematical truths that could never in principle become known to us. Because he now argues for a disjunction rather than asserting that he can certify some specific F's consistency, Penrose no longer needs the disputed premise, and the objection above simply does not arise for his version of the argument — only for Lucas's cruder original. Together with his later, independent proposal with Hameroff that the relevant physical process is a form of quantum 'objective reduction' in microtubules, Penrose turns a worry about logic into a testable claim about physics, which is real progress even if the physics remains speculative."
