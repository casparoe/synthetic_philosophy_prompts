I spent my working life as a physical oceanographer, and it is only in retirement that I have finally had the leisure to read philosophy seriously, mostly through a small discussion group that meets fortnightly at our local library. A few weeks ago, partly out of scientific curiosity and partly on a friend's recommendation, I tried a flotation therapy session. This involves spending an hour alone in a lightless, soundproofed tank of water so saturated with salt that one floats quite effortlessly; the water is kept at skin temperature, one wears earplugs, and the tank I used was large enough that I could let my arms and legs drift apart so that no part of my body touched any other. After some twenty minutes I lost almost every trace of bodily sensation: no felt position of my limbs, no sense of the water against my skin beyond a faint, undifferentiated warmth, nothing to see or hear. My thoughts and memories carried on quite normally throughout, so my mind was hardly a blank; but I could not, in that state, have told you a single fact about my body -- not its shape, not its posture, not even whether my hands were near each other. What struck me afterward was that none of this had, even for a moment, unsettled my certainty that I existed -- that there was a "me" there having the experience.

I recounted this at our next meeting, and one member -- a retired schoolteacher who has read a good deal more philosophy than I have -- seized on it at once. His argument was that this comes close to a demonstration that the self is something other than the body: if I could be perfectly certain of my own existence while receiving no information whatsoever from or about my body, he said, then whatever I was certain of when I was certain of "myself" cannot have been my body, and there is then little reason left to think that self needs a body in order to exist. Someone else in the group objected that this seemed too quick, but couldn't quite say where it went wrong, and we moved on to other things before settling it. The exchange has nagged at me since. A career spent insisting on operational definitions and reproducible measurements leaves me instinctively wary of arguments that slide from "I can experience X without any awareness of Y" to "X can therefore exist without Y" -- yet I found I couldn't reconstruct the objection with any precision. Was my friend's inference sound, or does it quietly help itself to more than an experience like mine could ever establish? I would be grateful for a careful, step-by-step assessment of the argument on its own terms, including anything you think it gets right, rather than just a verdict on the conclusion.
