I've been part of an informal "history of computing" reading group with a few colleagues from my team, and this month we finally read through Turing's original 1950 paper, "Computing Machinery and Intelligence," rather than relying on the potted summary of the imitation game that gets recycled in every introductory AI course. I got stuck on the part where he addresses, as his ninth objection, extrasensory perception. He treats the parapsychological evidence of his era surprisingly seriously, and worries that a human contestant with genuine telepathic ability could out-guess a machine on simple card-guessing questions, letting the interrogator unmask the human purely on that basis rather than on anything to do with actual thinking. As far as I can tell, the fix he ultimately settles on is to equip the machine with its own random-number generator, so that it can rack up correct guesses at roughly the same statistical rate as the psychic contestant, putting the two contestants back on equal footing.

If that really is his resolution, it strikes me as a strange concession from someone credited with founding a purely operational, behaviorist approach to machine intelligence: rather than simply ruling ESP out of the experimental setup altogether, he apparently patched the test by quietly giving the computer a manufactured, paranormal-equivalent capability of its own, as though sheer computation could not otherwise be trusted to keep the contest fair. Given that, I'm puzzled why this section is barely mentioned whenever people praise the Turing test for reducing "thinking" to a clean, purely behavioral criterion. Shouldn't Turing's own fix here count as a genuine crack in that behaviorist foundation, rather than the odd historical footnote it usually gets reduced to?
