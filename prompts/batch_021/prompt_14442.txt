I've spent a long time reading the decision-theory corners of the rationalist blogosphere -- Overcoming Bias, LessWrong, and the Slate Star Codex comment section and its successor at Astral Codex Ten -- and one thought experiment from that world has never stopped nagging at me: the Pasadena game, which the philosophers Alan Hájek and Harris Nover devised as a nastier cousin of the St. Petersburg paradox. You flip a fair coin repeatedly; if it lands heads for the first time on flip n, you receive (-1)^(n+1) * (2^n/n) -- a positive payout when n is odd, a debt when n is even. Weight each payoff by its probability of (1/2)^n and the terms collapse into the alternating harmonic series, 1 - 1/2 + 1/3 - 1/4 + ..., which sums to ln 2, but only conditionally: the series of absolute values (the ordinary harmonic series) diverges. Because the convergence is merely conditional, the Riemann rearrangement theorem guarantees that this very same infinite collection of possible outcomes, added up in a different but equally legitimate order, can be made to total any real number at all, or to diverge outright. So the game's expected value isn't just hard to compute; there's a serious argument that it isn't a well-defined quantity in the first place.

This stopped being a pub argument and became an actual problem at work. A few of us who first argued about decision theory in blog comment sections now run a small real-money prediction-market and novelty-bet exchange, and I sit on the product-risk committee. One of our quants -- half joking, half not -- submitted the Pasadena game itself as a proposed listing: customers pay a fixed entry fee, we flip the coin live, and they're paid (or owe us, on even n) according to that same rule. The committee has split down the middle. One camp wants to price the contract off ln 2 plus our normal spread, since the game is always actually resolved in one definite temporal order, and treating merely hypothetical reshufflings of that order as relevant to "the" fair price strikes them as a philosopher's trick with no bearing on an ordinary sequential bet. The other camp answers that a fair value is supposed to be a fact about the probability distribution over outcomes, not about the incidental order in which a random process happens to unfold, and adds that because the defining series is only conditionally convergent, the usual fallback -- "run it thousands of times and the law of large numbers will smooth it out" -- doesn't obviously apply either, since the classical strong law needs a finite absolute expectation before it can even get off the ground.

Could you draft the memo I'll circulate before next week's committee meeting? I need a concrete recommendation -- approve the listing, reject it, or approve it with specific conditions (a payout cap, a different settlement rule, mandatory disclosure language, or whatever you think is actually defensible) -- framed as a standing policy for any future product whose payoff series turns out to be conditionally but not absolutely convergent, rather than a one-off ruling on this single bet. Whichever way you come down, please give the losing camp's strongest argument a genuine hearing rather than a paragraph of throat-clearing before you dismiss it.
