I'm a PhD student in philosophy working mostly on decision theory, and a few of us in the department run a weekly reading group on formal epistemology. Last week we spent most of the session on van Fraassen's 'Judy Benjamin' puzzle. Judy is dropped somewhere in a region split into Red and Blue territory, each of which is further divided into a Headquarters Company area and a Second Company area, so before she hears anything she assigns 1/4 to each of the four quadrants. Her own headquarters then radios her that they can't be sure where she is, but that if she's in Red territory, the odds are 3:1 she's in the Headquarters Company area -- and at that point the radio cuts out. Because the message reports a conditional probability rather than handing Judy new probabilities for a partition outright, it doesn't fit ordinary Jeffrey conditionalization, so one influential proposal is that she should instead pick whichever posterior minimizes 'relative information' (cross-entropy) relative to her prior, subject to that 3:1 constraint. The catch is that doing so doesn't just split the Red quadrants three to one; it also pushes her credence in each Blue quadrant up from .25 to roughly .27, so that Blue as a whole ends up more likely than not (about .53) -- even though the message never said a word about Blue.

Two people in the group, Marcus and Lena, argued about what to make of this for most of the hour. (1) Marcus said Judy should just accept the minimize-relative-information posterior: it's the natural way of asking for the least drastic revision of her prior that's compatible with the new constraint, and the Blue increase, while surprising, is simply what that request delivers. (2) Lena objected that this gets the history backwards -- van Fraassen introduced the example precisely as a problem for people who update this way, not as an endorsement of it; a report that is entirely about the internal breakdown of Red territory shouldn't be able to raise Judy's credence in Blue, and a procedure that does that has been refuted, not merely surprised. (3) Marcus replied that 'the report says nothing about Blue' quietly assumes updating has to be local, whereas even ordinary Jeffrey conditionalization on a claim about one proposition can move your credence in an unrelated one once the two are correlated under your prior; he added that bare unintuitiveness is cheap evidence against a rule unless Lena can name a principled competitor that does better. (4) Lena answered that she had one: minimizing relative information isn't forced by the content of the message, since it presupposes a specific choice of partition and divergence measure that the report itself doesn't specify; she recalled a result -- Grove and Halpern's, she thought -- that instead models explicitly how the report could have been produced and conditions on that richer space in the ordinary way, arriving at an answer on which the Blue quadrants never move from .25 at all. So, she said, the spillover into Blue is an artifact of one formalization choice, not a discovery about what conditionals do.

Neither of them budged, and we had to break for class before anyone else weighed in. Could you go through Marcus and Lena's exchange and adjudicate it point by point -- addressing (1) through (4) in turn and saying who has the better of each, not just who wins overall -- and then add a short closing paragraph on whether there's a consideration neither of them raised that should actually settle how Judy ought to update?
