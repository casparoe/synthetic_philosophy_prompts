Skolem's paradox arises from a clash between two facts about first-order ZFC. The Löwenheim–Skolem theorem guarantees that ZFC, if it has a model at all, has a countable one; yet ZFC itself proves, via Cantor's theorem, that uncountable sets exist. It follows that a countable model of ZFC must contain some element that the model itself judges to be uncountable, even though the model's entire domain has only countably many members. Skolem took this to show that notions like "countable" and "uncountable" are not absolute properties of sets but relative to whatever model one is working in, and he drew a deflationary conclusion about whether first-order axioms could ever pin down a single, determinate universe of sets. The now-standard response instead treats this as a harmless artifact of first-order expressive limits: once "true in the model" is distinguished from "true of the model, from outside," the appearance of paradox dissolves and set-theoretic realism is left untouched.

Write an essay adjudicating between these two readings. Explain concretely why the model's "uncountable" set contains no internal bijection to the naturals even though one exists from the outside, and then take a position on whether moving to a second-order formulation of set theory with full (standard) semantics -- where the Löwenheim–Skolem theorem fails -- genuinely restores an absolute notion of uncountability, or merely relocates the same relativity into what counts as "all" subsets under that semantics. Defend a definite verdict on whether Skolem's paradox is a substantive difficulty for set-theoretic realism, or merely an artifact of first-order logic's expressive limits.
