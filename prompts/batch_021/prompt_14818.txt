I've been mulling over a thread on a rationalist-adjacent forum where several commenters were defending "substrate independence" -- the transhumanist-friendly idea that what matters for having a mind is the right causal-functional organization, not the specific physical stuff implementing it -- by appealing to Putnam's multiple realizability argument as though it had settled the question decades ago. The reasoning went: since pain (or belief, or any mental kind you like) can be realized by wildly different physical systems across species, type-identity theory is false and some version of functionalism must be correct, which in turn licenses the thought that minds could just as well run on silicon. That struck me as moving a bit fast, especially once I remembered there's a serious rejoinder in the philosophy of mind literature, associated with William Bechtel and Jennifer Mundale, to the effect that the apparent multiple realizability of mental states is largely an artifact of comparing psychological and neurological descriptions at mismatched grains of analysis.

I'd like you to compare Putnam's original multiple realizability argument with the Bechtel-and-Mundale-style "grain" objection to it. Please reconstruct both positions carefully enough that someone unfamiliar with the debate could see exactly where they part ways: what work the cross-species and cross-realizer examples are doing for Putnam, and precisely what goes wrong, on Bechtel and Mundale's view, once we hold the grain of psychological and neurological description fixed. Then defend your own assessment: does the grain objection succeed in rehabilitating something like type-identity theory, or at least in blocking the standard inference from multiple realizability to functionalism, or does it just show that both sides need to individuate mental and physical kinds more carefully, leaving the functionalist conclusion largely intact? I would also be curious whether, on your view, any of this actually bears on the substrate-independence claims that started the forum argument in the first place.
