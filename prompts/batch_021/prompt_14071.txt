I've been stuck on something about Harsanyi's aggregation theorem that I've never seen properly argued out. If every individual's preferences over gambles obey the usual expected-utility axioms, and a social planner, whose own preferences also obey those axioms, is required to agree with everyone whenever they're all indifferent between two gambles, then the only social ranking consistent with that is a weighted sum of individual expected utilities. But this seems to force the planner to be indifferent between two different procedures for handing out an indivisible good -- say, a single donor kidney between two equally matched patients -- as long as each procedure gives both patients the same odds beforehand. An honest coin flip and some convoluted points system rigged to produce the same 50/50 odds come out as morally interchangeable. That still bothers me: the coin flip seems to have a kind of procedural fairness going for it that isn't just reducible to the odds it produces, on pain of changing the subject. I've since found out this is close to an old objection the economist Peter Diamond raised against Harsanyi, but I've never seen the two sides actually argue it out properly. Could you write a dialogue between a committed defender of Harsanyi's theorem and a defender of the Diamond-style objection, debating in front of a hospital ethics board over which procedure to use? Each side should have to directly answer the other's strongest point rather than just restate their own view, and neither should be allowed to win by default.
