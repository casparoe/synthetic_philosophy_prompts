So I teach a grad seminar on normative ethics every other year, and this week we're doing the old Barcan Marcus vs. Williams vs. Donagan fight over whether genuine moral dilemmas are actually possible -- the strong kind, where an agent is under two all-things-considered obligations that just can't both be met, not merely a case where we're unsure which duty wins out. Most of my students come in having read Barcan Marcus's "Moral Dilemmas and Consistency" and the bit of Williams on moral residue, and they leave basically convinced the question's settled: if it's appropriate to feel something like regret or remorse even after you've done the right thing in a conflict case (Agamemnon at Aulis, Sartre's student torn between his mother and the resistance, the standard confidentiality-vs-duty-to-warn scenario), then surely the obligation you didn't act on was real too, and morality really can hand you a situation with no clean way out. I'd like to push back on that a bit before we meet again, and I'd rather walk in with a sharp opposing case than just wing it from memory.

Can you argue, as strongly as you're able, for the position that dilemmas of this strong kind don't actually occur -- that in every one of these cases exactly one obligation is binding all things considered, and that the residue people point to is fully explained by the loosing obligation having been real but merely prima facie (in Ross's sense), not by it being genuinely unresolved. I want you to actually engage with the logic of it rather than just asserting things: say something specific about the agglomeration principle (OA & OB -> O(A&B)) and why giving that up, which is more or less what you have to do to make room for real dilemmas in standard deontic logic, is the worse cost here. And take a real position on Donagan's old point that the hardest cases -- Agamemnon especially -- only look genuinely dilemmatic because the agent's own prior conduct created the bind, which on his view means they don't show that a clean moral theory generates conflicts for an agent who hasn't already gone wrong somewhere upstream. I've got the general shape of the debate covered already, so don't survey it for me -- I just want the best, most specific version of this one argument.
