I'm taking a seminar this term on the ethics of killing, and the last few weeks have moved from Aquinas's treatment of self-defense straight into the modern trolley-problem literature, on the premise that the doctrine of double effect is the thread connecting them. We got through the standard cases without much trouble: the "bystander" version, where you throw a switch to send a runaway trolley from a track holding five workers onto a track holding one, and the "footbridge" version, where the only way to save the five is to push a large man off a footbridge into the trolley's path so that his body stops it. My professor mentioned in passing that Judith Jarvis Thomson devised a further variant, usually called the Loop Track case, specifically to put pressure on the doctrine, but we never actually got to discuss it in class, and it's been bothering me since.

The case is this: the side track you can divert the trolley onto doesn't just have one man standing on it — it loops around and rejoins the main track before it reaches the five. So if the man weren't there, diverting the trolley would accomplish nothing: it would simply loop back and kill the five anyway. It's only because his body is heavy enough to stop the trolley in its tracks that throwing the switch saves anyone at all. Which means the act looks, physically, exactly like the innocuous bystander case — you're just throwing a switch, at a distance, without touching anyone — but the one man's death is doing precisely the work the doctrine says a death may not do: it is the means by which the five are saved, rather than a side effect of saving them.

What I'd like from you is a reconstruction of how Aquinas himself, on his own terms, would rule on the Loop Track case — not by way of the later four-condition checklist that scholastic and modern commentators distilled from him, but from the actual argument in Summa Theologica II-II, q.64, a.7, with its distinction between what is intended per se and what merely happens praeter intentionem, and its requirement that the force used be proportionate. Does that framework actually generate a determinate verdict on the Loop Track case, or does it strain in the same way many contemporary philosophers think the ordinary means/side-effect distinction strains here? And is there anything in Aquinas's broader treatment of voluntary action, such as his distinction between what is voluntary "in itself" and what is merely voluntary "in its cause," that could do argumentative work the later summary can't? I'd rather you work through his reasoning on its own terms than fall back on how double effect usually gets presented in an introductory ethics course.
