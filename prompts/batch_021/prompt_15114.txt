I'm taking an intermediate game theory course this semester, and this week's problem set is on iterated elimination of dominated strategies, specifically the version where a pure strategy can be eliminated if some mixed strategy dominates it, even when no pure strategy does. During our review session before the midterm, two people in my study group, Marcus and Ines, spent almost twenty minutes arguing over one of the practice games and never actually settled it, so I'd like a neutral outside read, since I've gone back and forth myself and no longer trust my own judgment on it.

The game is:

|   | L | C | R |
|---|---|---|---|
| T | 3, 2 | 2, 0 | 0, 2 |
| M | 1, 0 | 1, 5 | 1, 0 |
| B | 0, 2 | 2, 0 | 3, 2 |

(Player 1 picks the row and is the first payoff in each cell; Player 2 picks the column and is the second.) The argument went, in order: (1) Marcus checked T against M and B against M column by column, found that T beats M in L and C but loses to it in R, and that B beats M in C and R but loses to it in L, and concluded that since no pure row beats M in every column, M is not dominated and survives. (2) Ines objected that this wasn't enough, since the professor's definition also lets a strategy be dominated by a randomization over the player's other strategies, so they still had to check whether some mix of T and B dominates M. (3) Marcus pushed back that this couldn't matter: a mixed strategy's payoff in a column is just a weighted average of T's and B's payoffs there, and averaging two rows that are each sometimes better and sometimes worse than M should still be sometimes better and sometimes worse, not uniformly better. (4) Ines replied that this is exactly wrong here: playing T and B each with probability 1/2 gives Player 1 an expected payoff of 1.5 in L, 2 in C, and 1.5 in R, all strictly above M's constant payoff of 1, so M is in fact strictly dominated. She added that this isn't a minor technicality in this particular game: no pure strategy dominates any other pure strategy anywhere in the original matrix, for either player, so a solver who only ever checks pure-strategy dominance would conclude the game is already fully reduced and stop immediately, whereas correctly removing M first causes column C to become dominated by L on the very next round, since C only looked safe in the first place because of how well it does against M specifically. She ended by telling Marcus that he should now just assume that any strategy not dominated by a pure strategy is probably dominated by some mix instead.

Could you go through moves (1) through (4) and say who is right and who is wrong at each step, rather than just declaring an overall winner? In particular, address whether Marcus's averaging intuition in (3) is mistaken only for this matrix or reflects a more general error, and whether Ines's closing advice to Marcus is sound or an overreach. Please also state explicitly which strategy, or strategies, for each player actually survive iterated elimination once mixed strategies are correctly taken into account.
