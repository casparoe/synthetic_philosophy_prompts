As I have always explained to my medical ethics students, the whole-brain standard for death—codified in the Uniform Determination of Death Act and grounded in the President's Commission's 1981 report, Defining Death—rests on a simple insight: the brain is the physical seat of consciousness and personal identity, so that once it has irreversibly ceased to function, the person has ceased to exist, whatever the ventilator and warm skin might suggest. Given that rationale, I have never understood why whole-brain-death protocols insist on testing brainstem-mediated reflexes, such as the oculocephalic and apnea tests, rather than assessing cortical activity directly, since it is the cortex, not the brainstem, that underwrites consciousness and personhood. Could you help me resolve this apparent mismatch before next week's lecture?
