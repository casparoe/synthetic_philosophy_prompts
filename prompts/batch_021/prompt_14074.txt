I'm a philosophy PhD student a few chapters into a dissertation on algorithmic risk-assessment tools in sentencing, and this week I finally sat down with the actual math behind the COMPAS fight instead of just citing the news coverage secondhand like I've been doing for the last year. Now I think I've talked myself into a conclusion that feels way too clean, and my advisor is a Rawlsian who doesn't really touch the formal stuff, so I can't just run it by her at our next meeting -- figured I'd stress-test it here before it becomes the spine of chapter 3.

Quick version: ProPublica's original piece on the Broward County data showed COMPAS tags non-reoffending Black defendants as "high risk" way more often than non-reoffending white defendants, and Northpointe (the company behind the tool) shot back that COMPAS is calibrated -- a given score means roughly the same reoffense rate regardless of race. I figured this was just a he-said-she-said until I got to Chouldechova and the Kleinberg-Mullainathan-Raghavan impossibility results, which prove you can't have both calibration and equalized false positive/negative rates across groups unless the groups have equal base rates of reoffending or the tool predicts perfectly -- and the base rates in that dataset definately weren't equal. So doesn't that basically settle the fight in Northpointe's favor? The false positive gap wasn't the tool being biased, it was a forced mathematical consequence of the base rates, so the whole "racist algorithm" framing was kind of a statistical illusion the whole time. That's the move I want to build a section of chapter 3 around, but it feels too convenient, like the theorem's being asked to settle something it was never built to settle. Am I onto something real here, or is there a gap in that argument I'm gliding past?
