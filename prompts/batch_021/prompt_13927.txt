I hold a joint appointment in philosophy, which is how I ended up filling the "non-scientific member" seat that federal regulations require on my medical center's Institutional Review Board -- a role that mostly involves nodding along until a protocol like this month's lands on my desk. We are reviewing a Phase 2 trial of psilocybin-assisted therapy for existential distress in patients with terminal cancer, closely modeled on the studies now running at several academic medical centers. The science and safety sections are uncontroversial; the dispute is entirely over the consent form. Half the board is satisfied with language describing "altered states of consciousness, mystical-type experiences, and changes in mood, outlook, or values" alongside the usual physiological risks. The other half -- including our palliative care attending, who has sat with a great many dying patients -- argues that this boilerplate cannot do the work informed consent is supposed to do, since the therapeutic mechanism itself is an experience whose character and worth cannot be previewed from outside it, and since the trial's own outcome measures track durable increases in the personality trait of Openness, a trait normally quite stable past early adulthood -- suggesting that patients are not simply learning new facts about themselves but emerging from the study with different values than the ones they consented with.

I recognized this at once as a real-world version of the problem L.A. Paul raises about transformative choice, and I would like the committee's memo to engage it properly rather than settle for the truism that one cannot consent to what one does not understand. Paul distinguishes experiences that are epistemically transformative, which cannot be grasped without undergoing them, from those that are personally transformative, which remake the chooser's core preferences, and she argues that ordinary expected-value reasoning has no grip once a choice is both -- exactly the structure here. Her own way out is to stop asking the chooser to weigh anticipated outcomes and let her choose instead on whether she wants to discover who she will become, but I don't think that move is free: it risks licensing consent to almost anything so long as it is framed as self-discovery rather than as a gamble on outcomes. Please draft the memo I will circulate before Thursday's vote. It should reach one concrete recommendation -- approve the consent form as written, reject the protocol, or approve it contingent on specific, named revisions -- and it must take the view that the existing language is adequate seriously enough that a board member who holds it would recognize her own position in your account of why it loses.
