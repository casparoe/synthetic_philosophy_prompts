And here's the classmate's answer I have to mark:

(a) The debate is about whether our justification for believing what people tell us is basic or derivative. Reductionists, following Hume, think that testimonial justification reduces to more fundamental sources — especially perception, memory and induction: we're entitled to trust a piece of testimony only insofar as we have (or could in principle produce) independent grounds, built up from those other sources, for thinking that testimony of that kind, or from that speaker, tends to be true. Anti-reductionists, following Reid, deny this: they hold that testimony is itself a basic source of justification, on a par with perception, so hearing someone assert P can give you justification for believing P without your first having to establish, from independent evidence, that testimony is generally reliable.

(b) Global and local reductionism differ in how demanding they are. Local reductionism is the classical, more demanding view: it holds that our trust in testimony as a whole has to rest on one general inductive argument, made once, establishing that testimony is by and large a reliable guide to the truth — roughly the kind of argument Hume gestures at when he says our assurance in any matter of fact founded on testimony derives from our observation of the conformity between reports and reality. Global reductionism is the weaker, more contemporary refinement (associated with Elizabeth Fricker): instead of one grand argument covering testimony-in-general, the hearer just needs, for each particular piece of testimony, some positive reason specific to that speaker and occasion — reasons that can vary case by case rather than being drawn from a single global argument.

(c) One good argument for anti-reductionism is the "child" argument: young children clearly acquire an enormous amount of justified belief, arguably knowledge, through testimony from parents and teachers, well before they've built up anything like the inductive track record reductionism says is required to trust testimony. If we don't want to say a small child's testimony-based beliefs are all unjustified, that pushes us toward thinking testimony can justify without prior independent corroboration, which is just what anti-reductionism claims. An influential objection to anti-reductionism is that it makes trust too cheap: since testimony, unlike perception, is produced by agents who can lie, be mistaken, or have an axe to grind, treating it as a basic, default-entitled source risks licensing all sorts of gullible belief we'd never accept on a stranger's say-so alone.

(d) This is largely a fair characterization, and it's actually one of the more attractive features of anti-reductionism rather than a mark against it. If testimony is a basic source of justification the same way perception is, then just as you don't first have to rule out that you're dreaming before your visual experience can justify a belief, a hearer doesn't first have to rule out that the speaker is lying or mistaken before testimony can justify belief. Burge's Acceptance Principle captures exactly this: a person is entitled to accept as true whatever is presented to her as true. That's precisely why anti-reductionism is sometimes accused of being too permissive — on this view the default state is acceptance rather than suspicion, and checking testimony against background knowledge for defeaters is really a reductionist habit, a leftover from needing to accumulate independent evidence case by case, rather than something anti-reductionism itself demands of the hearer before belief counts as justified.
