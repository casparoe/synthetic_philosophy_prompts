Write a short dialogue between two philosophers debating why promises bind. One defends a practice-based, conventionalist account, in the tradition of Hume, Rawls, and Hart, on which a promise obligates because making one invokes a social practice of mutual trust whose benefits cannot fairly be claimed while refusing to be bound by its rules. The other defends an assurance-based account, of the kind developed by Scanlon, on which the obligation arises directly from the expectation that one person deliberately and openly leads another to form, independent of any wider practice or convention. Have them test their views against this case: alone and unobserved, someone promises a dying friend to carry out a small private wish after the friend's death; the friend dies moments later, and no one else will ever learn whether the promise is honored, so no practice is put at risk and no one remains who could be disappointed by a breach. Each philosopher should say what their own theory implies about whether the promise still binds, and each should press the other on whether their account, rather than a borrowed intuition, actually explains the answer.
