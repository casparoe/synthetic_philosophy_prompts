I'm a PhD student in political communication, and something I keep running into within my own family has started to bother me more than the usual explanations from my field can account for. Over the last several years, my uncle has become committed to an elaborate theory according to which a number of major world events were secretly orchestrated by a small hidden group, and nothing the rest of us say to him — most recently at a family wedding this summer — ever seems to gain any real purchase: he treats every document, statistic, or expert opinion we bring him either as untrustworthy on its face or as further confirmation of the cover-up. My colleagues would call this motivated reasoning or an epistemic bubble, and I'm sure that's part of the story, but lately I've started to suspect there's a more basic problem that those categories don't quite capture.

What brought this into focus was a strange exercise I tried on myself: attempting to state my actual grounds for believing things I would previously have said were simply obvious, such as that the world existed for a long stretch of time before I was born, or that the people around me have inner lives roughly like my own. I couldn't produce anything resembling an argument for either. They don't feel like conclusions I've reasoned my way to so much as fixed points I use to weigh every other claim — including my uncle's.

That's unsettling, because if his core commitments and mine are, in this sense, equally without ordinary evidential support, I don't see what entitles me to call my outlook rational and his merely stubborn, and yet it still seems clearly wrong to treat the two as simply symmetrical. My cousins are split on what to do about him: some think we simply haven't hit on the right piece of evidence yet, others think arguing with him has been a waste of breath for years. I'd genuinely like to know whether either side has the more defensible position, and, more fundamentally, whether there's a way of understanding what's actually going on between us that would tell us if — and how — someone in my position could still reach him.
