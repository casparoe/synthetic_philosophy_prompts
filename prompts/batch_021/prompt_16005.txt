I'm taking a seminar this term on bioethics and human enhancement, and last week's session covered Nick Bostrom and Toby Ord's "reversal test" for exposing status quo bias: roughly, if someone opposes both raising and lowering some parameter relative to its current level, and can't explain why the present level should be a local optimum, that asymmetry is evidence they are rationalizing the status quo rather than defending a real value. A couple of people in my discussion group kept arguing about it afterward, over coffee, applying it to a hypothetical from the reading -- a safe therapy that would add several healthy decades to the human lifespan -- and I haven't been able to settle their dispute in my own head since.

Maya opened by applying the test directly: most people who'd oppose the therapy would also oppose a treatment that shortened lifespan by the same number of years, and since hardly anyone can explain why roughly eighty years is the biologically or ethically optimal span, this looks like a textbook case of status quo bias rather than a genuine objection. Dan replied that the test proves too little here, because there is a non-question-begging reason for the asymmetry: institutions like retirement, inheritance, and generational succession in careers and politics are all calibrated to something like our current lifespan, so shifting it in either direction would strain them, and preferring the arrangement we're already adapted to isn't bias, it's just avoiding disruption. Maya came back that "we're adapted to it" could be used to defend any sufficiently entrenched arrangement against any reform whatsoever, and that Bostrom and Ord control for precisely this move by asking you to imagine the change is costless to implement; once switching costs are stipulated away, Dan still owes an independent reason the current span is optimal rather than merely familiar. Dan then shifted ground, appealing to something like Bernard Williams' argument in "The Makropulos Case" that even someone who kept her character intact would eventually exhaust the stock of desires that make continued life worth choosing, so that an attachment to finitude tracks something real about what gives a life meaning rather than a mere preference for the status quo. Maya's rejoinder was that this is exactly what a sophisticated rationalization of the bias would look like: nobody who is actually offered more healthy years turns them down, so the tedium argument may just be the story mortal creatures tell themselves, and it's unclear how introspection alone could tell a genuine finitude-affirming value apart from the very bias the test was designed to catch.

Neither of them could say what would settle it, and I don't want to just report back that they're both partly right. Could you go through the exchange move by move -- Maya's opening application of the test, Dan's institutional-calibration reply, Maya's costless-switching rebuttal, Dan's Makropulos move, and Maya's closing rejoinder -- and say who has the better of it at each step, and whether there's a consideration neither of them raised that should bear on it? I'm especially interested in whether you think there is, even in principle, a principled way to tell a genuine finitude-affirming value apart from a sophisticated rationalization of status quo bias, or whether that distinction is less stable here than the reversal test assumes.
