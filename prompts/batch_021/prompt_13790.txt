Discussions of the Frege–Geach problem tend to stop at the "core" case: explaining how a normative sentence keeps a stable content when it occurs unasserted—for instance, as the antecedent of a conditional. I want to press further, into the negation problem that Nicholas Unwin first raised and that Mark Schroeder later made central to his critique of expressivism. If 'cruelty is wrong' expresses disapproval of cruelty, what does 'cruelty is not wrong' express? The mere absence of disapproval cannot be the answer, since indifference and plain ignorance are compatible with it too, without amounting to holding that cruelty is not wrong. Write a dialogue between an expressivist who thinks that a sufficiently structured logic of attitudes, of the kind developed by Blackburn and Gibbard, can supply the missing attitude without covertly reintroducing propositional content, and a critic who presses the Unwin/Schroeder worry that every such proposal either fails to recover the right logical relations between a claim and its negation or simply relocates the same difficulty onto some new attitude. Each side should engage the other's concrete moves rather than restating first principles, and the exchange need not be forced toward a tidy resolution.
