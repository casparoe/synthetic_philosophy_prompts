My grandmother has advanced dementia, and last month, at a care-planning meeting, the staff at her memory unit introduced my family to what they call "therapeutic fibbing." When she asks after my grandfather, who died in 2011, we are coached to tell her that he is "at work" or "running an errand" rather than the truth, since correcting her devastates her for a few minutes and then simply dissolves, leaving nothing behind but fresh grief and none of the understanding that might make it bearable. My mother, who raised us in a fairly serious Catholic household, refuses to go along with it, and when I pressed her on why, she invoked Augustine, of all people, whom I hadn't thought about since an undergraduate theology seminar.

I've since gone back and reread what I could find of De Mendacio and Contra Mendacium, and I'd forgotten how uncompromising Augustine actually is on this subject: no lie is permissible even to save an innocent life, lies are graded into degrees of sinfulness but never reach zero, and the wrong seems to consist less in any harm done to the person deceived than in what the act does to the liar's own truthfulness before God. That last point is where I can't get a straight answer out of anyone, including the two priests I've asked about it, which is why I'm bringing it to you instead. Augustine's whole framework seems to presuppose a listener who can be deceived in the ordinary sense: someone who forms a false belief, retains it, and could in principle later discover the deception and feel betrayed. This is a case that could not quite have arisen in his own lifetime, when comparatively few people survived long enough to lose their memories this completely, and when no institution existed whose entire purpose was to manage that loss on a family's behalf. My grandmother cannot do any of the things his account of a wronged listener seems to require.

So here is what I'd like from you: reconstruct, as rigorously as you can from Augustine's actual arguments rather than from the popular Kantian "murderer at the door" version of "never lie" that tends to crowd them out, what his verdict on therapeutic fibbing would be. Does the listener's inability to retain or ever discover the deception register at all, given that his objection is aimed at the state of the speaker's own heart rather than at any transaction completed in the listener's mind, or does it leave the verdict completely untouched? Would he count the redirection our care team actually prefers, steering her toward old photographs instead of answering directly, as one of the silences and evasions he permits in place of a lie, or, since he seems to care about the intention to deceive rather than the literal truth of the sentence spoken, would he treat that kind of engineered misdirection as no better than an outright falsehood? And where, if anywhere, would a case like this fit into his hierarchy of increasingly venial lies, given that it resembles none of his own examples particularly closely?
