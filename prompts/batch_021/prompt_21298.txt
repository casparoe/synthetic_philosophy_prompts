quick one — I'm about four drafts into a novel set in a Lancashire cotton-mill family in the 1870s, and the son who's meant to inherit the business has picked up some ideas from a local co-op reading circle about turning the mill into a profit-sharing concern instead of just taking it over outright. To get his voice right I've been ploughing through Mill's "Chapters on Socialism" (the one that got published unfinished after he died), and I've hit a paragraph I genuinely can't parse, and I need to sort it out before I put a version of it in this kid's mouth.

Here's the bit, right after he's argued that private property will stick around for a long while yet becuase people won't give it up till something better has actually been proven to work:

"But though, for these reasons, individual property has presumably a long term before it, if only of provisional existence, we are not, therefore, to conclude that it must exist during that whole term unmodified, or that all the rights now regarded as appertaining to property belong to it inherently, and must endure while it endures. On the contrary, it is both the duty and the interest of those who derive the most direct benefit from the laws of property to give impartial consideration to all proposals for rendering those laws in any way less onerous to the majority. This, which would in any case be an obligation of justice, is an injunction of prudence also, in order to place themselves in the right against the attempts which are sure to be frequent to bring the Socialist forms of society prematurely into operation."

What I can't tell is whether "duty" and "interest" here are doing the same job or two different ones. Is he just saying "be fair, also it happens to be smart" — two independent reasons stacked up — or is it sneakier than that, like: reform now so that later, when people push for the real thing, you've already earned the right to resist them with a clean conscience? And "in the right" in whose eyes exactly — the public's, history's, his own utilitarian bookkeeping? Don't need a whole lecture on Mill, just help me pin down what's actually being claimed so I know if my mill-owner sounds calculating or genuinely torn when he says something like this to his son.
