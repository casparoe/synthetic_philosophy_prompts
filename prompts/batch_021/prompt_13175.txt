Two months ago I was sworn in as the newest public member of our state's Board of Pardons and Paroles, and I have just been handed my first real assignment: drafting the framing memo for next month's commutation hearing. The petitioner, whom the file calls Mr. Ortiz, was the getaway driver in an armed robbery sixteen years ago in which a store clerk was seriously injured; he has served sixteen of a twenty-two-year sentence and has an unusually clean disciplinary record. His petition leans heavily on a mitigating factor our guidelines allow the board to weigh, "diminished culpability due to coercion at the time of the offense," and he has always maintained that the robbery's organizer threatened to hurt his sister if he refused to drive.

The complication, and the reason I cannot simply write the obvious memo, is buried in the organizer's own post-arrest statement to detectives, which our file review turned up. The organizer said Ortiz "was already in" before any threat was ever made -- he owed the organizer money and wanted a cut of the proceeds -- and that the threat was only ever insurance: if Ortiz had shown the slightest hesitation, the organizer claims he would have had someone else make sure Ortiz drove regardless. Ortiz never hesitated, so no one ever had to force him. Half the board wants to treat this as proof the coercion claim deserves no weight, since the threat did no actual work; the other half argues that if Ortiz really could not have refused without being compelled anyway, that fact should still count in his favor, whether or not it was what actually moved him to act. This is the detail that nags at me, because it resembles, dimly, a thought experiment I half-remember from an undergraduate philosophy class -- something about a scientist who can force a man's hand but never has to, because the man acts on his own first -- and I cannot reconstruct the argument well enough to know whether it helps Ortiz's case or sinks it, or whether the board members on whichever side turns out to be losing that comparison have some further move available to them.

Could you draft the memo I will actually circulate before the hearing? It should land on one clear recommendation for the board to vote on -- denial, full commutation, or commutation to a reduced term with conditions -- lay out reasoning the other members could follow and contest on the merits, and take seriously, rather than dismiss by fiat, the strongest version of whichever side's argument the recommendation ultimately doesn't adopt.
