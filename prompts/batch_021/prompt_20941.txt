At work, two colleagues of mine got into a running argument over a small internal library one of them built for modeling bidding strategies in our ad-auction system. The library solves normal-form games, and a preprocessing step is supposed to prune obviously bad strategies before handing the game off to a Nash-equilibrium solver -- that's the step they argued about. I only heard about it secondhand through the pull request thread, but I'd like an outside read on who was right.

The test case that started it: our player has three strategies, U, M, and D; the opponent has two, L and R. Our player's payoffs are U = (3, 0), M = (1, 1), D = (0, 3), read as (payoff if the opponent plays L, payoff if the opponent plays R).

1. Derek argued the existing pruning function -- which only compares pairs of pure strategies and removes one if some other pure strategy beats it outright in every column -- was correct here: since neither U nor D beats M in both columns, M survives, and that's the right outcome.

2. Alina replied that the function is broken: a 50/50 mix of U and D pays (1.5, 1.5), which beats M = (1, 1) in both columns even though no single pure strategy does, so M should be pruned, and a checker restricted to pure-vs-pure comparisons will never catch this.

3. Derek conceded that example but pushed back that checking against mixed strategies turns each dominance check into a linear-programming feasibility problem rather than a handful of comparisons, and worried that this richer notion of dominance would make the final "reduced game" depend on the order in which strategies happen to get eliminated -- which he considered a serious problem for a preprocessing step meant to hand a single, canonical simplified game downstream.

4. Alina answered that order is a non-issue for this particular kind of dominance: iteratively removing strictly dominated strategies, whether the dominator is pure or mixed, always leaves the same reduced game no matter the elimination order. She added a warning, though -- that guarantee disappears the moment they also prune weakly dominated strategies (ties allowed) to shrink the game further, since that process is order-dependent and can strip out strategies that are actually played in genuine Nash equilibria of the original game.

5. Derek closed by saying this was a non-problem in practice: fix one deterministic rule for breaking ties among weakly dominated strategies (say, always eliminate the lowest-indexed one first), and every run on the same input gives the same output -- so calling the process "order-dependent" is a theoretical worry that doesn't survive contact with a real implementation.

Could you go through this move by move and tell me who's right at each of the five numbered points, not just who comes out ahead overall -- and flag anything important that both of them are missing?
