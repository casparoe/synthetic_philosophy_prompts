So in my metaethics seminar we've been going through fitting-attitude / buck-passing theories of value -- the Scanlon-ish idea that for something to be good just is for it to have properties that give reasons to favor it -- and this week we got to the wrong kind of reasons problem. I get the classic case fine: a demon threatens to torture you unless you come to desire some worthless saucer of mud, which obviously doesn't make the mud good, it just gives you a reason to want to want it (I think this is from a Rabinowicz and Rønnow-Rasmussen paper, "the strike of the demon" or something like that? not 100% sure I'm remembering the title right).

What actually threw me is when my professor said the natural fix -- just restrict buck-passing to reasons that are "object-given" (grounded in the object's own properties) rather than "state-given" (grounded in facts about what having the attitude itself produces) -- doesn't actually solve the problem, because apparently you can construct cases where the reason is grounded in a genuine property of the object and still intuitively counts as the wrong kind of reason. A friend in my class thinks this basically wrecks the whole fitting-attitude project, but I feel like I'm missing the actual argument rather than just the vibe.
