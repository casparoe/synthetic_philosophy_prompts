I am putting together a graduate seminar this term that reads Michael Dummett's technical papers on truth, meaning, and bivalence alongside his more occasional political writings, and a puzzle has come up while preparing it that I would like another philosopher's opinion on before I bring it to the class.

Dummett is well known for arguing, region by region, that the classical principle of bivalence—that every statement is determinately true or false—cannot simply be assumed wherever we have no way, even in principle, of verifying or refuting the statement in question, since on his view our grasp of a statement's meaning cannot outrun what could count as evidence for or against it. An undecided number-theoretic conjecture and a claim about the remote past for which every trace of evidence has vanished are his standard illustrations, and in both cases he concludes that our logic for such statements should be intuitionistic rather than classical. Given this well-known feature of his semantics, it strikes me as a genuine inconsistency that in his short book "On Immigration and Refugees" he argues, without anything like comparable hedging, for quite definite conclusions about what obligations states owe to refugees as against economic migrants—exactly the sort of contested normative question for which no proof or decision procedure settles the matter one way or the other. If Dummett's own account forbids us from simply assuming a statement is true or false wherever verification is unavailable, it seems that he, of all philosophers, should have been committed to a far more tentative, suspended stance on immigration policy than the one he actually takes. Could you help me work out whether this really is a tension in Dummett's overall body of work, or whether I am running together considerations that ought to be kept apart?
