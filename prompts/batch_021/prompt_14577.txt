A friend and I spent much of last night arguing about whether the "moral equality of combatants" can survive the rise of autonomous weapons systems, and neither of us walked away convinced. I brought up Rousseau's argument, in the Social Contract, that war is properly a relation between states rather than between individual men: soldiers are one another's enemies only accidentally, as instruments of their states rather than as men, and a soldier who lays down his arms reverts to being simply a man again, whom no one has a right to kill. That passage is often treated as an ancestor of the modern idea that combatants on opposing sides deserve equal moral standing regardless of which side's cause is just. My friend's objection was that this argument quietly presupposes a human being who bears arms, risks death, and can in principle surrender, and that it collapses once one side's "combatant" is an autonomous system incapable of fear, surrender, or moral agency.

I'd like your carefully reasoned best guess at how Rousseau, arguing strictly from his own premises rather than from later just war theory, would answer my friend. Would he relocate the status of "soldier" onto whoever designs, deploys, or commands such a system, treat the state itself as having become an undisguised direct party to the killing, or defend the state-to-state relation his argument depends on in some other way? And does his logic give any principled grounds for saying that a human combatant facing an autonomous weapon is, or isn't, owed the same moral standing as one facing another human soldier?
