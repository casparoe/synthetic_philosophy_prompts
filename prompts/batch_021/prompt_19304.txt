My mother taught the Zhuangzi for nearly forty years. She died eleven days ago, and I've been asked to give her eulogy. She loved retelling the scene in chapter 18, "Perfect Happiness," where Huizi visits Zhuangzi after his own wife's death and finds him not weeping but drumming on a basin and singing, and she left instructions asking for something closer to that than to a conventional wake: a plain, unembalmed burial, and music rather than unbroken solemnity, echoing Zhuangzi's own refusal, near the end of the book, of a lavish funeral for himself on the grounds that the crows would eat him regardless of the coffin. The room will mix former students and colleagues who will notice at once if I flatten this into stock wisdom about not fearing death, with family who have never opened the text and are simply grieving. Write the eulogy so that it honors what she asked for without implying that her death doesn't matter, or that missing her is some failure to understand her properly -- in that very scene, Zhuangzi says he grieved too, at first, before he ever reasoned his way to the drum.
