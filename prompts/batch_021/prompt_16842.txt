A friend of mine, who has a long-standing interest in decision theory, and I spent most of a long drive last weekend arguing about whether a rational agent should "pay up" in Yudkowsky's counterfactual mugging: Omega, a perfect predictor, flips a fair coin. If it lands tails, Omega asks you for $100. If it lands heads, Omega pays you $10,000, but only if Omega predicts that you would have paid the $100 had the coin instead landed tails. Suppose the coin does land tails, and Omega duly asks. My friend maintained that any decision theory that simply conditions on the fact that the coin already came up tails — whether causal or evidential — gets this exactly right: paying does nothing for you now, causally or evidentially, so refusing is the correct choice, and a theory recommending payment is confusing "the policy I would have liked to commit to in advance" with "what I have good reason to do now, given what I actually know." I argued the opposite: that judging rationality act by act, in isolation from the policy that generates the act, is precisely what makes an agent exploitable and gives the wrong answer in structurally similar cases like Parfit's hitchhiker, so that updateless decision theory — which ranks policies by their expected value from the standpoint of a prior, rather than ranking acts by expected value given updated credences — is the more defensible view, however strange it can feel from inside a single decision. Neither of us moved the other, so I would like a more rigorous third opinion.

Write an essay that adjudicates this dispute and defends a definite verdict. Begin by stating precisely what distinguishes an updateless, policy-based approach from decision theories (causal or evidential) that always condition on the agent's current evidence at the moment of choice; illustrate the difference using counterfactual mugging together with at least one further case, such as Parfit's hitchhiker or the transparent variant of Newcomb's problem, in which the contents of both boxes are visible before you choose. Bring in the older "resolute choice" versus "sophisticated choice" distinction from the dynamic-choice literature (McClennen and those who followed him) as one lens on what is really at stake. Weigh the strongest considerations on each side — dynamic consistency, vulnerability to exploitation or money-pumping, the apparent arbitrariness of privileging some "ex ante" vantage point or prior over the agent's actual situation, and whether resoluteness should count as a requirement of rationality itself or merely a useful feature to build into certain agents. Close by actually taking a side rather than surveying the field; a qualified verdict is acceptable (for instance, one restricted to certain kinds of predictors, or to situations the agent could have foreseen and planned for in advance), but it must be a real, argued verdict.
