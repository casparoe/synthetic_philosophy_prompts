In my practical ethics seminar this week we covered moral trade: the idea, modeled on comparative advantage in economics, that people with differing moral views can each end up doing more good by their own lights if they trade -- you do more of what I value, I do more of what you value -- than if each of us simply acted unilaterally on our own priorities. I help run my university's Effective Altruism society, whose dozen-odd members are united in wanting to do good but genuinely split: some are committed consequentialists prioritizing existential risk or animal welfare, others hold deontological commitments that make them uneasy about treating their own wrongdoing as something another member's good deed could offset, and a few see giving as a religious duty rather than a target to optimize. Right now we just debate and vote each year on where the society's shared fundraising budget should go. Please describe the most important implications of moral trade for how a group as morally divided as ours might actually approach that decision.
