I was recently appointed to my county's newly formed oversight board for pretrial risk-assessment tools, and at our first meeting the vendor and the public defenders' office talked past each other about what "fair" even means. The vendor maintains the tool is fair because it is well-calibrated within each racial group (a score of 7 out of 10 implies roughly the same rearrest rate whether the defendant is Black or white), while the public defenders maintain it is unfair because of the well-documented gap in false-positive rates across those same groups, the same pattern ProPublica reported in its investigation of COMPAS. I've since read that Kleinberg, Mullainathan, and Raghavan, and separately Chouldechova, proved that calibration and equal error rates across groups (sometimes called "equalized odds") cannot both hold once the groups' base rates of reoffending differ, but every proof I've found just manipulates conditional expectations, and I haven't managed to internalize why this has to be true rather than merely verifying that it is. Could you give me an intuitive, close-to-first-principles explanation of why a well-calibrated score is essentially forced into unequal error rates whenever base rates diverge across groups, ideally illustrated with a minimal worked example rather than the general algebra?
