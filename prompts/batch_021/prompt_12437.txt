So I've been kind of low-key obsessed for the past few weeks with this Jain idea of anekantavada (hope I'm even spelling that right) — I first heard about it from one throwaway line in a book about Gandhi mentioning he was influenced by it, and then fell down a wiki-and-random-pdf rabbit hole for like two weekends straight. Here's my problem: every source I read uses anekantavada, syadvada, and nayavada almost interchangeably, like they're just three fancy names for "there are many perspectives, man." But when I look closer it seems like they're actually supposed to be doing three different jobs — something about reality itself having infinite properties, something about how any single statement only ever comes from one limited standpoint, and something about how you're supposed to literally qualify your sentences with the word "syat." I can't tell if that's a real three-part structure I'm missing or if people are just being sloppy with the vocab and it's really all one idea wearing three hats.

The bit that really trips me up is the "seven-fold predication" thing (saptabhangi, if I've got the term right), where they'll say of the same jar that it "in some sense is," "in some sense is not," "in some sense both is and is not," and then theres even a slot for "in some sense is indescribable." On a first read that just looks like a license to say anything about anything and call it profound — and apparently rival schools back in the day made basically that exact objection, that it's either trivial relativism or a straight-up violation of non-contradiction. Also, "syat" apparently doesn't even mean "maybe" the way I'd assumed (like epistemic uncertainty) — it's supposedly more like "from this particular angle" — and honestly that clarification just confused me more instead of less. So what I'm actually asking is: what's the logical structure that's supposed to stop the seven-fold thing from collapsing into "well it depends, so everything's a little bit true"? And is there an actual Jain answer to the obvious "isn't the doctrine itself either one-sided or self-refuting" gotcha, or do they basically just have to bite that bullet at some point? (Also please don't just hand me the blind-men-and-the-elephant story — I've since learned that one isn't even originally Jain to begin with, and everyone uses it as a bit of a cop-out explanation.)
