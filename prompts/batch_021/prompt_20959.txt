I am drafting the theoretical section of a paper for a workshop on the metaphysics of shared agency, and before I commit the central argument to print I would like an interlocutor's eyes on it. The section sits at the point where Michael Bratman's planning-theoretic account of shared intention meets Margaret Gilbert's plural-subject account, and rather than send the whole draft, I have distilled the argument into the paragraphs below.

Bratman's account is resolutely reductive: a shared intention that we J is built entirely out of individual attitudes. I intend that we J, you intend that we J, each of us intends this in accordance with and because of meshing sub-plans, and all of this is common knowledge between us. No primitive we-intention and no plural subject is invoked; the whole apparatus is individual planning states plus mutual belief.

Gilbert's objection is that this leaves out exactly what makes joint action feel different from two people's plans merely dovetailing. Suppose two movers are carrying a wardrobe up a narrow staircase and one lets go without warning. The other has not simply had her private expectations disappointed, the way she would if a solo plan of her own had gone wrong; she has standing to rebuke him specifically, and he could not have discharged himself of the undertaking just by changing his mind — ending it properly required something like her concurrence. Gilbert takes this directed, debt-like obligation to be intelligible only on the assumption that the two had jointly committed to intend as a body, a status she holds to resist analysis into any conjunction of personal commitments, however tightly interlocking and however commonly known those commitments are.

My worry is that Bratman's later concessions — that the parties' intentions are interdependent, each conditional on the other's persisting, so that reneging without notice undermines the very basis on which the other was entitled to rely — still only deliver a complaint about impaired self-governance, not a debt owed to that other person as such. Interdependence explains why abandonment is costly, even irrational; it does not explain why the mover who kept holding on is owed completion by the one who let go, rather than merely disadvantaged by him, or why she, rather than some third party, is positioned to release him from the undertaking. So I think the objection survives the interdependence move, unless "entitled to rely on" is already smuggling in the non-reductive result it was meant to explain. I suspect Searle's we-intentions, though irreducibly collective in content, inherit a version of the same difficulty, since they too are simply had by individuals, one by one.

I would welcome your comments on three things in particular: whether I have represented Bratman's interdependence and self-governance moves fairly rather than as a straw man; whether the wardrobe case is doing real argumentative work or whether a better example would sharpen the contrast; and whether the gap I am describing is a principled limit on any individual-attitude-based account of shared intention — Searle's and Tuomela's included — or just a contingent shortfall in Bratman's particular construction that a more resourceful reductionist could still patch. I am less interested in a verdict on who is ultimately right about collective intentionality than in whether the argument, as stated, actually earns its conclusion.
