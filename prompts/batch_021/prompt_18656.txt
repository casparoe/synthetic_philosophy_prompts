I got summoned for jury duty a few weeks ago and ended up on a civil case I still can't stop thinking about. Basically, a delivery van clipped my neighbor's car and took off, and the plaintiff's whole argument came down to the fact that something like 80% of the vans matching the witness's description on that route belong to one specific company, so it was probably them. A bunch of us on the jury were really uneasy about finding the company liable on that basis alone, even though 80% is obviously well past "more likely than not," and our foreperson kept saying we can't hold a company liable off a math problem. I couldn't articulate why that felt so right until later, when I went down a Google rabbit hole and found out this exact setup has a name in legal philosophy (the "Blue Bus problem," related to something called the gatecrasher paradox) and is apparently a genuinely unresolved puzzle.

What really hooked me is that some of what I read connects this to the "norms of assertion" debate in epistemology - whether you're allowed to assert/believe/testify to something just because it's true, or because you have justified belief, or because you actually know it. The version that stuck with me: a verdict is basically a kind of assertion, and if assertion requires knowledge rather then just a high enough credence, and knowledge in turn requires your belief to be "sensitive" to the facts (roughly, you'd have believed differently had things been otherwise), that would explain why bare statistics feel insufficient - the 80% figure is exactly the same whether or not this particular van was the culprit. Except then I remembered DNA evidence is ALSO just a statistic (a random-match probability), and courts treat that as basically decisive, and same with epidemiological stats in toxic tort cases. So now I'm stuck on why one kind of "merely statistical" evidence is supposedly not sensitive/knowledge-generating while the other is fine. My housemate, who's finishing a philosophy MA, just said "yeah, that's a real tension people argue about" and went back to grading, which did not help.

So here's what I'd love from you: please argue that appealing to a knowledge norm plus a sensitivity condition can't actually do the work of distinguishing "naked" statistical evidence like the van case from evidence like DNA matches or epidemiological statistics - i.e., build the strongest case you can that if sensitivity is really what disqualifies the bus-company-style evidence, it should disqualify (or at least seriously threaten) the DNA and epidemiological evidence too, so the tidy "knowledge norm explains the legal asymmetry" story doesn't actually hold up. Don't just lay out both sides evenly here - actually commit to that conclusion and defend it.
