I'm a junior, and I organize the speaker visits for my high school's philosophy club. A few weeks ago, in a Comparative Philosophy elective, our teacher had us read Zhuangzi's butterfly dream passage from the Inner Chapters alongside Descartes's dream argument for a unit on skepticism, and a friend and I ended up arguing for most of lunch about whether Zhuangzi is really doing the same kind of thing Descartes is. That argument is actually what led me to reach out to a scholar of classical Chinese philosophy who has written specifically against the common assumption that the butterfly dream is Zhuangzi's version of Cartesian dream-doubt. Her position, roughly, is that the passage isn't posing a skeptical puzzle about which state is "real" at all, but using the unresolved ambiguity between Zhuang Zhou and the butterfly to illustrate what the text calls the transformation of things (物化, wu hua) -- loosening our grip on the idea that any one perspective, waking or dreaming, human or otherwise, has to be the privileged one. She's agreed to a video-call Q&A with the club next month, and since I'm the one who invited her, I've been asked to draft the questions.

Could you help me write eight to ten questions that go beyond getting her to restate that thesis? I want at least one question that presses on why explicitly posing an unanswered question about which of the two is dreaming doesn't already amount to a quiet form of skepticism; at least one on the tension between her reading and the passage right before it in the same chapter, where Zhuangzi seems perfectly willing to call some people fools for mistaking themselves as wide awake when they're really still dreaming, which sounds a lot like ranking perspectives rather than refusing to rank them; and at least one on how her reading fits with the scene elsewhere in the text, in the "Autumn Floods" chapter, where Zhuangzi confidently claims to know that the fish he's watching from a bridge are happy -- which looks like cross-perspective knowledge, not a principled refusal to privilege any one perspective. From what I can tell, her go-to move whenever she's pushed on points like these is some version of "you're still assuming somebody has to be right about who's dreaming," so for each of those three questions, add a short follow-up designed to stop her from resorting to that line again. Please end with one last question asking what, if anything, she thinks would actually count as evidence for the more straightforwardly skeptical reading.
