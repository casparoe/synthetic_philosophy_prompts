I'm taking a seminar on Locke this term, and this week we moved from the Essay to Some Thoughts Concerning Education. I've been stuck on the sections where he sets out his alternative to corporal punishment and material bribery, and I'd like help seeing whether there's a genuine problem here or whether I'm just missing something. Here is the passage (§§55–56 and 58, keeping the original capitalization):

"§55. Rewards, I grant, and Punishments must be proposed to Children, if we intend to work upon them. The Mistake I imagine is, that those that are generally made use of, are Ill chosen. The Pains and Pleasures of the Body are, I think, of ill Consequence, when made the Rewards and Punishments whereby Men would prevail on their Children; for... they serve but to increase and strengthen those Inclinations, which 'tis our Business to subdue and master. What Principle of Virtue do you lay in a Child, if you will redeem his Desires of one Pleasure, by the Proposal of another?... If a Child cries for an unwholsome and dangerous Fruit, you purchase his Quiet by giving him a less hurtful Sweet-meat. This perhaps may preserve his Health, but spoils his Mind... for here you only change the Object, but flatter still his Appetite... By this Way of proceeding, you foment and cherish in him that which is the Spring from whence all the Evil flows.

§56. The Rewards and Punishments then, whereby we should keep Children in Order, are quite of another Kind... Esteem and Disgrace are, of all others, the most powerful Incentives to the Mind, when once it is brought to relish them. If you can once get into Children a Love of Credit, and an Apprehension of Shame and Disgrace, you have put into 'em the true Principle, which will constantly work and incline them to the right... which I look on as the great Secret of Education.

§58. But Secondly, To make the Sense of Esteem or Disgrace sink the deeper, and be of the more Weight, other agreeable or disagreeable Things should constantly accompany these different States; not as particular Rewards and Punishments of this or that particular Action, but as necessarily belonging to, and constantly attending one, who by his Carriage has brought himself into a State of Disgrace or Commendation. By which Way of treating them, Children may as much as possible be brought to conceive, that those that are commended, and in Esteem for doing well, will necessarily be belov'd and cherish'd by every Body, and have all other good Things as a Consequence of it; and on the other Side, when any one by Miscarriage falls into Disesteem... he will unavoidably fall under Neglect and Contempt; and in that State, the Want of whatever might satisfy or delight him will follow."

Locke's case against bodily pleasure and pain as rewards and punishments seems to be that this method doesn't master the child's governing appetite, it just redirects it: bribing a child out of a dangerous piece of fruit with a sweetmeat still "flatters" the very appetite virtue is supposed to subdue, and so "foments and cherishes" the "Spring from whence all the Evil flows." Fine so far. But when he turns to his preferred alternative, Esteem and Disgrace, §58 tells us that for these to "sink the deeper" they must themselves be reinforced by attaching further "agreeable or disagreeable Things" to the child's state of being esteemed or disgraced — commendation is to bring with it being "belov'd and cherish'd by every Body" and "all other good Things," disgrace is to bring "Neglect and Contempt" and "the Want of whatever might satisfy or delight him." That looks structurally identical to the sweetmeat case: a state of the child is again paired with attached pleasures and displeasures so that he acts well to get one and avoid the other. The only thing that seems to have changed is where the pleasure and pain are located, not the mechanism.

Is there a principled difference here that I'm not seeing — something about the kind of pleasure and pain involved, or about the mediating role "Esteem" is supposed to play — or is this actually an unnoticed tension in Locke's own argument? And if it is the same mechanism just relocated, does the well-behaved child of §58 differ, on Locke's own terms, from the child who is simply bribed with sweets, or is he only a more thoroughly and durably conditioned version of the same thing?
