Students had a week to write, could consult any written material, but were capped at 500 words per question and told to use Hacking's own examples rather than inventing new ones.

The question was: "Ian Hacking argues that classifications of people, unlike classifications of things such as rocks or chemical elements, can exhibit a 'looping effect.' (a) Explain, using at least one of Hacking's own examples, what the looping effect is and why he thinks it distinguishes human kinds from natural kinds (or, in his later terminology, interactive kinds from indifferent kinds). (b) Does the existence of looping effects show that psychiatric diagnoses are not real in the way that a diagnosis such as a fractured femur is real? Defend your answer with reference to Hacking's own discussion of realism and social construction."

Here is the student's answer, verbatim:

"Hacking's central idea is that some classifications change the very people they classify, and are in turn changed by them, producing a feedback loop he calls the looping effect. His best-known illustration is multiple personality disorder (now called dissociative identity disorder). Once psychiatrists described the syndrome in detail and the description became public knowledge, partly through case histories like Sybil, the population of diagnosed patients did not just grow in number but changed in kind: new patients presented with symptoms, numbers of alters, and abuse narratives that tracked the publicized criteria, and clinicians revised the diagnostic criteria to fit these new presentations, which then shaped the next generation of patients, and so on. Hacking contrasts this with indifferent kinds like quarks or gold: however physicists classify a quark, the quark does not know or care that it has been classified, so there is no feedback loop. Because human beings are aware of, and can respond to, the ways they are classified, human kinds — or, as Hacking later prefers to put it, interactive kinds — are what he calls 'moving targets.'

This is, at bottom, the same insight as the labeling theory developed in the sociology of deviance: once a person is publicly labeled mentally ill, that label changes how others treat them and how they see themselves, which then reinforces or alters the very behavior the label was meant to describe. Hacking's contribution is to generalize this mechanism beyond stigmatized diagnoses to classification more broadly.

Given all this, I think looping effects do show that psychiatric categories are not real in the way a fractured femur is real. A femur fracture is what it is independently of whether the patient has ever heard of fractures, whereas something like multiple personality disorder is partly made up of the very expectations and self-understandings that circulate about it; take away the looping and there is no stable disorder left to diagnose. So while the patient's distress is obviously real, the diagnostic category itself is invented rather than discovered, and its apparent stability across editions of the DSM is really just the temporary settling point of an ongoing negotiation between clinicians and the classified, not the tracking of some independent kind."
