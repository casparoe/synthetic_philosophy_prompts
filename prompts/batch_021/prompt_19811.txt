So instead of trusting secondary summaries I spent a weekend with the actual texts -- On Kingship (the treatise he wrote for the king of Cyprus), the relevant bit of his commentary on the Sentences, and the sedition question in the Summa -- and the real picture turns out to be way messier and more interesting than the meme. I've got the opening of a short piece drafted below and would love your comments before I go further, specifically: (1) am I getting the substance of his actual position right or have I flattened something a specialist would wince at, (2) is the "myth vs. reality" framing actually earned by the texts or am I overselling the gotcha, and (3) would this land for someone who's never read a word of scholastic philosophy, or does it just read like homework. Don't be polite about it if a paragraph is dead weight.

Here's the draft so far:

"Ask around and you'll hear, with real confidence, that Thomas Aquinas -- the great systematizer of natural law, hardly a firebrand -- thought killing tyrants was fair game. It's a nice line for an op-ed: even the most buttoned-up theologian of the Middle Ages allowed for political murder. The trouble is that if you actually go to the texts, this is close to backwards. In On Kingship, Aquinas spends a good stretch of Book I arguing against private citizens taking it on themselves to kill a bad ruler at all -- not because tyranny isn't awful, but because he thinks the cure is usually worse than the disease. His worry is basically institutional: if 'somebody decided the king was a tyrant' becomes license to assassinate, you haven't found a check on tyranny, you've just made every king's life depend on the goodwill of whoever is strongest and angriest, and good rulers get killed right alongside bad ones. So his preferred remedies are almost boringly procedural: if the community had a hand in installing the ruler, the community -- not any one person -- can act to restrain or remove him; if some higher authority set him up, appeal to that authority; and if there's genuinely no one left to appeal to, his actual advice is to pray and endure, on the theory that tyrants are a scourge God permits and can also lift.

That's the cautious Aquinas, the one people skip. But he's not fully consistent with himself: in his earlier commentary on the Sentences, he seems to leave more room for a private person to move against a ruler who never had a legitimate claim to power in the first place, as against someone who holds power legitimately but is exercising it badly. That usurper/tyrant-by-conduct distinction is real, and I suspect it's doing most of the work in making people think Aquinas is more permissive than he actually is -- the meme is roughly true of usurpers, and false of the ordinary case he spends the most time on: a legitimately installed ruler who turns nasty."

Does that distinction hold up the way I've stated it, or am I more confident about how sharp it is in his own texts than I should be? Also tempted to add a paragraph contrasting this with John of Salisbury, who I understand was a lot more full-throated about tyrannicide, but I don't want to overload a short piece -- worth it, or better left as a footnote?
