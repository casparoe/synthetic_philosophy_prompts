I'm taking a graduate seminar on philosophy of language this term, and a recent session on slurs left me more unsettled than when it began, since the assigned readings all seemed to be arguing past one another rather than meeting on common ground. Content-based theories try to locate the derogatory charge in what the word itself means. On Christopher Hom's "combinatorial externalism," a slur semantically encodes a complex, negatively loaded property whose content is fixed externally by actual discriminatory institutions and practices (roughly: that the target ought to be treated in certain deprecating ways because of purportedly having certain stereotypical traits, on account of belonging to the relevant group); because no one could actually satisfy such a property, Hom concludes that the slur has a null extension (there is nothing it is true of), so that any sentence predicating it of someone comes out false rather than meaningless. A rival proposal, due to Timothy Williamson and developed further by Daniel Whiting, keeps the semantics much leaner: the slur and its neutral counterpart (say, a nationality's ordinary demonym versus the ethnic slur for the same group) express exactly the same descriptive content and pick out exactly the same people, and the slur merely adds a conventional implicature that the speaker holds the group in contempt. Anderson and Lepore's "prohibitionism" goes further still, denying that the slur carries any special content, encoded or implicated, over and above its neutral counterpart, and locating the offense entirely in a social convention against tokening that particular word-form at all, which is also why merely mentioning a slur, not just using it, can give offense.

Two pieces of data make me unable to settle which camp is right, and the readings did not agree on how much weight to give each. First, projection: descriptive content ordinarily loses its force under negation, but "Jones is not a [slur]" still seems to leave the speaker tainted, and the same holds once the slur is embedded in a conditional's antecedent or under "believes that" — a pattern critics such as Elisabeth Camp have pressed specifically against Hom's account, since a null extension should make the negated sentence simply and innocuously true. Second, reclamation: a term like "queer" can be taken up by the very group it once demeaned and used with pride rather than contempt, with no change whatsoever to its spelling, pronunciation, or reference, which is awkward for any theory — implicature-based or prohibitionist alike — that ties the offense to a fixed lexical entry or an unconditional ban on a word-form, since something about who is speaking, and from what social position, is evidently doing further work that none of the three accounts as stated makes room for.

Write an essay that weighs Hom's semantic account, the Williamson/Whiting conventional-implicature account, and Anderson and Lepore's prohibitionism against one another specifically in terms of how well each handles projection and reclamation, and that defends a definite verdict about which account, or which explicit combination of them, gives the best overall treatment of slurs — rather than concluding that the three are simply incommensurable.
