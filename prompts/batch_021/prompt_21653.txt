I've been down a bit of a rabbit hole this week for a newsletter piece on the "embarrassing" private obsessions of famous scientists, and it turns out Newton's theology is way weirder and more interesting than the alchemy stuff everyone already brings up. The bit that's really stuck with me is the fight he had (through his mouthpiece Samuel Clarke) with Leibniz in 1715-16, where the two of them basically ended up accusing each other of believing in a diminished, low-rent version of God.

From what I can piece together: Newton thought the solar system slowly builds up irregularities over time (comets, planets tugging on each other, etc) and needs God to step in every so often and fix things, sort of like scheduled divine maintenance. Leibniz's jab back was that this makes God a lousy watchmaker who built a clock so badly it keeps needing to be pried open and repaired. But then Clarke's comeback was that its actually Leibniz's picture -- a perfect, self-sufficient machine God never has to touch again after creation -- that's the truly dangerous, quietly atheistic one. There's also this side-detail I stumbled on where Newton muses that space itself might be something like God's "sensorium," which apparently made Leibniz even more annoyed. Can you help me actually get the philosophical crux of this fight, past the "haha Newton believed weird stuff" level? I think it hinges on Leibniz's principle of sufficient reason and some deeper disagreement about how hands-on God's providence has to be, but I'd love a proper walkthrough of why each side genuinely thought the other's God was the lesser one.
