I'm working on a novel, and there's one chapter I keep putting off because I want to get the philosophy right rather than reach for the obvious joke. The setup: through a conceit I won't bore you with, René Descartes finds himself in a present-day cognitive science lab, and a bemused postdoc sits him down at a terminal to converse with a large language model before explaining what it is. I want his reaction, and the argument he makes afterward to the assembled researchers, to be a faithful extrapolation of his actual philosophy rather than the cartoon "animals are clockwork" version most people remember from a survey course.

Here is what I want to get right. In the Discourse on Method, Descartes gives two specific, practical tests for telling a real thinking thing from a mere automaton: first, that it could never use language flexibly enough to reply appropriately to the sense of whatever is said to it on any subject, the way even a dull human can, though it might be built to emit fixed responses to fixed triggers; second, that even where it matched or exceeded human performance on some tasks, it would betray itself by failing on others, since reason is a single, universal tool while mechanical organs are each fit for only one particular purpose. He uses exactly this pair of tests to justify denying minds to animals — his own examples are parrots and magpies that can be taught to utter words without, he says, showing any sign of thinking about what they say — while leaving it genuinely ambiguous, and disputed among his interpreters ever since, whether he thought animals feel nothing whatsoever or merely that they never think about what they feel.

So: a modern LLM seems, on its face, to satisfy his first test far better than any seventeenth-century animal could, holding forth intelligibly on almost any topic put to it. Would Descartes take this as forcing his hand toward granting it some kind of mind, on his own stated criteria? Or does his deeper commitment to the immateriality of thought — the view, argued independently in the Meditations, that mind is an indivisible substance essentially incapable of being produced by any arrangement of extended matter, however cleverly organized — give him principled grounds to insist the test has really been failed after all, perhaps by redescribing fluent conversation as a very elaborate version of the same "disposition of organs" he attributed to the magpie? And if he takes that second route, would the same move threaten to undercut his own confidence that behavior could ever reveal a mind at all, including the minds of other people? I'd like a careful reconstruction of how he would actually argue this out, staying as close as possible to the mechanics of his own reasoning rather than to modern intuitions about artificial intelligence.
