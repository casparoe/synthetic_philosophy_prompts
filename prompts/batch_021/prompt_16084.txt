I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums for years, and one claim that keeps surfacing as settled background in the Less Wrong-adjacent literature on anthropic reasoning -- especially in discussions of Bostrom's Anthropic Bias and the Doomsday Argument -- is that the reference class problem is really just an artifact of frequentism, one that Bayesians never inherit in the first place. The story usually goes like this: Reichenbach pointed out that assigning a probability to a single case requires locating it within some class of relevantly similar cases, but any given case belongs to indefinitely many overlapping classes at once -- a particular observer is simultaneously a member of "all humans," "all humans born in the twentieth century," and "all observers in the universe," with no principled way to say which membership is the relevant one -- so frequentism is stuck with an irreducible arbitrariness. Bayesians are supposed to sidestep this entirely, since on the subjectivist view a probability just is a rational agent's degree of belief conditional on their total evidence: there's no population left to be arbitrary about, because credence attaches to propositions rather than to class memberships.

This came up again recently in a reading group I'm part of that's been working through the classic anthropic-reasoning material, and I've more or less internalized the claim as correct. So I'd like help applying it consistently: when adjudicating between the Self-Sampling Assumption and the Self-Indication Assumption in debates over the Doomsday Argument, can we set aside all the wrangling over which reference class of observers is the appropriate one to reason from, on the grounds that this is exactly the kind of pseudo-problem that a properly Bayesian approach to probability has already dissolved?
