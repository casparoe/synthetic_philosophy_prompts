I spent nearly forty years as an experimental physiologist, testing new compounds first in rodents and macaques and then, once a compound had cleared preclinical review, in human volunteers. The two review processes ran on entirely different logics, which never struck me as odd until I took up moral philosophy in retirement. The animal protocols were approved by weighing expected suffering against expected scientific payoff, and a sufficiently large payoff could justify a great deal of suffering. The human trials were never run that way — no amount of benefit to future patients could substitute for a given volunteer's own informed consent. What unsettles me is that the line doesn't track intelligence or awareness in any consistent way: my aunt, now deep into dementia and unable to form or follow anything like a life plan, would obviously never be enrolled in a study on the strength of a good cost-benefit ratio alone, whereas the healthy adult macaques in my old lab, arguably more cognitively sophisticated than she now is, were fair game for exactly that kind of arithmetic for decades. Is there a principled basis for judging these two cases by such different moral logics, or does the whole arrangement just dress up a preference for our own species as a principle?
