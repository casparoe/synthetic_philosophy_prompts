I'm a retired research chemist who's gotten pretty hooked on moral philosophy since I stopped working full-time a couple years back - mostly self-taught, plus a little book club of retirees that meets every other week to argue about whatever we've been reading. We just worked through Susan Wolf's "Moral Saints," which got us onto the topic of supererogation (acts that are good to do but not required). I went and read the Stanford Encyclopedia entry, which walks through the standard Urmson-and-onward line on it, but one of our members - a retired Presbyterian minister, so hes actually got real background here unlike the rest of us - mentioned that "supererogation" started out as a term in Catholic moral theology, tangled up with indulgences and the "treasury of merit," and that the Reformers wanted no part of it. That sent me down a rabbit hole, and I dug up Article XIV of the Thirty-Nine Articles (the 1571 Anglican confession), titled "Of Works of Supererogation." As best I can transcribe it:

"Voluntary Works besides, over and above, God's Commandments, which they call Works of Supererogation, cannot be taught without arrogancy and impiety: for by them men do declare, that they do not only render unto God as much as they are bound to do, but that they do more for his sake, than of bounden duty is required: whereas Christ saith plainly, When ye have done all that is commanded you, say, We be unprofitable servants."

I can follow the broad gist of it - it's clearly a shot at the theology behind indulgences - but the actual argument packed into that one sentence is slipperier than I expected. Why exactly does claiming to have done "more" than duty requires count as arrogant and impious here? Is the thought that our duty (to God, presumably) is somehow unbounded or maximal, so there's never any real surplus to take credit for, or is the objection actually about motive, or about who's even fit to judge sufficiency? And how is that "unprofitable servants" line (Luke, I'm guessing) supposed to clinch the point? What I'd find most useful, though, is whether this 16th-century theological arguement has any real bite against the secular notion of supererogation that Urmson, Wolf, and that whole literature work with today, or whether it's trading entirely on premises - grace, merit before God, salvation - that just don't carry over into a purely ethical context. Would appreciate a careful walk-through, since I've got no background in theology or in reading old texts like this closely, and I suspect I'm missing something that would be obvious to anyone trained in it.
