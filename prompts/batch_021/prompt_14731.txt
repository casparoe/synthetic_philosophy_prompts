I'm a software engineer, and a couple years back I was the technical co-founder of a startup that ended up not making it. Something about the whole experience has been rattling around in my head ever since a few of us got drinks after a friend's demo day last month, and I can't quite shake it. When we were raising our seed round, our lead investor did this absurdly thorough background check on me specifically before wiring the money — called old managers, a cofounder from a company I'd left years earlier, even people from my grad program — basically trying to predict whether I was the type who grinds through a brutal slump or quietly bails the second something better shows up. They decided I was a "grinder" and funded us.

About ten months in, we hit a genuinely brutal slump, and out of nowhere I got an amazing offer to join a bigger company, for real money, at basically zero reputational cost, since everyone just assumes startups die for a hundred boring reasons and nobody would've blinked twice. Here's the version of the question I keep turning over, stripped of the confounding variables the way you'd isolate a bug in a flaky test: say its truly one-shot — I'll never found another company or deal with that investor or their network again, so "keep your word for the sake of future deals" can't be the reason to stay. And say the money is already sitting in the company's account regardless of what I decide next; that part is done, full stop, unchangeable by anything I do now. Given all that, is there any actual reason — not a legal one, not a reputational one, an honest one — to grind it out instead of taking the exit?

A friend of mine said, pretty confidently, no: once the money's in the account, what you do next is just a fresh decision judged on its own merits from here on out, and feeling "bound" by the deal that got you funded in the first place is basically sunk-cost thinking wearing a loyalty costume. Something about that answer feels off to me, but I honestly can't articulate why, and I have a hunch this is one of those "classic named thought experiment" situations that I just don't recognize because I never took a philosophy class. So who's actually right here — is there a real, non-instrumental reason to stay, or is my friend basically correct and I'm just being sentimental about it?
