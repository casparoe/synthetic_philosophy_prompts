I am taking a philosophy of biology seminar this term, and after our unit on theories of function I want to pressure-test the disagreement between etiological (selected-effects) and causal-role (Cummins-style) accounts on a case that did not come up in our readings. In many herbivorous insects, cytochrome P450 detoxification enzymes evolved largely to break down plant secondary metabolites, yet the same enzymes now also metabolize synthetic pesticides that did not exist anywhere in the insects' evolutionary history, which is one reason metabolic resistance to a brand-new pesticide can spread through a population so quickly. Does the function of these enzymes include detoxifying the pesticide, or not?

Please write a short dialogue between a philosopher defending the selected-effects view (in the tradition of Wright, Neander, and Millikan) and one defending the causal-role view, working through this case together. Press each side on its characteristic weak point: the selected-effects theorist should have to say exactly how abstractly the "selected effect" may be redescribed before the view stops ruling anything out, while the causal-role theorist should have to explain what, if anything, stops every effect the enzyme has from counting as one of its functions once function is relativized to whichever capacity is under discussion. The two should genuinely engage with each other's specific objections rather than restate their own position, and neither should come out the clear winner.
