A friend of mine who's finishing a philosophy PhD recommended Rorty's Philosophy and the Mirror of Nature after I mentioned some old debates from my own field, and I think I've spotted a parallel that she seemed unconvinced by when I brought it up over dinner. Rorty's whole case against representationalism — the idea that the mind or language is supposed to "mirror" an independent reality, and that a belief counts as true because it accurately represents the world — sounds to me like a philosophical version of the argument Rodney Brooks and the behavior-based robotics researchers made against classical AI back in the 1980s and early 1990s: that you don't need an internal model representing the environment in order to act successfully within it, and that building cognition around internal representations was a mistaken picture rather than a genuine discovery about how intelligence works. Brooks' subsumption architecture famously threw out the sense-model-plan-act pipeline in favor of tight feedback loops between sensors and actuators with no world-model doing any work in between, which reads to me like the same anti-representationalist move Rorty makes about human knowledge generally, just worked out for robots instead of Cartesian egos.

Given how closely these two attacks on "representation" seem to track each other, am I wrong to treat Rorty's antirepresentationalism as basically a philosophical generalization of the case against internal representations in AI and robotics? I'd like to understand whether these two arguments are really doing the same work under different vocabularies, or whether I'm being seduced by a surface-level resemblance that hides the fact that Rorty and the roboticists are actually targeting quite different things.
