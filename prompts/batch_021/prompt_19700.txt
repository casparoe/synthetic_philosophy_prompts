So I'm in a decision theory reading group this semester (I'm a phil PhD student working mostly on formal epistemology) and the last two sessions got completely stuck on the money-pump argument for completeness -- not the boring transitivity version everyone learns in decision theory 101, but the version that's supposed to show that even just having a *gap* between two options (not preferring A to B, not preferring B to A, and not being indifferent between them either) is enough to get you money-pumped over a sequence of trades. One person in the group kept insisting the whole argument falls apart once you give the agent foresight -- i.e. if the agent can look ahead and just refuse whichever trade it can see is part of a loosing sequence (something like resolute or sophisticated choice, in McClennen/Rabinowicz's sense), it dodges the pump while keeping its preferences genuinely incomplete. Someone else (I think channeling Gustafsson here, though I might be misremembering who's move this is) shot back that this "just look ahead and refuse" trick is either question-begging or itself imposes a rationality cost on the agent, so the pump still does real philosophical work. We never resolved it and it's been bugging me since.

Can you write me an essay that actually settles this? Lay out the money-pump argument for completeness in its strongest available form, then lay out the best foresight/resoluteness-based escape from it, weigh them against each other, and end with a real verdict: does practical rationality require complete preferences, full stop, or can an agent be fully rational while having genuine preferential gaps between some options, provided it chooses the right way over time? A conditional verdict like "only if choice is myopic" is fine, but I don't want the "reasonable people disagree" non-answer -- pick a side and defend it. If it's useful for your argument, feel free to gesture at what's at stake for the broader "coherence arguments" literature (i.e. whether money-pump reasoning forces any sufficiently advanced agent toward expected-utility-maximizing, goal-directed behavior), but keep the core of the essay on the completeness case itself, since that's the part we couldn't crack.
