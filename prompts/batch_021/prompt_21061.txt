Strawson defends in "Freedom and Resentment"?"

And here's the student's answer, pretty much verbatim: "Strawson's Basic Argument tries to show that no one is ever truly or ultimately morally responsible for what they do, in the deep sense that would make sense of things like eternal reward or punishment. The argument runs: (1) you do what you do because of the way you are, i.e. your character, values and motives; (2) to be truly responsible for what you do you'd have to be truly responsible for being the way you are, at least in the relevant respects; (3) but you can't be truly responsible for being the way you are, since either you were just born that way (luck), or you made yourself that way through earlier choices, and those choices flowed from an earlier self you also didn't choose, so you get a regress that can never get started; (4) therefore you're never truly responsible for the way you are, and so, from (2), never truly responsible for what you do. This is basically a watertight argument for hard determinism, and Strawson calls it 'basic' because its the most fundamental route to that conclusion, more fundamental than arguments that start from physics. If indeterminism turned out to be true instead, that would actually block step (3) somewhat, since then not everything about you would trace back to factors outside your control. This actually lines up pretty well with his father P.F. Strawson's compatibilism in "Freedom and Resentment" — P.F. Strawson also thought moral responsibility rests on our natural reactive attitudes like resentment and gratitude rather than on libertarian free will, so Galen's argument can basically be read as putting that same naturalistic picture on more rigorous, argument-form footing."
