I write literary fiction, and for almost ten years now I've been tangled up in this small writers' residency out in the woods that I did a stay at right before my first book came out -- I go back most summers to teach a workshop, and I'm on the alumni list where all the recent drama has been unfolding. The place was started back in the 70s by a handful of poets who wrote this lovely, earnest little founding letter saying admission would be "decided on the work alone, blind to who the writer knows or where they studied." Except for basically the whole life of the residency, the actual practice has been that your application doesn't get read seriously unless a past fellow vouches for you -- nothing in the founding letter requires that, it just crept in decades ago as a way to sort through too many submissions, and now it means the same twenty MFA programs keep sending each other back in every summer. It all blew up publicly this spring when a rejected applicant posted her materials next to the two-line rejection and a bunch of former fellows piled on in the comments.

Now my whole cohort has split into two camps and everyone keeps asking where I stand. One camp says the place is rotten from the studs out -- somebody dug up an old newsletter where one of the founders admits she always pictured it as "a gathering of kindred spirits," and they're treating that as proof the exclusivity was the point from day one, so the only honest move is to walk away and build something new with actual enforceable rules. The other camp, where I keep landing, says the founding letter is the real deal everybody actually agreed to, that one founder's private daydream isn't binding on anyone, and that torching twenty years of grants, a building, and an endowment over a custom that was never even written down anywhere is throwing away the one lever we actually have for fixing the place. Being someone who spends all day thinking about what a text licenses versus what its author merely wanted, I can't tell anymore if thats a genuine argument or just professional bias talking -- my friend who quit academia over almost this exact dynamic says its obviously the second thing. So who's actually got the stronger case, the burn-it-down camp or the hold-it-to-its-word camp? Or is there some third option neither of us is seeing?
