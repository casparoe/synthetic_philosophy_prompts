Prompt they were given: "In On Immigration and Refugees, Dummett argues against the common assumption that a sovereign state has an unlimited right to exclude whomever it wishes. Reconstruct his argument for this claim, explain how and why he treats the case of refugees differently from the case of economic migrants, and say whether you think his overall position amounts, in effect, to a defense of open borders."

Their answer: "Dummett's book is an attempt to think through immigration policy without either indulging nationalist assumptions or collapsing into naive moralism. He starts from the observation that public debate usually just takes for granted that a state has a more or less unlimited right to decide who may cross its borders, the way a homeowner decides who to let into their house. Dummett thinks this analogy is doing a lot of unearned work: a state isn't a private household, and the fact that a territory falls under a government's jurisdiction doesn't by itself generate a moral permission to exclude any person, for any reason. He backs this up historically by walking through the ugly recent history of British nationality and immigration law -- the 1948 British Nationality Act, the 1962 and 1968 Commonwealth Immigrants Acts, and the 1971 Act's 'patriality' rule -- to show how supposedly neutral, general rules were in practice built to keep non-white Commonwealth citizens out while still admitting white ones. So one strand of the argument is genealogical: a lot of the actual exercise of the 'right to exclude' has been racist in intent or effect, which should make us suspicious of the right in the abstract.

The more purely philosophical part of the argument is the veil-of-ignorance move: Dummett asks us to imagine choosing the rules for international movement without knowing in advance which country we'll be born into. Since hardly anyone would choose to be born into a poor country, and since being born into a rich one is just luck, we can't consistently endorse a global system that lets the lucky keep the unlucky out. This is what really does the work of getting him to the conclusion that the 'right to exclude' has no principled, unlimited form.

Where Dummett does draw a real line is between refugees and economic migrants. He thinks the obligation to admit refugees -- people fleeing persecution or violence -- is very strong, close to absolute, because turning someone away there makes the state complicit in whatever then happens to them. Economic migrants are murkier for him, and he spends real time on problems like 'brain drain,' where rich countries recruiting doctors and engineers out of poor ones does genuine damage to the countries they leave.

Given all that, I'd say Dummett's position, whatever he calls it, functions as a defense of open borders in all but name. Once he's granted that the traditional unlimited right to exclude can't be justified, he never finds a stable, non-arbitrary place to stop the argument -- and a philosopher who thinks the right to exclude has no principled limit and needs fresh moral justification every time is, for practical purposes, an open-borders theorist who just hasn't said so outright."
