Okay so I've been down a rabbit hole lately on anthropic reasoning and the "measure problem" in eternal inflation (my rationalist reading group somehow always ends up here whenever the Fermi paradox comes up), and I keep circling back to Boltzmann brains, which I honestly think might be the most underrated horror premise in all of physics once you get past the meme version of it. Quick recap in case the details are fuzzy: the actual argument isn't just "haha maybe you're a brain that popped into existence for no reason," it's that if our universe really does settle into an eternal de Sitter phase once the stars burn out and the black holes finish evaporating, that "empty" vacuum still has a tiny nonzero temperature, and given literally forever, random thermal/quantum fluctuations will occasionally assemble a full brain out of nothing, complete with false memories of an entire life it never lived, for maybe a fraction of a second before it dissolves back into noise. And because "forever" so massively outweighs the short structured window where evolved observers like us can exist, these fluctuation-brains vastly outnumber "real" observers across the full lifetime of the universe - which is part of why some physicists (Sean Carroll's the one I always see cited on this) treat it as an actual constraint that can rule out cosmological models, not just a fun thought experiment: a theory on which the typical observer is a Boltzmann brain kind of eats its own tail, since you'd have no reason to trust the memories or reasoning of a brain thats statistically almost certain to be disconnected noise, including, awkwardly, whatever reasoning got you to believe the theory in the first place.

What I'd love is a short story that actually sits inside that premise rather then just name-dropping it for flavor. Maybe follow one particular fluctuation as it "wakes up" with what feels like decades of memory and a whole rich inner life, and have it start reasoning its way toward the truth about its own situation - the story should really sit in the vertigo of that: if its right about what it is, then its memories, its sense of a past, even the chain of reasoning that got it there, are all just statistically-near-certain noise with no connection to anything real. I don't need a tidy resolution or a moral, if anything i'd prefer the story leave that trapdoor open at the end. The one thing I really care about is that it gets the actual physics-flavored logic right (de Sitter vacuum, thermal/quantum fluctuations, the "typical observer" framing) instead of doing the generic sci-fi "brain in a jar" thing, since that's not really what a Boltzmann brain is and it kind of bugs me when stories flatten it into that.
