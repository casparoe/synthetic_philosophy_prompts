Quick one for my animal ethics seminar — I want to push past the "Kant thought animals don't matter" cliché. You know the actual argument from the Lectures on Ethics and the Metaphysics of Morals: we have no direct duty to animals since they aren't rational agents, but cruelty to them is still wrong, because it dulls the sympathetic feeling in us that we need for decent moral relations with other humans (his go-to example is the guy who shoots his own dog once its too old to be useful -- he hasn't wronged the dog, but he's revealed and reinforced a hardness of character that's bad news for how he'll treat people). The whole argument hangs on what a particular act of cruelty does to the character of the particular person doing it.

Here's the thing: factory farming doesn't work like that at all. Nobody buying a pack of chicken thighs personally performs or even witnesses an act of cruelty -- its automated and outsourced across a huge supply chain, done in pieces by workers running repetitive lines, not by anyone acting out of a cruel disposition toward a particular animal. So given that Kant's whole case against cruelty to animals is anchored in what individual acts do to an individual's character, does he actually have the resources to condemn factory farming as a system? Or would he have to bite the bullet that the industrialized, depersonalized version is in some sense LESS of a problem, on his own terms, than one farmer beating his own dog -- precisely because it doesn't corrupt anybody's character the way his argument needs it to? Don't just hand me the indirect-duty summary, actually work through whether the mechanism scales up to something this impersonal.
