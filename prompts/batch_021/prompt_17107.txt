So this happened at a dinner thing a couple weekends ago and it's been stuck in my head since, probably because it's exactly the kind of thing I'd want to write about for work (I cover science-and-ideas stuff for a living). Two friends of mine, Marcus and Deb, got into it over a local news story about two DUI sentencings out of the same courthouse, same week. Both drivers were first offenders with the same blood alcohol reading, and by the cops' own reports drove with basically identical recklessness -- same swerving, same speed. The only real difference was that one of them had a pedestrian in his path and killed her, and the other hit a parked car and hurt nobody. Guy #1 (no injuries) got probation and a fine. Guy #2 (the fatality) got twelve years.

(1) Marcus's opening move was that the twelve-year gap is unjust, full stop: the two men made the exact same choice and behaved with the exact same recklessness, so punishing one so much harder than the other just punishes him for pure luck -- whether a pedestrian happened to step off the curb at that exact second -- not for anything about him. (2) Deb's reply was that this has it backwards: the law and ordinary morality have always cared about outcomes, that's the entire reason attempted murder and murder are separate crimes, and scaling punishment to actual harm caused is retributive justice doing its job, not a bug in it. (3) Marcus came back that if outcome-tracking were really the principle, you'd have to keep scaling punishment for harms further downstream too (what if guy #1's parked-car victim has a heart attack from the shock two years later -- does he get re-sentenced then?), which shows what should actually set the punishment is the deterrent value of the reckless choice itself, fixed at the moment its made, not whatever happens after. (4) Deb's counter was that the law handles that regress just fine with foreseeability and proximate-cause rules, and, more importantly, that if you actually made Marcus's view consistent you'd have to punish every drunk driver who gets home safe almost as harshly as guy #2, since ex ante their choice was just as likely to kill someone -- and she doesn't think he actually wants to hand a decade in prison to the thousands of people who drive drunk and hit nothing.

Nobody at the table would referee it, and I've been chewing on it since. Could you go through moves (1)-(4) and give a verdict on each -- who's got the better of that particular exchange, not just an overall winner -- plus flag anything both of them are missing that someone who's actually read the retributivism / consequentialism / moral-luck literature would bring up? End with one line giving your overall lean, if you have one.
