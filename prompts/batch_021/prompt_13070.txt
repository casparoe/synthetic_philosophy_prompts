I'm taking a seminar this term on self-knowledge and assertion, and last week we spent a long session on Moore's paradox: the oddity of a sentence like "It's raining, but I don't believe that it's raining," which is perfectly consistent -- it could easily be true of someone else, or of me at some other time -- and yet seems impossible to assert, or even to privately think, about oneself right now. What struck me is that the seminar basically split into two camps about why it's absurd, and neither side seemed to be obviously winning.

One camp treats the absurdity as fundamentally a fact about assertion as a speech act: on a Gricean sincerity condition, or on Williamson's knowledge norm, to assert P is to represent yourself as believing, or even knowing, P, so asserting "P, and I don't believe P" commits you to representing yourself as believing P while simultaneously telling your audience you don't -- a pragmatic self-defeat located in the norms governing the act, not a contradiction in the content asserted. The other camp thinks this gets the order of explanation backwards. Following Gareth Evans's point that we settle "do I believe P?" by attending to the same evidence we'd use to settle "is P true?" (the transparency of belief), they argue the absurdity survives even without any assertion at all: quietly judging, or writing in a diary no one will ever read, "it's raining, and I don't believe it," seems just as irrational as saying it aloud. On their view -- compare Shoemaker's argument that a rational believer cannot be "self-blind" about her own mental states -- the puzzle is really about the first-person epistemology of belief, and treating it as an artifact of communicative norms gets things backwards.

I'm planning to write my term paper on this debate, so I'd like to see both positions pushed as hard as possible before I commit to one. Could you write a dialogue between a proponent of the assertion/speech-act view and a proponent of the transparency/self-knowledge view in which they present their accounts and then genuinely engage each other's strongest objections, rather than talking past one another? I'd like each side to take a clear stand on: (a) the unasserted diary case above, and whether it really is a counterexample to the assertion-based story; (b) why the paradox disappears under a shift of tense or grammatical person -- "it was raining and I didn't believe it" and "she believes it's raining but it isn't" are both fine -- which either view needs to explain; and (c) whether the two grammatical forms of the paradox, "P, but I don't believe that P" (the omissive version) and "P, but I believe that not-P" (the commissive version), receive a unified treatment on their account. Neither philosopher should simply fold; have the dialogue end with each one stating, honestly, what they'd need to grant the other in order to change their mind.
