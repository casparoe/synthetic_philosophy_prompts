I teach ethics for a living and I still couldn't referee this one, so I need an outside read. My cousin Dave promised his dying dad he'd keep the family hardware store open 'no matter what.' Its ten years later, the store looses money every year, and Dave's daughter needs that money for college. At Thanksgiving my aunt Carol said Dave's off the hook: a promise is owed to a person, that person is gone, and you can't wrong someone who no longer exists. My uncle Ray said that can't be right -- it would mean you could break a promise to a friend the second he's out of earshot forever, or to someone in a permanent coma who'll never know either way, and those feel like the cases where breaking it is worst, not most excusable. Carol said a coma patient still has a future the broken promise could damage; a dead man has no future and no interests left anywhere, so it's not the same. Ray said we don't keep deathbed promises because we think the dead are watching -- we'd keep one even knowing for certain we'd never be caught -- so whatever grounds the obligation, it isn't just 'someone might find out and be hurt.' Carol said fine, but then it can't be unconditional either: if keeping the promise meant Dave's daughter went without health insurance, even Ray would say break it, so it obviously can be outweighed. Ray agreed, but said losing some money every year isn't remotely the same magnitude as a kid losing health insurance, so admitting there's a limit doesn't mean the promise carries zero weight here. Carol's last line was: outweighed by what, exactly? With nothing real left on dad's side of the scale, 'outweighed' just means Dave's preferences win, which was her point from the start. Then we had to go carve the turkey. Can you go through this move by move, say who's got the better argument at each stage rather than just declaring an overall winner, and flag anything important both of them are missing?
