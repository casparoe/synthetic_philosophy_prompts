Over dinner last night, a friend and I fell into a long argument about Avicenna's flying man: the thought experiment in which a person is created whole and adult in a single instant, suspended in a void, sight and hearing withheld, limbs held apart so that no part of the body ever touches another. Avicenna holds that such a person would still affirm his own existence — "I am" — while affirming nothing at all about having a body. My friend took this as proof that the self is a substance genuinely distinct from the body; I argued it shows only the weaker, epistemic point that self-awareness doesn't depend on bodily awareness, and that reading more into it repeats the leap from conceivability to real possibility we would later fault in Descartes. Neither of us gave ground. Write a short story, set in whatever period or world you choose, that stages a version of this scenario — a mind first becoming aware of itself with no body available to its senses — and that settles the dispute one way or the other through what unfolds in the story itself, rather than through a character simply stating a conclusion.
