Okay so in my econ class we're doing a behavioral economics unit and my teacher put the Allais paradox up on the board like it was some huge deal. The setup was: Option A is a guaranteed $1 million, and Option B is an 89% chance of $1 million, a 10% chance of $5 million, and a 1% chance of getting nothing — and apparently most people pick A. Then for a second choice, Option C is an 11% chance of $1 million (and 89% nothing) versus Option D, a 10% chance of $5 million (and 90% nothing), and most people flip and pick D instead. She said this breaks something called the "independence axiom" in expected utility theory and that it's been messing with economists for like 70 years.

But ngl I don't really get why it's such a big deal. Isn't this just people being risk averse, like the same reason people buy insurance, or why a guaranteed $1,000 feels better than a 50/50 shot at $2,100 even though that has higher expected value? We already learned that utility curves for money bend (diminishing marginal utility and all that) specifically to account for stuff like that, so why can't you just say peoples' utility for money isn't a straight line and boom, that explains why they'd grab the safe million instead of the gamble? So is she actually right that this is mathematically impossible to patch with normal risk-aversion, and if so, why exactly?
