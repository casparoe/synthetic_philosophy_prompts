At a dinner party recently, two friends of mine -- both of whom studied philosophy and still enjoy relitigating old puzzles -- spent the better part of an hour arguing about the Sleeping Beauty problem, and I haven't been able to settle it in my own head since. The setup: Beauty is put to sleep on Sunday, and a fair coin is tossed. On heads, she is woken once, on Monday, and the experiment ends. On tails, she is woken on Monday, given a drug that erases her memory of that waking, and woken again on Tuesday. Whenever she wakes, she cannot tell which day it is or whether she has already woken once before. The question on the table: what should her credence be, upon waking, that the coin landed heads?

(1) One of them argued for 1/3: if the experiment were repeated a thousand times, the roughly 500 heads-runs would produce about 500 wakings while the roughly 500 tails-runs would produce about 1,000 wakings, so two-thirds of all her wakings overall would follow a tails-flip; matching her credence to that long-run frequency of being right just is what it means to be well calibrated.

(2) The other argued for 1/2: the coin is fair, and waking up gives Beauty no new information bearing on how it landed, since she knew with certainty on Sunday night that she would wake at least once no matter the outcome. A guaranteed event, she said, can't rationally move your credence about something it doesn't discriminate between.

(3) The first replied that Beauty does learn something upon waking: not merely that some waking is occurring somewhere, but that this particular waking is happening to her, now. We treat that kind of self-locating information as relevant to credence all the time in other cases, so refusing to count it here looks like special pleading.

(4) The second answered that self-location can matter in general, sure, but not like this: the Monday-tails and Tuesday-tails wakings aren't two independent chances for the coin to have come up tails, they're one already-settled toss being sampled twice. Letting multiple, subjectively identical observations of the same event inflate your credence in that event just lets the number of times you happen to be looking affect a fact that a single coin flip already fixed.

Neither budged, and neither seemed to be bluffing -- both moves felt like they were tracking something real. Could you go through moves (1) through (4) in order and say what each one actually establishes and where it falls short, rather than just declaring an overall winner? I'm especially interested in what the thirder needs to say to survive move (4), and what the halfer needs to say to survive move (3). End with your own overall verdict on which position is left in better shape, and note anything both of them are overlooking.
