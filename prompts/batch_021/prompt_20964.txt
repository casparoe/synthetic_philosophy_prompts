So I'm in a seminar this semester on ancient moral psychology and we just spent like three weeks on Nicomachean Ethics book VII, and now I keep noticing how casually people equate Aristotle's akrasia with hyperbolic discounting whenever it comes up in the behavioral econ course I TA for on the side. Everyone just goes "oh yeah, Aristotle basically discovered present bias" and moves on, but the more I sit with the actual practical-syllogism stuff -- the akratic guy who technically "has" the premise that nothing sweet should be eaten in his condition (or however exactly the example goes, im going off memory here) but somehow doesn't apply it in the moment, plus the whole impetuous (propetes) vs weak (asthenes) distinction he draws later in the book -- the less sure I am it maps cleanly onto something like "$10 today vs $20 in a month, except when today actually arrives you take the $10 anyway," which is the kind of preference reversal Ainslie and the discounting people study.

Here's what's actually bugging me: Aristotle is pretty explicit that unqualified incontinence is about bodily appetite specifically (food, drink, sex), and involves this weird "knowing but not exercising the knowledge" state, comparable to someone asleep or drunk -- theres an actual perceptual/appetitive hijack happening in real time. But most hyperbolic discounting experiments are just cold intertemporal money choices, no appetite anywhere in sight, and the reversal is stable and predictable rather than a one-off being swept away by feeling. So: if you handed Aristotle the whole discounting literature -- the curve shape, the preference reversals, Ainslie's picoeconomics picture of the mind as a bunch of competing temporary selves bargaining with each other -- do you think he'd say "yes, this is just my akrasia in graph form," and if so which bucket would he put it in, unqualified or the qualified/metaphorical kind he mentions for honor and gain? Or would he actually say it's a different phenomenon than the one he's diagnosing in book VII -- more like a flat calculation error, or not incontinence in his sense at all? I'd love an answer that really wrestles with the text instead of doing the usual "wow, ancient philosopher predicts modern science" thing.
