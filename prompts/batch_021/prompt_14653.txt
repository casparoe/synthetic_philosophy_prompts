So I spent my career in atmospheric chemistry, not physics, but retirement has turned me into one of those people who reads pop-physics books about relativity and can't let it go. The thing I keep chewing on lately is the "Andromeda paradox" setup (I think that's originally from a Roger Penrose book, don't quote me) -- two people pass each other on the sidewalk, one walking toward Andromeda and one away from it, and because of the relativity of simultaneity their planes of "now" out at that galaxy are tilted just enough that they'd disagree, by something like a few days, on whether some hypothetical alien fleet has already launched or is still just a possibility. I gather philosophers call this the Rietdijk-Putnam argument for why relativity forces you into the block universe.

Can you write me a dialogue between the two people on the sidewalk once they stop and actually start arguing about it -- one of them a died-in-the-wool presentist, the other a died-in-the-wool eternalist. Don't let the presentist just fold. I have a vague memory there are a couple of real countermoves here, something about there being one "true" frame that's privileged even though no experiment could ever detect it, and something else about dropping simultaneity altogether and defining what's "already real" off each person's own past light cone instead. Whichever move the presentist reaches for, make the eternalist actually push back hard on it -- is it ad hoc, does it even save the thing presentism was supposed to give us, a single shared objective now -- instead of just declaring victory. I'd like them to go back and forth a good while and actually end up locating the real bone of contention, rather than just reciting relativity 101 at each other.
