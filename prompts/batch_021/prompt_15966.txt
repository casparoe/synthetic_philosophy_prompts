So this week in my formal epistemology seminar we finally got to Joyce's accuracy-dominance argument for probabilism -- the move where you show that any credence function violating the probability axioms is accuracy-dominated by some coherent one, so incoherence is epistemically irrational all on its own, no pragmatic Dutch-book stuff needed. Walking my students through it I had to admit the argument only works once you restrict to "legitimate" ways of scoring how close a credence is to the truth, and Joyce's original 1998 axioms for legitimacy got hammered by Maher a few years later for being kind of ad hoc, chosen to get probabilism out rather than independently motivated. The fix people reached for afterward, if I'm remembering the dialectic right, was to drop Joyce's bespoke axioms and just require the scoring rule be strictly proper -- so your own credence always looks, by its own lights, like the uniquely best thing to report -- and then run a convexity arguement: any incoherent credence has a unique nearest coherent neighbor under the geometry that rule induces, and that neighbor dominates it. Predd and some coauthors have a nice paper laying this out, 2009 I think.

Here's the bit that's been bugging me. There are plenty of strictly proper scoring rules on offer -- Brier, log, spherical, and more -- and while they all agree that incoherence gets dominated, they don't agree on much else. The log rule punishes an extreme 0 or 1 credence in some contingent claim infinitely hard if you happen to be wrong, Brier doesn't, and that split feeds right back into old fights about Cromwell's rule and whether extreme credences are ever permissible. So probabilism itself comes out scoring-rule-invariant, but a lot of the other normative mileage accuracy-first epistemologists want -- claims about extremity, about which updating rule is uniquely rational, that kind of thing -- doesn't seem to survive the switch between rules.

So here's what I want: an essay that actually weighs in on whether this residual pluralism among legitimate scoring rules is enough to make accuracy-first epistemology loose its claim to being a purely alethic, non-pragmatic foundation for Bayesianism, or whether "probabilism is invariant and that's the part that actually matters, the rest can stay pluralistic" is a perfectly respectable place to land. I don't want a survey of considerations on both sides that shrugs at the end -- pick a verdict and defend it against the most obvious comeback to your position.
