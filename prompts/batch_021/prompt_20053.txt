After forty years as a field ichthyologist, I still sit on a state panel that rules on the conservation status of freshwater fish populations. We are at an impasse over two populations of darters — small stream-dwelling fish — that differ sharply in the males' breeding colors and courtship displays, favor subtly different stretches of the same river, and almost never interbreed where their ranges meet, yet differ by well under one percent in mitochondrial DNA and produce, in laboratory crosses, offspring that remain fully fertile across several generations. A colleague on the panel insists this settles the matter: since fertile interbreeding is the textbook test of species status, the two populations must be one species, whatever their habits in the wild. Something about her argument nags at me, though I have struggled to say precisely why it seems wrong, given how thoroughly these fish behave, in nature, as though they were two entirely different kinds of animal. Since taking up philosophy in retirement, I suspect this disagreement has a shape that philosophers would recognize at once. Could you help me see that shape, and tell me whether my colleague's reasoning is really as decisive as she believes it to be?
