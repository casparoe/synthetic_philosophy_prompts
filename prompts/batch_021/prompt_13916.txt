I've been reading Berkeley's *The Querist* for a reading group on the history of economic thought, and it's made me realize how little the standard philosophy curriculum has to say about this side of him. Everyone learns "esse est percipi" and the master argument against material substance, but almost no one reads the sequence of queries in which Berkeley works out a theory of money for a cash-starved Ireland: that money is not itself wealth but something closer to a counter or ticket, a device for recording and transferring claims on the community's productive "industry," so that a well-managed national bank could expand paper credit far beyond the colony's actual stock of gold and silver without thereby destroying real wealth. This sits oddly against John Locke's position a generation earlier in the 1690s recoinage debates, where he opposed William Lowndes's proposal to devalue the coinage and insisted that a coin's denomination must track a fixed weight of silver, on pain of undermining the "intrinsic" value that contracts and property ultimately rest on — an argument that helped push England toward the costly Great Recoinage of 1696.

I'd like you to compare Berkeley's and Locke's theories of money in more depth than the usual one-line "nominalist versus metallist" gloss. In particular: (a) say something about how each man's view of money does or doesn't grow out of his broader metaphysics or epistemology, given that Berkeley denies mind-independent material substance altogether while Locke builds so much of his epistemology and political theory on a robust distinction between real and nominal essence; (b) explain what each view implies about debasement, inflation, and the stability of contracts, and how that played out in the concrete policy disputes each man was actually engaged in; and (c) assess how anachronistic it would be to treat Berkeley's "ticket" conception as a genuine anticipation of Chartalism or Modern Monetary Theory rather than a superficial resemblance. Finally, which of the two positions do you think holds up better once we factor in what we now know about monetary history and theory — please justify your answer rather than treating the two views as equally defensible.
