I don't have any background in philosophy, but for the last few months I've been falling down a rabbit hole about the philosophy of probability, of all things. It started because a friend and I got into an argument about what a weather forecaster actually means by "30% chance of rain," and one thing led to another until I ended up reading about something called the Principal Principle. The rough idea, as I understand it, is that if you know the true objective chance of some event, your personal degree of belief in it is rationally required to match that number exactly. That seemed almost trivially sensible to me until I read further and learned that the philosopher behind the principle, David Lewis, also held that the objective chance of anything at a given moment is ultimately fixed by the entire history of the world, including everything that hasn't happened yet. Which means that information arriving later about the future can turn out to "undermine" the very chance you were supposed to be deferring to in the first place, by revealing that there was never a settled fact of that kind for you to have been tracking at all. I gather Lewis was bothered enough by this wrinkle to give it an unflattering nickname, something like "the big bad bug," and I have not been able to stop turning it over, wondering what it would actually feel like from the inside, rather than as a diagram in an encyclopedia entry.

I'd like you to write me a short story that puts a character inside exactly that predicament. Picture someone whose life or work depends on a genuinely reliable source of objective chances — a scientist, a professional gambler, an actuary, or just someone who happens to own a strange and trustworthy instrument — and who has made a real personal discipline out of always trusting that number over their own hunches, the way you might train yourself to trust a compass over your sense of direction. Partway through the story, I'd like something to happen that plays the role of "evidence from the future": not simply proof that an earlier number was wrong, but a revelation that unsettles whether there was ever a fixed fact there to be right or wrong about in the first place. I don't want the story to resolve this neatly with a moral at the end; I'm hoping for something closer in mood to Ted Chiang's quieter stories, where the unease comes from watching a person's whole framework for trusting their own beliefs come loose, and the ending leaves the reader sitting with that vertigo rather than explaining it away.
