So this happened at our decision theory reading group a couple weeks ago (I'm a few years into a philosophy PhD, we get a mix of philosophy and econ people showing up, meet every other Thursday in this one drafty seminar room) and it turned into a proper argument that never actually got resolved, so I wanted an outside read on it. We were working through the St. Petersburg paradox -- the game where you flip a fair coin repeatedly, the pot doubles every time it comes up heads, and you get paid out the pot the moment it first comes up tails -- so the expected payout is literally infinite, yet obviously nobody would pay much to actually play it. Two of the regulars, Marcus and Ilana, went back and forth for a while on whether Bernoulli's classic fix for this (swap expected monetary value for expected utility, using something concave like log of wealth) actually solves the problem, and I've tried to reconstruct the exchange below as faithfully as I can:

(1) Marcus said the paradox basically dissolves once you make the Bernoulli move: plug in u(w) = log(w) or anything similarly concave, and the infinite sum of tiny-probability-huge-payout terms collapses to something finite, so there's a perfectly well-defined finite price a rational person should pay to play.
(2) Ilana said that only looks like a fix because we're being handed a tame example -- you can always cook up a "super" St. Petersburg game with a prize schedule that grows fast enough in utility terms to blow the expectation back up to infinity, no matter which unbounded function you picked, log or otherwise. So Bernoulli's move doesn't kill the paradox, it just relocates it one level up, and the only way to actually rule it out for good is to require utility to be bounded above, full stop.
(3) Marcus said fine, bound it then -- that's barely a concession, since its independently plausible that a life, or a bank account, can only contain so much welfare anyway. Why not just admit utility saturates somewhere and move on?
(4) Ilana said bounding it is not a small concession at all, because it commits you to there being some finite amount of stuff past which literally nothing -- not one more dollar, not one more anything -- could add any further value for you ever again, which seems to conflict pretty badly with the intuition that resources can always in principle be converted into at least a little more good somewhere (one more bed net, one more scholarship). So he's just swapped the St. Petersburg weirdness for a satiation weirdness, not actually gotten rid of a weirdness.
(5) Marcus said those two weirdnesses aren't remotely symmetric: once you allow any unbounded utility function, Ilana's own construction guarantees there's some game out there that would force you to hand over your entire net worth for a single coin flip, whereas bounded utility only ever gives you an awkward idealized limit that no real decision anyone actually makes gets remotely close to. So it's not a wash -- bounded utility is still clearly the better deal.

We ran out of time before Ilana could respond properly, so it's just stuck there. Could you go through moves (1) through (5) one at a time and say whether Marcus or Ilana (or both, or neither) has the better of it at each step, rather than just declaring an overall winner -- and then close with whatever you think the biggest thing is that both of them are missing, if there is one?
