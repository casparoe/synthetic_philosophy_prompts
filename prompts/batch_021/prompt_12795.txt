I'm finishing up a longread on the philosophical roots of "meritocracy talk" in Silicon Valley, and I keep circling back to Nozick's Wilt Chamberlain example because two different founders I interviewed brought it up, almost word for word, as the philosophical proof that redistributive taxation is unjust. Quick recap, mostly for my own sake: Wilt signs a deal where 25 cents from every ticket goes straight to him, a million fans happily pay it over a season, and he walks away with a fortune while everyone else's holdings barely budge. The way these guys explained it (and how I've now seen it explained in a few blog posts too), the whole point is that Wilt is such a rare, singular talent that he obviously deserves every one of those quarters, and Nozick basically proved that once someone deserves their earnings, no "fairness" pattern imposed after the fact can override that without committing a real injustice against him.

Here's what's bugging me though: if the argument really does turn on Wilt deserving his money, why does almost every philosopher I've since gone and read seem weirdly allergic to the word "desert" when they explain entitlement theory? One professor I emailed even said Nozick was pretty openly skeptical of desert-based arguments in general, which feels totally at odds with how this example gets deployed in basically every op-ed defending low taxes on athletes and founders. Am I wrong that "he deserves it" is secretly doing all the work in this thought experiment, or is ther some other mechanism making the Wilt Chamberlain case go through that these founders (and honestly half the internet) are just glossing over?
