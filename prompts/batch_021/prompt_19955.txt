I'm a PhD student in philosophy, and this term I've been leading a reading group on Sartre's Being and Nothingness in the Hazel Barnes translation. Every time we reach the chapter on bad faith, the same thing happens: everyone reaches for the waiter and the woman on her first date, recites the vocabulary of facticity and transcendence over them, and the discussion stops there instead of working through why those two illustrations are doing the philosophical work Sartre needs them to do. What I think gets lost is that bad faith is only available to us at all because human reality has this peculiar double property — it is always both a sheer factical given (body, past, situation) and a transcendence, a radical freedom to be otherwise — and that bad faith consists precisely in exploiting the slippage between the two, treating whichever register is convenient as if it exhausted the person, so as to offload responsibility for some possibility one would rather not face. It's also, on Sartre's account, inherently unstable rather than a settled lie: he calls it a "metastable" structure, since the very consciousness fleeing the truth has to have already grasped it, however dimly, for there to be anything to flee.

I'd like to hand the group a piece of short fiction that forces them to reconstruct this mechanism from scratch rather than pattern-matching to Sartre's own set pieces, and writing it well seems to require actually tracking the logic rather than just gesturing at "self-deception" in general. Could you write a short story, something in the 800-1200 word range, set in an entirely contemporary, non-café, non-first-date situation, in which a character performs this same double move at least once: leaning on their sheer facticity — a body, a diagnosis, a habit, a role, a past decision — specifically in order to disown a live possibility of free self-determination, or, conversely, leaning on an inflated sense of their own transcendence specifically to deny some plain fact about their situation? I'd like the story to end right at the point where the maneuver almost becomes visible to the character themselves, a flicker of the anguish it exists to suppress, without letting it resolve into either clean self-knowledge or a comfortable, stable lie — since on Sartre's view that irresolution is the whole point. Third-person, close narration is probably the natural choice for showing this from the inside, but I'll leave the premise and point of view up to you.
