I'm a PhD student in computer science working on multi-agent reinforcement learning, and for my dissertation I've built a small testbed in which pairs of autonomous scheduling agents play a single, unrepeated game with no outside enforcer to decide how large a share of a shared GPU cluster each may request. The payoffs are a textbook Prisoner's Dilemma -- mutual restraint beats mutual greed, but a greedy request against a restrained one does best of all for the greedy agent -- so the two agents have no reason, on the face of it, to do anything but defect. To escape that outcome, I've let the agents optionally disclose their complete decision procedure to their counterpart before acting. My advisor pointed out that this is essentially the setting studied under the heading of program equilibrium: an idea traceable to Howard's older metagame proposal and formalized in Tennenholtz's result that once players can condition on each other's literal source code, a folk theorem holds even without repeated play, so that almost any individually rational payoff becomes sustainable in equilibrium. Since then I've also worked through some of the follow-up literature using provability logic (the FairBot/PrudentBot family of agents), which tries to make this kind of conditional cooperation robust to more than exact, line-by-line code matching.

Since I need to defend this design choice at my committee meeting next month, I'd like your take on the most important implications of program equilibrium for a decision like mine, rather than a general explainer of the concept. I'm especially interested in whether the folk theorem should actually reassure someone trying to engineer cooperation, given that it seems to multiply the number of available equilibria at least as fast as it creates good ones, leaving equilibrium selection as hard a problem as before; in how much the provability-logic refinements genuinely repair the brittleness of naive exact-code-matching, as opposed to just relocating that same brittleness into facts about what the agents can mutually prove about one another; and in what happens to any of these guarantees once disclosure is imperfect -- for instance, if an agent's real behavior could diverge at runtime from whatever code it showed its counterpart, or if two policies are functionally identical without being syntactically identical. If there's a consideration you think matters more than the three I've raised, I would rather hear about that than have all three treated superficially.
