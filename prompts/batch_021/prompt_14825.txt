I am revising the syllabus for this term's graduate seminar on legal philosophy, and before committing to it as a discussion question I want to pressure-test a point that the standard textbook framing of the Hart–Fuller debate tends to obscure. The usual presentation offers a tidy opposition: Hart, the positivist, holds that the existence of a legal rule is one question and its moral merit a separate one, while Fuller, the natural lawyer, holds that law has an "inner morality," such that a regime which sufficiently flouts certain formal desiderata forfeits its claim to be law at all. What gets lost in this version is that Hart does not simply deny any connection between law and morality; in the chapter of The Concept of Law devoted to "laws and morals," he offers his own doctrine of a minimum content of natural law, derived not from anything resembling Fuller's procedural desiderata but from a handful of truisms about the human condition — our vulnerability to violence, our rough equality of physical power, our limited altruism, the scarcity of resources, and our limited understanding and strength of will — which together, he argues, make certain substantive rules (restraining violence, some stable form of property, honoring undertakings) close to indispensable in any system of rules that could regulate social life at all.

I would like your help thinking through how this doctrine actually relates to Fuller's eight principles of legality — generality, promulgation, prospectivity, clarity, non-contradiction, possibility of compliance, constancy through time, and congruence between declared rule and official action. Both are routinely filed under the heading "connections between law and morality," yet they seem to me to be doing quite different argumentative work, and I doubt the standard exposition brings this out clearly enough for students to appreciate the difference. Please compare the two doctrines directly, addressing in particular: whether Hart's truisms and Fuller's desiderata are even answering the same question, given that one concerns substantive content and the other procedural form; whether Hart's minimum content should be read as a genuine concession to something like natural law thinking, compatible with his separability thesis only on a technicality, or as fully consistent with, and even in service of, his positivism; and whether a legal system could satisfy Hart's minimum content while still failing badly enough on Fuller's desiderata to forfeit the title of law (or the reverse), and what such a divergence would show about which doctrine is doing more of the real work in distinguishing law from naked coercion.

A colleague in the law faculty dismisses this as a non-issue, on the grounds that Hart's minimum content is far too thin and instrumental to count as any kind of concession to natural law, so that no real tension with Fuller needs explaining. I am not yet convinced that this response settles the matter, and would welcome a rigorous assessment before I decide how much weight to put on the point in seminar.
