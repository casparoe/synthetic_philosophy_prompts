A friend and I spent much of Sunday evening arguing about Kurt Gödel's private conviction that some kind of afterlife is philosophically likely -- not his well-known ontological proof of God's existence, but the far less discussed argument he set out in letters to his mother, Marianne, and in conversation with Hao Wang. Afterward I wrote up my understanding of the argument, together with the objection my friend pressed on me and that I never quite managed to answer. I would like your comments on it before I write back to her.

Here is what I have:

Gödel's argument, as I reconstruct it, is disjunctive. Either the world -- including the laws governing mind and matter -- is organized on rational principles, in something close to Leibniz's sense of a world answerable to a principle of sufficient reason, or it is not. If it is not, we have no special reason to trust reason's verdict on anything, including the finality of death, so agnosticism rather than confident disbelief is the honest response. If it is rational, though, Gödel found it strange that such a world would develop creatures with the vast, barely-exercised potential of a human mind only to annihilate that potential permanently after a few decades, most of them spent immature. A rational world, on this view, does not build capacities it has no intention of using, so some form of personal continuation after death is the more probable reading of the evidence, such as it is.

My friend's objection is that the two horns are not really parallel, because they quietly rely on different senses of "rational." The first horn only needs "rational" to mean orderly and law-governed, the sense that mattered to Gödel the logician. The second needs "rational" to mean something closer to considerate of individual flourishing, the sense that would actually license an inference to an afterlife. But a world can be perfectly law-governed and intelligible while being utterly indifferent to whether any given mind's potential is realized or wasted, in the way a valid proof is indifferent to whether its theorem turns out to be an interesting one. If she is right, the "rational" horn is fully compatible with universal, unmitigated extinction at death, and the whole argument quietly trades on equivocation rather than genuine disjunction.

I suspect there is a Kantian escape route for Gödel here, something like treating survival as a postulate needed to make moral striving intelligible rather than as a theoretical inference from how the world happens to be built, but he never states it that way himself, and I am wary of being too generous on his behalf. There is also the harder question of what to do with the fact that this is the same man who, later in life, starved himself to death from the conviction that his food was being poisoned. I do not think that biography refutes an argument made while he was in full possession of his faculties, but it does not feel like nothing, either.

I would appreciate your comments on three things in particular: whether I have represented Gödel's actual reasoning and its sources fairly, whether the equivocation charge genuinely sinks the argument or whether there is a more charitable reading of "rational" that saves it, and how much weight, if any, his later paranoia should get when we decide how charitably to reconstruct this part of his thinking.
