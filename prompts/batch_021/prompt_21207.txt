And here's the student's answer:

"The Allais paradox is a classic demonstration that people don't behave the way expected utility theory predicts. In problem 1 most people pick the safe option A over the gamble B because they're risk averse; in problem 2, once both options are risky, that risk aversion has no sure thing to attach itself to anymore, so people go for the bigger prize and pick D over C instead. Formally, the switch violates the independence axiom, which says that if you prefer lottery X to Y you should still prefer X to Y when both are mixed with some common third lottery Z in the same proportions. You can't fix this by just plugging in a more risk-averse (more concave) utility function, because concavity is exactly what generates the preference for the sure thing A over B in the first place, and that same concave function will keep generating a preference for C over D too - yet actual choices go the other way. It's basically the same rationality failure as the Ellsberg paradox, where people facing ambiguous urns similarly abandon expected utility theory, and both cases are usually cited as the founding evidence for Kahneman and Tversky's prospect theory, in particular the probability weighting function's overweighting of certainty (the 'certainty effect'), which is why the 1%-vs-0% gap looms so much larger in problem 1 than the same 1-point gap does in problem 2."
