Over dinner last week, a friend and I ended up in a long argument about Augustine's writings on lying, set off by a case she raised about hiding someone from a person clearly intent on harming him. I had assumed that his rigorism -- the position worked out first in De Mendacio and then hardened considerably in Contra Mendacium, that no lie is ever permissible, not even to save an innocent life -- was really a historical curiosity rather than something anyone still defends once the hard cases are on the table. She disagreed, pointing out that the standard escape routes people reach for, such as silence or strategic equivocation, are exactly the moves Augustine already wrestled with and was reluctant to bless, and that a fair amount of contemporary natural-law ethics is still built on his foundations. We never really settled the argument, especially over what to do with Rahab, praised in the New Testament for protecting the spies in an episode that seems to reward the very act his framework has to treat as sinful.

I host a small philosophy podcast for a general but fairly literate audience, and next month's guest is a moral philosopher who defends, in a recognizably Augustinian idiom, the view that lying is never permissible, regardless of the stakes. I don't want the episode to consist of forty minutes of her simply restating that thesis, so I'd like help drafting eight to ten interview questions that press on the places where the position is least comfortable.

I want at least one question each on: the persecutor-at-the-door case and Augustine's own proposed alternative of silence or refusal to answer, pushed hard on whether silence can itself amount to a deception in context; how she handles Rahab and other scriptural episodes where a lie appears to be rewarded rather than merely excused; the Contra Mendacium episode involving Consentius, in which Augustine condemns Catholics who had feigned conversion to infiltrate and expose the Priscillianists, and whether her own view therefore rules out essentially all deceptive infiltration, including undercover work against people who mean real harm; and whether the deepest rationale for the prohibition is actually about protecting the person being lied to, or, as Augustine seems to suggest, about the liar's own corruption -- and if it is the latter, why that self-regarding harm should ever outweigh an innocent life. For each of these four, please add a natural follow-up question keyed to the answer she's most likely to give, the way a good interviewer presses a second time rather than simply moving on.
