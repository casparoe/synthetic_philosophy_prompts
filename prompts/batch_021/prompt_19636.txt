After a forty-year career in experimental physics, I have spent much of my retirement sitting in on philosophy courses at the local university. A friend on the faculty who teaches the undergraduate seminar on free will and moral responsibility has asked me to help mark a set of final exams over the summer, and before I commit to a grade on one answer I would like a second, more expert opinion.

The question, worth 10 points, reads:

"Explain Galen Strawson's Basic Argument for the conclusion that no one is ever ultimately or truly morally responsible for what they do. Your answer should address (a) why the argument is supposed to hold whether or not determinism is true, and (b) at least one influential objection to it."

Here, verbatim, is the student's answer:

"Galen Strawson's Basic Argument tries to show that no one can be 'ultimately' or 'truly' morally responsible for their actions, in the strong sense that would justify ultimate praise or blame, such as eternal reward or punishment. The argument runs, roughly: (1) we do what we do because of the way we are — our beliefs, desires, and character; (2) to be truly responsible for our actions we would have to be truly responsible for being the way we are, at least in the relevant respects; (3) but to be responsible for being the way we are, we would have to have chosen, in some prior act, to have the character we have, and that choice would itself have had to flow from some yet-prior character we already possessed, generating a vicious infinite regress; (4) therefore ultimate self-creation is impossible, and so is ultimate moral responsibility. Since our present characters are always the product of earlier causes we did not choose, such as our genes, upbringing, and culture, and since determinism guarantees that this chain of causes stretches backward indefinitely beyond our control, the regress can never be stopped and true responsibility is unattainable.

One influential objection comes from Strawson's own reactive-attitudes approach, developed in 'Freedom and Resentment.' On this view, moral responsibility is not a metaphysical fact to be discovered about the ultimate origination of our characters, but is constituted by our natural, and practically ineliminable, tendency to feel resentment, gratitude, indignation, and similar reactive emotions toward one another. Since we could not give up these attitudes even if the Basic Argument were sound, the objection concludes that the argument, whatever its logical merits, is practically inert: it cannot touch the interpersonal practices in which responsibility actually consists."

Would you grade this out of 10, exactly as the instructor of record would, without going easy on it? Rather than an overall impression, I would like a point-by-point assessment: what the answer gets right, what it gets wrong or glosses over, and how much weight each point should carry in the final score. A short rubric in JSON (criterion, points possible, points awarded, brief justification), followed by the total, would suit me well.
