I'm prepping my grad seminar on phil of math for next week and we're finally getting to Skolem's paradox, and I keep going back and forth on how to teach it without just handing them the "resolution" like it's obvious. Every year half the room parrots back "countable is relative to the model, not absolute" without it costing them anything, and they definately don't register that this was Skolem's own conclusion too -- he actually thought it showed set theory couldn't pin down a determinate universe of sets -- or that Putnam later tried to leverage basically the same move into a full-blown argument against there being any perspective-independent facts about reference at all.

So instead of another handout, I want to try giving them a dialogue. Could you write one between two people who both clearly know the model theory -- one taking the Skolemite line that the paradox really does show cardinality is only ever relative-to-a-model, so talk of "the" cumulative hierarchy is at best a convenient fiction, and the other insisting this is a total non-issue once you're careful about transitive models, Mostowski collapse, and what it even means for a model to "correctly compute" a power set. I don't want the realist to get off easy just asserting there's obviously a fact about whether the continuum is really uncountable -- push them on how they'd ever know there own background theory of sets isn't itself sitting inside someone else's countable model (that's the regress Putnam leans on). And don't let the relativist just coast on the paradox sounding spooky -- make them own up to whether they can even state their view without secretly helping themselves to the absolute notions they're supposed to be denying. I don't want two monologues stapled together -- each one should actually be responding to what the other just said, ideally landing somewhere neither of them expected. A page or two is plenty, and it doesn't need a tidy ending -- I'd honestly rather it end with both of them realizing they disagree about something more specific than they thought (second-order logic? what "all subsets" even ranges over?) than with one side winning outright.
