In the formal epistemology seminar I'm taking this term, we've spent the last two weeks on self-locating belief and Bostrom's work on anthropic reasoning, and something from last week's discussion section is still nagging at me. As I understand the Self-Sampling Assumption, it tells you to treat your own existence as though it were a random draw from the observers in your reference class, weighted by how many such observers each rival hypothesis predicts; so once you conditionalize on the fact of your own existence, a theory implying a vastly larger population of observers than its competitor comes out overwhelmingly more probable, even absent any new evidence that distinguishes the two theories empirically. That looks to me like exactly the move made by Bostrom's presumptuous philosopher, who tells the physicists not to bother running their experiment, since she can already show, from the armchair, that the high-population theory must be true.

Given that this sort of population-driven overconfidence already seems built into SSA, I'm having trouble seeing why the Self-Indication Assumption gets treated in the secondary literature as the further, more radical step that anthropic reasoners take on specifically in order to escape the Doomsday Argument. Shouldn't SSA already be saddled with the presumptuous philosopher's problem, which would make adopting SIA on top of it look like a redundant addition rather than a distinct and independently costly commitment? My study group went back and forth on this for most of an hour without settling it, so I would like to understand what I'm missing about how the two assumptions actually differ.
