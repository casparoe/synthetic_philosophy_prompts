I'm a PhD student in philosophy working mostly in formal epistemology, and I've spent the last couple of weeks in a seminar working through Bas van Fraassen's "Belief and the Will," where he introduces the Reflection Principle: roughly, that my current credence in a proposition, conditional on the supposition that my credence in it at some specified future time will equal some value r, should itself equal r. The idea is to treat one's own future opinion the way Lewis's Principal Principle treats objective chance — as a kind of in-house epistemic expert to which present credence should defer. Van Fraassen backs this with a diachronic Dutch Book argument, showing that unless Reflection holds, a bookie can combine a bet placed now with a bet conditional on my future credence so as to guarantee me a loss no matter how things turn out. Followed step by step, the argument seems watertight, yet the conclusion feels much too strong: if I know that tomorrow a neuroscientist will implant a false memory in me, or that a pill will make me irrationally certain of some proposition, Reflection still seems to demand that I defer today to whatever credence that manipulated future self will have, which seems to get things exactly backwards.

I know van Fraassen's own response is to restrict Reflection to cases where I don't already expect my future credences to be shaped by something other than rational updating on evidence, and I know critics such as Christensen have argued that the sure-loss framing doesn't actually track epistemic (as opposed to merely practical) irrationality. But I haven't managed to get a satisfying, non-question-begging grip on either point — my advisor's gloss that "the argument conflates diachronic coherence with genuine epistemic rationality" hasn't really clicked for me. Could you talk me through, in fairly intuitive terms rather than through the probability calculus, why the Dutch book seems to force Reflection on us so generally, exactly which hidden assumption about an agent's self-knowledge of her own future updating procedure is doing the real work, and why restricting Reflection to "expected rational updating" either trivializes the principle or quietly reintroduces the very question it was meant to settle? I'd like to walk into next week's seminar with an actual gut-level feel for the tension, rather than just the ability to reproduce the formal argument.
