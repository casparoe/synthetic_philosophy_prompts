I'm a high school junior, and I've been part of my school's philosophy club since freshman year; we meet at lunch on Wednesdays and usually spend half the period working through a short reading before we discuss it. A couple of weeks ago the reading was a short piece on why an indicative conditional like "if it rained, the game was cancelled" and its counterfactual-sounding cousin "if it had rained, the game would have been cancelled" don't always stand or fall together, even though they can sound like the same claim in a different tense. We were still turning that idea over in the abstract when an actual case landed in our laps: somebody had pulled the fire alarm as a prank during fourth period, and once the school checked hall-pass records and cleared everyone else in the building, it became common knowledge, accepted by the whole club, that only two students, Owen and Lena, could possibly have done it -- so "Owen pulled it or Lena pulled it" was treated by everyone as flatly true.

Two people in the club, Theo and Naomi, then got into an argument that outlasted the meeting. Theo opened by saying that this disjunction hands us two conditionals for free: "If Owen didn't pull it, Lena did" and "If Owen hadn't pulled it, Lena would have" -- since whichever of them turns out innocent, logic hands us the other's guilt, in the real case and the hypothetical one alike. Naomi disagreed: the second conditional can't just borrow the first one's certainty, she said, because in the real-world case we still get to keep all our background knowledge that the alarm did go off, so Lena's guilt follows the moment Owen's is ruled out -- but in the hypothetical where Owen simply never pulls it, we've taken away part of the reason we ever thought an alarm-puller existed at all, so nothing guarantees Lena would have stepped up on her own. Theo replied that this proves too much, since we accept exactly this kind of counterfactual all the time without a second thought -- like when only two students could possibly have left a classroom window open overnight, and nobody hesitates to say that if one of them hadn't left it open, the other would have; so, he said, there had to be something specific to the alarm case that Naomi hadn't actually pinned down. Naomi answered that the window case differs in exactly the relevant way: leaving a window open by accident isn't aimed at anything, so there's no reason the second student's chances would depend on the first's, whereas pulling a prank alarm is exactly the kind of thing where one person's decision can hinge on the other's presence or encouragement -- and everyone already suspected that was true of Owen and Lena specifically. So the lesson wasn't that these counterfactuals never work, only that they quietly need an independence assumption the indicative never needed, and that the assumption happens to fail here. Theo's closing point was that this only shows the counterfactual needed one further, contingent premise in this particular case -- it doesn't show the two kinds of conditionals differ in kind, since in the window case they were exactly as strong as each other, and Naomi was just dressing up a technicality as a principle.

Could you go through Theo and Naomi's exchange turn by turn and say who has the better of it at each stage, rather than just declaring an overall winner? I'm especially curious whether Theo's closing move actually works -- whether showing that the counterfactual only fails here because of a fact specific to Owen and Lena really does mean there's no deeper, structural difference between the two kinds of conditionals, or whether Naomi was onto something his reply never actually answers. And if there's a general moral about when it's safe to convert an indicative "if not this person, then that one" into the matching counterfactual, I'd genuinely like to hear it, since none of us could settle this before the bell rang.
