Your answer should explain the reference class problem and state whether propensity theories succeed in avoiding it."

And here's the student's answer, copied exactly (I cleaned up a couple of typos but left the actual content untouched):

"On the frequentist view, the probability of an outcome is the limiting relative frequency of that outcome within an infinite reference sequence of repeatable trials -- what Reichenbach called a 'collective.' Since a single trial isn't itself a sequence, strict frequentism can't assign a probability to an individual case except derivatively, by picking out some collective the case belongs to and borrowing its frequency. This generates the reference class problem: any single event belongs to indefinitely many collectives at once (this patient is a 68-year-old, a diabetic, someone operated on by this particular surgeon, etc.), each with a different relative frequency of survival, and nothing in the frequentist framework tells you which collective fixes 'the' probability of survival for this patient.

Propensity theories were developed partly to make sense of single-case probabilities in quantum mechanics, where an individual decay event seems to just have a chance of occurring. Popper's idea is that probability is an objective propensity: a physical disposition of a repeatable experimental set-up to produce outcomes with a given frequency in a hypothetical long run. Because the propensity belongs to the single trial itself rather than to any statistical class, propensity theory sidesteps the reference class problem entirely -- there's no need to decide which reference class the case belongs to, since the probability was never a fact about a class of cases to begin with, just a dispositional fact about this set-up, here, now."
