Ok here's one thats always bugged me about Leibniz. He needs this to be the best of all possible worlds, picked out by God via the principle of sufficient reason -- but he also insists our choices (including our sins) are genuinely free and that we're morally responsible for them, which seems to require that we could've chosen otherwise. His escape hatch is the distinction between moral necessity and absolute necessity, backed by the infinite analysis theory of contingency: a truth counts as contingent rather than absolutely necessary just becuase proving that its negation is contradictory would take an infinite analysis instead of terminating, after finitely many steps, in an identity. Robert Adams and others have pushed back that this only tells you WE can't finish the analysis -- it doesn't show the choice was ever really open -- so the whole system quietly collapses into necessitarianism. Write me an essay on whether Leibniz actually pulls off the freedom his ethics needs, or whether he's secretly a necessitarian despite himself. Weigh both readings fairly, but don't leave it wishy-washy -- pick a side and defend it.
