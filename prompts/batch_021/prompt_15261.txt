I'm working on a piece about how people make big, hard-to-reverse life decisions, and I stumbled into unexpectedly good material for it at a dinner party last weekend. My friend Priyanka has two job offers sitting in front of her right now: a senior UX role at a big fintech company with great pay and a totally predictable day-to-day, versus running the small education wing of a marine science nonprofit for way less money but work she's clearly lit up by. Shes been going back and forth on it for like six weeks now and it's genuinely agonizing her. At dinner, two other friends of ours, Owen and Ferdy, ended up in a whole side-argument about why it's even hard for her to decide in the first place, and it's been rattling around in my head since.

Owen's position was basically that this is an information problem and nothing more: Priyanka is torn because she can't yet know how she'll feel two years into either job, and once she has more evidence -- picturing her life in more detail, talking to people further along each path, whatever -- one option will come out ahead and the "hardness" will just dissolve. Ferdy disagreed completely. He said some choices are hard not because we're missing facts but because the two things on offer are, in his words, "on a par" -- comparable, sure, but neither better, worse, nor exactly equal to each other, and no amount of extra information changes that, because the difficulty is sitting in the values themselves, not in Priyanka's head. Owen shot back that if neither job is actually better then it shouldn't matter which one she takes, yet it obviously matters enormously to her, so "on a par" just explains away the very feeling it's meant to account for. Ferdy's comeback: picture sweetening the nonprofit offer slightly, say a small raise -- that tiny bump obviously wouldn't make it the clear winner over the fintech job, which shows the two options aren't exactly tied either (a tiny improvement should be enough to break a genuine tie); but a big enough improvement to the nonprofit offer would eventually flip things, which shows they're not simply incomparable in the way a number and a color are. So, Ferdy said, Priyanka's agonizing is real and rational -- she isn't failing to detect some fact about which job is better, she has to more or less decide what to care about and commit to it. Owen wouldn't budge either: he said all Ferdy had shown is that our concept of "better career" gets fuzzy at the edges when two options are close, not that there's some extra fourth relationship between values hiding behind better/worse/equal.

Neither of them gave an inch, and I didn't feel qualified to referee it myself. Could you go through Owen and Ferdy's exchange move by move and say who's got the stronger argument at each stage -- not just who wins overall -- and flag anything important you think both of them are missing?
