I'm a philosophy PhD student finishing a dissertation chapter that puts classical Nyāya epistemology into conversation with Buddhist pramāṇa theory, and I keep running into the same problem when I try to explain the material to my committee, most of whom were trained in mainstream Anglophone epistemology: they want the disagreement over universals staged as an actual argument, not summarized as "Nyāya is a realist about universals, Buddhists are nominalists." The specific flashpoint I care about is whether ordinary perception (pratyakṣa) ever directly discloses a real universal (sāmānya) — cowness as it inheres in some particular cow, say — or whether all talk of universals is just a byproduct of the Buddhist mechanism of apoha (exclusion) operating on conceptual construction (vikalpa) after the fact. I know the basic shape of each position: the Naiyāyika appeal to determinate perception (savikalpaka pratyakṣa) as already presenting the universal alongside the particular it qualifies, versus the Dignāga–Dharmakīrti line that perception proper involves no conceptual construction whatsoever and grasps only unique particulars (svalakṣaṇa), with universals demoted to convenient fictions generated by excluding what a thing is not. What I haven't managed to find anywhere is the two positions actually colliding, argument for argument, rather than just being laid out side by side.

Could you write me a dialogue, roughly a page long, between a Naiyāyika and a Buddhist epistemologist in the Dignāga–Dharmakīrti tradition that stages this disagreement properly? I'd like the Naiyāyika to open with the classic charge that apoha is secretly parasitic on the very universals it claims to dispense with — you can't say what a given exclusion excludes without already grasping the positive kind you're trying to avoid positing — and I'd like the Buddhist to answer with the strongest reply actually available in that tradition, rather than folding immediately. Then have the Naiyāyika respond to that specific reply rather than simply repeating the opening move, and let the exchange run for a few more rounds. Please keep the two sides evenly matched: I don't want the dialogue quietly stacked for either the realist or the nominalist, and I'd rather it end in genuine unresolved tension than in a tidy concession on either side, since that's a reasonably accurate picture of how this debate actually stood historically. If it helps you calibrate tone, the Naiyāyika can note in passing how closely this tracks realism-about-universals disputes in the Western tradition, but I'd like the substance to stay anchored in the Sanskrit categories rather than drifting into a generic nominalism-versus-realism script.
