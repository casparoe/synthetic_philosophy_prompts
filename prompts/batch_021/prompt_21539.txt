Over the past year or so I've been doing a lot of independent reading on ethics -- nothing formal, just books and philosophy discussions I've come across online -- and along the way I ran into the idea of "negative utilitarianism": the view that what matters morally is reducing and preventing suffering, rather than the usual utilitarian idea of totaling up pleasure and pain and maximizing the net balance between them. My first reaction was that this felt almost obviously right. Stopping someone's agony seems far more urgent than adding a little extra enjoyment to a life that is already going well, and a lot of what actually troubles me about the world -- factory farming, chronic pain, extreme poverty -- does seem to call for exactly this kind of priority on suffering over everything else.

Then I came across an objection that gave me real pause. If the only thing that intrinsically matters is driving suffering down toward zero, it seems to follow that if some sufficiently powerful being could press a button and painlessly, instantly end all sentient life in the universe, forever, that would be the single best thing that being could do -- since a universe with no one left in it has no suffering left in it either. That struck me less like a difficult conclusion a committed negative utilitarian should simply accept, and more like a sign that something has gone badly wrong somewhere in the reasoning. And yet, from what I can tell, plenty of people who still find the core idea -- that suffering deserves special priority over happiness -- genuinely compelling have not simply abandoned the view; instead they have proposed more careful, weakened versions of it that are meant to block this conclusion. Not having any formal training to lean on, I am finding it hard to judge these responses myself. Could you (1) explain as precisely as you can why the "painlessly end all sentient life" conclusion really does follow from the simplest, purest version of negative utilitarianism, (2) walk through the main strategies people have used to try to block that conclusion while still keeping something distinctively suffering-focused about the resulting view, and (3) give me your own honest assessment of whether any of these patched-up versions actually succeed, or whether they simply collapse back into ordinary utilitarianism, or just relocate the very same problem one step further down?
