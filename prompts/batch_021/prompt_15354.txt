I've been a longtime lurker of the rationalist diaspora (LessWrong, the EA Forum, Overcoming Bias, and the blogs that grew out of them), and one piece of decision-theory folklore I keep coming back to is the "surrogate goals" proposal for defusing threats -- a proposal that I believe originates in Tobias Baumann's old Foundational Research Institute post on the subject. As I understand it, an agent worried about being threatened can commit in advance to caring a great deal about some invented, low-stakes "surrogate" goal that mimics the structure of whatever a threatener would naturally target. If the threat is ever carried out, it lands on the surrogate rather than on anything the agent actually values, which is supposed to remove the threatener's incentive to threaten in the first place, since a rational threatener who foresees this gets nothing from following through. The canonical motivating cases are exotic: superintelligences that can inspect one another's source code or run high-fidelity simulations of each other, in the tradition of the old worries about Roko's-Basilisk-style acausal blackmail between agents that can never causally interact at all.

Discussing this with friends recently, we found ourselves wondering how much of the underlying logic -- redirect a threat's target so as to shrink the threatener's payoff from carrying it out -- has any real purchase on mundane human cases: blackmail, hostage-taking, a manipulative colleague, or a government's declared policy of never negotiating with kidnappers. None of us could tell whether we were looking at a genuinely exportable insight or just a piece of decision-theoretic exotica that happens to sound like it should generalize.

Please lay out what you take to be the most important implications of the surrogate goals proposal, and the broader threat-deflection logic behind it, for how an ordinary person or a small organization should think about deterring real-world extortion attempts. In doing so, please be explicit about which parts of the argument depend on capacities -- credible precommitment, transparency to the threatener, common knowledge of one's own disposition -- that are readily available to the idealized agents of the acausal-trade literature but not to flesh-and-blood humans. I'd also be curious whether you think any of the standard objections to surrogate goals in the AI safety literature -- for instance, that a sufficiently sophisticated threatener can simply threaten the surrogate and the real goal both -- have close analogues in the human case.
