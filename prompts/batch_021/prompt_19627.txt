Random question that's turned into a bit of a rabbit hole for me. I'm working on a novel where one of the two central characters, Eleanor, is a retired classics professor who just got a terminal diagnosis, and she spends a good chunk of the book fending off her grown daughter's grief by quoting Epicurus at her, very dryly, whenever things get too emotional. I didn't want to write those scenes based on secondhand summaries I half remember from an undergrad class, so I dug up an old Loeb edition at a used bookstore and actually read the Letter to Menoeceus properly. There's a bit I want Eleanor to say almost verbatim:

"So death, the most terrifying of ills, is nothing to us, since so long as we exist, death is not present, and when death is present, we no longer exist. It is therefore nothing either to the living or to the dead, since for the living it is not, and the dead no longer exist."

Sitting with it instead of just nodding along like I usually do, I've talked myself into being confused about what's actually doing the work in this argument. It only seems to land if you already accept that nothing can be bad for someone unless that person exists, at that time, to undergo it — otherwise "when death is present, we no longer exist" doesn't actually tell you anything about whether death is bad. Is Epicurus just helping himself to that assumption in this passage, or is it something he's entitled to from earlier in the letter? A friend in my Tuesday philosophy meetup swears the argument is basically airtight and that only sentimental people find it unconvincing, which annoyed me enough that I want to actually understand it properly rather then fake it in the book. I also have this nagging half-memory from college of some 20th century philosopher (Nagel, maybe?) arguing that you can be harmed by things you never live to experience at all, like a betrayal you never find out about, and that this was specifically meant to cause trouble for passages like this one. Can you walk me through what's actually holding this argument up, and whether that objection really does sink it, or if I'm misremembering the whole thing?
