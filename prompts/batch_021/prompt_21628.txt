In 1974, David Miller and Pavel Tichý independently proved that Popper's qualitative definition of verisimilitude has a fatal consequence: on that definition, no false theory can ever come out closer to the truth than another false theory, which is exactly the comparison his account of scientific progress required. The standard repair has been to abandon Popper's purely content-based ordering in favor of similarity metrics defined over states of affairs, along lines developed by Niiniluoto, Tichý himself, and Oddie. Miller has since pressed a further objection against these similarity measures: they are not invariant under permissible changes of descriptive vocabulary, so that whether one theory comes out closer to the truth than another can be an artifact of how the relevant possibilities happen to be carved up linguistically, rather than a fact about the theories themselves. Please argue that this language-variance objection is no more damaging to similarity-based verisimilitude than the parallel language-relativity of Carnapian inductive logic (and the associated grue-style worries about projectible predicates) is to Bayesian confirmation theory, and that rejecting the former while tolerating the latter is therefore unprincipled.
