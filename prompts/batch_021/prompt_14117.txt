So I've been down a rabbit hole this week that started with re-reading Yudkowsky's old "Circular Altruism" post (the one with "shut up and multiply" and the dust-specks-vs-torture stuff) and somehow ended up on this old John Taurek paper, "Should the Numbers Count?", which I guess was a big enough deal at the time that Parfit wrote a whole rebuttal to it called "Innumerate Ethics." Taurek's setup is basically: you've got a drug, and you can use it to save one stranger, or split it up and use it to save five different strangers, but not both. The "obvious" utilitarian answer is save the five, but Taurek argues that each person's claim not to die is complete in itself and doesn't get diluted just because there happen to be other claims sitting next to it on the table -- so he thinks you're permitted (maybe even required) to just flip a coin instead of always saving the greater number.

This feels like exactly the kind of thing that should definately bother a good LWer, since "the numbers obviously count" is basically load-bearing for the whole shut-up-and-multiply / cause-prioritization worldview. I vaguely remember theres some contractualist move, maybe from Scanlon or someone responding to him, involving a "weighted lottery" that tries to split the difference between Taurek's 50/50 coin flip and full aggregation -- but I've never seen it laid out clearly next to Taurek's actual argument and Parfit's actual rebuttal. Could you write me an essay that (a) states Taurek's argument as strongly as you can, in his own terms and not a strawman, (b) lays out the best responses to it that you're aware of, and (c) actually picks a winner? I don't want a wishy-washy "there are considerations on both sides" ending -- give me a real verdict (a specific position, even if it's some hybrid view), and defend it the way you'd defend it to someone who already knows the literature and will call you out on hand-waving.
