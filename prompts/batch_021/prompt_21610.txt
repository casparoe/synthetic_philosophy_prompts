I'm working on a piece about constitutional "eternity clauses"—provisions like Article 79(3) of Germany's Basic Law, which places human dignity, the federal structure, and a handful of other principles permanently beyond the reach of amendment, no matter how large a future majority wants to change them. Every scholar I've interviewed explains these clauses the same way: as a Ulysses contract writ large. Just as Ulysses had his crew bind him to the mast and ignore whatever he screamed once the Sirens' song took hold, a founding generation ties the hands of its future selves against passions it can already predict it will come to regret. That seems to imply that the more irrevocable the constraint, the more faithfully it reproduces what Ulysses's rope accomplished, so that an eternity clause should be the purest, most rational form of precommitment a constitution can offer. Is that the right way to extend the analogy, or does insisting on permanence actually give up something essential to what made Ulysses's own bargain work?
