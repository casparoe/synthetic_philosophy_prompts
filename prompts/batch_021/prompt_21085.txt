I'm a retired analytical chemist -- spent about thirty-five years running water-quality testing before I hung it up -- and since retiring I've fallen into reading political philosophy, currently working through some stuff on democratic theory that keeps using the phrase "the boundary problem," meaning the puzzle of how you justify who's even in the group that gets to vote on something, as opposed to how the voting should work once you've got your group. It clicked into focus for me because of an argument I watched two neighbors of mine, Martin and Carol, have after our town's planning board meeting last month, and I havent been able to let it go since.

The meeting was about doubling the size of the industrial park at the edge of town. The county's own environmental report said most of the extra particulate pollution would drift into Millbrook, the little unincorporated settlement just past the town line, whose residents obviously dont get a ballot in our referendum. Afterward, over beers, Martin argued (1) that this is fine, because a town vote exists to decide what the town does, and the town just is the people bound by its laws; if "anyone affected" gets a vote instead, you'd have to enfranchise Millbrook, then the whole county since some of it's downwind too, until "the public" means everybody and the vote means nothing. Carol answered (2) that this has it backwards: the reason a decision needs the consent of the people on the receiving end is precisely that they bear its costs, so letting only the beneficiaries vote just uses the machinery of democracy to launder a one-sided deal onto people who never agreed to it -- the town line is a map artifact, not a moral one. Martin came back (3) with the seasonal crew at the plant, who work there all year, live in a trailer court just past the line, pay no local tax, and don't vote either -- are they in Carol's "affected" club too, and if so what about the out-of-state buyers who'll get marginally cheaper parts if the plant expands, since they're affected too, just less? At some point, he said, "affected" has to cash out to something narrower or it explains everything and therefore nothing. Carol granted (4) she has no crisp cutoff, but said a crisp line drawn in the wrong place is no improvement -- the town boundary was set by a surveyor in the 1890s for reasons having nothing to do with affectedness or consent, so Martin doesn't get credit for precision when his precision is arbitrary too. Martin then tried patching his view (5) by saying what actually matters isn't being affected but being subject to the town's laws while present in it, which lets him enfranchise the seasonal crew without having to let in the distant buyers. Carol's reply (6) was that Millbrook counts as "subject to" the town's law in exactly the relevant sense too, whenever a permit the town issues legally authorizes a harm to land on them with no recourse -- the only real difference is that the crew can quit and leave, and Millbrook can't.

Neither of them budged, and being an old lab-notebook type I actually jotted the six moves down afterward, which is why I can number them for you like that. Could you go through the exchange move by move and say who comes out ahead at each numbered point and why, rather than just handing one of them the overall win? I'd also like to know what, if anything, you think they're both missing -- whether either position can be patched up to survive all six moves intact, or whether "the boundary problem" is genuinely just a spot where democratic theory runs out of road.
