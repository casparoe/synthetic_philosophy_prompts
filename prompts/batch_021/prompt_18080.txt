For my seminar on the metaphysics of social kinds, we've spent the last few weeks reading Ian Hacking's *The Social Construction of What?*, John Searle's *The Construction of Social Reality*, and Sally Haslanger's paper "Gender and Race: (What) Are They? (What) Do We Want Them To Be?" A classmate and I got into an argument after class about whether these three are really analyzing the same phenomenon or just borrowing each other's vocabulary, and I'm now stuck on how to even begin the short position paper we've been assigned for next week. Searle's account of money seems to rest entirely on collective acceptance of a status function via a constitutive rule of the form "X counts as Y in context C," with no essential reference to hierarchy or subordination between persons. Haslanger, by contrast, explicitly defines gender and racial categories in terms of occupying a social position that is systematically privileged or subordinated, a position assigned to someone on the basis of bodily features that are, rightly or wrongly, taken within a given context to mark them as female or as belonging to a given ancestry group. Hacking's own apparatus of "interactive kinds" and looping effects pulls in yet a third direction, since it is centered on how being classified changes the self-understanding and behavior of the classified person -- which seems to fit psychiatric categories far more naturally than it fits either money or, arguably, race and gender themselves.

Could you help me get clear on whether these three accounts are best read as competing analyses of a single phenomenon called "social construction," or as accounts of genuinely distinct mechanisms that merely happen to share a label? In particular, I'd like you to address whether Haslanger's hierarchy-based analysis could, even in principle, be stretched to cover something like money, which doesn't obviously involve subordination, and whether Searle's status-function machinery could, conversely, be stretched to cover gender and race, which don't obviously reduce to an explicit or even implicit constitutive rule. And if the three frameworks really do turn out to be about different things, I'm also curious what, if anything, should still justify grouping race, gender, and money together under the single heading of "the social construction of X," rather than treating that heading as a loose and potentially misleading equivocation.
