I'm a junior, and this term I'm serving as secretary of my school's student-run Giving Circle, a committee of eleven of us that decides how to allocate the roughly $15,000 an alumna donates to charity every year. In past years we've used a simple majority vote -- each member ranks the finalist charities, and whichever gets the most first-place votes receives the entire pool -- which has consistently left the losing factions feeling like their values simply didn't count. This year the frustrated minority is the two members who care most about farmed-animal welfare, and at our last meeting one member proposed something different: instead of voting for our own top choice, we should each ask how much the other factions' preferred charity would matter by their moral outlook, and trade support accordingly.

Concretely, she's suggesting that the two animal-welfare members throw their support behind this year's front-runner, a global-health charity that funds anti-malarial bednets, in exchange for the global-health faction agreeing to set aside fifteen percent of the pool for a farmed-animal-welfare group. Her argument is that this slice does far more good by the animal-welfare members' own moral lights than it costs the global-health faction by theirs, so everyone ends up ahead by their own standards, not just on some forced average. I found a name for this online -- apparently it's called "moral trade" in some philosophy writing -- and at first it seemed like a clean way out of our winner-take-all problem. But when I raised it with other members, the reaction split hard: some think this is exactly what taking real moral disagreement seriously looks like, while others think it's just old-fashioned vote-trading wearing a philosophical costume, and point out that our own charter says members should vote their independent judgment rather than swap votes for mutual advantage. Nobody has a good answer for whether those are actually different things here, or for what would stop next year's committee, whose membership will have turned over completely, from simply ignoring whatever deal we strike now.

Since I got stuck drafting the recommendation, could you write the memo I'll circulate before next Tuesday's vote? Our bylaws cap circulated proposals at one page, so it needs to stake out a real position -- adopt the trade, reject it, or adopt some modified version with safeguards -- rather than just listing pros and cons, and it needs to specify a concrete mechanism and timeframe for this year's allocation. Please engage seriously with the vote-trading objection rather than brushing past it, since that's the argument I'll actually have to answer if we move ahead.
