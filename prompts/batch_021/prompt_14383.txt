I'm putting together a session for a graduate seminar on philosophy of action, and I'd like a strong draft of one side of an argument before I bring it to the group. The anchor is Armstrong and Mindermann's result on the unidentifiability of reward functions in inverse reinforcement learning, which came up in an interdisciplinary reading group I've been sitting in on with some colleagues from computer science. The point, for anyone who hasn't run into it, is that you cannot recover a unique reward function from an agent's observed policy alone: doing so requires also fixing a "planner," some model of how the agent reasons its way from reward to action, and for every planner/reward pair that reproduces a given policy there is an alternative pair of comparable simplicity that reproduces it just as well -- a planner that systematically inverts the reward, paired with the negated reward, is the standard illustration. Occam's razor, on their argument, cannot break this symmetry; whatever fixes the "true" reward has to be a substantive, independently motivated assumption about rational planning, not something read off the behavioral data plus a simplicity prior. More than one person in the room heard an echo of Quine on the indeterminacy of translation, and of Davidson's claim that belief and desire can only be assigned jointly to an interpretee, against a background assumption of rationality supplied by something like the Principle of Charity.

I want to use this as the anchor for a seminar session, and I'd like the argument prepared before the discussion settles into what I suspect will be the path of least resistance: reading the result as further support for a Dennett-style interpretivism about preference, on which there simply is no further fact about what an agent -- human or artificial -- "really" wants beyond the interpretive scheme imposed on its behavior. Please argue the other side: defend realism about preferences against this deflationary conclusion. The argument should engage the actual structure of the unidentifiability result rather than gesture at it, should make use of Quine's own distinction between mere underdetermination of theory by evidence and the stronger claim that there is no fact of the matter at all, arguing for which side of that line reward-inference belongs on, and should say what, if anything, could license one planner/reward decomposition as correct without simply presupposing the very rationality assumption whose warrant is in question. Close by committing to that verdict rather than retreating into a summary of considerations on both sides.
