Since retiring after thirty-five years in experimental condensed-matter physics, I have taken up two pastimes I would not have expected to intersect: keeping a plot in our neighborhood's community garden, and, mainly to keep my mind from atrophying, auditing an evening seminar on theories of distributive justice at the local university. A dispute that arose at the garden association's last annual meeting has been nagging at me in light of what I have been reading, and I would value your help thinking it through properly, rather than simply being told that both sides have a point.

The association was founded eleven years ago on deliberately egalitarian terms: each of the roughly forty households received an identically sized plot and paid the same modest annual fee into a common fund for water, tools, and compost. Since then, an informal economy has grown up alongside the formal one, entirely off the association's books: members swap cuttings and preserves, trade rare seed stock, and sell surplus produce to one another and, on Saturdays, to passers-by. A few members, some of whom simply arrived already knowing far more about plants than the rest of us, have done extremely well out of this, building up both valuable seed stock and a tidy side income, entirely through long sequences of individual trades and sales that both parties judged worthwhile at the time; nobody was coerced, deceived, or shut out of any deal. At the meeting, several members proposed that, given how far holdings have since diverged from that equal starting point, the successful traders should now pay a share of their gains into the common fund for redistribution; others called this proposal a kind of theft, since nothing improper happened at any individual step along the way.

As a physicist, I am used to systems in which, if every local interaction obeys the relevant conservation laws, nobody thinks to complain about the resulting macroscopic state, but I suspect fairness among people may not work quite so simply, and that is exactly where I have become stuck. Does the fact that this inequality arose entirely through a chain of individually consensual exchanges settle whether the association may now redistribute it, or must something further be true of the starting point, or of the trades themselves, for that to follow? And does it matter, in your assessment, that a few members began already better equipped than the rest of us, even though every plot and every fee was identical?
