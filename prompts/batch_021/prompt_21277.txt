I'm a PhD student in philosophy specializing in formal epistemology, and this term a few of us have been co-running a reading group with some economics students on the value of information. We started with I. J. Good's 1967 result that a Bayesian who updates by conditionalization can never expect to be made worse off by cost-free access to further evidence before she has to act: at worst the evidence leaves her expected utility unchanged, so a rational agent should never refuse a free glance at more data before deciding. This seemed to me to settle, at least in principle, a family of live policy disputes about mandatory disclosure: whether insurers should be allowed to see applicants' genetic test results, whether firms should be forced to release material forecasts earlier, whether regulators should publish economic data as soon as it is collected rather than sitting on it. If a rational Bayesian's expected utility can only rise, or at worst stay flat, when she is handed more free information before choosing, it seemed to follow that any policy that hands agents more information before they act should be weakly good for everyone affected, and that the burden of proof falls entirely on anyone who thinks a specific disclosure requirement could be harmful.

One of the economics students pushed back with what she called "the Hirshleifer effect," which I gather traces to Jack Hirshleifer's 1971 paper on the private and social value of information. She said it's a standard result showing that giving an entire market more information, even for free, can leave everyone worse off, and that this is part of the textbook justification for why some insurance and forecasting markets actually function better when certain information is withheld rather than made public. I couldn't tell whether she was describing a genuine counterexample to Good's theorem, which seems strange for something billed as a proven result, or whether she and I are simply talking past each other because "value of information" means something different in the two arguments. Could you untangle this for me? Specifically: does the Hirshleifer effect actually contradict Good's theorem, and does the policy conclusion I drew from Good's theorem above actually follow from it?
