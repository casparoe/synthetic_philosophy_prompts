In addition to teaching epistemology, I host a philosophy podcast aimed at graduate students and advanced undergraduates, and next week's guest has built her recent book around a strictly non-epistemic reading of the "hinge" propositions in Wittgenstein's On Certainty: on her view, sentences like "I have two hands" or "the earth has existed for a long time" are neither known nor justified nor even properly assessable as rational or irrational, since they form the arational scaffolding that makes doubt and justification possible in the first place, and no further evidence could ever make them more or less secure than they already are. She is admirably consistent about this, and whenever a questioner tries to get her to concede something positive about the epistemic standing of a hinge, she reaches for some version of the same line: "you're asking the hinge to swing, and a hinge that swings has stopped being a hinge."

Please draft nine or ten interview questions, sequenced so that this deflection gets progressively harder for her to reach for. I want at least one question that uses Wittgenstein's own discussion of a community that consults oracles rather than physicists, to press on whether her position can avoid collapsing into a relativism of frameworks that Wittgenstein elsewhere seems keen to resist; at least one that asks her to reconcile "hinges aren't known" with the fact that all our ordinary empirical knowledge is supposed to rest on them, in a way that doesn't just sound like a polite surrender to the skeptic; at least one that brings in Crispin Wright's rival proposal that we hold hinge-type commitments by a kind of non-evidential "entitlement" rather than by no epistemic standing whatsoever, and asks her to justify preferring flat silence to Wright's more generous story; and at least one that uses Wittgenstein's own river-bed image -- on which the hinges themselves can shift over time even though the water's flow depends on the bed staying put for now -- to ask how a rational transition from one set of hinges to the next is even supposed to be possible if hinges are stipulated to sit outside the space of reasons altogether. For each of these four, add a short follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give. Close the sequence with a question asking her to name something, anything, that would count against her reading of Wittgenstein, since so far every objection seems to have been absorbed as one more illustration of it.
