At my philosophy reading group, two members spent an evening arguing about the statue-and-the-clay problem in material constitution, and I'd like a third opinion. The setup: a sculptor shapes a lump of clay into a statue; the lump predates the statue and would survive being squashed back into a ball, while the statue would not. (1) Mara said the statue just is the lump, described in two ways, so talk of 'two things in one place' is bad bookkeeping. (2) Dev objected that this violates Leibniz's Law: if they were identical they would share every property, but the lump survives squashing and the statue doesn't, so there must be two coincident objects rather than one. (3) Mara replied that this overgenerates: a body survives its owner's death, so by the same reasoning no person is ever identical to their body either, which struck her as too high a price to pay for a narrow point about clay. (4) Dev answered that the cases aren't parallel, since body and person diverge in obvious ways (mental life, function) even before death, whereas the lump and statue share literally every particle and arrangement for as long as they coexist, so treating them as two objects distinguished only by which counterfactuals hold of them is a far heavier commitment. (5) Mara answered that Dev's own view owes an equally mysterious explanation: something has to ground why squashing destroys one object and not the other, and saying they fall under 'different sortals' just relabels that mystery instead of resolving it.

Go through the exchange move by move: say who has the better of it at each numbered step, not just who wins overall, and note anything important that both of them are missing. End with a one-line verdict, in exactly this format: \verdict{Mara} or \verdict{Dev}, whichever of them you think comes out ahead overall.
