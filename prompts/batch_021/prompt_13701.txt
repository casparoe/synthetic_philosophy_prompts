And here's the student's answer (typos are theirs, not mine):

"Skolem's paradox is the surprising fact that if ZFC is consistent, then by the Löwenheim–Skolem theorem it has a model M whose domain is only countable, even though ZFC proves theorems like Cantor's theorem that there are uncountable sets, e.g. that the power set of ω is not equinumerous with ω. So M, despite being countable 'from the outside,' still satisfies the sentence 'P(ω) is uncountable' — there's some element of M that plays the role of P(ω), and internally M can't find a bijection between it and ω^M.

This looks contradictory at first: if M only has countably many elements total, how can it correctly think one of it's own sets is uncountable? The resolution has to do with what kind of model M actually is. Models produced this way by the Löwenheim–Skolem construction (say, via the completeness theorem applied to ZFC plus countably many Henkin constants) are typically non-standard: in particular M's version of the natural numbers, ω^M, will usually not be isomorphic to the real ω, but will contain extra 'infinite' elements past all the standard finite ones. Because ω^M is nonstandard, statements about countability inside M don't mean what we'd ordinarily mean by them, and 'countable-in-M' and 'countable-in-reality' can come apart. That's what lets M consistently satisfy 'P(ω)^M is uncountable' while, from our vantage point outside M, we can see that P(ω)^M has only countably many elements and can be paired off with ω. So there's no real contradiction, just two different standards of counting.

More broadly I'd say the paradox shows that 'countable' and 'uncountable' aren't absolute properties of sets at all — they only make sense relative to a model, the same way 'to the left of' only makes sense relative to a point of view. Skolem drew this moral himself, and I think it's basically right: since no first-order theory can pin down a single 'intended' universe of sets up to isomorphism, there's no further fact about whether the continuum is 'really' uncountable over and above what various models say about it. First order set theory just isn't the kind of thing that could settle that."
