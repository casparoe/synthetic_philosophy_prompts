So in my formal epistemology seminar we spent a whole session on Good's theorem — the result that for a Bayesian agent about to make some decision, the expected value of getting to peek at a bit of free evidence first (and then choosing optimally given whatever you see) is never negative. The intuition is basically that whatever you would've done without the evidence is still available as an option after you've seen it, so looking can only weakly help and never hurt, at least in expectation. I buy that completely for a lone decision-maker picking among some fixed menu of actions.

What I'm less sold on is how quickly people turn this into a blanket slogan like "a rational agent should always want more free information, full stop." A few of us in the group chat started throwing out cases that feel like they break this even for perfectly rational people: friends who genuinely don't want to find out their baby's sex even though it wouldn't change a single thing they're doing either way, someone who deliberately avoids getting a solid "read" on the other side mid-negotiation because they know they'll act differently once they have it, and in a way that tips their hand, or a company that steers clear of an internal audit because whatever it turns up could leak and spook a deal that's fine as it stands. Our professor waved all of this away as "just disguised costs, or plain irrationality," which felt a bit too convenient to me.

I want you to actually build the case, as carefully as you can, that Good's theorem's guarentee genuinely does not survive being generalized past the narrow setup it's proven in -- not because the agents involved are irrational or the "free" information secretly has hidden costs, but for principled reasons. Focus especially on two routes: (1) what changes once there's another strategic agent around whose behavior toward you depends on what you know or seem to know, and (2) what changes once an agent's utility isn't purely a function of the action they eventually take, but also depends on their beliefs or anticipatory states along the way. Walk through, concretely, how a fully rational Bayesian who updates correctly and then acts optimally can still end up worse off in expectation for having received genuinely free evidence in cases like these, and try to pin down exactly which premise in Good's original argument stops holding.
