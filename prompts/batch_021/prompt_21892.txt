I've been going down a total rabbit hole reserching Émilie du Châtelet for a piece on women written out of the history of science, the kind of thing where most people (if they know her at all) know her as "the woman who translated Newton" or "Voltaire's mistress who basically did his physics for him," which annoys me because she was doing plenty of original stuff of her own. The thing that peaked my interest is her Discourse on Happiness, which she apparently wrote in the last few years of her life while having an affair with a much younger poet. In it she argues that being happy basically requires cultivating illusions — not seeing through your own vanity, not being brutally honest with yourself about your prospects or how much someone loves you back, leaning hard into your passions (she gets into gambling, romantic obsession, all of it) instead of reasoning your way toward some cool, detached clarity about your life.

What's bugging me is that this is the same woman who spent years painstakingly defending and popularizing Newton's Principia, and who wrote a whole separate treatise trying to reconcile Newtonian physics with Leibnizian metaphysics using the principle of sufficient reason — someone whose day job was basically ruthless devotion to figuring out what's actually, provably true. I brought this up with a friend who does philosophy for a living and she said it's an obvious contradiction, that you can't coherently be that kind of rationalist in your physics and then turn around and claim self-deception is a precondition for a good life. Something about that feels too quick to me though.

Can you make the strongest possible case that there's actually no real inconsistency here — that her position on illusions and passions is fully compatible with, or maybe even demanded by, the same rationalist commitments driving her physics and metaphysics? I'd rather not get a generic "reason and emotion aren't really opposed" answer; I want something that actually grapples with the specifics of her view of happiness and her epistemology, since that's the part I need for the piece.
