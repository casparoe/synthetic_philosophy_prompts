I am preparing the session on contractarian ethics for my graduate seminar on the foundations of justice, and this year I want to present David Gauthier's technical apparatus in *Morals by Agreement* more carefully than I have in the past. As I understand it, his principle of minimax relative concession — on which each bargainer surrenders the same proportion of the distance between the non-cooperative baseline and her own most-favored outcome — is, in the two-party case, formally identical to the Kalai–Smorodinsky bargaining solution: Gauthier's 'claim point' plays the role of the ideal point, and his 'initial bargaining position' plays the role of the disagreement point. Ken Binmore, in *Game Theory and the Social Contract*, is unpersuaded by this apparatus and argues instead for grounding the social contract closer to the Nash bargaining solution, largely on the strength of its non-cooperative foundations in Rubinstein-style alternating-offers bargaining — foundations that no one has supplied nearly as naturally for Kalai–Smorodinsky.

Before I present this contrast to my students, I would like your help thinking it through properly. Please compare the Nash and Kalai–Smorodinsky solutions on the single axiom that divides them once Pareto optimality, symmetry, and scale invariance are held fixed — independence of irrelevant alternatives against monotonicity — and explain what is philosophically, rather than merely mathematically, at stake in trading one for the other when the solution concept is being asked to underwrite an account of what morality requires of us. Say something as well about whether the comparative lack of a clean non-cooperative implementation for Kalai–Smorodinsky should worry a contractarian in Gauthier's position, and about how well any of this survives the move from two-person to many-person bargaining, since Gauthier needs a solution for an entire scheme of cooperation rather than a single pair; my recollection is that the Kalai–Smorodinsky side of the comparison runs into real trouble once there are more than two bargainers, in a way the Nash solution does not, though I'd want you to say exactly what goes wrong. Finally, tell me plainly which solution concept you think Gauthier should have reached for, and whether the choice actually changes any of his substantive conclusions about the content of morality, or merely dresses the same conclusion in different formal clothing.
