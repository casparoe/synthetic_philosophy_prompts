In my metaphysics seminar this week we paired Max Black's 1952 paper "The Identity of Indiscernibles" with the Leibniz-Clarke correspondence. Black asks us to imagine a universe containing nothing but two spheres, perfectly alike in every intrinsic property, situated in an otherwise empty and perfectly symmetric space, so that whatever is true of one sphere, intrinsically or relationally, is equally true of the other; he offers this as a counterexample to Leibniz's principle that no two distinct things can be utterly indiscernible, since here there would be two objects and not one, yet nothing whatsoever to tell them apart. What struck me is that Leibniz's own argument against Newton and Clarke turns on a strikingly similar symmetry: he holds that God could have had no sufficient reason to place the material world at one point in absolute space rather than a few feet over, or to create it one moment rather than another, precisely because the alternatives would be indiscernible from each other -- and from this he concludes that space and time are merely relational, not that both alternatives are somehow equally real. Reasoning strictly from Leibniz's own commitments -- the identity of indiscernibles, the principle of sufficient reason, and his relational theory of space -- how do you think he would have responded if Black's two-sphere scenario were put to him directly, and would that response be consistent with the argument he actually gives Clarke, or does Black's example expose a genuine strain in his system?
