I'm taking a seminar this term on nineteenth-century political thought, and for the final paper we've been asked to trace a philosophical trajectory across a single author's career rather than simply summarize one text. I've chosen to argue that Mill's late writings on socialism amount to more than the cautious hedging that most survey courses attribute to him. Our TA mentioned in section that there's been a wave of recent scholarship pushing back against the standard textbook image of Mill as a classical liberal who merely flirted with cooperative production, and I want my paper to defend a version of that stronger claim -- but I'm worried I'm bending the primary texts to fit a thesis I already like.

Here is the core paragraph of my current draft, which I would like you to look at closely:

"Mill's revisions to the chapter 'On the Probable Futurity of the Labouring Classes' between the first and third editions of the Principles of Political Economy are not merely stylistic. Where the 1848 text treats the eventual replacement of wage labour by cooperative production as a distant and uncertain possibility, the revised chapter names it as the more probable and more desirable future, pointing to the early cooperative workshops in France and England as evidence that labourers can act as their own capitalists. The unfinished Chapters on Socialism, written some two decades later, only deepens this commitment: Mill concedes that socialists are largely right that the existing distribution of property owes more to historical force and legal privilege than to desert, and he treats the objections he does raise -- about incentives, about population, about the danger of a single centralized employer -- not as principled objections to socialism as such but as empirical cautions about sequencing and scale. On this reading, Mill's famous insistence that socialism be settled by experiment rather than by a priori argument is not a liberal's polite way of shelving the question indefinitely; it is the position of someone who expects the experiments to succeed."

What I can't tell is whether the paragraph is doing something illegitimate: it leans on the same two moves -- the "this is merely an empirical question" line and the population-and-incentives worries -- to do two different jobs depending on which way I want the argument to go, and I can't decide whether that is a real tension in Mill himself or just sloppiness in my draft. Could you comment on whether this reading holds up against what Mill actually argues in those two texts, on whether I'm eliding a distinction I shouldn't be, and on whether the paragraph is more persuasive or more overreaching than I currently think it is? I'd also welcome a note on which single sentence is doing the most, and which the least, argumentative work.
