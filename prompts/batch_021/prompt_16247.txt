I'm a PhD student in philosophy currently drafting a dissertation chapter on the principle of sufficient reason, and I've been asked to prepare the opening round of questions for a work-in-progress colloquium our department is hosting next month. The visiting speaker defends a broadly Leibnizian/Thomistic cosmological argument from contingency -- that the totality of contingent facts cannot explain itself and therefore requires a necessary being as its explanation -- while also holding to classical divine simplicity, on which there is no real distinction between God's essence, existence, intellect, and act of will. Crucially, the speaker insists that God's creating this particular contingent universe, rather than some other one or none at all, is still a genuinely free (in a libertarian sense) and contingent act on God's part. I'd like the Q&A to move past a restatement of the argument's premises and into the places where exactly this combination of commitments has actually been pressed in the literature.

Could you draft eight to ten questions along those lines, with each one anticipating the speaker's likely reply and adding a short follow-up keyed to it? I want at least one question revisiting the old "taxicab" charge against the principle of sufficient reason -- that the theist rides the principle only as far as a necessary being and then dismisses it rather than asking what explains that being's own existence -- pressing on whether treating a necessary being as self-explanatory or exempt from the demand is principled or just a stipulation to stop the regress at a convenient place. I want at least one that puts the modal collapse worry to the speaker directly: if God's act of willing the world is strictly identical to God's necessary essence, how can what is willed -- this cosmos rather than some other, or none -- fail to be necessary too, and doesn't that quietly erase the distinction between necessary and contingent being that the contingency of the cosmos was supposed to rest on in the first place?

Finally, I want at least one question testing whether the standard escape routes actually solve the problem rather than just redescribing it -- for instance, relocating the contingency into the creature's relation of dependence rather than into any distinct element within God, or appealing to something like a Scotist "synchronic contingency" that doesn't require temporal succession between alternatives. Please close the set with a question asking what specific consideration, if any, would lead the speaker to abandon either divine simplicity or the claim that creation is genuinely contingent.
