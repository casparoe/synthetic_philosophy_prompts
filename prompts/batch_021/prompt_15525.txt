"Epistocracy is the view that political power should be distributed according to competence rather than shared equally among all citizens, as in democracy. The classic version is Plato's proposal in the Republic that philosopher-kings, who alone grasp the Form of the Good, should rule. Contemporary defenders such as Jason Brennan revive the idea, arguing that most voters are 'hobbits' (apathetic about politics) or 'hooligans' (tribal partisans) rather than well-informed 'vulcans,' so political power should be weighted or restricted accordingly, for example through competence tests, plural voting, or an epistocratic council empowered to veto bad legislation. John Stuart Mill made a related move, restricting the franchise to the educated on the grounds that knowledge, not raw numbers, should decide policy.

David Estlund, who coined the term 'epistocracy' in his work on democratic authority, argues that political questions typically have correct answers and that some citizens are demonstrably better than others at finding them, so legitimate authority should track that expertise rather than sheer numbers.
