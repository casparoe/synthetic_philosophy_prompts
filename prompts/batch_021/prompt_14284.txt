Three months ago I started sitting in on a weekly reading group on Nyāya epistemology, led by a retired professor of Sanskrit at my university, after my own doctoral work on internalism and externalism in mainstream analytic epistemology hit a wall and my advisor suggested I look at how classical Indian philosophers handled a structurally similar problem. We have spent the last two sessions stuck on the dispute between the Naiyāyikas and Kumārila Bhaṭṭa's Mīmāṃsā school over what grounds the validity, or prāmāṇya, of a cognition (jñāna). The Naiyāyikas -- their view worked out most fully by Gaṅgeśa in the fourteenth century -- hold parataḥ-prāmāṇya: a cognition's validity, like its invalidity, must be certified by some further cognition, typically an inference from the cognition's practical success (saṃvāda) or from the ascertained presence of the right causal excellences (guṇa) and absence of defects (doṣa) in the conditions that produced it. Kumārila's school holds the opposite, svataḥ-prāmāṇya: every cognition is valid by default, simply in virtue of arising as a cognition, and only invalidity needs to be established by something extrinsic, such as a later cognition that contradicts it. Each side presses a stock objection against the other: the Mīmāṃsakas accuse the Naiyāyikas of an infinite regress (if cognition C1's validity needs a certifying cognition C2, what certifies C2?), while the Naiyāyikas accuse the Mīmāṃsakas of being unable to explain how error is even possible, or why anyone would ever bother checking a cognition, if validity is simply the default, self-certifying status of any awareness whatsoever.

My reading partner, who comes from a family with a long line of Mīmāṃsā scholars, is adamant that svataḥ-prāmāṇya is obviously correct and that the regress objection is a bad argument; I lean the other way but keep losing the thread of why whenever we argue about it over coffee afterward. Rather than let the disagreement sit unresolved until next week's session, I would like a piece of writing I can bring in that actually settles the matter for me rather than just restating the standoff. Please write an essay that lays out the strongest version of each side's positive case together with its best response to the other side's signature objection -- including how the rival khyāti-vāda theories of illusion bear on the Naiyāyikas' error objection to svataḥ-prāmāṇya -- and then commits to a verdict rather than leaving the two positions in equipoise. If you think the truth is some hybrid or asymmetric position (for instance, agreeing with Kumārila about validity but with the Naiyāyikas about invalidity, or vice versa), that is a permissible verdict, but only if you argue for it rather than simply noting it as a possibility.
