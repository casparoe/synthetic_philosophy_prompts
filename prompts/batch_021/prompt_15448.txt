My closest friend from college is getting married in the spring, and she has asked me, rather than any of her more eloquent relatives, to give the toast. I have no formal training in philosophy, but for a few years now I have been working through old thought experiments on my own time, largely because she introduced me to them back when we were students staying up far too late arguing about free will. The one I have never quite been able to put down is Gregory Kavka's toxin puzzle: the story of the eccentric benefactor who will pay you a fortune the moment you sincerely intend, by midnight, to drink a foul but harmless toxin the following afternoon, with no further requirement that you actually go through with it once the money has been paid. I have come to think this puzzle is secretly about wedding vows. A person stands up at the one moment in life when they have every possible incentive to promise "until death do us part," and is then expected to remain bound by that promise for decades after the incentives of the day itself — the guests, the ceremony, the euphoria — have long since been spent.

I would like you to write this toast for me. It should run to about four or five minutes spoken aloud, for a room in which perhaps two or three guests will recognize the reference and everyone else will need to be brought up to speed on the puzzle as the toast unfolds, ideally without it ever feeling like a lecture. What matters most to me, though, is that the toast not simply land the joke and move past it: I want it to take a real position on the hard part of the puzzle, which is whether a wedding vow can be a sincere intention in Kavka's sense at all, given that the vow's "payment" — the wedding itself, the day's rush of feeling — arrives up front, while the cost of honoring it falls due for the rest of both their lives. Does that structure quietly condemn every vow anyone has ever made, in the same way it seems to condemn the toxin-drinker's midnight resolution, or is there something about how a real marriage generates its own reasons for keeping faith as it goes along, so that the analogy breaks down after all? I would like the toast to arrive somewhere genuinely warm and persuasive on that question, rather than merely clever.
