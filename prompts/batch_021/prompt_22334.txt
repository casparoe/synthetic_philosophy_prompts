I sit on the investment committee of a small angel-investing syndicate — an unusual sideline for someone in my line of work, but a rewarding one — and a disagreement that surfaced at our meeting last month has stayed with me, partly because it proved surprisingly hard to referee. We were deciding whether to fund a single early-stage diagnostics company. Our lead analyst had built a model, trained on the recorded outcomes of several thousand previously funded startups sharing similar characteristics (funding stage, sector, team composition, and so on), which produced a headline figure: this particular company has a "23 percent chance of reaching profitability within seven years." One committee member, a semi-retired statistician, objected that the figure is close to meaningless as a description of this venture: we will only ever fund this exact company once; it will either become profitable or it will not; and there is no experiment being repeated here whose outcomes, over the long run, we could be tallying. On her view, all the model has really told us is a fact about a different population of other companies, dressed up as though it were a fact about this one. The analyst answered that if her objection were sound, it would equally discredit the risk score a surgeon quotes an individual patient before an operation, and the default probability a rating agency assigns to a bond that will only ever be issued once — cases where we routinely, and seemingly reasonably, treat a number derived from many past cases as informative about a single, unrepeated one.

I raised the case in my graduate seminar the following week, half expecting the students to converge quickly on a verdict; instead, they split along almost exactly the same lines as the committee had, and I found I could not adjudicate between them either, which is a rare enough experience that it has stayed with me since. I would be grateful for your help thinking through what is really at stake between the statistician and the analyst, and whether either of them can be shown to have the better argument, or whether they are simply operating with two different, equally defensible notions of what a figure like "23 percent" means when it is attached to a one-off case like this.
