I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and next week I'm interviewing a philosopher for a feature on the moral status of digital minds. She defends computational functionalism about moral status: on her view, what fixes whether a system deserves moral consideration is the abstract pattern of computation it realizes, not the material that pattern runs on, so a sufficiently sophisticated AI could in principle matter exactly as much as a person does. Her standard answer to the obvious trivialization worry -- that almost any sufficiently complex physical process can be redescribed, under some mapping, as implementing almost any computation, so that a wall or a rock could technically qualify too -- is that "matter doesn't hand out implementations for free": a mapping only counts, on her view, if the system's causal structure would have produced correspondingly different states had the computation's formal states been different, and ordinary inanimate objects fail that counterfactual test.

Draft eight to ten interview questions that don't just let her restate this answer. I want at least one question on the intuitive force of the trivialization worry itself and on why a fix custom-built to keep rocks out and brains in doesn't just look like an ad hoc patch, at least one on whether that same fix quietly smuggles a biology-shaped bias into a position that's supposed to be substrate-neutral, and at least one on how, given that any physical system admits multiple competing computational descriptions, we could ever check from the outside which computation a system such as a large language model actually implements in her strict sense. For each of these three, add a follow-up question keyed to the answer she's most likely to give. Close with a question asking what observation or argument would actually count as evidence against her view.
