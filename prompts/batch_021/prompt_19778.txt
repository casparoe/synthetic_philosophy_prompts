And here is the student's answer, reproduced as submitted apart from fixing a couple of typos:

(a) Hom's Combinatorial Externalism holds that a slur's semantic content is not merely expressive but a complex, truth-conditional property whose satisfaction conditions are fixed externally by the discriminatory institutions and practices of the community that uses the term -- roughly, something like "ought to be treated adversely in virtue of possessing trait T, for reasons endorsed by racist practice P." The trouble is that if this is really the literal content, then whenever a slurring predication of some individual is true, it looks like the racist practice's own normative claim about that individual must also be true, which seems to make competent speakers who felicitously use the term implicitly committed to a racist claim being correct of somebody. Hom and May respond by arguing that the relevant property, because it is partly constituted by the discriminatory practice's own (false) normative claims, can never actually be satisfied by anyone: nobody really "ought" to be treated adversely for the reasons the practice specifies, so the property has a null extension across the board. Consequently, every simple slurring predication is false, and speakers who use such sentences are never thereby committed to any true racist claim, because the sentence just doesn't correctly describe anyone. This secures a kind of semantic innocence for ordinary speakers even though the term remains, on this view, genuinely offensive to use.

(b) Prohibitionism generalizes this innocence result and gives it a cleaner explanation. On Anderson and Lepore's view, slurs are simply prohibited words: the community stipulates a taboo on uttering the word itself, and the resulting offense has nothing to do with what the word contributes to what is said. Since the slur's content is null no matter what, its offensiveness cannot be located in that content -- so instead Anderson and Lepore locate the prohibition directly in the term's emptiness, treating the word as a kind of prohibited empty name whose lack of genuine semantic value is exactly why communities have had to legislate against its use by fiat rather than rely on its meaning to do the work. This lets Prohibitionism explain data that trips up content-based theories, such as the fact that merely mentioning a slur -- not using it to describe anyone -- is still offensive: a purely semantic theory has trouble with this, because on the standard picture mentioning a word doesn't activate what it refers to or expresses, yet the offense is present anyway.

(c) The derogatory component survives negation ("She's not Boche" still conveys something derogatory about Germans) and, relatedly, cannot be cancelled by a disclaimer like "no offense, but...". Both facts are used to argue that this component is not part of the ordinary at-issue content of the sentence (otherwise negation should be able to target it) but is instead conventionally triggered by the word itself, comparable to Potts's conventional implicatures for expressives like "damn." Since this derogatory element is nonetheless a kind of implicature rather than part of what is strictly said, we should expect it, like Gricean implicatures generally, to be cancellable in principle -- a speaker could add something like "but I don't intend any disrespect" to try to cancel it -- which shows that even though the content is conventionally tied to the word, it remains, in the relevant sense, detachable from the word's core meaning.
