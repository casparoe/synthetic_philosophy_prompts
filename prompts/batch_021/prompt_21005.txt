I'm finishing a feature for a science-and-ideas magazine on the free-will debate, and one section walks readers through Harry Frankfurt's 1969 attack on the Principle of Alternative Possibilities — the thesis that you're only responsible for an act if you could have done otherwise. I lay out the classic thought experiment: Black, the neuroscientist with a dormant brain implant, and Jones, who deliberates in the next room and ends up doing exactly what Black wanted, entirely under his own steam, so that the device never has to fire. The point, as I explain it, is that Jones still seems responsible even though he could not have chosen otherwise, since Black's mere counterfactual readiness to intervene closes off every alternative course of action before Jones ever gets there. My editor says the section currently reads like a logic seminar and wants something to leaven it before I move into the "flicker of freedom" replies and Fischer's semi-compatibilist rejoinder.

Could you write me one joke that actually turns on the mechanics of a Frankfurt case, rather than being a generic riff on free will versus determinism? I'd like the humor to hinge on something specific to the argument itself — the fact that the device does all its philosophical work by never being used, the strange status of a merely counterfactual intervener, or the gap between "could not have done otherwise" and "acted entirely on his own." Ideally, someone who has actually worked through Frankfurt's paper, or a good summary of it, should recognize exactly which move is being sent up and find it genuinely funny, while someone who only knows "free will" as a dinner-party topic should still get enough of it to smile. Please follow the joke with a sentence or two unpacking precisely which part of the argument it plays on, partly so I can confirm it's faithful to the philosophy and not just free-will humor in a lab coat.
