Last night a friend and I spent the better part of two hours arguing about superrationality without either of us budging an inch, and I keep turning the disagreement over in my head. The idea, which traces back to Douglas Hofstadter, is that sufficiently similar rational agents facing a perfectly symmetric prisoner's dilemma should cooperate, not because one agent's choice causally affects the other's, but because their reasoning processes are so alike that whichever conclusion one of them reaches, the other reaches too. In effect, this makes mutual cooperation and mutual defection the only real options on the table. My friend, who thinks in fairly orthodox decision-theoretic terms, insisted that this is evidential decision theory in a new outfit, and that once there is no causal channel between the two choices, ordinary dominance reasoning settles things: defection does at least as well as cooperation no matter what the other player does, so a rational agent defects regardless. I countered that treating the two choices as independent inputs to a dominance argument already assumes what is in question, given that the entire premise of the thought experiment is that both choices are outputs of the very same underlying computation.

Since we couldn't resolve it between the two of us, I would like to see the argument played out properly by two people who can each represent their side at full strength. Please write a dialogue between a committed causal decision theorist and a committed superrationalist, set in the moments before each must privately and simultaneously choose to cooperate or defect in a one-shot prisoner's dilemma against the other. The two are stipulated to be perfect psychological twins (or, if you would rather, two separate instantiations of the same decision algorithm), with common knowledge of that fact and no possibility of communication. Have each side genuinely engage with the other's strongest objections rather than simply restating their own starting intuition: the causal decision theorist should press the dominance argument and perhaps invoke something like the smoking-lesion case to suggest that "subjunctive dependence" is just correlation wearing a fancier name, while the superrationalist should have to explain what, if anything, actually distinguishes the twin scenario from mere correlation between two independently varying agents. Neither character should be allowed to win simply by getting the last word; I would like the disagreement to feel as genuinely unresolved on the page as it did between the two of us.
