I'm a PhD student in cognitive science working at the boundary of philosophy of language and NLP, and a question raised in a reading group on Kripke's Wittgenstein on Rules and Private Language has stuck with me. Kripke's Wittgenstein argues that no fact about a speaker's past behavior or dispositions can settle whether, by "+", she has meant plus rather than some deviant quus-like function that agrees with plus on every case she has actually computed and diverges beyond it. His skeptical solution does not recover truth conditions for meaning facts; instead, on the model of Hume on causation, it grounds talk of correct and incorrect use entirely in a community's practice of endorsing and correcting its members' performances -- a resource that, on his own private-language argument, is unavailable to an isolated individual.

Now consider a modern large language model: trained purely on the aggregated linguistic output of a community it was never a participant in, and possessing nothing that looks like a disposition beyond a set of learned statistical weights. Such systems are well known to compute reliably on sums resembling their training distribution and to drift in less predictable ways beyond it -- arguably close to the exact scenario the plus/quus argument turns on. Fine-tuning on human feedback afterward superficially resembles the community's corrective role without obviously being it.

Arguing strictly from the resources Kripkenstein allows himself -- no appeal to intrinsic meaning facts, no covert mentalism -- what verdict do you think he would reach on whether such a model can be said to follow the addition rule at all, and why? I would rather you commit to a specific verdict and defend it than simply survey the possibilities.
