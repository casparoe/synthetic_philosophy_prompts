I am reporting on the philosophical case for longtermism, and in the course of my research I have come across the literature on so-called "infinite ethics." As Nick Bostrom and others have argued, once a population — or a stream of welfare spread across time — is allowed to be genuinely infinite, the standard utilitarian procedure of summing welfare to rank outcomes stops being well-defined, since such sums are typically only conditionally convergent: merely reordering the very same gains and losses, say by time rather than by person, can change the total, or even make a change that improves every single individual's welfare look neutral or worse in the aggregate. Given that longtermism's central argument rests on comparing actions by their expected effect on the total welfare of a future population that may turn out to be vast or even unbounded, please describe what you take to be the most important implications of this aggregation problem for that argument, and whether it points toward a technical fix or toward something longtermists cannot easily patch around.
