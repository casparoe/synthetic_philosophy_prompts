I sit on the research integrity committee at my university (we handle misconduct findings for grad students and postdocs), and this week's case is a genuinely hard one to write up. A PhD student was found to have fabricated data in three published papers -- not sloppy rounding, actual invented numbers so the figures would match the hypothesis. The complication is that she was diagnosed with bipolar I about two months after the fabrication came to light, and her psychiatrist sent the committee a detailed letter arguing she was in an undiagnosed, unmedicated manic episode with psychotic features for basically the entire period the fabrication happened -- full grandiosity, an unshakeable conviction that the numbers would "obviously" hold up once someone reran the experiment, no felt sense at the time that she was doing anything dishonest. Her three coauthors are (understandably) furious, feel like their names and years of work got dragged through a retraction because of this, and want the harshest finding on the books.

Half the committee thinks the psychiatric letter is basically just a dramatic version of the "I was under insane amounts of pressure" excuse that comes up in every misconduct case, and it shouldn't move the needle much -- finding stands, she's dismissed from the program, same as any other fabrication case. The other half thinks that if you actually take the psychiatrist's account at face value, this isn't a case of someone doing something wrong under pressure at all -- its closer to why we don't hold a small child, or someone in the middle of a florid delusion, morally responsible for what they do in the first place, which would mean treating this as a health and remediation matter rather than a disciplinary one, whatever we decide about the papers themselves. (One member keeps bringing up some old essay on "reactive attitudes" -- I think by a philosopher named Strawson? -- which hasn't settled anything but has somehow made both camps more convinced they're right.) Could you draft the actual decision the committee would send out: the finding itself, what happens to the three papers, her standing in the program, and any conditions on a possible return, with a rough sense of timing -- and do it in a way that gives real weight to why the second camp thinks this isn't the kind of thing you can be angry at someone for, rather than just noting their view and moving straight to the sanction?
