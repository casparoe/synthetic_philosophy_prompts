Over the last year or so I've spent a fair amount of my spare time reading around the automation and universal basic income debates. That eventually led me to sit down and read the 1930 essay that people trace the "fifteen-hour work week" idea back to: John Maynard Keynes's "Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren." I'd assumed it was mostly an economic forecast, so I was surprised at how much of it is actually about the psychological aftermath of getting what we wished for. Keynes predicts that within a century we'll be several times richer and that work will shrink to only a few hours a day so what remains can be shared out more widely (he even has a memorable image for it: wanting to "spread the bread thin on the butter"). He then worries that people who've had the instinct to strive bred into them for generation after generation might suffer something close to a collective nervous breakdown once that instinct has nothing left to press against.

What struck me even more, once I dug a bit further, is that Keynes had already worked out, decades earlier as a young man under G.E. Moore's influence, what he thought the good life would actually consist of once scarcity stopped dictating everyone's days: a kind of private, secular religion in which nothing really matters except certain states of mind, chiefly love, the contemplation of beauty, and knowledge pursued for its own sake. Tellingly, in a later memoir called "My Early Beliefs," he admitted that this whole outlook had rested on a naively optimistic picture of human nature, one that left out how irrational people actually are, himself included. So his full answer to what we should do once scarcity ends turns out to be two claims stacked on top of each other, one economic and one deeply personal — and later in life he seems to have half-doubted the second one himself.

I'd like to see this turned into a short story rather than an essay. Could you write one set some generations into the future, in a society where Keynes's economic prediction has essentially come true and material want is gone for most people, following a character (or a couple of characters) who is genuinely up against the problem he described, rather than a generic post-scarcity utopia or dystopia? I'd especially like it to sit with the specific texture of his worry: needs that are "absolute" being fully satisfied while the status-driven, comparative ones stubbornly refuse to disappear; purposelessness curdling into something like the breakdown he feared; and his own Moore-inspired answer of love, beauty, and contemplation being quietly tested against his later doubts about whether ordinary, imperfectly rational people could actually live that way. It doesn't need to resolve the tension neatly by the end — I'd rather it feel genuinely unresolved than tidy.
