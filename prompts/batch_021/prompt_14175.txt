I have no formal background in philosophy or theology, but over the past year I've spent a good deal of my spare time reading about the relationship between divine providence and human freedom, ever since an argument with a religious friend got me interested in whether God could know in advance what someone would freely do in circumstances that never actually come about. That question led me to Luis de Molina's idea of "middle knowledge," then to Alfred Freddoso's introduction to his translation of the Concordia, and then to Thomas Flint's book defending the position -- both of which kept gesturing back at the older Bañezian objections raised during the Congregatio de Auxiliis controversy, objections that the popular Calvinism-versus-Arminianism-versus-Molinism explainers I'd read online never really engage with. I would like to see that older, more technical dispute staged properly rather than summarized.

Could you write me a dialogue between a contemporary Molinist and a contemporary Bañezian Thomist, each defending their own account of how God can providentially govern free creatures without destroying their freedom? I would like the Thomist to open by pressing the grounding objection in its strongest form -- not simply asserting that counterfactuals of creaturely freedom are "ungrounded," but forcing the Molinist to say what, if anything, could make it true that a possible person would freely do one thing rather than another in circumstances that never obtain, prior to any divine decree and with no actual exercise of will to point to. I would like the Molinist to give a real reply drawn from the literature -- for instance, denying that every truth needs a truthmaker in the relevant sense, or arguing that the counterfactual is explanatorily rather than causally posterior to the choice itself -- rather than a placeholder answer. Then I would like the Thomist to come back with the complaint that even granting the counterfactuals are somehow true, their truth-values lie outside God's control, so his supposedly "provident" use of them looks more like good fortune than genuine governance, and I would like the Molinist to answer that without simply repeating the first move. Finally, I would like the Molinist to turn the tables and press the Thomist on whether physical premotion secures libertarian freedom any better than ordinary determinism does, and I would like the Thomist to have a real answer, drawing on the distinction between sufficient and efficacious grace and the idea that premotion moves a free cause "in a manner" suited to its nature, rather than simply conceding the point. Please keep the exchange a genuine back-and-forth in which each side responds to the specific thing the other has just said, and don't resolve it artificially -- it's fine if the dialogue ends with both sides still owing the other a further answer.
