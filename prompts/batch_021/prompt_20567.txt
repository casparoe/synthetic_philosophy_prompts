My younger sister Sarah is getting married in a few weeks to her fiancée Jo, and I somehow got voted toast-giver, which is funny because I write novels for a living and am apparently much better at inventing the interior lives of made-up people than saying five sincere minutes as myself. The complication is that Sarah did her whole PhD on Confucian role ethics, the Roger Ames/Henry Rosemont version where the claim isn't just "family duty matters too" -- its that there's no separate self underneath the roles to begin with, you're constituted by them, and treating that as some hierarchy bolted onto free individuals already misreads the whole tradition. She's lovely about it ninety percent of the time, but she will visibly wince if I do the standard toast move of "never lose who you really are in this marriage, always stay true to yourself," because she's explained to me, unprompted, at more than one family dinner, exactly why that sentence is confused rather than sweet.

So I don't want to write around the philosophy, I want to actually use it, without turning the toast into a seminar with a ring exchange bolted on -- half the room is Jo's family, who are wonderful and have never heard of Confucius's translators and just want to laugh and tear up a normal amount. Could you draft the actual toast, something that runs three to five minutes read out loud? I keep thinking there's real material in that line about harmony not being the same thing as sameness (he-something-tong, I'm butchering the pinyin, sorry) as a much better wedding sentiment than "two become one," and in the "rectifying names" idea, that being a wife isn't a box you check once at the altar but a name you have to keep making true every day after. If you can also gently land that the old five-relationships version of this was pretty unequal and gendered and these two are knowingly keeping the bones while rebuilding the house, without it curdling into a grievance or a footnote, even better.

Basically the two or three philosophers in the room should feel like I actually got it right, and my grandmother, who could not pick Confucius out of a lineup, should just think it was a lovely speech about two people who love each other.
