I sit in on a philosophy of mind reading group that has spent the last few weeks working through causal and teleological theories of mental content, moving from Dretske's early informational semantics to Millikan's proper-function account, which was developed partly in response to problems with theories like Dretske's. At the most recent session, two of the regular members, Owen and Fatima, got into a disagreement that the rest of the group never managed to resolve. The case at issue was the standard illustrative one about frogs: a frog has a neural mechanism that fires whenever a small, dark object moves erratically across its visual field, which is why it snaps reflexively not only at flies but also at BB pellets flicked past it in a lab. The question on the table was what that mechanism actually represents, and whether appealing to its evolutionary function, rather than just to what it causally correlates with, can settle this.

Owen argued that Millikan's account straightforwardly solves the disjunction problem here: the mechanism's content is "fly," not "fly-or-BB-pellet" or "small dark moving thing," because its proper function is to catch flies, so firing at a BB pellet counts as a malfunction relative to that function rather than an accurate detection of something disjunctive -- which is exactly the room for error that purely causal covariation theories were criticized for lacking. Fatima objected that this doesn't actually settle anything: in the frog's ancestral environment, being a fly and being a small-dark-thing-moving-erratically were near enough coextensive, so nothing in the selection history favors describing the content as "fly" rather than the more general property, and Owen has simply asserted a fix rather than earned one. Owen replied by invoking the rest of Millikan's apparatus: what does the work isn't bare correlation but the "Normal explanation" of why the mechanism was selected, and that explanation runs through nutrition -- the trait proliferated because responding this way got the frog fed on flies, and nutritional value is a property of flies rather than of small dark moving things in general, so the tie is broken after all. Fatima's rejoinder was that this move only relocates the problem: frogs use the very same snapping mechanism to catch plenty of prey besides flies, so "led to eating something nutritious" picks out "small edible moving thing" just as well as it picks out "fly," and Owen still hasn't identified anything in the mechanism's actual selection history, as opposed to our own explanatory habits, that privileges the narrower content.

Neither side budged, and I don't know this literature well enough to referee it myself. Could you go through the exchange move by move, saying for each of the four moves whether it succeeds on its own terms and why, rather than just declaring an overall winner? I'd also like to know what you think the exchange as a whole shows: whether the disjunction problem has actually been solved for a case like this, merely relocated, or something in between -- and whether there is a consideration neither Owen nor Fatima raised that would help settle it.
