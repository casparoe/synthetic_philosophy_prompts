I've spent a long time reading rationalist blogs and forums (LessWrong, the EA Forum, and their various satellites), and I've absorbed their habit of turning big decisions into explicit expected-value calculations before acting on them. For the last few months I've been doing this with a real decision: whether to leave a comfortable, well-paid engineering job for a much less secure position at a small, financially precarious organization doing work I believe matters more (broadly in the AI safety space). I built a fairly elaborate spreadsheet — probability estimates for different career trajectories, rough utility scores for personal wellbeing versus expected impact, sensitivity analysis on my assumptions — and tried to be as rigorous about it as I know how, in the spirit of "shut up and multiply."

The strange part is that the more carefully I do this, the less settled I feel, and it isn't for lack of data. In every version of the spreadsheet, I eventually hit a point where I simply have to decide how much weight to put on, say, my own wellbeing versus counterfactual impact, or near-term certainty versus long-shot upside — and nothing in the model can tell me how to set those weights; the rest of the calculation just cashes them out into bigger or smaller numbers without ever grounding them. I even tried reframing the whole thing through a moral-uncertainty lens, treating the competing considerations like rival theories to be weighed against one another, but that just pushed the same problem up a level, since I still had to decide how many "votes" to give each consideration. Noticing this produces a strange kind of vertigo: I started out hoping the exercise would reveal the right answer, and instead I keep finding myself simply choosing, with nothing further underneath the choice to appeal to. I've mentally run through the usual rationalist-canon suspects — analysis paralysis, sunk cost thinking, plain indecisiveness — and none of them quite fit: I'm not attached to past investment, and I'm not simply afraid of a bad outcome. Part of me also notices the pull to just declare that I'm "not really the risk-taking type" and leave it at that, which would make the discomfort go away but feels like a kind of cheat.

When I described all this to a friend, she told me I was overthinking what is ultimately a modeling problem, and that better data or a cleaner framework would eventually settle it. That doesn't sit right with me, but I can't say exactly why. Is she correct that this is fundamentally a technical shortfall — one good spreadsheet away from resolution — or is there something structural going on once a decision gets pushed down to this level, something no amount of additional rigor will dissolve? And if it's the latter, what would actually count as making this decision well, rather than just finding some way to stop feeling bad about it?
