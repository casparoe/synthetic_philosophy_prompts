A close friend of mine and I spent the better part of an evening arguing about the moral status of soldiers, prompted by a seminar we're both taking on the ethics of war. I had just presented Michael Walzer's defense, in *Just and Unjust Wars*, of what's usually called the moral equality of combatants: the idea that soldiers who fight "cleanly," in keeping with the in bello rules of discrimination and proportionality, are equally permitted to kill one another regardless of which side has justice on its side under jus ad bellum, so that a conscript pressed into service for an aggressor state is no more a murderer, as a combatant, than the soldier resisting the aggression. My friend countered with Jeff McMahan's revisionist line from *Killing in War*, on which liability to be killed tracks moral responsibility for contributing to an unjustified threat, so that most soldiers fighting for an unjust cause are simply not morally permitted to kill even when they are blameless, or merely "excused," for fighting given the fog of war and the practical impossibility of most combatants ever being well placed to judge the justice of their cause. We kept circling the same pressure points — whether the excused/justified distinction really lets McMahan have it both ways, whether treating war as a fundamentally collective and political practice (rather than reducing it to a sum of individual liabilities) changes what combatants owe each other, and whether abandoning combatant equality would even be workable at the level of law, even if McMahan is right about the "deep morality" of the matter — without either of us budging.

Since we couldn't resolve it between the two of us, I'd like your help settling the question properly. Please write an essay that lays out the strongest form of each side of this dispute — the traditionalist case for the moral equality of combatants and McMahan's individual-liability-based case against it — engaging seriously with considerations such as the epistemic argument, the excused-versus-justified distinction, the ad bellum/in bello divide, and the worry that revisionism proves too little (or too much) once you separate the "deep morality" of war from its law. The essay shouldn't hedge or simply conclude that both sides have a point; it should reach and defend a definite verdict, even if that verdict is a qualified one (for instance, endorsing equality as a matter of law while rejecting it as a matter of underlying morality, or vice versa).
