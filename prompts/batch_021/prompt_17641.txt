I'm a PhD student in philosophy, and I've been asked to give the toast at the wedding of my closest friend from the program, who is finishing a dissertation on G. E. Moore, and her fiancé, who works in finance and has never read a word of philosophy. Roughly half the room will be our department, who will catch it immediately if I get Moore wrong; the other half is family and old friends who have never heard the phrase "organic unity."

She has told me, half as a joke and half not, that the account of personal affection in the final chapter of Principia Ethica is the closest thing she has to a philosophy of her own marriage. Moore's two paradigm cases of intrinsic value there are the appreciation of what is beautiful and personal affection between people, and both, on his view, are organic unities of appropriate emotion together with a true, accurate cognition of their object — so love grounded in a flattering or imagined picture of the beloved is worth less, not more, than love grounded in an accurate view of a person's actual qualities. Ordinary toast hyperbole, on this view, would cheapen the very thing it's trying to celebrate.

Write the toast. It has to praise the couple using specific, real, defensible claims about who they actually are rather than superlatives, and it has to quietly defuse — without turning into a seminar — the obvious worry that grounding love's value in a person's particular qualities makes that love conditional on those qualities lasting. Five minutes, warm, and something the philosophers in the room can recognize without anyone else noticing they're doing it.
