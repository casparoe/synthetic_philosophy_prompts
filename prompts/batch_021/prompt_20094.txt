For a jurisprudence seminar I'm taking this term, we just finished the unit on Hart and Fuller, which has easily been the most interesting stretch of the course, though also the part where I trust my own judgment least. As a class exercise, our professor had us pair off and grade a partner's short-answer response to a question on the Hart–Fuller exchange, using a rubric she'll later compare against her own; each of us has to turn in a numeric score out of 20 plus written comments justifying it. Since I'd rather not be unfair to my partner in either direction, I want a careful, independent read of the answer before I commit to a score and comments of my own.

The question she set was: "H.L.A. Hart and Lon Fuller disagree about how a legal system should treat the Nazi-era 'grudge informer' case, in which a wife denounced her husband to the authorities for making disparaging remarks about Hitler while he was home on leave from the army, resulting in his conviction under wartime statutes; after the war, a German court had to decide whether to convict the wife of unlawfully depriving her husband of his liberty. In about 400 words, explain (a) how Hart's and Fuller's proposed treatments of this case differ, and (b) how each treatment follows from that theorist's broader jurisprudential commitments."

My partner's answer reads: "Hart and Fuller agree that the husband in this case was convicted under a Nazi statute that we would now regard as monstrous, but they disagree sharply about what a postwar court should do with that fact. Hart, as a legal positivist, insists on the separability thesis: whether a rule counts as law is one question, and whether it is a good or just law is a separate one. On his view, the statute under which the husband was condemned really was valid law at the time, however wicked it was, because it had been enacted by the recognized lawmaking authority of the German state. If the wife is to be punished, then, it should not be done by pretending the statute was never law in the first place, since that blurs the very distinction between law and morality that positivism exists to preserve. Instead, Hart recommends that the postwar legislature pass a frankly retroactive statute openly punishing the wife for what she did, so that the moral judgment being made is transparent rather than smuggled in through a fiction about validity. Fuller takes the opposite route, by way of his theory of the 'inner morality of law': eight principles of legality (generality, promulgation, prospectivity, clarity, non-contradiction, possibility of compliance, constancy through time, and congruence between rule and official action) that any system of rules must largely satisfy to count as law at all. Because the Nazi regime governed so heavily through secret directives, retroactive decrees, and ad hoc terror rather than through rules meeting these criteria, Fuller argues that its enactments had drifted far enough from law's inner morality that the postwar court was entitled to treat the relevant statute as never having been valid law, without needing Hart's retroactive fix. This is, on Fuller's view, effectively the same point Hart himself concedes when he allows a 'minimum content of natural law': that any legal system, to function as law, must incorporate a core of principles like the eight above, so that Fuller's inner morality is really just a fuller statement of what Hart's own minimum content already requires. And because a system that consistently honors all eight principles cannot easily be turned to ends as evil as the Nazis' were, Fuller's procedural natural law shows that a sufficiently 'legal' legal system will tend also to be a substantively decent one, which is the deeper reason he thinks the statute forfeited its claim to validity."
