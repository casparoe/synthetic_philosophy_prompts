I've been down a rabbit hole this week researching a piece on psychology's replication crisis, and I keep circling back to the Daryl Bem "feeling the future" saga from 2011 -- the one where he ran nine experiments and got solidly significant p-values seemingly showing that people could sense random future events before they happened. The way its usually told to me is a clean morality tale: frequentist null-hypothesis testing let a wild hypothesis sail through peer review, then Eric-Jan Wagenmakers and his co-authors redid the numbers as Bayes factors and showed the "evidence" was actually weak at best, and Bayesians everywhere took a bit of a victory lap. But it's messier than that -- a few years later Galak and colleagues ran big preregistered replications using completely ordinary frequentist tests and also found nothing, and I've seen statisticians like Andrew Gelman argue the real disease is what he calls the "garden of forking paths," all the small researcher degrees of freedom in how you clean, code, and analyze data, which he thinks can wreck a sloppy Bayesian analysis just as easily as a p-hacked one.

So going into writing this section, I'm stuck on what the actual moral is. Could you write me an essay that properly weighs this out: does the Bem episode, and the replication crisis more broadly, really vindicate Bayesian statistics as meaningfully more resistant to false positives than frequentist significance testing, or is that the wrong lesson to draw, with the actual fix being things like preregistration and constraining researcher degrees of freedom regardless of which statistical philosophy you use? Don't leave it on some wishy-washy "both sides have a point" note -- I want an actual verdict defended, along the lines of "mostly a wash, except for X" or "no, Bayes really does buy you Y" -- whatever you conclude, just make me believe it.
