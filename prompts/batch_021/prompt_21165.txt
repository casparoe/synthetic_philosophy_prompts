Quick bit of context: I've spent the last several months slowly rewriting a massive legacy service at work, module by module, without ever taking it down — my teammate keeps calling it "the ship of Theseus" and honestly the joke stuck with me more than it should have. It sent me down a genuine rabbit hole reading about personal identity, and that's how I ended up on Buddhist "no-self" (anatta) stuff, specifically a guy named Vasubandhu and his book the Abhidharmakosa.

From what I can piece together (no philosophy background here, just wikipedia and some pdf's at 1am), Vasubandhu spends a whole section arguing against a rival school — the Pudgalavadins, I think? — who wanted to keep some minimal "person" around behind the scenes to explain memory and karma. His own answer is basically that a person is nothing but a causal series of momentary mental states, each one conditioned by the last, kind of like a linked list where every node only points backward — there's no separate "self" object holding the list together, the pointers ARE the person.

Ok here's my actual question. Vasubandhu wrote centuries before anyone could "fork" a mind. Say you could snapshot someone's complete mind-state, the way you'd snapshot a VM, and boot two fully independent copies that then live out separate lives from that point on. Given his specific arguments against the Pudgalavadins, would he say there's simply no problem here (there was never a "self" to duplicate in the first place, so who cares if the series branches)? Or does his whole framework quietly assume the causal series stays a single unbranching line — especially since karma is supposed to "ripen" onto later members of that same series — so that forking it in two actually breaks something in his system that a modern reductionist like Parfit doesn't really have to worry about? I'd like an actual argued answer, engaging with the specifics of his position, not just "Buddhism says there's no self so branching is fine."
