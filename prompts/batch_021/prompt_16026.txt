The take-home question read: "State Harsanyi's Aggregation Theorem precisely, identifying which assumption is doing the real work and what the conclusion actually asserts. Then assess the following objection: 'The theorem doesn't really vindicate utilitarianism. The weights it attaches to each person's utility are artifacts of how that person's von Neumann–Morgenstern utility function happens to be scaled, and the theorem itself supplies no way of comparing utility across people, so it cannot tell us how to weigh one person's gain against another's loss.' Is the objection correct, and if so, is anything left of the claim that the theorem grounds utilitarianism?"

Here is the student's answer, verbatim:

"Harsanyi's theorem considers a society of individuals, each of whom has preferences over lotteries on social outcomes satisfying the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms, so each is represented by a cardinal utility function unique up to a positive affine transformation. Society itself is assumed to have a preference ordering over the same lotteries that also satisfies the vNM axioms, giving it its own cardinal representation, U_0. The assumption doing the real work is Pareto indifference: whenever every individual is indifferent between two lotteries, society is indifferent between them too. From this, Harsanyi derives that U_0 must be an affine combination of the individual utility functions, U_0 = a_0 + Σ a_i U_i — social utility is a weighted sum of individual utilities, which is why the theorem is treated as a rationality-based argument for utilitarianism.

The objection is right that the weights are not innocent: since each U_i is only pinned down up to its own positive affine transformation, the a_i in the representation depend on how each person's utility happens to be scaled, and the theorem does nothing to fix that scale on its own. This is precisely why Harsanyi has each person's judgment run through the device of an impartial spectator who imagines an equal chance of turning out to be any given member of society; it is that equiprobability device, not the Pareto premise, that actually pins the weights down and gives the sum its utilitarian content, rather than leaving it a merely formal aggregation. Fortunately, the remedy is simple: renormalize every individual's utility function to run from 0 to 1 before aggregating, and the a_i become fully determinate, so the arbitrariness the objection raises disappears and the theorem's utilitarian credentials are restored."
