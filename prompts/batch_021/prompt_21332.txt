Since retiring from a career in theoretical physics — mostly nonlinear dynamics and statistical mechanics — I have been spending my mornings working through the foundations of decision theory with an informal reading group at the local university, made up mostly of other retirees and a few patient graduate students. Lately we have been stuck on the "Death in Damascus" puzzle, and I wrote up my own attempt at making sense of it for the group. Before I inflict it on them, I would appreciate a more expert reading, particularly since I suspect I have been too eager to force the philosophy into the vocabulary I know best.

Here is the note as it stands. Death in Damascus, due to Gibbard and Harper, asks us to imagine a man confronted by Death in the Damascus marketplace, who is told an appointment awaits him tomorrow and must decide overnight whether to stay put or flee to Aleppo. Death is an essentially perfect predictor of where he will actually be, so whichever place he is disposed to end up in is the place she has already arranged to be. The puzzle is not merely that both choices look equally hopeless — it is that causal decision theory's own recipe never settles down. Whatever act currently maximizes expected utility stops doing so the moment you factor in the fact that you have settled on it, since that resolve is exactly the sort of evidence a perfect predictor would have used; the other act then looks better, until you factor in your resolve to do that instead, and so on. In the jargon I have picked up, no pure act is "ratifiable": none maximizes expected utility once your credence that you will perform it is pushed to one.

The response I have found in the literature is to point out that this has the structure of a zero-sum prediction game, like matching pennies, and such games have no equilibrium in pure strategies but do have one in mixed strategies: a man who commits to a coin flip gives neither himself nor Death any information to exploit, so the 50/50 mix is ratifiable even though staying and fleeing individually are not. I mapped this straight onto language from my old field: a pure act behaves like a fixed point that is unstable under the theory's own updating dynamics, while the randomized act is closer to a stationary distribution that a noisy system relaxes into once you stop demanding a deterministic equilibrium. What I cannot decide is whether this is a genuine solution or just a relocation of the disease: the whole appeal of causal decision theory is that it tracks the real causal upshot of your act rather than managing what your act reveals, yet the argument for randomizing is explicitly that it keeps you ignorant of your own act for long enough that it cannot be used as evidence against you — which sounds like exactly the kind of reasoning causal decision theorists are supposed to reject, merely moved up a level. I have a nagging, secondhand memory — possibly from Andy Egan, though I read it on a long flight some years ago and would not trust myself on the details — that further variants of this sort of case can be constructed in which even the unique ratifiable act, mixed or not, is the intuitively wrong one, which would mean the patch does not generalize.

I would be grateful for comments on three things: whether my reconstruction of the puzzle and of the mixed-strategy response to it is accurate to how the literature actually uses these notions; whether the dynamical-systems analogy is doing real conceptual work here or is a physicist's habit that obscures more than it reveals; and whether my closing worry about randomization smuggling in evidential reasoning back in is a real, recognized objection, or whether I am simply confusing myself about what causal decision theory is committed to.
