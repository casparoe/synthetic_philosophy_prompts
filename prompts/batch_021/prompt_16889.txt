So I've been teaching a grad seminar on rational choice this term, and last week's session on money-pump arguments turned into a genuine fight that we never actually resolved, so now I kind of want to see it played out properly. Setup, in case it's useful to anchor the dialogue: an agent has cyclic preferences over three options X, Y, and Z — X beats Y, Y beats Z, and Z beats X — and a bookie offers, at each step, a trade toward whichever option is currently more preferred, for a small fee. A "naive" chooser who just takes whatever trade looks best in the moment gets walked around the cycle forever and fleeced for as much as the bookie likes, and nobody in the room disputed that this is bad news for the naive chooser. Where it got heated was over what happens once the agent gets to use foresight — either by being "sophisticated" (using backward induction to see the trap coming and refusing trades it would otherwise want) or "resolute" (committing to a plan up front and sticking with it even when a later self would rather deviate). Half the room thought sophistication or resoluteness completely rescues the cyclic agent: since they're never actually pumped, there was nothing wrong with the cyclic preferences to begin with, and the real requirement rationality imposes is diachronic (don't be predictably exploitable over time) rather then synchronic (have a transitive ordering at any given moment). The other half thought this was basically a dodge — that a sophisticated or resolute agent has to, at some point, choose against the very preference they currently hold, and that cost is exactly what shows the intransitivity was the disease all along, just relocated rather than cured.

Could you write me a dialogue between two people who hold these two positions, arguing it out directly over the bookie scenario (they're welcome to tweak the details of the setup together if it helps them make a point)? I want real back-and-forth — if one of them points out that the sophisticated chooser has to refuse a trade that, in isolation, they prefer, the other side needs an actual answer to that rather than a change of subject, and vice versa. Neither one should be the obvious pushover; play both positions at their strongest, the way the two camps in my seminar actually did. It doesn't need to end in agreement — it didn't in real life either. (There's apparently a further complication about whether the same rescue works for incomplete, rather than cyclic, preferences, but feel free to leave that aside unless it comes up naturally.)
