I'm teaching a graduate seminar on decision theory this term, and last week we went through the classic Davidson-McKinsey-Suppes money pump argument for why preferences have to be transitive -- you know, the bit where a vendor sells you A for B, then B for C, then C for A, charging a small fee each time, until you've been fleeced despite ending up right back where you started with A. One of my students brought up McClennen's old point about "resolute" choosers: an agent who commits to a plan at the outset and just doesn't reconsider mid-sequence never actually gets pumped, even though her preferences over A, B, and C stay perfectly cyclical the whole time. That seems basically right to me, but it left me stuck on a bigger question that I keep going back and forth on.

Does the resolute-choice reply show that the money pump argument never really established a synchronic constraint on preferences (i.e., that they be transitive at any given moment), and that it was only ever smuggling in a diachronic constraint about not revising your plan partway through? My TA insists these two collapse into the same requirement once you actually spell out what being "resolute" commits you to, and I'm not so sure she's right. Could you walk me through, in some detail, why the resolute-choice reply does or doesnt succeed at prying apart the synchronic and diachronic readings of the transitivity requirement -- and where "sophisticated" choosers (who anticipate the whole sequence but don't bind themselves to a plan) fit into that picture? Would love a sharper take on this before I have to face the class again Thursday.
