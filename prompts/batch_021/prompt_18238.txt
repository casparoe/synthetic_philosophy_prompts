I teach an upper-level seminar on philosophy of religion, and this term's visiting speaker is a philosopher who has built her career on a sophisticated divine command theory in the lineage of Robert Adams: the view that moral obligation is grounded not in bare divine fiat but in the nature of a God who is essentially loving, precisely so as to escape the classic horns of the Euthyphro dilemma. She gives a public lecture on Thursday, and I am moderating the Q&A that follows, with a dozen of my students in the front rows who have already worked through Plato's dialogue and the standard textbook statement of the puzzle; an hour spent re-litigating whether it is good because God commands it, or God commands it because it is good, would be an hour wasted on someone who has thought about little else for thirty years.

Please draft eight to ten questions for that Q&A that push past the opening move. I want at least one question on the worry that grounding obligation in God's nature simply relocates the arbitrariness horn rather than closing it off, since we can now ask whether God is loving as a brute, unexplained fact or loving by reference to a standard that would hold whether or not God existed; one on the charge that if the relevant nature -- loving-kindness, justice, and so on -- can be specified independently of any reference to God, then God appears to do no further work in the metaphysics, and a non-theist could help themselves to the same standard without the theological apparatus; one on the resulting problem of moral epistemology, given that scripture attributes to God commands that look, on their face, hard to reconcile with a loving nature -- how is a believer meant to distinguish a genuine command from a mistaken attribution without already possessing an independent grip on what love requires?; and one on whether anchoring necessary moral truths in God's nature sits comfortably with the robust divine freedom the theory otherwise wants to preserve. For each question, add a short follow-up keyed to the answer a well-prepared defender of the view is most likely to give, so the exchange does not stall at her first parry.
