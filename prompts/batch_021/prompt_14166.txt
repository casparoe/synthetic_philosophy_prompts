So I fell down a bit of a rabbit hole this week. It started with a philosophy podcast episode about "evolutionary debunking arguments" in ethics — the basic idea (I think?) is that if evolution fully explains why we have the moral beliefs we do, that's supposed to be a problem for thinking those beliefs are actually tracking some objective moral truth, since natural selection only cares about fitness, not truth. Somebody in the episode's comments said this whole line of thinking basically starts with Darwin, so instead of just taking their word for it I went and dug up the actual passage in The Descent of Man, in the chapter on the "moral sense" (chapter 4, I think). This is apparently the bit that gets cited constantly in the philosophy papers on this topic:

"If ... men were reared under precisely the same conditions as hive-bees, there can hardly be a doubt that our unmarried females would, like the worker-bees, think it a sacred duty to kill their brothers, and mothers would strive to kill their fertile daughters; and no one would think of interfering."

Reading it in context, Darwin had already spent a few pages arguing that basically any sufficiently smart social species would develop some kind of conscience — this bit is him making the seperate point that the specific *content* of that conscience depends on whatever social instincts the species happens to have. What I can't figure out is how skeptical he's actually being. Is he saying the bee-people would be flat out wrong that killing their brothers is a sacred duty, just psychologically certain of it? Or is he more of a relativist about it here, where given their instincts it actually would be their duty, full stop? And either way, does this passage really get you all the way to the modern "debunking" conclusion (that we should distrust our own moral convictions because of where they came from), or is crediting Darwin with that a bit anachronistic — reading 150 years of extra epistemology into a guy who was mostly just doing natural history? I don't have any real philosophy background so I genuinely can't tell if there's an obvious step I'm not seeing in how you're supposed to get from "here's the causal origin of a belief" to "therefore you should be skeptical of the belief."
