A friend and I spent half of last night arguing about persistence through time, and neither of us budged. She defends endurantism: a person is a three-dimensional thing wholly present at every moment it exists, numerically one and the same entity from birth to death. I took the perdurantist line: a person is really an extended four-dimensional "worm," and what's wholly present at any one moment is just one temporal part of it, never the whole thing. The case that really tested both views was fission — some process, not a teleporter (too easy to hand-wave away), that splits one person into two equally good successors. On my view this is almost tidy: there were two overlapping worms sharing every stage up to the split, which simply diverge afterward — except that this commits me to saying there were, strictly, two people in the room the whole time, sharing one body and one stream of thought, which she found absurd. On her view, fission is worse: since strict identity can't branch, either the original person ceases to exist the instant the split occurs, replaced by two newcomers, or she must accept that one thing became, impossibly, identical to two distinct things. Write a short story built around a fission case along these lines, one where the plot's actual mechanics, not just the exposition, force a reader to feel the cost of each option — rather than gesturing vaguely at "two copies" the way most duplication fiction does.
