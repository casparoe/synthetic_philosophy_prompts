I'm a software engineer, and lately I've been going down a Quine rabbit hole that started, embarrassingly, because I liked the pun in his line "to be is to be the value of a bound variable" (bound variables being a concept I deal with every day, just not in that sense). That led me through "Two Dogmas of Empiricism" and the idea of the "web of belief," and eventually to a short, obscure piece called "On the Nature of Moral Values," where Quine tries to draw a parallel between moral values and the "theoretical values" — simplicity, conservatism, and so on — that guide theory choice in science.

What I can't shake is a tension I think I've spotted. Elsewhere, Quine is perfectly comfortable granting existence to things nobody has ever observed — numbers, sets, the whole apparatus of mathematics — purely because they're indispensable to our best overall scientific theory (apparently this is what people call the Quine-Putnam indispensability argument, once Putnam ran with it). But in the moral values essay, he seems to pull back from doing the same for ethics, leaning on the idea that scientific "values" are ultimately answerable to observation sentences in a way moral values are not, and using that asymmetry to keep morality on a lower epistemic rung than science. Given how central holism and "no first philosophy" are to the rest of his system — the idea that every posit, however abstract, is justified the same way, by its contribution to a simple, workable overall theory — this move looks to me like it might be an inconsistency, or at least an unearned exception, rather than something Quine is actually entitled to.

I'd like you to make the strongest case you can that a consistent Quinean naturalist should treat moral values the way Quine treats mathematical objects: as posits we're entitled to believe in just insofar as they're indispensable to our best overall theory of the world, including our theories of human behavior and society, rather than as the kind of second-class, non-cognitive attitude-expressions his observation-sentence asymmetry seems to push toward. Please engage directly with that asymmetry rather than talking past it. I don't want a general survey of moral realism; I want an argument built specifically out of Quine's own commitments — naturalism, holism, indispensability, the rejection of first philosophy — that shows why he should have gone further than he did, and I'd like you to anticipate the best Quinean reply on his behalf before answering it.
