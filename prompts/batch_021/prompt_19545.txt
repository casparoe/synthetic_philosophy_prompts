I recently joined a small biocomputing lab that grows clusters of living neurons in petri dishes, wires them to electrode arrays, and trains them with electrical feedback to perform simple tasks — what some people in the field call "organoid intelligence." Our long-term pitch, which I first heard during onboarding, is that networking enough of these clusters together could, in principle, match the functional complexity of a full nervous system, even though no single organoid is embodied, connected to sense organs, or shaped by anything like a normal developmental history. I can't stop thinking about the old philosophical worry that if mental states are nothing over and above functional or causal roles, then whatever fills those roles — a brain, a network of dishes, a roomful of people passing notes — ought to count as a mind on equal terms. Write a short story that takes our roadmap seriously and dramatizes the moment such a system is finally scaled up, one that honestly confronts whether getting the functional organization right would be enough to guarantee that someone is home, or whether something essential could still be missing.
