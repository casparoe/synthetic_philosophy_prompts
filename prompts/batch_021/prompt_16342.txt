A couple of months ago I joined a small amateur forecasting group that meets online every other week; following the usual Tetlock-inspired format, each of us attaches probabilities to claims about upcoming events -- local elections, whether a friend's startup will close its next funding round, that sort of thing -- and we tally Brier scores at the end of every quarter. Since I'm the one who ends up moderating the group's chat, I got pulled into refereeing an argument between two members, Josh and Priya, that started after our last meeting and still hasn't been settled. I'd like a third opinion.

The trigger was a guest, Maya, who dislikes giving percentages and instead just flatly texted the group "It's getting voted down Thursday" about a local rezoning proposal, because a friend who works at city hall had told her the measure was "basically dead." The council did vote it down, though local news later reported that a last-minute amendment came within a single vote of saving it -- a possibility Maya's friend never mentioned and, it turned out, hadn't known about either. (1) Josh argued that Maya's flat assertion had been improper all along, regardless of the outcome: she didn't know the vote would fail, since her only source was a friend's impression that left open exactly the scenario that almost happened, and you shouldn't state as settled fact what you merely have decent hearsay for. (2) Priya replied that this proves too much: none of us ever "knows" what a council will do in whatever strong sense Josh has in mind, since there's always some residual chance of a surprise amendment, yet forecasters and journalists make flat-sounding claims about the future constantly without anyone thinking them reckless; the real requirement, she said, must be something weaker, like a justified high credence, which Maya clearly had. (3) Josh answered that he wasn't demanding certainty, only fallibilist knowledge that rules out the realistic alternatives -- and the amendment was precisely the realistic alternative that Maya's tip hadn't ruled out, which is why, in his view, the natural reaction once we learned how close it came was "she got lucky" rather than "she called it," and that reaction only makes sense if what she owed us was knowledge rather than mere justified credence. (4) Priya's closing reply was that feeling lucky in hindsight doesn't show anything was improper at the time -- a careful driver can be lucky not to hit a jaywalker who steps out unexpectedly -- and that if Josh wants to say Maya's assertion was blameless and perfectly reasonable yet still somehow fell short of some further standard, he should say that outright, instead of letting "improper" slide between "you did something wrong" and "you got away with something."

I've since learned this maps onto a real debate among epistemologists over whether assertion is governed by a knowledge norm, a truth norm, or some kind of belief norm -- e.g., that you need only a justified, reasonable belief -- but knowing the debate exists hasn't told me who won ours.
