I have been asked to give the toast at the wedding of my former doctoral student, Elena Marsh, who is marrying her partner Daniel — a structural engineer who, by his own cheerful admission, had never encountered the word "epistemology" before he met her — in three weeks' time. Elena's dissertation defended a non-doxastic reading of Wittgenstein's hinge commitments in On Certainty, taking a broadly Pritchardian line against views, like Coliva's, on which hinges are simply a peculiar species of arational belief, and when she asked me to speak she added, only half joking, that I was permitted to "do the hinge thing" as long as I didn't turn her wedding into a conference panel.

Here is my difficulty, and why I would rather commission this than improvise it after a glass of wine. The easy, crowd-pleasing version of this toast is exactly the version On Certainty rules out: I am tempted to say that I "knew" the two of them were right for each other almost from the start, as though their certainty in one another were a conclusion reached once enough confirming evidence had come in. But on Wittgenstein's picture a hinge is not a belief arrived at by weighing evidence and answerable, in principle, to more of it; it is what has to stand fast, exempt from that whole practice of weighing, before doubt or persuasion about anything else is even intelligible. So I cannot present their commitment as well-evidenced without quietly reintroducing the picture the text spends its last notebooks dismantling — and I have taught this text often enough to know how easily that happens without anyone noticing. At the same time, "groundless" is not an insult in Wittgenstein's vocabulary: hinges are groundless in needing no further grounds, not in being arbitrary, and I want that distinction to actually land for a room that will be half philosophy department and half people who have never heard the phrase "hinge proposition" and will not need to hear it again to enjoy the toast. I would also like the toast to make some use of the river-bed passages — the thought that what stands fast can itself shift, almost imperceptibly, over the very years whose ordinary doubts, negotiations, and reconciliations it quietly makes possible.

Could you draft this toast for me? It should run to about five minutes, be warm and genuinely funny in places rather than solemn throughout, and it needs to work as a toast that could actually be delivered aloud at a wedding reception, rather than as a summary of On Certainty with the couple's names inserted into it.
