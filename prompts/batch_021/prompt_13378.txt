I'm a novelist, and last year I published a novel, *The Third Realm* — the title borrows Frege's own term, from his late essay "The Thought," for the objective domain where numbers and thoughts live, untouched by any individual mind — that tries to imagine the interior life of Gottlob Frege during the months when he was simultaneously filling one notebook with vicious nationalist and antisemitic political opinions and, in another, quietly concluding that the logicist project he had spent his career on didn't actually work, and that arithmetic might have to rest on geometrical intuition after all, the way geometry itself did. An old friend from graduate school, now on the philosophy faculty at Jena, has asked me to give the eight-minute opening remarks at a small one-day workshop her department is holding this spring, a hundred years on from that diary. The room will be a genuine mix: Frege specialists and a couple of visiting logicians who could probably recite the diary from memory, several of her graduate students, and, thanks to a write-up in the local paper about "the centenary of a scandal," a handful of townspeople who know Frege, if they know him at all, only as the man who invented modern logic.

Her one real constraint is that my remarks can't pre-empt the day itself: two keynote speakers scheduled right after me are going to argue opposite sides of the actual question the workshop exists to discuss — one, that the diary is an ugly but philosophically inert biographical fact, no more relevant to assessing the Begriffsschrift or the sense/reference distinction than Newton's alchemy is to the calculus; the other, that Frege's lifelong hostility to "psychologism," his insistence on a realm of thought answerable to no one's opinion, and his hunt for a notation scoured of every natural-language ambiguity are of a piece with the very impatience for purity that shows up in the diary's contempt for parliamentary Weimar and for what he calls the great mass of ordinary voters. She doesn't want my opening remarks to scoop either speaker, or to sound like I'm refereeing the debate before it has even started; what she wants is for the room to leave my eight minutes actually understanding what would have to be true of Frege's logic for the diary to bear on it philosophically, as opposed to merely being a fact about him, so that when her speakers disagree later, everyone can tell what they are disagreeing about.

I'd like your help drafting these remarks — something in the neighborhood of a thousand words, so they run to time. In the novel, I mostly ducked this question by keeping the two notebooks in separate chapters and never once letting my narrator draw the connection outright, a trick I don't think will survive eight minutes in front of a room half full of people who study this for a living. I don't want to open with an apology for not being a philosopher, I don't want the diary trotted out for shock value or the logic used as a shield against it, and I don't want to hand the room a tidy verdict my friend has explicitly asked me not to deliver. I just want everyone walking into the first keynote knowing exactly what is, and isn't, at stake.
