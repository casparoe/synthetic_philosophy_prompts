For my AP U.S. History class, we each had to choose a primary source connected to a broader course theme for our end-of-unit paper, and I picked the section of Benjamin Franklin's Autobiography where he describes what he calls his "bold and arduous project of arriving at moral perfection." I found the system itself fascinating: the list of thirteen virtues, the little book ruled into columns for each day of the week so he could mark a black spot every time he slipped on a given virtue, and the plan to concentrate on only one virtue per week so that he could work through the entire list four times a year. What interested me even more, though, is how honestly he describes the project's limits. He admits that Order was the one virtue he could never master no matter how many times he cycled through the list, and at one point he tells a story about a man who brings a dull axe to a blacksmith wanting the whole surface polished bright, only to give up partway through the grinding and decide that a "speckled" axe suits him just fine — a conclusion Franklin then half-jokingly applies to himself, wondering whether he is really just rationalizing his own laziness. He even adds Humility to the list at the very end, after a friend tells him he comes across as proud, and then admits that if he ever did fully conquer his pride, he would probably just end up proud of his humility instead.

In AP Psychology, we have been covering a body of research that I cannot stop connecting to that passage: the work on self-compassion associated with researchers like Kristin Neff and Paul Gilbert, which has fairly consistently found that people who respond to their own setbacks with kindness, rather than strict self-monitoring and self-criticism, tend to sustain behavior change longer and feel less anxious about backsliding than people who treat every failure as a mark against them — which is essentially the model Franklin's chart runs on.

That contrast raises a question I would like your help thinking through. Based on the actual specifics of how Franklin ran his project and wrote about it afterward, what do you think he would say if this research on self-compassion were explained to him? Would he take it as basically vindicating the conclusion he had already half-reached with the speckled axe, or would he suspect it is just a more respectable-sounding version of the excuse he already worried he was making for himself? I would like a genuine argument for whichever position you take, grounded in the specifics of the project and how Franklin talks about it, rather than a generic answer about him being a practical Enlightenment figure who would presumably welcome new self-improvement ideas.
