I teach political philosophy and sit on the philosophical advisory panel for a constitutional drafting commission in a country emerging from decades of one-party rule. One clause has deadlocked the session I must report back on: it would place the constitution's guarantee of regular, competitive elections beyond amendment by any future majority, however large. Defenders invoke Jon Elster's analogy between such 'eternity clauses' and Ulysses ordering his crew to bind him to the mast and disregard whatever he begs once the Sirens start singing: a people just escaped from authoritarian rule knows, in its lucid founding moment, exactly what it will later be tempted to give away. Opponents reply that the analogy fails where it matters most -- Ulysses bound only himself, briefly, against a single danger he could name in advance, whereas this clause would bind citizens not yet born, indefinitely, against dangers no one can name, which looks less like self-binding than like one generation disenfranchising the next, essentially the objection Jefferson raised against Madison when he argued that the earth belongs to the living. Draft the memo I will circulate before the session: recommend whether the commission should adopt the clause, in what modified form if not this one, and what, if anything, should count as a legitimate procedure for revisiting it later -- and give the disanalogy objection a genuine hearing before you reach a recommendation.
