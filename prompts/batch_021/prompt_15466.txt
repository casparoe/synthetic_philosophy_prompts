Marion Voss, who ran the investigations desk at the paper where I cut my teeth, is retiring after thirty-eight years, and I've been asked to give the toast at her send-off dinner next month. I started there as her intern before moving over to covering science and ideas full-time, but I've never stopped thinking about the ethics seminar she used to run for new hires, built around a thought experiment she adapted from Bernard Williams and Thomas Nagel's classic paired essays on moral luck: two reporters work from an identically thin anonymous tip and file essentially the same story after the same amount of diligence; one tip holds up and wins a prize, the other quietly falls apart in a way neither reporter's fact-checking could have caught, and a source's life is wrecked. She would ask the room which reporter deserved the bigger raise, then spend an hour making everyone uncomfortable with how many of us wanted to say "the one who got lucky."

Half the people at this dinner sat through some version of that seminar and will know exactly what I'm gesturing at if I say her whole career amounted to a standing argument against judging journalists by resultant luck rather than by process and character; the other half will be her husband, her kids, and a couple of publishers who have never heard the phrase "moral luck" and just want a warm, funny toast about someone they love. Marion has also made something of a principle, for decades, of refusing praise for the calls that happened to turn out well while quietly accepting blame for the ones that didn't — exactly the asymmetry that made Williams and Nagel so uneasy about the whole topic, since it cuts against the idea that we should only be judged for what was within our control.

Could you write the toast? I'm picturing about five minutes, ending on an actual line for raising a glass. I want the moral-luck framework doing real work in it, not just decorating it: it can't praise Marion simply because so many of her gambles happened to pay off, since that is the one move she would never forgive, but it also can't tip into the more vertiginous Nagelian thought that almost nothing anyone does across a whole career is really "theirs" once you subtract everything they never controlled. And underneath all of that it still has to work as a toast — funny and warm enough that her kids are laughing and a little teary by the end, rather than sitting through a philosophy lecture with wine.
