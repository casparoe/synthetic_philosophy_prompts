I teach a graduate seminar on the ethics of war, and every couple of years we spend a week on Anscombe's "Mr Truman's Degree" -- the pamphlet she wrote against Oxford's decision to give Truman an honorary degree over Hiroshima and Nagasaki. Students always find it bracing: her flat claim that choosing to kill the innocent as a means to your ends is murder, full stop, whatever "reasons of state" are on offer, and her general contempt for the kind of consequentialist reasoning that gets people off that hook. This year I want to push the class past the historical case, and I tried the extension below on a colleague in the politics department first, who though the parallel was too strained to be useful -- so now I want a second opinion, from you, before I decide whether it's worth putting in front of the seminar.

Anscombe died in Jan 2001, well before Putin made explicit nuclear threats into a running feature of the war in Ukraine -- not using a weapon, just repeatedly signalling a willingness to, as one coercive tool among others in an active, otherwise conventional war against a non-nuclear neighbour. I recall she (and other double-effect theorists of her generation) argued about Cold War deterrence too, something about whether forming the conditional intention to slaughter millions, even if the trigger is never pulled, is already murder in the heart -- but that was two blocs holding each other hostage in the abstract over decades, not a live, repeated threat issued by one head of state in the middle of a war he started. Reconstruct, using her actual moves from the Truman essay and whatever else of hers genuinely bears on this rather than a generic just-war-theory paraphrase, what she'd most plausibly say: is the threat itself already the crime for her, independent of whether any bomb is ever used, and does it change anything morally that its being wielded to coerce an ongoing war rather than, as with Truman, to end one already underway? I'd like it to sound like her -- cranky and specific -- not like a neutral summary of "the Anscombean position."
