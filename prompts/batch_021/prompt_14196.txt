I'm a novelist, and I've been asked to contribute to a magazine series on the question of whether you can separate the art from the artist. Everyone else on the roster is apparently writing about Wagner, Céline, or Heidegger, so I want to make my case through someone stranger: Gottlob Frege, the founder of modern formal logic. In the last full year of his life, Frege kept a private diary that surfaced only decades after his death; in it he argues for authoritarian rule over parliamentary democracy, writes with contempt for the Weimar Republic, and urges that Jews be deprived of political rights, at points floating their expulsion from Germany outright. Michael Dummett, who did more than anyone to canonize Frege for English-language philosophy and was himself a lifelong anti-racism campaigner, has written candidly about how unsettling he found the discovery. And yet almost no one treats the Begriffsschrift or the Grundgesetze as compromised by it, in a way we would never let a nationalist novel go untouched by its author's nationalism.

Below is the core argument of my draft. Before I take it further, I want your honest reaction to whether the central move actually works, and not merely whether the prose does.

—

The reflexive defense of Frege is the "genetic fallacy" move: a proof's validity has nothing to do with the character of the person who found it, so the diary is biography, not philosophy. I don't want to rest on that, because it is exactly the move we refuse to grant a novelist. No one accepts "the sentences still scan" as a defense of Céline, because a novel is made of the very material — portraits of persons, judgments of worth, an implied sense of whose lives matter — that a political commitment can inhabit. So the real question isn't whether an author's politics can ever be quarantined from the work; it's what makes a piece of work quarantinable in the first place.

My working claim is that Frege is close to a limit case for the view that the answer lies in the content of the work, not in some general fact about authorship or interpretation. An axiom about the extension of a concept, or a proof that a number is a class of classes, has no subject in the relevant sense: nothing in it has room for a thought about who belongs to a nation. That is why the diary can sit beside the Grundgesetze without touching it, in a way it could never sit beside a novel of German rural life. "The death of the author" isn't a general truth about reading; it's a special-case truth about work whose content is maximally indifferent to persons, and critics who export it to fiction are wrong about how indifferent fiction's content actually is.

There is a complication I can't write my way around, though. Frege's own logic isn't as indifferent as I want it to be. He insisted that a genuine concept must have a sharp boundary — that a predicate failing to return a determinate true or false for every object isn't confused at the edges, it simply isn't a concept at all. That is uncomfortably close in shape to his political conviction that a nation is corrupted by a population it can neither cleanly absorb nor cleanly exclude. I can't tell whether that is a real seam connecting the logic to the politics, on the model of what people argue about Heidegger's Being and his Nazism, or just a pattern I am forcing onto the material because it would make a better essay.

—

Tell me plainly: is the sharp-boundaries worry a genuine threat to the content-based line I want to draw, or a false analogy between a semantic doctrine and a political one? If content-neutrality is the wrong place to put the line between cases where an author's politics contaminate the work and cases where they don't, where should it go instead? And flag anywhere my account of Frege himself seems wrong or overstated.
