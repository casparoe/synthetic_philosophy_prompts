In my Latin class, we have been reading Cicero's De Senectute, in which Cato rebuts the complaint that old age is miserable because it strips us of life's pleasures. His central move is that this is not a genuine loss, since nature diminishes our desires at roughly the same rate it diminishes our capacities, and if a person no longer wants something, lacking it does not count as deprivation. This argument seems to rest on an assumption, rather than a guaranteed fact, that desire and capacity always fade together. Could you construct one specific, realistic scenario in which an elderly person's desire for a particular pleasure clearly outlasts their capacity to enjoy it, and briefly explain whether Cato's argument could be revised to accommodate such a case or would have to give up its central claim?
