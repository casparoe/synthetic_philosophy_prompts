Two people from the philosophy discussion group that grew out of our local Astral Codex Ten meetup got into a real argument after last month's session, and since both have separately asked me afterward who "won," I'd like a more principled answer than I can give them myself. The session's topic had been population ethics. (1) Elena, who had just finished Benatar's Better Never to Have Been, laid out his asymmetry argument for antinatalism: the presence of pain is bad and the presence of pleasure is good, but the absence of pain is good even when nobody exists to enjoy that absence, whereas the absence of pleasure counts as bad only if some actual person is deprived of it. Because every life contains at least some suffering while non-existence contains none, she argued, coming into existence is always a harm, regardless of how happy the resulting life turns out to be.

(2) Sam objected that this looked like smuggling the conclusion into the premises: why treat "absence of pain is good" and "absence of pleasure is bad" so differently, unless you already need that asymmetry to reach the antinatalist verdict? (3) Elena replied that Benatar doesn't merely posit the asymmetry -- he supports it with further asymmetries we seem to accept in everyday moral judgment: that there is a duty not to create a child who will suffer greatly but no comparable duty to create a child who will be happy, and that we would never feel sad, for the sake of merely possible happy people, that some perfectly hospitable but empty planet has no inhabitants, even though we would be troubled to learn that planet was inhabited by suffering creatures. These, she said, are independent intuitions that the main asymmetry merely systematizes, not props invented to save it. (4) Sam countered that ordinary person-affecting reasoning explains all of those intuitions just as well, with none of Benatar's stronger commitments, and that if the asymmetry really carries the weight Elena wants, it commits her to holding that a swift, painless end to all sentient life would be a positive improvement -- which he took as grounds for rejecting a premise, not for accepting the conclusion. (5) Elena's response was to accept this consequence outright: she said Benatar himself embraces that axiological verdict while denying it licenses anyone to act on it, so there is no tension between judging extinction better and insisting nobody may bring it about. (6) Sam thought this only relocated the awkwardness rather than dissolving it, since we would still be saying the end of everyone we know would make the world strictly better, which any of us would fight to prevent.

I know enough of this literature to tell that both of them are making moves real philosophers have made, but not enough to say who is actually ahead at any given step. Could you go through the exchange move by move -- (1) through (6) -- and say who has the better of it at each stage, including whether Elena's supporting asymmetries do genuine independent work or quietly assume what they are meant to establish, and whether Sam's "relocated awkwardness" charge actually undermines the axiology/permissibility distinction Elena is relying on? Please close with an overall verdict on who comes out ahead on balance, and note anything important you think both of them are overlooking.
