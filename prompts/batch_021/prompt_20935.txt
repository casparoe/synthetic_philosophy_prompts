I've been reading about Leibniz's claim that a perfectly good, rational God must have created the best of all possible worlds, and it reminds me of a problem that comes up in optimization work: a search space can fail to have a best solution at all, offering only an unattained supremum, when every candidate has a strictly better neighbor — the way the sequence 0.9, 0.99, 0.999... climbs toward 1 without any term of it ever reaching 1. If the space of possible worlds behaves the same way, with every world surpassed by a better one and no actual maximum, then "the best possible world" may fail to pick out anything, and Leibniz's argument seems to collapse before the problem of evil even enters the picture. What makes this especially damaging for Leibniz in particular, as I understand him, is his principle of sufficient reason: without a uniquely best world, God would seem to have no sufficient reason to prefer any one world over the next, slightly better, one — exactly the kind of unmotivated choice his system is built to exclude.

Leibniz's own resource against this, as far as I can tell, is compossibility: the idea that not every combination of goods can coexist within a single world, so "goodness" is not a simple unconstrained scale running off to infinity but a trade-off — between variety and order, say — that could plausibly top out at an actual maximum rather than an endlessly improvable one.

Please write a dialogue between a contemporary defender of Leibniz's best-possible-world claim and a critic pressing this "no-best-world" objection. Have the critic push the analogy as far as it will go, have the defender draw on compossibility and any other move genuinely available to Leibniz, and have each respond directly to the other's actual argument, including an honest reckoning of what the defender would have to concede — such as retreating from "the best" to merely "good enough" — if compossibility does not fully settle the matter.
