I spent my working life as a chemist, and now that I'm retired Ive been reading my way through epistemology, which has got me properly stuck on this idea of "epistemic akrasia" -- roughly, believing something while also believing your own evidence doesn't support it.

Here's the case that won't let go of me. Say a researcher is up til 4am and finally nails a tricky derivation -- checks it three times, totally solid as far as she can tell. Then a colleague reminds her that people who've been awake that long reliably start making more logical slips, and usually can't tell from the inside that anything's off, the reasoning just feels as crisp as ever. She goes back over her own steps again and still can't find anything wrong.

So does she now have good reason to distrust her own conclusion, even with no actual flaw she can point to? Keep believing it, and she seems to be doing the textbook akrasia thing -- believing P while granting that her evidence (her own faculties, this time) doesn't support P. Drop her confidence instead, and she's not responding to any flaw in the argument, just deferring to a statistic about people in her situation.

Could you write me an essay that actually engages with this, something like the "enkratic" view that this sort of akrasia is never rational, versus "level splitting" type views that say it's fine for her to go on trusting the object level reasoning anyway. Don't just describe both camps and leave it there, tell me which one actually wins and why -- I've read plenty of philosophy essays that end in a shrug already.
