Here is the question as it was assigned, worth 20 points:

Question 3. In "Minds, Machines and Gödel" (1961), J. R. Lucas argues that Gödel's first incompleteness theorem shows that the human mind cannot be equivalent to any Turing machine.
(a) [8 points] Reconstruct Lucas's argument. Be precise about which sentence a machine modeling a given formal system F is unable to prove, and about why we are supposed to be able to see that this sentence is true.
(b) [7 points] State Benacerraf's objection to the argument, from "God, the Devil, and Gödel" (1967), and explain how it differs from the objection that Lucas is not entitled to assume that F is consistent.
(c) [5 points] In Shadows of the Mind, Penrose replaces the assumption that F is consistent with the stronger assumption that F is sound. Does this help the argument survive Benacerraf's objection? Explain your answer.

And here is the student's response, reproduced exactly as submitted:

(a) Lucas's argument starts from Gödel's theorem that any formal system F powerful enough to represent elementary arithmetic cannot, if it is consistent, prove its own consistency from within; call this statement Con(F). Since we are not confined to reasoning within F, we can stand outside it, satisfy ourselves that F is consistent, and thereby recognize that Con(F) is true, even though F itself is barred from ever producing Con(F) as a theorem. If a machine's outputs are exhausted by what some particular F proves, then Con(F) is a truth the machine can never assert but that we can. Because this holds for any F that might be proposed as a model of a person's reasoning, Lucas concludes that no machine can capture everything a mind is capable of recognizing as true, so the mind cannot simply be identical with any Turing machine.

(b) Benacerraf's objection grants that the Gödelian construction works exactly as Lucas describes it, but denies that this shows minds are not machines. His point is that Lucas could never actually identify, in advance, which formal system F he himself instantiates: the moment a particular F is proposed as a model of Lucas, Lucas is entitled to take that system's Gödel sentence, add it as a further axiom, and thereby produce a strictly stronger system that proves more than F did, so no proposed F can ever be made to stick as the correct description of him. But this is really just a version of the worry that Lucas can never be sure the F he is being compared to is the right one, or even that it is consistent: in both cases, the objection is that Lucas cannot pin down a fixed formal system to legitimately claim victory over, whether because he can always leapfrog it by adding axioms or because he cannot certify its consistency in the first place. So although Benacerraf's objection and the consistency objection are usually presented as two separate worries, they come down to the same underlying problem for Lucas: an inability to fix the value of F.

(c) Penrose's shift from consistency to soundness does not rescue the argument from this kind of worry, and arguably makes Lucas's position harder to defend rather than easier. Soundness is a strictly stronger property than consistency: a sound system is automatically consistent, but plenty of consistent systems prove falsehoods and so are not sound. Claiming to know that F is sound therefore requires everything that claiming to know F is consistent requires, and more besides; there is no obvious reason a mind should be in a better position to certify soundness than consistency, and if anything the extra content of soundness makes the required act of self-certification even less available to us. This is more or less the assessment most logicians who have engaged with Shadows of the Mind have reached: Penrose's more elaborate construction is generally regarded as an ingenious attempt that does not succeed in putting the argument on firmer ground.
