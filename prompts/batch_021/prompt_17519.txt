I'm a software engineer, and a few of us on my team have a side project formally verifying part of our job-scheduling library in Lean. During a recent code review, two teammates -- Owen and Maya -- got into a back-and-forth in the PR comments that I couldn't fully referee myself, and it's been nagging at me since.

The theorem in question was roughly: "for any batch of overlapping jobs, some job can be rescheduled to remove the overlap." Owen proved it by assuming no such job exists and deriving a contradiction -- a textbook non-constructive existence proof. (1) Maya objected that this isn't good enough here: the whole point of formalizing the scheduler is to get a runnable "reschedule this job" suggestion out of these proofs, and a proof by contradiction doesn't give you that -- it only rules out that no job works, without saying which one does. (2) Owen replied that the theorem is still true and fully checked, so this is a tooling complaint, not a flaw in the proof: classically, "some job works" just means "it's not the case that none do," and that's exactly what he proved. (3) Maya said that's precisely the problem, not a defense: constructive existence proofs guarantee you can mechanically pull a witness out of them, classical ones don't, and that gap is exactly what their codebase needs closed. (4) Owen countered that their batches are always small and finite, so either way you can just brute-force check each job to find one that works -- the algorithm is trivial to get regardless of proof style, so Maya's point, whatever its general merits, has no teeth here. (5) Maya conceded that the finite case is recoverable, but said that's a reason to redo the proof constructively, not to wave the distinction away -- especially since the plan is to reuse this exact pattern next quarter on the unbounded, streaming version of the scheduler, where there's no finite list left to brute-force over.

Could you adjudicate this move by move rather than just declaring an overall winner?
