I'm taking a graduate seminar this term, cross-listed between philosophy and computer science, on the formal side of AI value alignment, and this week's unit was on inverse reinforcement learning. What has stuck with me more than the algorithms themselves is a foundational worry the readings kept circling back to: since infinitely many reward functions rationalize any given policy — the all-zero reward function trivially makes every policy "optimal" — the inference from behavior to preference is radically underdetermined unless you smuggle in extra assumptions. One of the later papers pushes this further and argues that even a strong simplicity prior over reward functions can't rescue the project, because you also have to specify how an agent might deviate from optimality (its biases or planning quirks), and a "trivial" pairing — where the reward function is just defined as whatever the agent happened to do, combined with a trivial planning procedure — turns out to be exactly as simple as the pairing you actually wanted to recover. A few of us were arguing after class that this is really an old philosophical problem dressed in new formal clothing: whether anyone's "true" interests can ever be read off their behavior once akrasia, addiction, or unreflective habit are live possibilities, now pursued with a level of precision that debates about paternalism and autonomy rarely reach.

For the final assignment we're allowed to submit a short story instead of a term paper, provided it engages substantively with a course concept, and I'd like to use it to explore the problem above rather than write another "the AI turned on us" story. Could you write me a short story, somewhere around 1000-1800 words, in which a character — human, AI, or some combination — has to act on someone else's behalf, at whatever scale suits you: one person, a household, a ship's crew, an entire population? I'd like them to be forced to confront this exact ambiguity in the middle of a concrete decision — that the behavior in front of them is equally well explained by "this is what they truly want" and by "this is a mistake they would disavow if they could see it clearly," with no further fact about behavior available to settle which reading is correct. I'd rather the plot's central turn hinge on that specific fork than on more familiar territory like an AI deceiving its principal or optimizing some flashily misspecified goal. Feel free to invent whichever setting brings the dilemma out most sharply; I'd rather leave that choice to you than hand you a fully specified premise.
