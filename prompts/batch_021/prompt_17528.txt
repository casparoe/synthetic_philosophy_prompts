So I've been stuck on Keynes's 1930 essay "Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren," the one where he predicts the "economic problem" will basically be solved within a hundred years -- so, like, right about now -- and everyone will suddenly have to face what he calls the real permanent problem of the human race: what do you actually do with a life once you don't have to work for survival. The part that gets me isn't the utopia bit, its the pessimism underneath it -- he uses the idle rich of his own day as a kind of preview of how the rest of us will handle abundance, and reckons they mostly failed the test, bored and restless with no idea how to fill the hours. He even figures society will keep people on ceremonial three-hour shifts just to give the "old Adam" in us something to gnaw on, and that thrift and ambition, the stuff our grandparents were raised to treat as virtues, will end up looking to everyone else like a mild personality disorder, the way we'd size up a hoarder now. Could you write me a short story, maybe 1500-2000 words, literary not sci-fi-expository, set in a world that's actually hit that point -- centered on someone who can't shake the old striver mindset, doing their token shift, watching people who were never wired that way glide through leisure without any of the guilt or aimlessness they feel. Don't give me a generic UBI-ennui story, I want it wrestling with the specific thing Keynes flagged: that nobody gets issued the same capacity for a life without a purpose bolted onto it.
