I spent my working life as an experimental physicist, and it is only since retiring that I have taken up philosophy in earnest. Max Black's 1952 dialogue on the identity of indiscernibles caught my attention right away: his thought experiment describes a universe containing nothing but two perfectly similar spheres, offered as a refutation of Leibniz's principle even once relational properties are allowed to count toward discernibility, and it kept reminding me of something from my own field -- the indistinguishability of identical particles in quantum mechanics. Please write me an essay that first takes the spheres seriously on their own terms, engaging with at least one serious rebuttal to the thought experiment -- the classic move of redescribing the whole scenario as a single sphere encountered twice, in a suitably curved space, is one option, though you need not restrict yourself to it -- and then asks whether quantum statistics changes the verdict: does the "weak discernibility" that some philosophers of physics have tried to ground in relations such as having opposite spin rescue the principle for fermions, even though the same trick seems to fail for bosons that can share a single quantum state outright? Don't leave the matter open. Tell me which formulation of the identity of indiscernibles, if any, you think actually survives, and defend that verdict.
