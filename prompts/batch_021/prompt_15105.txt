Over the past several months I have been serving on the committee organizing a local currency for my town: a paper scrip, redeemable only at participating local businesses, meant to keep spending circulating within the community rather than leaking out to national chains. Almost every skeptical resident raises some version of the same objection -- what actually backs the scrip, given that it is neither gold nor legal tender -- and in trying to formulate a serious answer I have ended up reading Berkeley's Querist, a text I had not expected to bear on a twenty-first-century scheme like ours.

Please describe the most important implications of Berkeley's views on money for a project of this kind -- in particular his claim that money is merely a ticket or counter for the power to command the industry of others rather than wealth in its own right, his insistence that the frequency of circulation matters more than the sheer quantity of specie in a country, and his proposal for a bank issuing credit not fully backed by gold or silver. I would like the discussion to cover not only what his position would let us say to skeptics, but also what a careful Berkeleyan would worry about in a scheme like ours, since he was hardly an uncritical advocate of unlimited paper credit.
