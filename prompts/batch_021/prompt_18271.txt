I work at a small nonprofit, and last spring I sat on a three-person hiring committee for a program-officer role. Our rubric specified that an offer should go out only if a finalist cleared two independent conditions: a strong performance on a written case-study exercise, and strong references. When we tallied our private ballots, the case study passed by majority — two of the three of us, including my friend on the committee, rated it a pass — and the references passed by majority as well, though this time it was myself and our third colleague who agreed. So on each condition taken on its own, a majority said pass, and by our own stated rule that should have produced an offer. But when I then asked the three of us to vote on the bottom-line question directly — offer, yes or no — two of us, my friend included, said no.

My friend and I argued about this for most of an evening afterward. She maintained that the direct, bottom-line vote is the only one with any procedural standing: we were empowered to decide whether to extend an offer, a majority said no on exactly that question, and the fact that a different majority forms on each underlying condition is beside the point. I argued this can't be the end of the matter, since it means the committee's own stated rationale for its decision — two independent conditions, each apparently cleared by a majority of us — fails to support its own conclusion, which struck me as a kind of institutional incoherence we shouldn't just shrug off. Neither of us managed to show the other where their reasoning broke down, and we eventually let it drop.

I've since gathered that this kind of split has a name in social-choice and judgment-aggregation theory — something like the "discursive dilemma" or "doctrinal paradox" — and that it's sometimes mentioned in the same breath as Arrow's impossibility theorem, as though the two results were relatives. What I'd like from you is not a restatement of the example above, which I already have, but a gut-level account of the underlying mechanism: why does voting issue-by-issue on logically connected propositions and then applying the rule diverge from voting on the conclusion directly, even though every individual on the committee was perfectly internally consistent throughout? Is there a precise sense in which this is the same phenomenon as a Condorcet cycle, or as whatever drives Arrow's theorem, or is that comparison more of a loose family resemblance? And is there an actual impossibility result here — I believe List and Pettit's names are attached to something along these lines — showing that no aggregation procedure can dodge this tension in general, as opposed to premise-based and conclusion-based voting just being two reasonable but flawed options among various others that might do better? I'd like to come away able to reconstruct, from first principles and without looking anything up, why my committee's two voting methods were always liable to disagree.
