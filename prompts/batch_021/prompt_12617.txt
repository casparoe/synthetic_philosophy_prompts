I spent my career as an evolutionary biologist studying reciprocal food-sharing in vampire bats, so Axelrod-style tit-for-tat and the iterated prisoner's dilemma were bread and butter for me long before I ever retired. Since hanging up my lab coat I've fallen down a bit of a rabbit hole reading decision theory and philosophy of rationality for fun, and a few weeks ago I ran into something that's been nagging at me ever since: "program equilibrium." The original Tennenholtz idea seemed simple enough - if two players submit programs instead of actions, and each program gets to read the other's source code before computing its move, you can apparently get mutual cooperation as a genuine equilibrium in a one-shot prisoner's dilemma, not just the repeated game I'm used to from the animal literature. Fine, I can sort of see that. But then I read about fancier versions like "FairBot," which cooperates with you if and only if it can prove you'll cooperate with it, and the whole trick apparently rests on a result from provability logic called Löb's theorem, which is supposed to stop the reasoning from collapsing into infinite regress ("I cooperate if you cooperate if I cooperate if you...").

Honestly I keep flip flopping on wether this is a genuine discovery about when trust between rational agents is warranted, or just a clever bit of formal sleight of hand that proves cooperation by assuming it into the setup rather than actually deriving it. I brought it up with my old reading group and we ended up just talking past each other for an hour, mostly because none of us could explain the Löb's theorem step well enough to actually defend it, which was kind of embarrassing for a room full of retired scientists.

So here's my request: write me a dialogue between two people who genuinely know this material cold - one an old-school game theorist convinced that "program equilibrium" is a shell game (defection is still dominant in the real one-shot dilemma, and letting agents read each other's source code just quietly turns it into a commitment game in disguise), and the other a proponent who thinks Löbian cooperation is a real, non-circular result about rational trust. I want actual back-and-forth, not two people talking past each other like my poor reading group - have the skeptic press hard on the self-reference and "isn't this just circular" worries, and make the advocate actually walk through what work Löb's theorem is doing rather than just gesturing at it. Neither one should get an easy win over the other. Set it wherever feels natural to you - old grad school friends arguing over email, a whiteboard after a seminar, whatever - I mainly just want the real substance of the disagreement, since that's the part I haven't been able to find spelled out clearly anywhere.
