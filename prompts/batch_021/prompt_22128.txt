My father, a logician who spent much of his retirement absorbed in Gödel's unpublished notebooks and correspondence with his mother, did not merely study Gödel's argument that a genuinely rational world must allow for some form of personal survival after death -- he adopted it as his own considered view, one he was always careful to distinguish both from the family's nominal Catholicism and from Gödel's separate (and, he insisted, logically unrelated) ontological proof of God. He died ten days ago, having left written instructions that whoever spoke at his service should neither soften this into talk of "a better place" and "watching over us," nor let tact dissolve it into vague agnosticism. I am giving that eulogy, to a room that will include his sister's family, who have never heard of Gödel, and several former colleagues in logic, who will notice immediately if the argument is garbled or quietly reduced to a version of Pascal's wager. Please write it: about five minutes long, warm enough to comfort the room, but faithful enough to what he actually believed that none of his colleagues could fairly call it a misrepresentation.
