Earlier this year I joined the clinical ethics committee at the hospital where I work, partly because of how unprepared my own family felt when my father spent his last two weeks on a ventilator after a stroke -- we never really resolved among ourselves whether continuing treatment was giving him a chance or simply postponing the inevitable, and I wanted to understand these questions better than I'd managed to at the time. I didn't expect a case with the same shape to land on the committee within my first few months, but one has, and I've been asked to draft the short opinion I'll circulate before we reconvene.

The patient is a woman in her late seventies who suffered a prolonged cardiac arrest ten days ago and has been ventilated in the ICU ever since with a severe hypoxic-ischemic brain injury; the medical team is unanimous that she will never recover awareness, and they now want to start dialysis for the kidney failure that has followed, which is what has forced the issue. Our policy lets the committee authorize withdrawing non-beneficial treatment over a family's objection once there has been "genuine engagement with the family's values and beliefs," but it never says what that engagement has to consist of, and this case is testing that gap. The patient's two adult children, who share decision-making authority, both describe themselves as devout, and both say they've consulted the same imam, yet they have reached opposite conclusions from what turns out to be the very same premise: that no created thing -- not a ventilator, not a doctor's hand -- is ever the true cause of anything, since God alone causes every event, including the moment of death, which is already fixed in advance. Our chaplain confirmed this is a serious, mainstream position with a long history, tracing it to al-Ghazali's argument that fire does not itself burn cotton, God does, the contact between them being customary rather than necessary; she added, almost in passing, that the same theologians never doubted that people remain answerable for what they do, which none of us knew how to square with the rest of it. The son takes the shared premise to mean that switching off the ventilator would not kill his mother, since the machine was never doing the causal work of keeping her alive to begin with, so there's no religious reason to keep escalating care the doctors already consider futile. The daughter starts from exactly the same premise and refuses consent to withdrawal anyway, reasoning that if no human action is ever the true cause of death either way, she would rather not be the one who acts at all, and would prefer to let God bring the outcome about through whatever means He chooses.

Some members of the committee want to set the theological argument aside entirely and just apply our ordinary best-interests and futility criteria, treating the siblings' disagreement as noise the committee shouldn't referee; others think that would quietly dodge the very thing the family is asking us to engage, and would cut against a policy that demands "genuine engagement" rather than a medical override dressed up as neutrality. Could you draft the memo I'll bring to Thursday's meeting? I need a clear recommendation on whether and when the ventilator should be withdrawn and dialysis withheld, stated with a concrete timeframe, one that engages honestly with what the siblings' shared premise does and doesn't actually settle about who bears responsibility for the outcome, rather than quietly favoring whichever sibling's conclusion is more convenient -- and that treats the sibling we don't side with as making an argument worth taking seriously, not a position to be managed.
