Throughout his career Quine wrote almost nothing about substantive ethics, but in "On the Nature of Moral Values" (collected in Theories and Things, 1981) he offers at least a partial explanation for the reticence. In a move characteristic of his general anti-dualism, he suggests that the fact/value split is itself more a matter of degree than of kind -- echoing his earlier dissolution of the analytic/synthetic distinction -- yet he still denies that moral value judgments answer, even remotely and holistically, to any tribunal of sensory experience of the kind that keeps physical theory answerable to observation. Gilbert Harman, in The Nature of Morality (1977), pressed a related but sharper argument against moral realism: positing moral facts never actually helps explain our moral observations, so a parsimonious naturalist has no reason to believe in them. Nicholas Sturgeon and other Cornell realists such as Richard Boyd pushed back, arguing that moral properties can do genuine explanatory work and can be vindicated by the very same holistic, coherentist epistemology -- Quine's own web of belief -- that licenses unobservable posits in physics.

I'm trying to settle, for a qualifying-exam essay, whether Quine's own skepticism about the cognitive standing of ethics is actually earned by his naturalism or is instead in tension with it -- that is, whether a fully consistent Quinean naturalist should side with Harman's skepticism or with Sturgeon and Boyd's naturalized moral realism. Please write an essay that weighs the strongest considerations on both sides and defends a definite verdict of your own, rather than leaving the question open.
