Kurt Gödel's private notebooks and his conversations with Hao Wang reveal a thinker who described his own outlook as "rationalistic, idealistic, optimistic, and theological," and who set down among his considered philosophical theses that "the world is rational" and that "materialism is false." In letters to his mother, Marianne, he is reported to have pressed this rationalism into a direct argument for survival after death: a rationally ordered world, he suggested, could not sensibly permit a mind painstakingly developed over a lifetime of learning simply to be annihilated at death. Yet the same Gödel was a committed anti-mechanist about mental life. In his 1951 Gibbs lecture, he argued that either the mathematical powers of the human mind outstrip those of any finite algorithm, or else there exist absolutely unsolvable Diophantine problems that defeat human minds just as they would any machine.

Bringing these two strands of Gödel's thought to bear, how would he most plausibly have assessed today's "digital afterlife" services, which train AI systems on a deceased person's letters, recordings, and other data so that the bereaved can go on "conversing" with a simulated version of them? Would he likely have treated such systems as offering some independent support for his rationalist case for survival, or would his skepticism about minds being captured by any finite mechanical procedure have led him to dismiss them as a kind of category mistake — an ingenious puppet with no real claim to continuing the person it imitates? Answer by engaging with the actual structure of Gödel's own arguments, rather than resting on his general reputation as a believer in an afterlife.
