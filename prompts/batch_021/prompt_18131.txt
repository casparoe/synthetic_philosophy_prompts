In my school's ethics elective, our teacher introduced the idea of 'standing to blame' — roughly, the question of whether someone can be entitled to call out a wrongdoing even when the content of what they're saying is true — and it immediately reminded me of something that happened in our National Honor Society chapter last month. Our chapter president, Maya, is the one who certifies members' service hours before they're submitted anywhere official. She discovered that another member, Diego, had logged the same twenty hours of food-bank volunteering toward both his NHS requirement and a local scholarship whose application explicitly stated that hours already claimed elsewhere couldn't be counted, and she called him out for it in the chapter group chat, telling him he needed to amend the application. A few days later, someone found an old spreadsheet showing that Maya had done essentially the same thing herself the previous year, for a different award, and nothing had ever come of it. Two friends of mine, Noah and Elena, neither of whom was involved in either incident, got into a genuine argument about it over lunch, and I'd like help sorting out who actually had the better of it.

Noah opened by saying Maya had no right to call Diego out, since she had quietly done the same thing and never owned up to it: you don't get to enforce a rule on someone else that you yourself have broken. Elena answered that this conflates two different questions — whether the double-counting is wrong, and whether Maya is a hypocrite — and that pointing at Maya's record doesn't make Diego's claim any less true; that's just the 'you too' move, and if anything we should be glad somebody finally said something, since apparently nobody said it to Maya. Noah replied that he wasn't disputing the truth of the claim at all, only Maya's standing to be the one making it, since some other member with a clean record could level the identical accusation and be completely in the clear doing so. Elena pushed back that this doesn't actually cash out to anything: if the claim is true and about a rule everyone in the chapter is bound by, then anyone bound by it can invoke it, and requiring a clean record first would make almost all criticism, everywhere, impossible, since practically nobody's record is clean. Noah answered that blaming someone isn't merely stating a fact — it's holding them to a standard while implicitly treating yourself as exempt from it, and that inequality is a distinct wrong, present even when the underlying accusation is completely accurate. Elena's final move was that this shows, at most, that Maya should have owned up to her own past violation in the same breath, not that she had no business flagging Diego at all — and besides, Maya wasn't speaking as a private individual passing judgment on a moral equal, but as chapter president doing the specific job of certifying hours, which isn't a role that requires a spotless personal history to carry out correctly.

Could you go through this exchange move by move and say who is right at each point, rather than just declaring an overall winner? I'm especially interested in whether 'the accusation is false' and 'the accuser has no standing to make it' really are two separate failures, as Noah wants, or whether Elena's deflationary reading is correct and there's nothing left over once the accusation's truth is granted. I'd also like your take on Elena's closing point specifically — does acting in an official capacity like Maya's actually change the standing analysis, or is that beside the point? And finally, is there anything important that neither of them raised?
