I cover science and ideas for a living, which is apparently the reason an old university friend has asked me, rather than any of her fellow philosophers, to give the toast at her wedding next month: her theory is that if I can make a new physics paper sound interesting to a stranger, I can make G. E. Moore sound bearable to her family. She is finishing a doctorate on Moore, specifically on the final chapter of Principia Ethica, "The Ideal," where he argues that the two greatest goods human beings can imagine are personal affection and the contemplation of beautiful things, that a state of mind combining the two is worth far more than the sum of its parts — his "organic unities" point — and that even a beautiful world nobody will ever see is better than an ugly one nobody will ever see. Her fiancé, an art historian, is a division of philosophical labor she has not stopped pointing out: she gets personal affection, he gets beautiful objects.

Here is my problem. A good third of the room will be her graduate cohort, who will notice immediately if I flatten Moore's actual position to make a nicer speech, and his actual position is not the crowd-pleaser it sounds like: he was notably cool, even faintly suspicious, toward ordinary sexual and romantic passion, reserving his real enthusiasm for something closer to a contemplative admiration of a person's character. She has, in fact, written the one paper of her graduate career so far arguing more or less exactly this — that Moore's ideal smuggles a curiously thin, bloodless theory of romantic love inside its much richer theory of personal affection — and I have been warned, half-jokingly, that she will be listening for whether the toast shows I've actually read it.

Could you write the toast? It should be deliverable in four or five minutes and built around Moore's actual argument — the pairing of affection and beauty, the point about a whole exceeding the sum of its parts — rather than just name-checking him, and I'd like it to find the joke that's sitting right there in the gap between what a wedding toast is supposed to say about love and what Moore actually thought, without losing her uncle at table six, who has never heard of Principia Ethica and shouldn't have to. It should end up being a toast about the two of them and not a seminar on Moore, and it should avoid the mistake I keep making in my own drafts: offering up warm, contented feeling about how lovely the day is without the true perception and appropriate emotion Moore thought had to come together before any of it counted for anything.
