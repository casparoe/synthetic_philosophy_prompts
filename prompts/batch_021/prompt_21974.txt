Q: Ann and Bob are Bayesian agents considering the same event E. It is common knowledge that Ann's posterior for E is 0.7 and Bob's posterior for E is 0.4.
(a) State the common prior assumption and explain the role it plays in Aumann's "agreeing to disagree" result.
(b) Given the scenario above, what does Aumann's theorem tell us must be false?
(c) Explain why the theorem does NOT show that two people who both know each other to be perfectly rational Bayesians can never end up with different posteriors given the same evidence.

Student's answer:
(a) The common prior assumption (CPA) says that before recieving any private information, all agents assign the same probability distribution to the underlying state space; any disagreement in their later beliefs comes only from having different information, not from starting out with different beliefs. This is the assumption Aumann builds on in his 1976 paper, and it's what lets him show that if two people's posteriors on some event are common knowledge, those posteriors have to be equal. Without it the theorem doesn't get off the ground, since otherwise two people could reach totally different posteriors from the very same information just because they started from different priors.

(b) Because it's common knowledge that Ann's posterior is 0.7 and Bob's is 0.4, and 0.7 ≠ 0.4, Aumann's theorem tells us that Ann and Bob can't really have common knowledge of each other's rationality. If they were both known by the other to be perfect Bayesian reasoners updating off a shared prior, the theorem forces their posteriors to coincide, so this mismatch means at least one of them isn't actually being rational.

(c) Even when two people are both known to be rational Bayesians, the agreement theorem's force depends on it being common knowledge not just what their posteriors are, but on their whole information partitions being mutually known — each has to know exactly what evidence the other could have received, in every state. That's an extremely strong requirement that basically never holds outside of toy models: in real conversations people reveal bottom-line opinions ("I'm 80% on X") without revealing the fine-grained partition that produced that number, and the proof doesn't go through unless the partitions themselves, not just the resulting numbers, are common knowledge. That's really the main reason smart, honest people keep disagreeing even after "agreeing to disagree" talks.
