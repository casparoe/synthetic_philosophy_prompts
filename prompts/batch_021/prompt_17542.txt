I'm a PhD candidate in Buddhist philosophy, and for almost a decade I've also been the senior student at a small, fairly rigorous Madhyamaka study center, where my teacher trained her core students on Nāgārjuna and Candrakīrti the way other centers drill breath counting. She died three weeks ago, partway through a year-long seminar that had just reached the Nirvana chapter of the Mūlamadhyamakakārikā — the tetralemma verses asking whether the Tathāgata exists after death, does not exist, both, or neither, and refusing every option in turn — so several of us had, days earlier, been sitting with her over the very passage we now have to reckon with for real. The center's board has asked three of her senior students to speak at Saturday's memorial and has given me the "dharma talk" slot specifically, on the theory that I'm the one who can be trusted not to turn it into either group therapy or a seminar.

The difficulty isn't just tact, though. She spent years training us out of exactly the consolations this room will reach for: that she's at peace somewhere now, that she's watching over us, or — the move a clever student might substitute instead, thinking it more rigorous — that she has simply ceased, full stop, the way an Epicurean would put it. The whole point of the tetralemma as she taught it is that none of the four positions survives analysis, and she was especially insistent that "neither" is not some safe fifth resting place, as if vagueness itself were the correct answer rather than one more thing to let go of. I also don't want the speech to dodge into the older move of the Buddha's silence about "unanswered questions," since her actual point was that each alternative can be shown to fail, not merely that asking is unhelpful or impolite. Meanwhile close to half the room — her mother especially, who is eighty-six and Catholic — has no idea what catuṣkoṭi means and is owed something that actually comforts a grieving parent, not a logic lesson dressed up as a eulogy.

Could you draft the remarks themselves? They should run to about seven minutes read aloud, they can't lean on any of the moves above for their comfort, and I'd like them to do something positive with the two-truths distinction and with her own favorite point from that last seminar — that Nāgārjuna treats saṃsāra and nirvāṇa as not ultimately distinct — rather than just avoiding pitfalls. It should read as something a room full of grieving non-philosophers can actually sit through, not as a paper with the footnotes taken out.
