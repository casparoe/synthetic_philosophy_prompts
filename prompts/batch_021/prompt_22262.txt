For some time now, with no formal training in either neuroscience or philosophy, I have been puzzling over so-called split-brain patients: people whose corpus callosum -- the main bundle of fibers joining the brain's two hemispheres -- was surgically severed decades ago as a last-resort treatment for severe epilepsy. The classic demonstrations associated with Sperry and Gazzaniga seem to show two separate minds operating side by side in a single brain: in one famous trial, a snow scene is flashed only to a patient's right hemisphere and a chicken's claw only to the left; asked to point to a related picture, the patient's left hand chooses a shovel while the right hand chooses a chicken, and the speaking half of the brain -- which never saw the snow scene -- calmly invents a reason for the shovel involving cleaning up after chickens. On the strength of results like this, Sperry himself concluded that each hemisphere amounts to its own separate center of conscious experience, the two running in parallel within one skull. A 2017 study complicated the picture for me: it found that the same kind of patients could correctly detect and locate objects anywhere in their visual field no matter which hand, or which hemisphere's speech, was used to answer, even though they still could not identify what a contralateral object actually was -- as if where something is can cross over between the hemispheres even when what it is cannot. I would like an essay that takes both sets of findings seriously and argues its way to a definite verdict: are these patients best understood as two separate subjects of experience, as a single subject whose internal information-sharing is merely imperfect, or as a case where the question "one mind or two?" has no correct answer at all? Please do not end by declaring the matter simply unresolved -- commit to one of these three positions and defend it against the other two.
