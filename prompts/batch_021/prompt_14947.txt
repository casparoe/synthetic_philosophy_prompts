For a unit on formal epistemology in my graduate seminar, I'd like a dialogue that does more than restate the textbook version of the Dutch Book argument: write one, set at a racetrack, between two philosophers of probability who notice that a fellow bettor's odds across the field in the next race imply degrees of belief that don't sum to one, leaving her vulnerable to a Dutch book. The first philosopher treats this as vindicating the classical argument for probabilism: a set of bets can be built, each one fair by her own lights, that jointly guarantee her a loss no matter which horse wins, and the converse Dutch book theorem shows that no such trap can be laid for anyone whose credences are already coherent, so her incoherence is a genuine defect of rationality and not a mere quirk of taste. The second philosopher objects that this only works if the bettor is pictured as accepting each bet the moment it is offered; someone sharp enough to see the whole combination coming would simply decline the package, lose nothing, and thereby show nothing about her rationality one way or the other. Have the two press each other on one question in particular: if the first philosopher relocates the irrationality somewhere that no longer depends on any bet actually being placed or lost, has she saved the classical argument, or has she quietly swapped it for a different one that never needed bets or bookies to begin with? Let the exchange end without a tidy resolution, but with each side having pinned down exactly what the other still owes an account of.
