At a wedding reception a few weeks ago, two acquaintances of mine fell into a disagreement I still haven't been able to settle. The background: Dana had agreed to water Rosa's rare orchid collection while Rosa was away for three weeks; several of the orchids died anyway. (1) Felix said Dana's failure to water them straightforwardly caused their deaths -- if she had watered them as promised, they would have lived -- and that she owes Rosa for the loss. (2) Dana replied that Rosa's dog-walker and her adult son also visited during those three weeks without watering anything, and that either of them watering the orchids would have kept them alive just as surely as her watering would have; if "would have prevented it" is what makes an omission a cause, all three non-waterings are equally causes, which is absurd -- so whatever picks her out in particular can't be causation itself. (3) Felix answered that Dana, unlike the other two, had actually agreed to do it, and that this is exactly what makes her inaction, and not theirs, the cause: hers was the one non-watering that was supposed to be a watering. (4) Dana said that just relocates the problem -- pointing to her promise explains why we blame her, but that's a fact about obligation, not about the causal structure of the world, and once "cause" is doing no more than tracking who had a duty, there's no further causal fact left for it to name; we're just dressing up a moral verdict in causal-sounding language.

Neither budged, and it reminded me of stories I've covered about engineering failures -- a bridge inspector who never finishes the checklist, a lab safety officer who skips the drill -- where the same move and countermove seem to recur.
