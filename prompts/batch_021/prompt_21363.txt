I am a PhD student in linguistics working on common ground and convention in pragmatics, which has had me returning to David Lewis's *Convention* fairly often this year for its account of how a regularity can become self-perpetuating without anyone ever deciding on it or enforcing it. Last weekend a disagreement broke out between two friends of mine, Renata, who works in transportation planning, and Marcus, who studied philosophy as an undergraduate, over the etiquette of merging at a lane closure. The relevant background: traffic engineering generally favors the "zipper merge," in which drivers in the closing lane stay put until the very last car length and then alternate one by one with the through lane, over the far more common practice of merging as early as possible once the warning sign appears. Several transportation departments have run public information campaigns promoting the zipper merge, generally with limited success, and drivers who use the empty lane up to the taper and slot in at the front are routinely honked at and tailgated, including by drivers several cars back who stand to gain nothing personally from that particular driver's compliance.

The disagreement went roughly as follows. (1) Marcus opened by claiming that early-merge etiquette is a textbook Lewis convention: an arbitrary solution to a genuine coordination problem, sustained purely by the fact that everyone expects everyone else to follow it, with no need for law or enforcement. (2) Renata objected that a convention sustained only by mutual advantage ought to be easy to shift once the superior equilibrium becomes common knowledge, which is exactly what the public campaigns tried to achieve; since nothing has changed, and since the hostility directed at late mergers looks like moral outrage at a "cheater" rather than mere resignation at a failed coordination, something beyond mutual expectation must be doing the enforcing. (3) Marcus replied that Lewis never required a convention to be efficient or easy to dislodge: a coordination equilibrium can be badly suboptimal and still perfectly stable, because unilaterally deviating while everyone else still expects early merging really does impose a cost on them, so the anger is just what an ordinary coordination problem looks like from the inside, not evidence of some separate, moralized enforcement mechanism. (4) Renata answered that this cannot be the whole story, since drivers several cars back, who lose nothing personally from one particular driver's late merge, are visibly indignant too; that kind of impartial, third-party reaction, rather than mere private annoyance at being personally inconvenienced, is exactly the signature of a norm that has become something stronger than a bare convention, however it may have originated.

Neither of them conceded anything, and I did not think it was my place to referee, given how much time I have spent with the primary text this year.
