I've been down a rabbit hole this week reporting on weird longtermist decision theory for a piece, and I keep running into this idea called "evidential cooperation in large worlds" (ECL). I think Caspar Oesterheld originally floated a version of it under a clunkier name back around 2017, and people at the Center on Long-Term Risk have been building it out since. The rough idea, as I understand it: if the universe (or multiverse) is big enough that there's a huge number of causally disconnected civilizations out there who can never affect each other, you should still act cooperatively toward agents with totally different values than you, because your choice to cooperate is *evidence* about what similarly-reasoning agents elsewhere will choose, and since value systems aren't perfectly zero-sum, there's gains from trade to be had acausally. It's a genuinely elegant argument on the surface, but every time I try to explain it to my editor I realize its actually held up by six or seven separate load bearing assumptions, and I don't have a great feel for which of those is the weakest link.

So here's where I could use your help. Below is my list of the main premises I think the ECL argument depends on. Could you rate each one, independently of the others, on how plausible/solid you personally find it -- 0 meaning basically indefensible, 1 meaning as solid as premises get in this kind of philosophy? I'm not asking for a verdict on ECL overall, just your credence in each individual plank.

1. The universe/multiverse is spatially infinite, or otherwise contains vastly many causally isolated agents.
2. Evidential (acausal) decision-theoretic reasoning is action-guiding here, not just a curiosity that causal decision theorists get to shrug off.
3. Your decision is robustly correlated with the decisions of relevantly similar but differently-valued agents elsewhere, surviving the sheer diversity of minds presumably out there.
4. There are real gains from trade between different value systems (helping other values is often cheap relative to the value it creates for them).
5. We have, or could realistically get, enough epistemic access to the distribution of other value systems in the universe to act on this instead of just guessing.
6. Aggregating the interests of an effectively infinite population of other agents doesn't collapse into the standard paradoxes of infinite ethics.
7. Deliberately acting on ECL actually changes your behavior relative to just being a generically cooperative, pluralistic agent under moral uncertainty.
