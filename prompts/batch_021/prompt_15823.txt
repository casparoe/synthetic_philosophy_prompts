So I'm giving the toast at my friend Daniel's wedding this Saturday, and last night my friend Priya (she's doing her philosophy PhD, so she never lets these things go) and I ended up arguing about G.E. Moore for like two hours instead of finishing the seating chart. I told her I wanted to build the toast around that stretch of Principia Ethica, chapter six I think, "The Ideal," where he says the best things we can even imagine are personal affection and the appreciation of beauty, and that when you actually get both in one state of mind at once — really loving someone AND rightly seeing what's good in them — its worth way more than either piece on its own, that's his whole organic unity thing. Priya's issue is that somewhere close by Moore also has this fussy caveat where the love or admiration only counts for full value if its tracking something real about the person, so if you're admiring qualities they don't actually have, the whole thing is worth a lot less, maybe even bad, even though the warm feeling part might be identical. She says building a wedding toast on that is basically insane because it means the toast has to imply "I hope you two aren't wrong about each other," which is a weird and honestly kind of ominous thing to bring up at someone's wedding. I think she's missing something and that you can turn it into a genuinely moving line instead of a backhanded one, I just couldn't quite land it in the argument to shut her up.

Here's the complication though: Daniel and Meg were best friends for like four years before they dated, so the "personal affection" angle isn't just a romance thing, it's a friendship thing too, which I think actually helps. But the groom's side is six of his econometrics PhD friends who will 100% clock the Moore reference and try to start a fight about the naturalistic fallacy over cake, while the bride's side is entirely non-philosophy people, including her very devout grandmother, who is not going to want love described to her as "a state of consciousness." And Priya will be sitting at table four the whole time, so she'll know immediately if I just quote the nice half of Moore and quietly drop the true-belief caveat to dodge the problem. Can you actually write the toast, something like four minutes read aloud, that uses Moore's specific claims — the affection-plus-beauty combo, the organic unity bit, and the true-belief caveat — as real load-bearing content rather than a name-drop, and that resolves the tension Priya raised instead of tiptoeing around it? It still has to sound like a toast for two specific people and not a seminar.
