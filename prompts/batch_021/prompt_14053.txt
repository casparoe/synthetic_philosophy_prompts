Over dinner last night, a friend and I spent nearly two hours arguing about whether I should leave my hospital job for a longtermist AI-safety research position funded by an effective-altruist grantmaker. She invoked consequentialist cluelessness: because the civilization-scale effects of either path ripple outward for centuries in ways too diffuse for either of us to trace, let alone determine whether they are net positive or negative, she argued that the expected-value case for the AI-safety option collapses, leaving the more legible, near-term good of clinical medicine as the safer bet. I countered that the same reasoning undermines her preferred option too, since a medical career also has countless unforeseeable long-run effects on population size, resource use, and even on who ends up around to shape the future. Rather than adjudicate who won, I would like your own analysis: what are the most important implications of consequentialist cluelessness for a decision like this one, and does Hilary Greaves' distinction between simple cluelessness, which can safely be set aside, and complex cluelessness, which cannot, give either side of our disagreement a genuine advantage — or does it instead show that neither of us can rationally prefer one path to the other on consequentialist grounds alone?
