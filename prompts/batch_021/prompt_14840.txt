As a retired experimental physicist who has spent much of retirement finally reading philosophy in earnest, I've been working through the literature on peer disagreement, and Adam Elga's Equal Weight View troubles me on quantitative rather than purely conceptual grounds. In my own field, when two well-regarded laboratories report conflicting measurements of the same constant, the community's eventual combined estimate is never obtained by simply averaging the two reported values: it depends on each measurement's precision, on how much of each lab's uncertainty is independent rather than shared systematic error, and it can end up more extreme than either original figure once you properly account for what each result implies about the likely direction of the other's mistake. Yet philosophers writing on the Equal Weight View seem to treat "splitting the difference" between a peer's credence and one's own as the default rational response, debating only whether that response is rationally required. Please make the strongest case you can that this is a substantive error rather than a harmless simplification: a properly Bayesian treatment of peer disagreement will often recommend a posterior more extreme than either party's private opinion, never a split-the-difference average, so Elga-style conciliationists have mischaracterized what genuinely conciliating requires. This should not be read as vindicating the steadfast view either, since ignoring a peer's credence outright is no more defensible than naively averaging it with one's own.
