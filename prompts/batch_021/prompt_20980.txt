Ok so quick backstory: I never went to uni for any of this, I got into philosophy a few years back purely through late-night wikipedia/SEP spirals, usually triggered by arguing with randoms online. The one that really hooked me was a debate about whether an all-knowing god would have to already know every single true thing that will ever happen, and somebody linked me the Stanford Encyclopedia page on Fitch's paradox of knowability. As far as I understand it, that paradox shows that if you believe every truth is at least possible to know, you get forced (through this weird little proof involving a sentence like "p is true and nobody knows it") into the conclusion that literally everything true is already known by someone, wich is obviously nuts. Ever since then I've been kind of obsessed with how people try to wiggle out of it.

Anyway, here's the actual situation: I help run a monthly "philosophy in the pub" meetup in my city, and next month's guest speaker has built basically her whole research career around one specific fix for this paradox. Her move (from what I've read of her papers) is that the "knowability principle" shouldn't cover literally every truth, only what she calls "Cartesian" propositions -- roughly, truths that wouldn't get falsified by the mere fact of someone knowing them -- and since the troublemaking "it's true and nobody knows it" sentence doesn't count as Cartesian, it was never a real counterexample to her restricted version of the principle in the first place.

I'm moderating the Q&A after her talk and really don't want it to turn into her just re-explaining the proof to a room full of people who already read the same wiki page I did. Could you draft me 8 to 10 sharp questions? For each of those, throw in your best guess at how she'd actually respond plus a follow-up that keeps the pressure on instead of letting her coast on "well, every solution has some cost."
