So this is a slightly odd one, but I think you're the right person to ask. I sit on my university's research ethics committee, and we've got a protocol that has split the room right down the middle. A colleague over in psychology wants approval to run a properly controlled test of a local self-described medium -- basically redoing, with modern safeguards, what Sidgwick and the early Society for Psychical Research used to do in their sittings: pre-registered protocol, confederates, catch trials, the works. About half the commitee thinks approving this makes the university look ridiculous no matter how careful the methodology is, and that we shouldn't be spending institutional credibility on a hypothesis with a prior this low. The other half of us think refusing a good-faith, falsifiable test just because the claim sounds silly is its own kind of dogmatism, and that Sidgwicks instinct -- take it seriously enough to actually go check, under conditions rigorous enough that a negative result would count as a real disconfirmation, and report it honestly either way -- was basically the right one. There's also a genuine consent wrinkle: for the test to mean anything the medium can't be told the exact form the controls will take, and a couple of members are treating that alone as a dealbreaker.

I've been re-reading Sidgwick for a graduate seminar on Victorian moral philosophy, and it keeps nagging at me that his whole psychical research project -- the sittings, the surveys, the stuff that fed into things like the Census of Hallucinations -- was never idle curiosity for him. He was hoping to find something that would settle the "dualism of practical reason" he couldn't close by argument alone. Which makes me wonder whether wanting this sort of research to succeed should make a committee more suspicious of a proposal like this, not less, and whether that consideration cuts for or against my colleague.

Anyway -- could you draft the actual memo I'd circulate before we vote next week: a real recommendation (approve, reject, or approve with conditions), reasons my colleague could actually push back on point by point, and something honest about the strongest version of whichever side you don't land on.
