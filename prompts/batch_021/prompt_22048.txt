My father suffered a massive intracerebral hemorrhage seven weeks ago, and after several days of testing the neurology team declared him dead by neurological criteria — "brain dead," as the attending put it — before we withdrew support. During one of the family meetings, a resident mentioned, almost in passing, that if he had instead suffered a cardiac arrest that could not be reversed, the hospital would have used an entirely different bedside protocol, waiting out a brief observation period after his heart stopped rather than running the battery of brainstem reflex tests and the apnea test, yet would have arrived at what she insisted was exactly the same conclusion: that he had died. That remark has stayed with me ever since, because it treats death as a single biological event that simply becomes visible through two different windows — the brain or the heart — rather than as two distinguishable conditions that medicine has, for practical reasons, decided to call by the same name.

Partly to make sense of what happened, and partly out of a stubborn engineering habit of wanting definitions to actually hold together, I have since been reading around the subject. I gather that this supposed unity between the circulatory and whole-brain standards, which is written into statutes like the Uniform Determination of Death Act, is far more contested among philosophers and bioethicists than the hospital staff let on. I have come across Alan Shewmon's case reports of patients diagnosed as whole-brain dead whose bodies nevertheless went on healing wounds, fighting off infections, growing, and in a handful of documented cases sustaining a pregnancy for weeks or months before delivery, and I have seen Robert Truog and Franklin Miller argue that the whole-brain standard functions less as a description of any real biological fact than as a kind of legal fiction built to accommodate transplant medicine.

Could you walk me through the actual philosophical argument for why irreversible loss of all brain function and irreversible loss of circulation are supposed to be two different routes to detecting the very same underlying condition — something like the permanent loss of the organism's "integrated functioning" as a whole — and then give an equally careful account of exactly where critics such as Shewmon and Truog think that shared rationale breaks down? I would rather have the actual structure of the arguments on each side than another reminder that reasonable people disagree.
