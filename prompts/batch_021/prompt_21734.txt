I keep running into Skolem's paradox in slightly different guises, and I'd like to get past the point of being able to recite its resolution without really feeling why the resolution works. The setup: the downward Löwenheim-Skolem theorem guarantees that if ZFC is consistent, it has a countable model M, yet M still satisfies the sentence "there exist uncountable sets," including some set that M thinks is the power set of ω. The standard move is to invoke what set theorists call the non-absoluteness of uncountability: there is a genuine bijection between M's version of ω and M's version of P(ω) sitting in the background universe, it's just not among the sets that happen to be elements of M. I follow that much, but I want a more gears-level account of why it's specifically an existence claim like "no bijection exists" that comes apart between the inner and outer perspective this way, when a claim like "this set has exactly seventeen elements" doesn't — what is it about the logical shape of the two claims that makes one hostage to which model you're evaluating it in and not the other? And once that distinction is clear, I'd like your take on Putnam's further move: treating this same relativity of set-theoretic notions as license for a much broader model-theoretic argument against metaphysical realism about reference — a move that Bays has argued misapplies the very theorem it leans on, though Button has pushed back in Putnam's defense. Does Putnam's extension survive that charge, or does it trade on a conflation that the original set-theoretic case doesn't actually support?
