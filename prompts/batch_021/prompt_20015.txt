I'm a retired analytical chemist (spent thirty-odd years running mass spec in an industrial lab), and the last few years I've fallen into philosophy pretty hard with no formal training, just library books and too much free time. It started with Hofstadter's Godel, Escher, Bach, like everyone's gateway drug into this stuff, and lately I've been chewing through an old decision theory textbook that has a chapter on epistemic logic. That chapter explains that on the standard "possible worlds" treatment of knowledge, a perfectly rational agent turns out to be "logically omniscient" - they automatically know every single logical consequence of anything they know, including every mathematical truth that follows from axioms they already accept. The book admits this is wildly unrealistic for actual people and lists a bunch of proposed fixes, awareness logics, impossible worlds, step logics, that sort of thing, to make the models more true to life.

Here's the bit that's been bugging me on my evening walks: doesn't Godel's incompleteness theorem already show, back in 1931, that logical omniscience isn't just unrealistic but flat out impossible even for a perfect or infinite mind? After all, Godel proved that in any system strong enough for arithmetic there are true statements that never follow from the axioms, no matter how ideal the reasoner examining them is. If thats right, the "ideal reasoner" that game theorists and decision theorists lean on constantly, all that common-knowledge-of-rationality machinery, isn't resting on an idealization thats merely too strong for humans, its resting on something that's internally broken. I ran this by an old colleague from the lab, a spectroscopist who's sharper than me with formal stuff, and neither of us could spot the hole in it. So why does everybody, textbook authors included, keep proposing ever more elaborate patches instead of just admitting the foundation has a Godel-shaped hole in it?
