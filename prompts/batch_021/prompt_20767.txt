For a column on how philosophical soundbites take on a life of their own, I keep returning to Karl Popper's most misappropriated line, and I'd like a sharper counterfactual than the usual one. In his 1976 intellectual autobiography, Unended Quest, Popper called natural selection "not a testable scientific theory, but a metaphysical research programme"; a few years later he publicly recanted, explaining exactly what had persuaded him that the theory did, after all, make risky, testable claims — a recantation that intelligent-design writers still leave out when they quote him. That misuse isn't what interests me, though: Popper died in 1994, well before evolutionary biologists themselves split over Kevin Laland's campaign for an "extended evolutionary synthesis" (built on niche construction, developmental plasticity, and non-genetic inheritance) against traditionalists such as Jerry Coyne, who argue standard neo-Darwinian theory already covers this ground. Reconstructing the specific reasoning behind Popper's recantation, rather than the slogan everyone quotes, which side of that dispute do you think he would have judged to be doing the more scientific work, and why?
