Question: In 1956, G. E. M. Anscombe wrote a pamphlet opposing Oxford University's decision to award Harry Truman an honorary degree, on the grounds that in ordering the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki he had committed murder.
(a) Reconstruct Anscombe's argument, making clear what work is done by the distinction between intending an effect—as an end, or as a means chosen to reach that end—and merely foreseeing it as a side effect of one's action.
(b) Explain why, on her view, this distinction still matters even for someone who grants that the bombings shortened the war and saved lives overall.
(c) Critics have objected that Anscombe's position, followed through consistently, collapses into pacifism, since almost any sustained bombing campaign will foreseeably kill civilians. Briefly say whether you think this objection succeeds, and why.

Student answer: Anscombe's pamphlet was written to stop Oxford giving Truman an honorary degree, and her central charge is that he was a murderer, not merely someone who made a regrettable wartime decision. Her argument turns on the doctrine of double effect, which she introduced to distinguish two ways an agent can be responsible for a bad outcome: intending it, either as the very end being sought or as a means chosen to get there, versus merely foreseeing it as a side effect of doing something else. Only the former counts as fully attributable to the agent in the way that matters for calling an act murder; the latter can be permissible if there is proportionate reason for the act as a whole. Anscombe's claim about Hiroshima and Nagasaki is that the civilian deaths were not a side effect of bombing some other, legitimate target: the cities and their populations were the target, precisely because killing large numbers of civilians was the mechanism by which Japan's government was supposed to be terrorized into surrendering. So the killing was intended, as a means, and that is what makes it murder on her view, regardless of how the operation is described in strategic terms.

On (b), Anscombe would say that a good further end does not launder an intrinsically wrongful means: you may not do evil that good may come of it, and the fact that the bombings arguably shortened the war and saved a great many lives on both sides does not change the moral character of what was chosen as a means to that end. This is part of a wider disagreement she has with any approach that would simply weigh total lives saved against total lives lost, treating the moral status of an act as fixed entirely by its consequences.

On (c), I actually think the pacifism objection succeeds to a significant extent, because Anscombe's real complaint about Hiroshima and Nagasaki is fundamentally a matter of scale: the number of civilians killed was so enormous, and so far in excess of anything a legitimate military target would require, that it is hard to imagine her raising the same objection to a much smaller operation with an otherwise similar structure. If the underlying wrongness is really about the harm being disproportionate to the military benefit, then her position just reduces to an unusually strict reading of the proportionality requirement in just war theory, which in practice will end up condemning most large-scale bombing campaigns and looks very close to pacifism about aerial warfare specifically.
