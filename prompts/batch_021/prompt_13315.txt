I spent about thirty years as an exploration geophysicist before I retired, and now that I've got time on my hands my daughter talked me into reading some philosophy of science, starting with Kuhn's *Structure of Scientific Revolutions*. Funny enough, people used to throw the term "paradigm shift" around constantly at conferences and I don't think half of them had actually read the book. Anyway, it got me thinking back to something my old structural geology professor used to say about the plate tectonics revolution: that Wegener basically had the goods already in 1912 — the jigsaw fit of the continents, matching fossils and rock sequences on either side of the Atlantic, glacial striations lining up across Gondwana — and yet almost the whole field, especially in the US, dismissed it as what one skeptic apparently called "geopoetry," right up until the old fixist generation had mostly retired or died off and a new generation raised on paleomagnetic data just took mobilism for granted. Kuhn even quotes Planck's old line in the book, something to the effect that a new scientific truth doesn't so much convince its opponents as outlive them.

So here's my question: doesn't that make plate tectonics a pretty clean real-world example of what incommensurability actually amounts to — the fixists and mobilists were working from pictures of the earth different enough that people on one side genuinely could not be argued into the other, so the "revolution" was really won by attrition and generational turnover rather then by anyone's mind being changed by better evidence or arguments? And if that's how it played out even in a hard, quantitative field like geophysics, doesn't it follow that calling plate tectonics "truer" than fixism is mostly just something the winning side gets to say in hindsight, since there was never a neutral, rational procedure for choosing between the two paradigms to begin with? That seems like a strange thing for a working science to rest on, so I'd genuinely like to know: am I reading Kuhn (and this bit of geology's history) right, or is there a hole in this somewhere?
