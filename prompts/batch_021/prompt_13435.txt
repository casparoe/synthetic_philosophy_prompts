But there is no present King of France, so a sentence containing this phrase should either lack a truth value or be flat out meaningless. Yet the sentence seems perfectly meaningful, and most speakers' first instinct is to call it false rather than say it lacks a truth value — so something's gone wrong on the naive view.

(b) Russell's fix is to deny that "the present King of France" is a referring term in logical form at all. "The F is G" really abbreviates "there is an x such that x is F, and x is G" — a general, existential claim rather than a subject-predicate one. Since no x is presently King of France, the existential claim is false, so the whole sentence is false rather than truth-valueless, and excluded middle is preserved either way. This differs from Frege, who held the sentence has a sense but no truth-value because the description fails to refer, and from Strawson, who held the sentence is neither true nor false because using it carries a false presupposition rather than a false assertion.

(c) This machinery also explains why "The present King of France is not bald" has two readings, which Russell calls primary and secondary occurrence — the same contrast Donnellan later draws between the referential and attributive use of a description. On the primary occurrence (Donnellan's referential reading), the description takes wide scope over the negation: "there is a present King of France, and he's not bald," which is false, since it asserts existence outright. On the secondary occurrence (Donnellan's attributive reading), the negation takes wide scope over the whole claim: "it's not the case that there's a present King of France who's bald," which is true, since there's no king around to make the inner claim true in the first place.
