So in my decision theory seminar we covered the absent-minded driver problem last week. Quick recap in case its useful: a driver needs to take the second of two exits to get home, but the exits are completely identical and she has no memory of whether she's already passed one, so whatever rule she uses has to apply the same way at both -- something like 'keep driving with probability a, exit with probability 1-a.' The first exit is worth 0 to her, missing both and looping back through the next town is worth 1, and the second exit is worth 4. The textbook answer is that the best fixed a is 2/3, for an expected payoff of 4/3 -- better than always exiting first (0) or never exiting (1).

Two people in my study group, Marcus and Nadia, argued about what this actually shows. (1) Marcus said 2/3 settles it: that's the number that maximizes expected payoff, so that's just what a rational driver does, end of story. (2) Nadia said sure, that's the best plan, but the driver never gets to consult a plan -- she's stuck at an intersection with no memory of having passed one, so whatever she does has to be justified by what she believes right then. She said ordinary conditionalization gives you 1/(1+a) for the odds you're at the first intersection and a/(1+a) for the second, given you're using probability a of continuing. Plug those in, and no matter what a is, 'keep going' beats 'exit' once you weight the two locations: continuing from the first intersection still gets you the 4 (you'll just take the second exit), so exiting there for a guaranteed 0 is a bad trade, and that swamps the fact that continuing from the second intersection only gets you the 1 instead of the 4 you'd get by exiting there. So the in-the-moment calculation says keep driving forever, full stop, which flatly contradicts '2/3.' (3) Marcus said that has to be a mistake somewhere -- it can't be rational to guarantee yourself the worst outcome on the table -- and whatever the plan says has to win, since the whole point of planning ahead is so you don't have to re-derive the answer, possibly wrongly, under time pressure at the wheel. (4) Nadia didn't budge: she said that's begging the question, since if nobody can say what's actually wrong with reasoning from your beliefs in the moment, we don't have an account of what she's supposed to do when she's actually driving with no memory -- and a theory of rationality that only works before you have any information isnt a complete theory of rational choice.

Neither of them would budge and honestly I can't tell who's right. Can you go through this move by move?
