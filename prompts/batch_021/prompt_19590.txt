The question was: "Explain the multiple realizability argument against mind-brain type-identity theory, and explain how functionalism is supposed to improve on type-identity theory in light of it. Then briefly present ONE influential objection to functionalism itself, and assess whether you think it succeeds."

Here's the student's answer:

"Type-identity theory says a mental state type, like pain, just is a physical state type, e.g. C-fiber firing. The multiple realizability argument, due to Putnam, points out that this can't be right: an octopus, a Martian, or a sufficiently advanced robot could all be in pain despite having totally different physical setups than us. If pain = C-fiber firing, then nothing without C-fibers could ever be in pain, which seems false. So type-identity theory is too restrictive.

Functionalism fixes this by defining mental states not by what they're made of but by the causal role they play - what typically causes them, what they cause, and how they relate to other mental states. Pain is whatever state occupies the 'pain role' (caused by tissue damage, causes avoidance behavior and the belief something is wrong, etc), and that role can be filled by C-fibers in humans, something else in octopuses, silicon in robots, and so on. Because mental states can't be pinned to one physical realizer across all these cases, functionalism holds that mental states aren't physical states at all, just the abstract functional roles themselves, which is exactly what lets them be multiply realizable in the first place.

One famous objection to functionalism is Searle's Chinese Room. Imagine the entire population of China, each person given the job of acting like a single neuron and passing signals to each other by phone according to a simple rulebook, together implementing the exact functional organization of a mind for an hour. Even though the system satisfies the functional description perfectly, it seems crazy to say this vast telephone network has genuine feelings or conscious experience - there's no subject there having the experience. I think this objection succeeds: it shows functionalism captures something real about the organization of mental states, but not what makes a state actually feel like anything, which functionalism was supposed to explain in the first place."
