I'm about a third of the way into a courtroom novel and I've hit a scene I keep rewriting because I don't actually understand the underlying logic well enough to fake it convincingly. My protagonist clerks for a three-judge appeals panel, and the case she's assigned has this infuriating structural quirk I fell down a rabbit hole about last week — I think its called the "doctrinal paradox" or the "discursive dilemma," some law review footnote used both terms like they were interchangeable and I never sorted out if they actually are the same thing.

Here's the setup, in case it helps: it's a breach of contract suit, and the defendant is liable only if (a) a valid contract existed AND (b) the defendant breached it. Judge one thinks both (a) and (b) are true, so she votes liable. Judge two thinks (a) is true but (b) is false, so he votes not liable. Judge three thinks (a) is false, though she'd agree (b) is true if there had been a contract — so she also votes not liable. Now tally the votes on (a) and (b) separately: each gets a 2-1 majority of "true," which by the panel's own stated rule means liable. But tally the bottom-line votes directly instead and its 2-1 for not liable. Same three people, same case file, opposite verdicts depending on how you do the arithmetic.

What I want is the scene where two of the clerks — call them Priya and Marcus — get into it over which method the panel should actually use to write the opinion, before it goes back to the judges to sign off. Priya should push hard for tallying premise-by-premise, since that's the only method that actually respects each judge's real reasoning on the legal questions the case turns on, rather than just asking who "wins" without saying why. Marcus should push just as hard for going with the direct vote on the outcome, since the premise-by-premise method basically installs the one judge whose overall view happens to match the winning combination of premises, even though the other two both rejected that exact bottom-line conclusion. I want genuine back and forth — Marcus needs a real answer to the charge that outcome-voting produces a legally incoherent opinion (the panel majority-believes both ingredients of liability yet rules "not liable"), and Priya needs a real answer to the charge that her method lets a minority position win by getting split into pieces small enough to each grab a majority on their own. Neither of them should clearly come out on top; as far as I can tell actual legal and social-choice theorists are still fighting about this one. Keep it to maybe 500-600 words of actual dialogue, with a bit of character voice, not two position papers wearing a trenchcoat.
