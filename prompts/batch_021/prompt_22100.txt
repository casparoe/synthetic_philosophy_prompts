I spent 34 years as a hospital epidemiologist before I retired, and one thing that stuck with me from training was Rothman's old "causal pie" model, where a disease outcome needs a whole pie of several "component causes" before it happens and no single slice does anything by itself. I never thought of that as philosophy until this past year, when I started reading the actual literature for fun — Mackie on INUS conditions, Lewis's counterfactual-dependence account, Woodward's interventionist alternative — and realized Rothman's pies were basically a folk version of exactly the argument philosophers are still having, minus any answer to which test you're supposed to use (counterfactual dependence, or intervening on one factor while holding the rest fixed) when two pies finish baking at almost the same moment. Anyway, I'm apparently that person at dinner parties now, and it's turned out to be more than a hobby, because I still sit as the outside member on my old hospital's Patient Safety & Root Cause Analysis Committee, and this month we've got a case that is, near as I can tell, that exact unresolved question wearing a hospital gown.

Here's the case, details changed. A patient with severe sepsis had a critical antibiotic delayed about six hours by an order-entry error — a clear process failure on its own terms. Roughly five hours after the antibiotic should have been given, the same patient's previously undiagnosed aortic aneurysm ruptured, and she died within the hour; the surgeons are confident that once it ruptured nothing could have saved her regardless of how the sepsis was being handled, and the ICU numbers suggest the septic shock was independently on track to kill her on about the same timescale. Our policy says a death only counts as a reportable Serious Safety Event — which triggers mandatory disclosure to the family and goes on the nurse's record — if the error was "a but-for cause of the outcome," and it never defines that phrase further. Half the commitee reads it as a strict counterfactual test: there's no nearby world where fixing the antibiotic error saves her life, the aneurysm gets her regardless, so it isn't a but-for cause, log it as a process deviation and move on. The other half, including our chief of medicine awkwardly enough, thinks that's the wrong question, and that you should instead ask what happens if you intervene on just the antibiotic timing, holding everything else fixed, aneurysm included — and under that test the delay clearly made her septic course worse and probably changed how and when she died even if not whether. I find both readings genuinely defensible and I definately don't want to paper over that.

Could you draft the short memo I'll circulate before next Wednesday's meeting? I need it to actually recommend how this case gets classified and reported, propose some standing language for how we read "but-for cause" in future cases so we're not refighting this every time, and take the losing side seriously enough that whoever ends up on it doesn't feel strawmanned — these are people I have to keep working with.
