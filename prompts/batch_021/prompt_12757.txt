Thomas Schelling built his theory of bargaining on a paradox: a negotiator can strengthen their position by visibly destroying their own room to maneuver, since a threat or a refusal to compromise becomes credible only once retreat is no longer possible. In recent debates over multi-agent AI safety, theorists have extended this logic into a worry they call the "commitment race": as bargaining agents gain the ability to commit to positions faster, more transparently, and more irreversibly than any human negotiator could, each side comes under pressure to lock in an uncompromising stance before it has had time to reason the situation through or discover what its counterpart has already committed to — risking an outcome that ruins both sides, worse than if neither had committed to anything. Drawing on Schelling's own accounts of brinkmanship, credible commitment, and the reciprocal fear of surprise attack, how do you think he would have judged this concern? Would he have treated the commitment race as a genuinely new hazard created by artificial agents' capacity for rapid, verifiable precommitment, or as a familiar instability that his own framework had already diagnosed and supplied tools to manage — and what safeguards do you imagine he would have proposed?
