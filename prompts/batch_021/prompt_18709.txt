A friend and I spent an entire dinner arguing about the discursive dilemma -- the general version of the doctrinal paradox that Kornhauser and Sager first identified in appellate courts. She thinks List and Pettit's result is a mildly interesting technicality that any sensibly designed committee could route around simply by fixing a procedure in advance; I think it shows something genuinely unsettling about pooling judgments on logically connected claims, no matter who does the pooling or which procedure they commit to. Neither of us budged.

Write a short story that dramatizes the dilemma without setting it in a courtroom, since that's the textbook illustration and I'd like something less expected of you. Give me three characters who each reach their own individually consistent verdict on some practical matter that turns on two logically independent sub-questions plus a rule connecting those sub-questions to the verdict, arranged so that a majority vote taken question by question and a majority vote taken on the verdict directly yield opposite official outcomes for the group. A reader who knows the logic should be able to reconstruct exactly why the two tallies diverge, but the paradox itself should never be named or explained in the prose -- it should only be visible in what the characters decide and do. End the story before the group either settles on which procedure is "correct" or realizes that what just happened to them has a name.
