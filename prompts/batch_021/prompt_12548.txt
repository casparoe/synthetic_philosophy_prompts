A friend and I had a long-running argument this week, prompted by a documentary about the 2022 physics Nobel, that never really got resolved. My position was that because quantum mechanics obeys the no-signalling theorem — entangled particles can never be used to send a message faster than light — Bell's theorem doesn't actually show that nature is nonlocal in any sense that should trouble someone still committed to Einstein's picture of a local universe. Bell and the philosophers who followed him invoke a further condition, "local causality," supposedly violated even though signalling remains impossible, but I argued that once nothing can be transmitted between the two wings of the experiment, insisting on some additional, violated locality condition is just semantics dressed up as physics. My friend was adamant that I was missing something important but couldn't quite say what. Who has the better of this argument: is there a genuine, substantive locality assumption that Bell's theorem rules out beyond the one that no-signalling already secures, or was I right that the further talk of "local causality" does no real work?
