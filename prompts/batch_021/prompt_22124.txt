So a coworker and I fell down this whole rabbit hole last week after he linked me some old LessWrong posts on decision theory (we started out just arguing about whether our trading bot should ever "precommit" to a strategy ahead of time, and it kinda spiraled from there). I'd never heard of "updateless decision theory" (UDT, sometimes lumped together with "FDT") before, but the basic pitch seems to be: instead of just picking whatever action looks best given what you currently know — which is what both causal decision theory and evidential decision theory do — you're supposed to work out, from behind some kind of veil of ignorance over every way the world could've gone, which single overall policy (a full mapping from possible observations to actions) has the best expected value, and then actually execute that policy later even if it feels like a terrible deal once you're standing in one specific branch of reality.

The example that really got us going is "counterfactual mugging." Omega (a perfect predictor, basically) flips a fair coin. If it lands tails, Omega would've handed you $10,000 — but only if Omega predicted you'd hand over $100 in the case it landed heads instead. The coin actually comes up heads, and Omega shows up asking for the $100. An agent that only reasons from where it currently stands (it's heads, the tails-world never happened, paying is pure loss with zero causal or evidential upside) just keeps its money. UDT says pay anyway, because "pay on heads" is part of the one overall policy with the highest expected value averaged across both branches before the coin was even flipped — even though this particular you will never see a cent of that $10k. My coworker thinks refusing to pay is "leaving utility on the table" and basically irrational, but it bugs me that UDT seems to assume some version of you already had commitment power over the you that's standing their in the heads-branch, who never personally agreed to anything. There's also this "commitment races" worry I saw floating around in the comments, where if everyone reasons updatelessly and races to lock in commitments as early and blindly as possible, that seems like it could go badly wrong, especially once you're talking about AI agents bargaining with each other instead of humans.

Can you write me an essay that actually settles this, at least for cases like counterfactual mugging: should a rational agent be "updateless" rather than reasoning the standard causal/evidential way? Go through the strongest arguments on both sides (Newcomb's problem and Parfit's Hitchhiker are probably fair game too), but don't let yourself off the hook with a "reasonable people disagree" ending — actually defend a specific verdict, even if it's something narrower like "be updateless only under conditions X and Y."
