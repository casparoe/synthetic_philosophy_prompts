"Gödel (1938/40) and Cohen (1963) jointly established that the Continuum Hypothesis (CH) is independent of ZFC. (a) Briefly explain what each result contributes to this conclusion. (b) Does this independence result show that CH lacks a determinate truth value, or could there still be a fact of the matter? Defend your answer, engaging with at least one of: Gödel's own view of the continuum problem, the set-theoretic multiverse position, or a specific contemporary axiom candidate proposed to settle CH."

Here is the student's answer:

"The Continuum Hypothesis (CH) states that there is no set whose cardinality lies strictly between that of the natural numbers and that of the real numbers — i.e., that 2^ℵ₀ = ℵ₁. Gödel's contribution was to construct the constructible universe L: starting from the empty set, one builds up sets in stages using only what is definable from earlier stages, continuing through all the ordinals. Gödel showed that L satisfies both ZFC and CH, and that L can be built inside any model of ZFC. This gives a relative consistency proof: if ZFC is consistent, so is ZFC + CH, so ZFC cannot refute CH.

Cohen's contribution, a quarter-century later, was the method of forcing, which lets you extend a model of ZFC by adjoining a carefully chosen 'generic' set to produce a new model in which some previously undecided statement holds. Cohen used forcing to build a model of ZFC in which CH fails, showing ZFC + ¬CH is also consistent relative to ZFC, so ZFC cannot prove CH either. Together, the two results show CH is independent of ZFC: the axioms alone don't settle it either way.

I don't take this to show CH is meaningless or a matter of convention. But it's worth noting that Gödel himself moved toward exactly that kind of view once Cohen's result was in hand: much as mathematicians stopped asking which geometry is 'really' true once non-Euclidean geometries were shown equiconsistent with Euclidean geometry, Gödel came to treat CH as true in some legitimate models of set theory and false in others, with nothing further to discover, and he set the continuum problem aside for the rest of his career.

A more promising route, I think, is to look for new axioms that are independently well-motivated. The Proper Forcing Axiom (PFA) is a good example: it implies that the continuum has cardinality ℵ₂. Since PFA is now the axiom most set theorists reach for when they want a well-behaved theory of the real line, its consequence that CH is false is about as close as we are going to get to a real resolution of the continuum problem."
