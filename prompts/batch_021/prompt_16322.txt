Last weekend a close friend and I spent nearly the entire car ride home arguing about G. E. Moore, prompted by a seminar on early analytic ethics I am taking this term, for which we had both just read the relevant chapters of Principia Ethica. Moore spends a great deal of the book building his case against hedonism, deploying the open-question argument to insist, against both Sidgwick and Mill, that "good" cannot simply mean "pleasant." Yet when he finally sets out his own positive view of what is intrinsically valuable, in the chapter on "the ideal," he lands on something in the neighborhood of the enjoyment of beautiful objects and the pleasures of personal affection between people who genuinely appreciate good qualities in one another.

My friend's contention, which I was not able to answer satisfactorily at the time, was that this conclusion amounts to a kind of philosophical sleight of hand. Having spent so much of the book arguing that no natural property, least of all pleasure, can be what we mean by "good," Moore turns around and tells us that the best things in life are, quite transparently, certain pleasures: the pleasure of contemplating something lovely, the pleasure of a friend's company. On my friend's reading, everything surrounding this conclusion, the talk of non-natural properties, the principle of organic unities, the method of imagining objects in complete isolation to test their value, is really just an elaborate way of dressing up a fairly conventional hedonism, so that Moore ends up much closer to Sidgwick than his own earlier chapters would suggest. I could not clearly reconstruct, on the spot, how Moore's thought experiment comparing two permanently unobserved worlds, one exquisitely beautiful and one a mere heap of refuse, or his account of organic unities more generally, was supposed to block exactly this reading. Was my friend's objection actually sound, or are they overlooking something in Moore's argument that keeps it from collapsing back into the very hedonism it was designed to refute?
