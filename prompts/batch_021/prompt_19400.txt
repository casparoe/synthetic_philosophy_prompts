I'm taking a university seminar on the philosophy of artificial intelligence, and this week we read Turing's 1950 paper "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" in full rather than just the opening sections everyone already knows secondhand. I was genuinely surprised by the last of the objections he considers, the one from extrasensory perception: Turing doesn't dismiss it out of hand, but flatly says "the statistical evidence, at least for telepathy, is overwhelming," and takes the possibility seriously enough to propose running the imitation game under telepathy-proof conditions, so that a human contestant couldn't give the game away simply by guessing a hidden card more often than chance would allow. Our instructor mentioned, almost in passing, that this sits in odd company with Turing's own wartime work at Bletchley Park, where the Banburismus procedure he helped design accumulated "weight of evidence" in units he called bans and decibans -- a sequential, odds-based way of updating belief on scanty data that historians of statistics now treat as an early, rigorous form of Bayesian reasoning.

That contrast has stuck with me, and I'd like your help thinking through how it might have played out if Turing had lived to see later developments in exactly this area. In 2011, the psychologist Daryl Bem published "Feeling the Future," a paper using ordinary null-hypothesis significance testing across nine experiments to argue for precognition; it became one of the flashpoints of the replication crisis once Eric-Jan Wagenmakers and colleagues re-analyzed the same data with Bayesian methods and argued, in the same journal issue, that the apparently significant effects were far weaker than they looked, helping spur the broader push toward preregistration and away from naive reliance on p-values. Given how Turing actually reasoned about evidence -- his openness to telepathy in 1950 alongside his own sequential, likelihood-ratio habits of mind from codebreaking -- what do you think he would have made of the Bem affair and the Bayesian critique that followed it? I'm less interested in a generic prediction that he would have turned out to be a skeptic than in your best reconstruction of which way his specific, documented epistemic commitments would actually have pulled him, and of any point at which those commitments might have pulled against each other.
