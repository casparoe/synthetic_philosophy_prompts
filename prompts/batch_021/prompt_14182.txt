I'm a backend engineer with no formal training in philosophy, but a friend of mine who's finishing a philosophy MA got me interested in the literature on dispositional properties -- things like fragility or solubility -- after I complained to her about a Heisenbug: a race condition that reliably crashed one of our services under load, except that it always mysteriously vanished the instant we attached a debugger or added logging to catch it in the act. She said this was structurally identical to a classic problem case in metaphysics called a "finkish disposition." That sent me down a rabbit hole through C.B. Martin's "electro-fink" counterexamples and into David Lewis's 1997 paper "Finkish Dispositions," where he tries to repair the naive analysis of dispositions in terms of counterfactuals (roughly: x is fragile iff x would break if struck) after Martin's cases sink it. I can follow most of the paper, and I gather "masks" are a separate objection I haven't gotten to yet, but I'm stuck on the part where Lewis proposes, and then knocks down, a tempting quick fix. Here's the passage:

"Given that dispositions must have causal bases, and given that causal bases must be intrinsic, we might hope to stay closer to the simple conditional analysis. How about this, for instance? The glass is fragile iff, if it were struck and its intrinsic character were unchanged, it would break. [...] the solution does not work, because holding fixed the intrinsic character of the glass means holding fixed altogether too much. If the glass were struck and its intrinsic character were unchanged, it would indeed retain the intrinsic causal basis of its fragility. But also it would be not at all deformed, not at all compressed, not at all afflicted with vibrations or shock waves, etc. So it would not break. What it would do is astonish a sufficiently knowledgeable observer. We can agree that the glass does have a disposition to astonish such an observer -- an extremely finkish disposition, with the entire intrinsic character of the glass as its causal basis. [...] Yet it is this disposition, and not any opposite disposition, that our present proposal deigns to notice."

A few pages later, in his own "official" reformed analysis, Lewis again holds something about the glass fixed across the counterfactual, but this time it doesn't seem to bother him: "Something x is disposed at time t to give response r to stimulus s iff, for some intrinsic property B that x has at t, for some time t′ after t, if x were to undergo stimulus s at time t and retain property B until t′, s and x's having of B would jointly be an x-complete cause of x's giving response r."

What I can't work out is why holding the glass's whole intrinsic character fixed counts as illegitimate overkill, while holding just one intrinsic property B fixed doesn't commit the same sin -- both moves are, on their face, ways of protecting some feature of the glass from the finkish mischief, so what exactly is the principled difference that lets B get away with it? And separately, is the line about the glass merely having "a disposition to astonish a sufficiently knowledgeable observer" just a joke at the failed proposal's expense, or is it doing real argumentative work -- for instance, is Lewis using it to show that the broken proposal doesn't merely fail to track fragility, but actually succeeds in picking out some other, trivial disposition instead, which is the real diagnosis of what went wrong?
