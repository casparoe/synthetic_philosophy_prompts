Ok so I'm doing a physics PhD but half my friends are in the philosophy department, and at a bar last week two of them, Rina and Owen, got into it about whether objects endure or perdure through time, and I've been chewing on it since even though I couldn't referee it in the moment.

Rina opened with the classic argument that endurantism is inconsistent: if Owen is bent over tying his shoe at t1 and standing up straight at t2, and the whole of Owen is supposed to be wholly present at both times, then the whole of Owen has two incompatible intrinsic shapes at once, which is a contradiction -- so really it must be different temporal parts of him doing the bending and the standing. Owen said that's a cheap way to win: just say he's bent-at-t1 and straight-at-t2, no contradiction, no need to slice him into parts. Rina shot back that this only works by turning shape into a relation to a time, when shape is supposed to be a textbook intrinsic property -- how bent something is shouldn't depend on some other time any more than it should depend on some other object, so he'd dodged the contradiction by giving up the very thing that made shape interesting in the first place. Owen's comeback was that perdurantism doesn't get off the hook either: on Rina's own view its really only the temporal part that's bent or straight, full stop, and the four-dimensional "whole Owen" only counts as bent in some borrowed, derivative sense, so shes relativizing too, just across parts instead of times, and it's not obvious that's any less costly.

Neither of them budged. Can you go through the exchange move by move and say who's got the better argument at each step, and then say whether Owen's closing move actually neutralizes Rina's original argument, or whether there's a real disanalogy between "relative to a time" and "relative to a part" that saves perdurantism after all?
