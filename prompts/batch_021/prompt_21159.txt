Me and a couple friends watched one of those documentaries about the risk-assessment algorithms some courts use to help decide bail and sentencing (kind of like that COMPAS thing, if that rings a bell), and it turned into a whole dinner-table argument between my friend Dana and my friend Marcus that never really got resolved. I don't have any background in this stuff, no stats classes or anything, and when I tried googling it afterward, most of what came up went way over my head, but I feel like their actual argument is something a normal person should be able to sort through.

Here's roughly how it went: (1) Dana said the tool is basically racist, because among defendants who never actually went on to reoffend, Black people got labeled "high risk" way more often then white people did. (2) Marcus said no it's not biased at all, because if you only look at people the tool gave the same score to, the reoffending rate ends up basically identical across race -- so a "7 out of 10" means the same thing for everybody, and that IS what fair means. (3) Dana said that's not really answering the point, its just restating the problem in nicer language: if the two groups have different reoffending rates to begin with (because of stuff like policing patterns, not because one group is actually "more criminal"), then matching the score to real outcomes is automatically going to throw more false alarms at whichever group has the higher underlying rate. So Marcus's "fairness" doesn't get him off the hook. (4) Marcus admitted that, but said fine, so equalize the false alarm rates directly then -- except then he realized that since the two groups' rates are different, the only way to actually do that is to use a lower cutoff score for Black defendants than for white defendants, meaning two people with the exact same score get treated differently purely because of their race, which honestly seemed worse to both of them, and maybe isn't even legal. (5) Dana's fallback was: fine, then just don't tell the algorithm anyone's race. (6) Marcus's comeback was that this doesn't fix anything, since stuff like zip code and prior arrests are already basically stand-ins for race, so leaving it out just hides the disparity instead of removing it, and probably makes the tool dumber for everyone in the process.

That's where they left it, just kind of staring at each other. Can you go through it point by point, 1 through 6, and say who actually had the better of it at each step -- not just an overall winner?
