A friend and I spent a long evening arguing about a tension in Plato that neither of us could talk the other out of. In Book Nine of the Republic, the portrait of the tyrannical soul treats drunkenness as one of the tell-tale conditions — alongside erotic passion and something like madness — that let the soul's most lawless appetites override its own rational rule; indulging it, on that picture, looks less like education than like training for the tyranny of the self. Yet in Book One of the Laws, the Athenian Stranger defends state-supervised drinking parties as valuable civic education: wine, taken under a sober symposiarch's watch, induces something like the emotional volatility of youth, letting the lawgiver test and strengthen a citizen's self-mastery under pleasure much as fear tests a soldier's courage. My friend thinks this is a genuine, consistent extension of Plato's psychology of habituation to a later stage of life and a different faculty; I think it is a real reversal, since no amount of external sobriety in the symposiarch can vouch for an internal rational rule that has itself been dissolved by the wine. Write a dialogue of eight to ten exchanges between two equally well-informed interlocutors defending these two readings, in which each directly engages with and tries to undercut the other's strongest argument rather than simply restating their own, and see whether the tension can be dissolved or only sharpened.
