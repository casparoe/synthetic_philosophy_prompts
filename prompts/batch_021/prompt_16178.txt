So me and a couple of friends from a metaphysics reading group got into it last week over counterpart theory vs. transworld identity — like, David Lewis's view that no individual literally exists in more than one possible world (you just get similar-but-distinct "counterparts" in each), versus the more Kripke-flavored idea that Humphrey himself, the actual guy, could really have won that election in some other possible world, no stand-ins required. One friend claimed this whole fight is basically a rerun of something Leibniz was already tangled up in three centuries before Lewis or Kripke existed, and now I can't shake the thought.

Here's what's bugging me. In the correspondence with Arnauld (following up on the Discourse on Metaphysics), Leibniz says every individual substance has a "complete concept" containing literally every predicate true of it, past and future — Caesar's concept includes crossing the Rubicon, Adam's includes sinning, etc. Taken straight, that sounds like full superessentialism, where changing even one property gives you a different individual entirely — which sounds a lot like counterpart theory (no one's really the same guy across worlds, just a resembling stand in). But then Leibniz keeps talking as if "if Adam had not sinned" refers to that same Adam having gone a different way, and he even has this move about a "vague Adam" picked out by just a few general features, which sounds like hes trying to preserve genuine sameness across possibilities after all. On top of that his possible worlds aren't concrete universes floating out there like Lewis's; they're just unrealized ideas in God's understanding, which might scramble the comparison even further.

So, if you actually pinned Leibniz down and made him choose a side in the modern debate using only his own equipment (complete concepts, identity of indiscernibles, principle of sufficient reason, the vague-Adam move), which way does he end up leaning, and is his actual position even coherent, or is he just quietly playing both sides between "complete concept Adam" and "vague Adam"? I don't want a wishy washy "it depends how you read him" answer — commit to a reading of Leibniz and defend it.
