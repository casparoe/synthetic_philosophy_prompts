"(a) The type-identity theory holds that each mental state type is identical to some brain-state type (e.g., pain = C-fiber firing). Putnam's multiple realizability argument objects that the same mental state can be realized by wildly different physical states in different organisms: the pain of a human, an octopus, and a hypothetical silicon-based Martian are presumably realized by different physical states, given how different their underlying physiology is in each case. If pain is a single mental state type but has no single physical type it is identical to across all these cases, then pain cannot be identical to any one physical state type, so type-identity theory cannot be right as a general theory of mental states. Functionalism escapes this problem because it individuates mental states by their causal and functional role -- their typical causes, effects, and relations to other mental states -- rather than by their physical composition, so the same functional state, and therefore the same mental state, can be realized by many different physical substrates. This is usually presented as a point in functionalism's favor, since it lets psychology count as a science in its own right, autonomous from the details of neuroscience or any other 'implementing' science.

(b) Block pointed out that this same flexibility creates a new problem. If we individuate mental states purely by their causal and functional organization, we risk being too liberal: any system that reproduces the right organization counts as being in the relevant mental state, however alien its physical realization. Block's homunculi-headed robot thought experiment makes the point vivid: imagine a robot whose 'brain' is run by a huge number of little people, each doing a simple lookup-and-relay task, wired up so that the whole system reproduces the same functional organization as a human brain in pain. If functionalism is right, this system is in pain, which strikes most people as implausible, since it seems like there is nobody home to feel anything. Block makes the same point on a larger scale with his 'Chinese Nation' example, where the entire population of China is drafted to simulate the neurons of a brain by radio. Searle's Chinese Room thought experiment is standardly grouped together with these two: a room full of people shuffling symbols according to a rulebook can reproduce the functional organization of a mind, and Searle draws the identical moral about absent qualia from it, so all three examples are really versions of one and the same objection to functionalism's account of consciousness. Functionalism, in trying to avoid the chauvinism of type-identity theory, which wrongly denies mentality to any creature without our kind of brain, ends up guilty of liberalism instead: wrongly granting mentality to systems that merely mimic the right functional profile.

(c) Kim's argument turns the multiple realizability point back against the functionalist. He grants that pain is realized differently in humans and octopuses, but asks what causal work the disjunctive, cross-species property 'pain' is doing that is not already being done by its physical realizer in each case. Since the physical domain is causally closed and we should not multiply causal overdetermination, whatever causal effects pain has in a human seem to be fully accounted for by the human realizer alone, leaving nothing left over for 'pain-in-general' to explain, and the same goes for the octopus and its own realizer. Kim takes this to vindicate Putnam and Fodor's original insight that multiple realizability guarantees the causal and explanatory autonomy of psychology from neuroscience, since it shows that no single physical kind could ever do the job that the mental kind does across species. I am not sure functionalists have a full answer to this, though they might respond that the same argument would equally 'exclude' the causal role of any multiply realizable property in the physical sciences, such as temperature, which suggests something has gone wrong with the argument in general rather than with functionalism specifically."
