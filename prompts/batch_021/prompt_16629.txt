I came across the "dollar auction" game some time ago: an auctioneer sells a dollar bill, bids rise in small fixed steps, the top bidder pays their bid and wins the dollar, and the second-highest bidder also has to pay whatever they bid but gets nothing for it. When this is actually run, bidding reportedly blows straight past the dollar's value, sometimes by a lot, and I've noticed people explain why in two quite different ways that I've never managed to adjudicate between myself.

One camp treats it as a portrait of a bias: once money is on the table, loss aversion and sunk-cost thinking take over, and people keep topping each other rather than accept a total loss, when a level-headed bidder would have fixed a ceiling in advance and honored it. The other camp says that framing gets the mechanics backwards: once you're in second place, stopping guarantees a loss, while bidding again keeps alive some chance the other bidder, who faces the identical squeeze, gives up first, so carrying on can be the individually rational move under real uncertainty about how much money or resolve the other person has left — the same logic behind prolonged "wars of attrition" between animals or rival firms.

Could you write a dialogue between two people, each defending one of these views, working through the disagreement together? I'd like them evenly matched, genuinely responding to each other's last point rather than restating their own opening position, and pressing on the hardest spots: whether the "rational" account can really explain bids that overshoot any sensible estimate of the odds, and whether "the other bidder might fold" is actually distinguishable from just rationalizing the bias after the fact.
