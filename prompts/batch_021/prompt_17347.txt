Question (20 pts): Hobbes is famous for treating geometry as the paradigm of scientific knowledge, going so far as to call it the only science "it hath pleased God hitherto to bestow on mankind." (a) Explain the philosophical grounds for this view and how it connects to Hobbes's materialism and nominalism. (b) Explain how Hobbes hoped to extend this method to civil philosophy. (c) What does the decades-long dispute with Wallis over the quadrature of the circle show about the limits of Hobbes's confidence in this method?

Student's answer: For Hobbes geometry is the paradigm science because its a field where we, not nature, supply the objects of study. A geometer constructs points, lines and figures out of motion according to definitions he lays down himself, so a demonstrated property of a triangle is really just the unpacking of his own construction. Because the causes of geometrical figures are of our own making, we can have full and certain knowledge of them, whereas in natural philosophy we're stuck reasoning back toward causes we didn't put there ourselves and so can never be sure we've got the right ones -- which is why physics only ever reaches hypothetical, not demonstrative, knowledge for Hobbes. This fits his nominalism too: since universal terms are just names imposed by us and truth and falsehood are properties of speech rather than of things, correct science just is reasoning correctly from definitions we ourselves fix. Taken all the way, this is also why Hobbes ultimately treats matter itself as a construction out of our definitions rather than as something existing independently of the reasoner -- his geometry and his metaphysics rest on the same idea.

He wanted to extend the same method to politics. In Leviathan and De Cive he starts from a handful of precise definitions -- of man, of the state of nature, of right and law -- and deduces the rest the way a geometer deduces theorems, hoping a civil science built this way would be as free of reasonable dispute as geometry, and so put an end to civil war.

The Wallis dispute complicates this picture though. In De Corpore (1655) Hobbes claimed to have squared the circle, and Wallis tore the proof apart almost immediately in his Elenchus. The two went back and forth in pamphlets for years, and the dispute finally ended when Hobbes accepted his construction didn't work and conceded the mathematical point to Wallis, even while staying hostile to the Royal Society's experimental style of philosophy more generally. The episode suggests Hobbes's faith that one method could deliver certainty everywhere was harder to sustain in practice than his official theory of geometry implied.
