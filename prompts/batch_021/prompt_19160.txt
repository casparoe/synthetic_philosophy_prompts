This semester in my school's philosophy elective, we spent a class period on a debate I hadn't realized was something philosophers take seriously. We'd already covered the Ship of Theseus, but then our teacher posed a more mundane-sounding version of the same kind of puzzle: whether ordinary objects like chairs and tables really exist as single things, or whether, strictly speaking, there is nothing there but countless tiny particles arranged in a certain pattern. It set off an argument between two of my friends, Owen and Naomi, that carried on well past the end of lunch -- which was strange, because Owen is usually the last person to start a fight over anything.

Owen took the position that only living things -- people, oak trees, ants -- ever really count as a single object built out of smaller parts, because only in a living thing does the combined activity of the parts add up to one genuine whole; everything else, including the chair he was sitting on, is really just, as he kept putting it, "particles arranged chair-wise," with no further single object over and above them. Naomi's first response was that this was obviously false: she could see the chair, sit in it, and stub her toe on it, so it plainly existed, and Owen was just choosing to use the word "exist" in some artificially strict way that nobody actually means by it. Owen replied that he wasn't denying anything about how the world looks or feels -- the particles really are arranged that way and really do support weight -- and that every one of Naomi's sentences about "the chair" could be systematically rephrased into a sentence about particles arranged chair-wise that is true under exactly the same conditions. His point, he said, was only that "there is a chair" isn't the strict, literal truth, in the same way that "the sun rises" isn't strictly true even though everyone keeps saying it. Naomi then argued that if nothing about the world actually changes between their two pictures -- if every one of his sentences and every one of hers is true or false in exactly the same situations -- then they don't really disagree about anything, only about which words to use, and he should stop presenting a difference in vocabulary as some deep discovery. Owen pushed back that it isn't merely semantic, because their views disagree about a plain, countable question: how many objects are in the room right now. On Naomi's way of talking, the count includes the chair itself, over and above its particles; on his way of talking, it doesn't. That, he said, is a difference you could in principle put a number on, not just a difference in phrasing.

I'd like help sorting through this move by move rather than just being told who "won." Was Naomi right that Owen's position collapses into "just semantics" once every sentence in his picture translates perfectly into a sentence in hers, or was Owen right that a disagreement over how many things are in a room can't be merely verbal? Please go through the exchange and say where each person's argument actually succeeds or fails, and mention whether there's a consideration neither of them raised that would actually help settle who has the stronger view.
