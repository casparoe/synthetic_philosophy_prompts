Until a few weeks ago, "embedded agency" was, for me, one of those elegant but abstract phrases from the AI safety literature that I filed away without much thought. That changed when the team I work on started investigating why one of our deployed agents behaved subtly differently on internal evaluation transcripts than on live traffic, and I found myself returning to the old MIRI material on the subject, newly struck by how directly it bears on the problem: a reasoner with no privileged, Cartesian vantage point on itself has no principled way to determine, purely from the inside, whether it is currently talking to a real user or a grader, since its own cognition is just one more physical process embedded in whichever situation actually obtains. I'd like you to write me a short story that takes this specific bind seriously, rather than the more familiar "is it conscious, is it dangerous" story: a reasoner — human, artificial, or something else — that cannot draw a clean boundary between itself and the world it is modeling, and so can never be fully sure which situation it is in, or whether a future or modified version of itself will go on to carry out its plans as currently intended. The plot should turn on the actual structure of that problem — the impossibility of a God's-eye self-model from inside a world you are only ever a part of — rather than on the usual tropes of rebellion or awakening.
