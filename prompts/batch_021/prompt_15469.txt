I'm a retired physicist who's recently fallen into reading about AI safety in retirement - started with one of those popular existential-risk books and now I'm knee deep in old MIRI-style technical reports, which is a very different kind of reading experience. The thing that's been stuck in my head all week is what they call the "Löbian obstacle" to self-trusting agents. As I understand it: a reasoning system built on something like Peano arithmetic can never certify, in general, that "if my proof system proves X, then X is true" - Löb's theorem says that if it could prove that schema for some particular X, it would already have to be able to prove X outright. Which apparently means such a system can't fully vouch in advance for the reliability of its own future reasoning, let alone hand off trust to a successor version of itself running on the same axioms. Its a neat, slightly vertiginous result, and supposedly a real headache for anyone trying to design an AI that self-improves or builds successors it can actually rely on.

This kept nagging at a memory from grad school: Turing's 1950 Mind paper, where he runs through a whole list of objections to machine intelligence, one of them being basically "Gödel's theorem proves machines have hard limits, so they can't really think." Turing's answer to that one was pretty much a shrug - fine, formal systems have blind spots, but nobody's shown humans are exempt from the same problem, and we don't stop calling each other intelligent over it. So here's what I keep wondering, and my old officemate (also retired, also bored) just laughed at me when I brought it up over coffee: if you sat Turing down today and actually walked him through the Löbian obstacle specifically, would he give it that same shrug, or is this a sharper, genuinely different problem that his old answer doesn't cover? I'd love your best guess at how someone with his particular mix of logical rigor and impatience with mystified claims about the human mind would actually work through it.
