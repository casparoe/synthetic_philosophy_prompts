I'm a PhD student in philosophy specializing in formal epistemology, and this term I've been sitting in on a computer science seminar on AI alignment where we spent a session on the Löbian obstacle to self-trusting agents: the fact, following from Löb's theorem, that a proof-based reasoner cannot in general derive the schema "if my formal system proves that a given action is safe, then it is safe" for an arbitrary action, since doing so would require it to already possess an outright proof that the action is safe — so a system able to derive this schema across the board would thereby be able to prove anything. What has stuck with me is a toy case one of the computer science students raised afterward: an agent A0 wants to write the source code for a successor A1, sharing its own proof system, and wants to certify once and for all, using nothing but formal proof, that any action A1 takes on the strength of a safety-proof will actually be safe, rather than merely re-deriving safety piecemeal for each action A1 happens to consider. A few of us have kept arguing about this over email since then, and I'd like a careful outside take before our next meeting.

Could you lay out the two or three most important implications of the Löbian obstacle for a case like this, ranked by how much they should actually change how we think about the problem, rather than a general survey of the literature? I'm especially interested in whether the obstacle is a genuinely fatal barrier to that kind of once-and-for-all certification, or only to one particular naive strategy for achieving it; what it would take for a proposed workaround — for instance, relating the successive systems by differing proof-theoretic strength, or replacing proof-based certification with something more like the probabilistic self-trust you get from logical induction — to actually dissolve the obstacle rather than merely relocate it a level up; and whether there's a lesson here that should make us doubt, on general philosophical grounds, that any reasoner, artificial or human, could ever give itself a fully rigorous, non-circular guarantee of its own future trustworthiness.
