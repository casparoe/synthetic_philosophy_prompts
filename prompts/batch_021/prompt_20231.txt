Random turn my life has taken: I'm a phil of mind PhD student, and I just got added to my university's animal care and use committee as the "unaffiliated member with ethics expertise" (they'd apparently been out of compliance without one for months, and my advisor volunteered me). A few years back the committee decided to start reviewing cephalopod and decapod protocols the same way as vertebrate ones, even though the federal rules don't technically require that for invertebrates, and my first real case is a comparative-cognition lab's proposal to run octopuses and shore crabs through mild electric shock and a noxious chemical stimulus, testing avoidance learning and "motivational trade-off" behavior -- whether the animal will cross a zone it normally avoids to reach food or shelter. It's basically the same style of test that's been cited to argue octopuses and some decapods feel somehting pain-like, and part of why a couple of countries have extended legal welfare protections to them over the last decade. The PI doesn't want to use anesthesia, partly because nothing is known to reliably work on these nervous systems and partly because it would confound the results. Two committee members want to wave the protocol through with minimal oversight, on the grounds that nobody actually knows if there's anything it's like to be a crab; the vet on the committee wants the full package -- mandatory humane endpoints, a hard cap on stimulus intensity, review of every session.

Here's what's actually eating at me, and why I want your help instead of just my usual reading group: I've been living inside illusionism about phenomenal consciousness for a seminar paper -- Frankish's strong version especially, not the weaker "we just mislabel some qualia" reading, but the claim that phenomenal consciousness itself, the felt, ineffable quality of experience, is an introspective confabulation, full stop. If that's right even for us, then "does the crab really feel it" was never a well-formed question, even about humans, which seems like it should stop being the thing the committee is implicitly trying to settle. But I can't tell if that observation actually helps -- by shifting the criterion onto the functional profile (nociception, flexible avoidance, trade-offs) that octopuses arguably share with us -- or if it's actually a gift to the two members pushing for minimal oversight, since "the badness of pain is itself an illusion" is one short step from "so maybe none of this matters as much as we assumed," for crabs and humans alike.

I need to send the committee something before we reconvene next week, not a philosophy lecture. Could you draft the memo I'd actually submit: a real recommendation on the protocol (approve as submitted, approve with specific conditions, or send it back for revision), reasoning the PI and the vet could each actually engage with, and an honest account of the strongest version of whichever reading of illusionism you don't end up relying on -- rather than just reaching for whichever conclusion feels nicer.
