Over the past month I have had to make a decision that I keep describing to people as "impossible," even though nothing about it is actually unclear to me: I have been offered a permanent, well-paid position as a quantitative researcher at a trading firm, and in the very same week I learned that I had been awarded a fully funded PhD place in mathematical logic, working with a supervisor I have admired for years. However I turn the choice over, I cannot get the two options to line up on a single scale. The trading job is clearly better on security, income, and my ability to support my parents; the PhD is clearly better on intellectual fulfillment, autonomy, and a kind of long-term meaning I care about deeply; and neither cluster of advantages seems to straightforwardly outweigh the other, no matter how carefully I try to weigh them against each other.

When I mentioned this to a friend who is doing graduate work in philosophy, she pointed me to Ruth Chang's writing on hard choices, and in particular to her claim that choices like mine are hard because the alternatives stand in a distinctive fourth value relation she calls "parity": neither better than, worse than, nor exactly as good as one another, yet still fully comparable rather than simply incomparable. What surprised me is that this is apparently quite contested. My friend explained that philosophers in a line associated with Derek Parfit and James Griffin resist positing any such extra relation, arguing instead that what looks like parity is really just imprecise, or "rough," equality between values that remain comparable on one scale in principle, just not with perfect precision. Chang, apparently, has a specific reply to this move, known as the small-improvement argument: roughly, if two options were merely imprecisely equal, making one of them slightly better should be enough to tip the balance decisively in its favor, whereas in a genuinely hard choice a small enough improvement to one option does not resolve the difficulty at all -- which is supposed to show that something other than mere imprecision is at work.

I would like to see this debate properly staged rather than just summarized for me. Could you write a dialogue between two philosophers, one defending Chang's parity thesis and the other defending the rival imprecise-equality view, who are each trying to convince the other that they have correctly diagnosed my situation? Please have them engage directly with the small-improvement argument rather than talk past it, giving the imprecise-equality side a genuine opportunity to resist or reinterpret it rather than simply concede, and have both characters apply their view concretely to the choice between the PhD and the trading job rather than resting on abstract examples. I would rather neither of them come across as the obvious winner, since part of why I am asking is to find out whether this distinction should actually change how I am thinking about my own decision.
