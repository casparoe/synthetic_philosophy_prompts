I lead cross-cultural mediation trainings, and I recently began citing Jain anekāntavāda in my materials as historical precedent for treating several stakeholders' perspectives as simultaneously valid. A colleague with an Indology background reviewed the module and pushed back hard, arguing that I had flattened a carefully constructed epistemological position into garden-variety relativism, and that anekāntavāda was specifically built to avoid exactly that charge. Before I revise the training, I'd like a genuinely intuitive, gut-level explanation -- not the standard blind-men-and-the-elephant gloss -- of two things: how syādvāda's sevenfold predication (saptabhaṅgī) is supposed to keep "many-sided truth" from collapsing into "every viewpoint is equally true regardless of context," and how Jain philosophers answered the obvious self-referential worry that asserting "every statement is only conditionally true" is itself either self-undermining or a smuggled-in absolute claim.
