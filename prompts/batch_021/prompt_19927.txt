I'm prepping a session for my grad Formal Epistemology seminar on van Fraassen's Reflection Principle, and I keep flip-flopping on which of the classic objections actually sink the *unrestricted* version of the principle versus which ones a clever defender can talk their way out of. Quick refresher in case its been a while since you looked at this stuff: Reflection says your current credence in A, conditional on your future credence in A being r, should itself equal r -- basically a duty to treat your future self as an expert witness, which van Fraassen originally defended in "Belief and the Will" via a diachronic Dutch book argument.

Over the years people have thrown a handful of cases at the bare principle (not the hedged "so long as the update is honest, competent, and doesn't involve forgetting" version van Fraassen retreats to later): (1) his own Ulysses-and-the-Sirens case, where you already know you're about to become irrational or get manipulated; (2) the memory-loss cases, like Talbott's "spaghetti" example and Arntzenius's Shangri-La path story; (3) Christensen's "Clever Bookies" line -- that diachronic Dutch books never had real normative force to begin with, so the whole argument for Reflection was broken from the start; (4) the Sleeping Beauty problem, especially the way Elga's argument for thirding leans on something reflection-adjacent; and (5) the generic "what if somebody's about to mess with my head without my knowledge" evidence-tampering cases, which are the actual reason van Fraassen has to bolt on the honesty/reliability restriction in the first place.
