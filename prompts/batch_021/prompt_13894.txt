The student's answer: "Radical translation, for Quine, is the project of translating a language with no shared history, cognates, or bilingual speakers to consult, so the linguist has only the natives' verbal behavior and the observable circumstances that prompt it to go on — what Quine calls stimulus meaning. This is the setting for the example he introduces in 'Two Dogmas of Empiricism': a rabbit runs past, the native utters 'gavagai,' and the linguist's manual renders this as 'Rabbit.' But 'Undetached rabbit part,' 'Rabbit-stage,' and 'An instance of rabbithood' would license exactly the same assent and dissent under exactly the same stimulations, since a whole enduring rabbit is never present without its parts, its momentary stages, and its kind also being present. More fieldwork can't break the tie, because the tie is built into the evidence itself and not into how much of it we happen to have collected. In that sense gavagai is really just a sharper illustration of the same underdetermination that afflicts physical theory generally: exactly as rival theories can be compatible with every observation we could ever make, rival translation manuals are compatible with every stimulation we could ever record, and in both cases the moral is that there is a truth here that no amount of evidence could ever put us in a position to identify."
