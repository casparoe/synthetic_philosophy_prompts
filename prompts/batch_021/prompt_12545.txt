I spent my career as an experimental physicist and only came to philosophy after retiring. My closest friend from graduate school, a physiologist, died last month at eighty-four. For his final twenty-five years he was consumed by the question of whether death is truly the end, and he insisted on pursuing it as Henry Sidgwick and the early Society for Psychical Research had: testing mediums and claims of telepathy under strict controls, treating fraud and self-deception as the default hypothesis, and refusing to let hope stand in for evidence -- because, like Sidgwick, he worried that without some ultimate reckoning, the case for living morally rather than self-interestedly is incomplete. He died, as Sidgwick had, without reaching a settled answer, and said so plainly. He left instructions that whoever spoke at his memorial should neither assure the room that he is "at peace somewhere now" nor treat twenty-five years of careful work as time wasted. I am giving that ten-minute eulogy to a room split evenly between family wanting comfort and former colleagues from his research circle who will know at once if I fudge the epistemics. Please write it.
