I'm a retired physicist (spent most of my working life doing laser optics, of all things) and since I retired I've gotten hooked on philosophy, mostly through a little discussion group that meets every other week at my local library. A couple sessions back we got onto supertasks, and Thomson's lamp specifically, and I walked away thinking I'd basically solved it, except nobody else in the group agreed with me, so I want an outside opinion.

Here's my thinking: Zeno's paradox of Achilles and the tortoise gets dissolved once you notice that the shrinking time intervals Achilles needs to close the gap form a convergent geometric series that adds up to a finite number -- theres nothing mysterious left over once you've done the sum, its just freshman calculus. Thomson's lamp is built on the exact same skeleton: flip the switch after 1 minute, then after another 1/2 minute, then 1/4, then 1/8, and so on, and by the 2-minute mark the whole infinite run of flips is finished, same as Achilles finishes his race in finite time. So it seems to me the lamp is just Zeno's paradox with a light switch bolted on, already put to bed by the same convergent-series fact, and there's no real puzzle left to argue about. But one of the younger guys in the group, who's doing a philosophy PhD, insists people are still writing serious papers treating this as an open, meaningful problem. So: is there some actual mathematical or conceptual difference between the two cases that I'm not seeing, or is this really just a case of professional philosophers dressing up a solved calculus problem as something deeper than it is?
