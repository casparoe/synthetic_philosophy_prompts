Explain the philosophical puzzle this scenario is meant to illustrate, then critically assess ONE proposed solution to it (you can pick the constitution view, perdurantism/four-dimensionalism, or contingent identity — whichever you covered in section). And here's the student's answer, verbatim:

"The puzzle arises because the statue Bucephalus and the lump of clay that makes it up seem to occupy exactly the same space and be made of exactly the same matter at every moment they both exist, which tempts us to say they're one and the same object. But intuitively they're not: the lump existed before the sculptor started working (as a shapeless blob) and could survive being squashed back into a ball, whereas the statue couldn't survive that — squashing it would destroy it. Since the lump has a property (being able to survive squashing) that the statue lacks, by Leibniz's Law the statue and the lump can't be identical, even though they're made of all the same parts at the same time. This is sometimes called the 'modal argument' for the distinctness of statue and clay.

One solution is the constitution view, defended by Lynne Baker and, in a different form, by David Lewis. On this view the lump constitutes the statue without being identical to it: constitution is a real relation, weaker than identity but stronger than mere spatial overlap, that lets one object 'borrow' properties from another. The lump has properties like being made of clay non-derivatively, while the statue has that same property only derivatively, in virtue of being constituted by the lump; conversely, the statue has properties like depicting a horse non-derivatively, and the lump has that property only derivatively, in virtue of constituting the statue. This view handles the modal argument nicely, since constitution isn't identity, there's no violation of Leibniz's Law in saying the lump could survive squashing while the statue couldn't — we were never committed to their identity in the first place.

The trouble with the constitution view is that it multiplies objects in a way that seems extravagant — for every statue there are now at least two coincident objects sharing all the same matter, which strikes many people as an ontological free lunch that isn't really free. A cleaner alternative is to just deny that the lump exists anymore once the statue is formed, as the perdurantist does, since only four-dimensional space-time worms are ultimately real and 'lump' and 'statue' turn out to just be two names for temporal parts of the same underlying whole."
