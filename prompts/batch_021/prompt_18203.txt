At our weekly game night, two friends of mine — I'll call them Dan and Mia — have a running side bet they play between board games. The rules are simple: a small pot starts at, say, five dollars, and over as many as six turns, a player can either "stop" — ending the game immediately and keeping 75% of the current pot, with the other person getting the remaining 25% — or "pass," which doubles the pot and hands the decision to the other player. If the sixth turn is also a pass, the game ends right there with the pot split fifty-fifty rather than doubling again. A few weeks ago, before they'd even picked up the chips, Dan announced that he'd worked out that the "rational" thing to do is stop on turn one, no matter what, and refused to play it any other way. That set off an argument I ended up watching from the sidelines without ever weighing in myself.

Dan's opening move went something like this: on turn six, whoever's turn it is should obviously just stop and keep 75%, since passing at that point only hands over an even fifty-fifty split of the very same pot — there's nothing to be gained by waiting. Knowing that, the player on turn five should stop as well, since passing just lets the other person grab the bigger share on turn six, leaving the turn-five player worse off than if they'd stopped directly. That reasoning is supposed to carry all the way back to turn one, so the only "rational" move right now, Dan said, is for him to stop immediately. Mia's comeback was that this can't be right, because if it were, the two of them would never get anywhere near the much bigger pot they'd both clearly prefer to split further down the line — a theory of rationality that tells you to grab a few dollars when a much larger sum was within reach has to have gone wrong somewhere. Dan replied that "I'd rather have more money" isn't an objection to the argument, it's just restating the problem: at the very last turn, preferring more money is exactly why you stop, and that single fact is what unravels everything else once you both know the other person is rational and reasons the same way. Mia's response was that the whole argument leans on each of them having common knowledge that the other will always follow the reasoning all the way down — but if Dan ever actually saw her pass on turn one, wouldn't that be evidence that the "common knowledge" premise was false all along, rather than evidence that she was being irrational? She said the argument seems to assume the very thing that a real deviation from it would call into question. Dan granted that the whole argument depends on assuming neither of them will ever deviate, but said that only shows it's a claim about what ideally rational agents would do, not a prediction about actual behavior — so it isn't undermined just because real people, including the two of them, might not follow it. Mia ended by saying that if the theory's recommendation only makes sense for agents who could never learn anything from watching each other actually play, then it isn't really advice for their situation at all, and that the failure sits with the framework, not with her instinct to keep passing.

Neither of them would let it go, and when they turned to me to settle it, I honestly had no idea what to say. I looked into it a bit afterward and found out this connects to something game theorists call "common knowledge of rationality" and "backward induction," but the discussions I found got technical fast, and I couldn't tell how seriously they take an objection like Mia's. Then give me your overall take on two things: whether backward induction really does recommend stopping on turn one here, and whether Mia's objection about common knowledge "unraveling itself" is a genuine problem for the argument or a misunderstanding of what it claims. I'd also be curious whether there's some consideration you think both of them are missing entirely.
