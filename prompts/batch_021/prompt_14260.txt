So for my poli-sci elective we had to read part of Nozick's Anarchy, State, and Utopia, and I got kind of obsessed with the Wilt Chamberlain argument - the part where he says imagine any distribution you like (say everyone's totally equal) and then a million people each voluntarily hand over 25 cents extra to watch Wilt play, so he ends up with $250,000 and everyone else has a tiny bit less. Nozick's point is that since the starting distribution was just and every single transfer was voluntary, the resulting (unequal) distribution has to be just too, which is supposed to show that no "patterned" principle of justice (like Rawls's, or strict equality) can be maintained without the state constantly interfering with peoples free choices.

My teacher mentioned in passing that this argument gets attacked alot in the literature but we didn't actually go over the responses, so I went and read around on my own and found a few of the big ones: (1) G.A. Cohen's "socialist society" reply, where he imagines people agreeing ahead of time to a scheme that redistributes the extra money Wilt gets, so the pattern survives without blocking anyone's choices; (2) Cohen's separate point that even if we grant everyone fully owns themselves, that doesn't automatically mean the external stuff (the stadium, the tickets, the money itself) gets to be privately owned in the first place, so the argument kind of smuggles in a theory of property it hasn't actually earned; (3) Nagel's complaint that Nozick never really argues for why we should treat self-ownership as this absolute side-constraint instead of just one value to be weighed against others - he mostly just asserts it; and (4) the pretty obvious "why not just tax the $250,000 afterward instead of banning the ticket sales in the first place" response.
