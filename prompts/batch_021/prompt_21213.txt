Nate buys meat only from a small farm that raises its animals on pasture and slaughters them fairly young, and he defended this with an argument that I've since learned is sometimes called the logic of the larder. (1) His claim was that the specific lambs raised on that farm would never have existed at all if there weren't a market for their meat, and since their lives, while short, are by ordinary standards decent ones, they come out ahead relative to the alternative of never being bred in the first place, so buying that meat is, if anything, a way of bringing more such lives into being. (2) Sofia answered that this would equally excuse a company that cloned a person specifically to harvest their organs at age thirty, since that person also wouldn't have existed otherwise and could be stipulated to have a good life up to that point; the mere fact that a being owes its existence to some arrangement doesn't make the arrangement permissible, or else almost anything could be justified this way. (3) Nate replied that the cases aren't parallel: we can easily picture the cloned person existing and simply never being harvested, so the harvesting isn't what brings them into being, whereas on his farm it's specifically the demand for lamb — and so the eventual slaughter — that causes the animal to be bred at all; remove the slaughter and you remove the animal, not just something that later happens to it. (4) Sofia granted that this is how his farm works but said it doesn't show slaughter is necessary for that kind of breeding in general, since some people breed miniature pigs purely as house pets with no slaughter anywhere in the picture; she added that even where an animal's existence really does depend on its eventually being killed, that dependence still seems like the wrong kind of fact to hang permissibility on, in the way that "he wouldn't have this job if we hadn't underpaid him for it" doesn't make the underpaying fine. (5) Nate conceded the pet-pig point but fell back on a narrower claim: lambs, unlike Sofia's cloned person, presumably have no long-term projects or sense of a future to be cut short, so their death is plausibly a smaller harm to them than a comparable death would be to a person, and he thinks it's really this difference, and not the "wouldn't exist otherwise" point on its own, that makes the farm defensible. (6) Sofia said that concession quietly changes his argument: it might show that killing the lamb is less bad than killing a person, but "less bad than killing a person" is nowhere near "raising it to be killed is good for it," which was Nate's original claim, and she thinks he's traded a positive case for a merely mitigating one without admitting it.
