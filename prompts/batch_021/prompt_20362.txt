A few weeks ago my company ran a negotiation-skills workshop, and as a warm-up exercise the facilitator had us pair off and play what she called the "centipede game": the pot starts small, and on each turn you can either take it (ending the game immediately and pocketing the larger share, leaving your partner the rest) or pass it (the pot grows a little and the decision moves to your partner); if nobody has taken after ten turns, the game ends automatically and the pot is split evenly. She then walked us through the "backward induction" solution: on turn ten you should obviously take, since passing there only helps your partner; knowing that, whoever is on turn nine should also take rather than hand turn ten to a partner who will grab the bigger share there; and so on, all the way back to turn one -- so two players who are rational, and know the other is, and know that they know it (what she called "common knowledge of rationality"), should both take immediately and walk away with almost nothing. Almost nobody in the room actually did that; most pairs passed a few times before someone finally grabbed the pot.

What I'd really like adjudicated is the argument this touched off between two of my colleagues, Owen and Mira, which ran across our team Slack channel for the next two days. (1) Owen said the derivation is simply correct: it's the same kind of induction you'd use to prove something about a loop, and you can't reject the conclusion without rejecting one of the individual steps, each of which is trivial on its own. (2) Mira said the steps aren't so innocent, because every one of them assumes the other player stays rational for the rest of the game -- yet the conclusion is that two rational players never even reach turn four. So suppose the game is actually at turn four: does whoever's deciding there still believe her partner is rational, ignoring the three passes that are exactly the evidence the theory says shouldn't exist? Or does she stop believing it, in which case the reasoning that was supposed to tell her what to do next has lost the assumption it depended on? (3) Owen replied that this isn't a real contradiction, just a counterfactual -- no different from an induction proof reasoning about a loop iteration that, by the base case, will never actually run. (4) Mira answered that there's a difference between a conditional that's purely formal and one that's meant to license an actual belief for an agent sitting inside the scenario: the belief at turn four is only justified if reaching turn four isn't itself evidence against it, and on Owen's own setup, it is. (5) Owen then invoked the fix the facilitator had mentioned in passing -- treat any deviation as an accidental "trembling hand" slip rather than evidence about who you're playing against, which keeps rationality intact going forward. (6) Mira said that move concedes the point: if passing might just be noise, a player has to weigh the chance her partner is a cooperator or a fumbler rather than a ruthless calculator, and once that chance is big enough, passing again can be her best move too -- reopening exactly the sustained cooperation that backward induction was supposed to rule out. Owen's last word was that something still has to give right on the last turn, whatever anyone believes about the other player, since there's no future left there to protect.

Neither of them budged, and I don't have the background to referee it myself. Could you go through the six moves and, for each one, say who has the better of the argument and why, rather than just declaring an overall winner? Please also flag anything you think both of them are missing, and close with your own verdict on the question they never actually settled: does Mira's objection genuinely undermine backward induction as the uniquely rational solution, or does Owen manage to recover it -- whether through move 3, move 5, or his closing point?
