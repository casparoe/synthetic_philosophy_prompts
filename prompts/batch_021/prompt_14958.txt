I'm about two-thirds through a novel whose central character is a household android built to be indistinguishable from a person in every outward respect: she flinches when she's hurt, describes how the color of the evening sky makes her feel, and grieves for the old woman she was built to look after. The plot turns on whether there's anybody home in there, or whether she's simply an extremely convincing simulation of someone who is. To pressure-test the premise, I laid out the setup at a dinner party, and two friends of mine, Renata, who is finishing a PhD in philosophy of mind, and Oskar, a computational neuroscientist, ended up in a running argument about philosophical zombies that I haven't been able to stop turning over since.

Renata went first: the fact that my android is conceivable at all -- something physically and behaviorally identical to a person, but with nobody in there -- already tells us something, because if such a being is conceivable without contradiction, then it's metaphysically possible, and if it's possible, then consciousness can't be a physical or functional property, since a full duplicate of a conscious being would then be unable to lack it. Oskar answered with the standard Kripkean rejoinder: conceivability doesn't guarantee possibility, since it was once perfectly conceivable that water might turn out not to be H2O, and that turned out to be incoherent once the underlying chemistry was known; maybe "a physical duplicate of a person with nobody home" will look the same way once we actually understand what consciousness is. Renata's comeback was that the analogy breaks down, because "water" picks out whatever stuff happens to fill our lakes and rivers, which leaves room for the word to latch onto something other than H2O in a scenario we're merely imagining, whereas our concept of consciousness doesn't work by picking out "whatever produces these effects" -- it picks out its object directly, by that object's own felt character -- so there's no equivalent gap for a zombie scenario to be secretly exploiting, and the inference from conceivable to possible goes through cleanly in this one case even where it fails elsewhere. Oskar's reply was that this gets the dialectic backwards: a physicalist can grant that phenomenal concepts are special in exactly that way -- direct, non-descriptive, unmediated by any contingent reference-fixing story -- and still hold that what they refer to is perfectly physical, because it's precisely that distinctive, unmediated feel of the concept that generates the illusion of conceiving a zombie, without its being any evidence that the underlying property is non-physical. Renata's last word, before we were interrupted by dessert, was that this just moves the bill somewhere else: if physicalism is true, there has to be some explanation of why these particular concepts, alone among all our concepts, have this uniquely direct, gap-generating character, and "they just do" is not an explanation so much as a place where the explaining has stopped.

Neither of them budged, and I was too busy refilling glasses to adjudicate on the spot. Could you go through the exchange move by move and say who has the better of it at each stage, rather than just declaring an overall winner, and flag anything both of them seem to be taking for granted that is actually doing a lot of work?
