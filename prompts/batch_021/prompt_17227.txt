Last week a friend and I ended up in a long, increasingly heated argument after a seminar on Kierkegaard's "Two Ages," specifically the part usually excerpted on its own as "The Present Age." She maintained that his diagnosis of "the public" — an abstract, phantom-like collective conjured into being by the press, whose characteristic vice is leveling (nivellering) rather than the concrete commitment involved in a genuine "association" — is basically correct, and if anything sharper now than when he wrote it, given how much of contemporary life runs on anonymous, reflective chatter rather than passionate engagement. I pushed back with something closer to a Deweyan line: that Kierkegaard's own remedy — retreat into the "single individual" (den Enkelte) and inward, religious existence — isn't really a response to the political problem he raises so much as an evacuation of it, and that what he calls leveling looks a lot like what real democratization feels like to someone with an aristocratic temperament and a personal grudge against the satirical newspaper that had spent months turning him into a caricature. We never settled whether the disanalogies between an 1840s Copenhagen broadsheet and today's algorithmically curated feeds actually weaken the argument or make it worse.

I'd like you to write a dialogue between two characters who pick up roughly where we left off: one a committed Kierkegaardian defending his account of the public and the press, the other a pragmatist arguing along the lines Dewey used against Lippmann's skepticism about the competence of "the public" — namely, that publics can be sites of collective intelligence rather than mere leveling. Don't let either side simply restate talking points and declare victory: the pragmatist should have to engage specifically with Kierkegaard's distinction between "the public" and an "association," and with the claim that reflection without inwardness is corrosive in a way that mere disagreement or diversity of opinion is not; the Kierkegaardian should have to face the charge that "the single individual" has no political content whatsoever, and that the Corsair affair is doing more work in the theory than its defenders like to admit. Please give me several real rounds of back-and-forth rather than two speeches talking past each other, and end with each side conceding at least one genuine point without the disagreement simply dissolving.
