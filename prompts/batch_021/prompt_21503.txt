I have served on my hospital's ethics consultation committee for two years, but until last week the role was mostly theoretical. A case has now arrived that I find genuinely difficult, and it has sent me back into decision theory in a way I never expected my job to require. Eighteen months ago, a patient with a long history of severe bipolar I disorder executed a psychiatric advance directive containing what her outpatient psychiatrist calls a "Ulysses clause": while euthymic, she specified that if she ever entered a manic episode meeting stated severity criteria (fewer than two hours of sleep for three consecutive nights, spending above a fixed dollar threshold, or sustained refusal of her mood stabilizer), she was to be hospitalized and medicated regardless of her objections at the time, and that the directive itself could be revoked only in writing while she was stable, with her psychiatrist's concurrence. She explained her reasoning in the document: two previous untreated episodes had already cost her a marriage and a job, she described her manic refusals as belonging to "a self I don't want making decisions for me," and she said she would rather be overridden every single time than lose what remained of her life a third time.

She is now in exactly the episode the clause anticipated, and the case has turned out to be harder than any of us expected. She does not meet our jurisdiction's threshold for involuntary commitment on ordinary grounds, since she is neither violent nor suicidal, only severely impaired in judgment. More awkwardly, the attending's formal capacity assessment concluded that she meets the standard criteria for decisional capacity regarding this specific choice: she is coherent, oriented, and able to give a fluent argument for refusing treatment, saying the medication "takes away the only version of me that has ever gotten anything done" and that honoring the clause now would let "someone who doesn't have to live with today" decide for her. Her sister, who is named in the directive, wants it invoked immediately, citing the money already given away this week; the patient's court-appointed advocate counters that no document can validly pre-empt a contemporaneous, articulate refusal like this one without effectively cancelling her standing as a person for the duration of every future episode. I should mention, since it seems relevant, that this is not the first cycle: she signed a similar clause after an episode two years ago, and here we are again. That repetition is what sent me looking outside our usual clinical-ethics toolkit, where I kept running into money-pump arguments and the distinction between "sophisticated" and "resolute" choice in decision theory, both of which seem to bear directly on whether a person can rationally bind a later self at all -- though I don't know how to turn either into something a hospital committee can actually vote on.

Could you draft the short opinion I would circulate before our meeting in two days? Everyone agrees we cannot simply wait the episode out; the committee needs to say something within 48 hours. I need the opinion to recommend one concrete course of action for this patient with that time frame attached, to state explicitly whether and how this case should shape a standing hospital policy on Ulysses clauses going forward, and to take the advocate's objection seriously on its own terms rather than quietly resolving it into an ordinary capacity determination.
