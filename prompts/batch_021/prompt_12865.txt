I'm reporting a piece on the intellectual history of "realist" arguments in international relations -- basically the claim that a world with no periodic war would actually be worse off, not better -- and I keep circling back to Hegel's Philosophy of Right, since apparently a lot of this stuff traces back to him. Every philosopher I've interviewed so far gives me more or less the same reassuring gloss: Hegel wasn't *endorsing* war, he was just making a cold structural point -- since there's no supreme authority standing over sovereign states the way the state stands over individuals, conflict between them can't just be legislated away the way Kant hoped with his perpetual peace essay, so periodic war is a permanent, regrettable possibility rather than something to celebrate. Basically "realism, not romance."

But when I actually sat down and reread the relevant stretch myself (the bit on international law and sovereignty, plus that odd passage comparing war to wind keeping a pond from going stagnant, and the later remarks on soldierly courage as a "formal" rather than "material" kind of sacrifice), it reads to me like something stronger. It sounds like Hegel thinks periodic war is actively good for a people's ethical health, and not only because there's no cop on the beat internationally, but because of something going on *inside* civil society -- all that private property and "system of needs" stuff produces an atomized, self-interested citzenry that periodically needs reminding, via the demand for sacrifice, that the state isn't just a vehicle for their private ends. If I'm reading this right, war would still be doing useful work for Hegel even in a world with basically no real external threats.

So here's what I'd actually like from you, for a sidebar my editor wants: make the strongest case you can that this "merely descriptive/prudential" defense of Hegel doesn't survive contact with the text, and that he's committed to the stronger, internal-ethical-health thesis -- i.e. that on his own view a state facing no genuine enemies would still, in some sense, need war (or need to manufacture the conditions for it) in order to stay ethically sound. I'm not asking for a tour of the secondary literature, just the tightest version of that specific argument you can build directly from the moves Hegel actually makes, not from his reputation.
