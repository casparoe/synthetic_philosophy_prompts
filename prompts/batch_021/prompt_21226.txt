A couple of people in my online philosophy discussion group had an argument a few weeks ago that I still haven't been able to settle for myself.

It started when Theo brought up the doomsday argument: something like a hundred billion humans have been born so far, and if he treats his own position among everyone who will ever live as a random draw from that group, then the mere fact that he turns out to be this early is itself some evidence that the group won't end up being astronomically large -- that is, evidence for a comparatively modest human future, quite apart from anything we actually know about asteroids, pandemics, or climate.

Mara's first objection was that this can't be legitimate: it lets you derive conclusions about wars and asteroids purely from arithmetic about your own birth rank, with no actual fact about the world entering in anywhere. Theo answered that we already reason this way elsewhere: part of why physicists take seriously the worry about "Boltzmann brains" (freak observers that fluctuate briefly into existence out of the random jostling of particles in an otherwise empty, high-entropy universe) is that if such brains vastly outnumbered ordinary evolved observers under some cosmological model, a randomly chosen observer would almost certainly be one of them -- so the fact that we, apparently, aren't counts as evidence against models that predict this. Same move, he said, just run on cosmology instead of history.

Mara countered that it's actually the opposite move: the Boltzmann brain argument favors whichever theory implies more observers like you, while doomsday reasoning requires sampling only from beings who actually end up existing, not from every observer who could have. Running both moves at once, she said, licenses a "presumptuous philosopher" who, told that physicists are about to spend years and a great deal of money determining whether the universe contains a trillion observers or a trillion trillion, could settle the question from his armchair right now, simply because the second theory implies vastly more beings like him. She'd since read that the two of them were each leaning on one of two competing rules philosophers argue about -- the self-sampling assumption and the self-indication assumption -- and that Theo couldn't just invoke whichever one gave him the conclusion he wanted. Theo maintained that his version used the more modest of the two rules and didn't commit him to anything so extravagant.

Could you go through this move by move -- Theo's original argument, Mara's objection, his Boltzmann-brain analogy, and her reply -- and say who has the better of it at each step? I'd also like to know whether there's a principled way to decide when facts about your own position among other observers are allowed to carry evidential weight and when they aren't, or whether Theo and Mara are each just reaching for whichever rule saves them from the conclusion they like least.
