Over the past few weeks I've fallen down a rabbit hole that started with a passing reference to Aristotle's sea-battle argument in a LessWrong thread on logical fatalism, and ended with me reading through the SEP's entry on future contingents and a couple of the papers it cites. What strikes me is that almost every popular treatment stops at "Aristotle denied that future contingent statements are true or false yet," when the live dispute among philosophers of time turns out to be much more specific. Supervaluationists hold that "there will be a sea battle tomorrow" is neither true nor false today, even though the disjunction "there will be a sea battle tomorrow, or there will not" remains a logical truth; defenders of the Thin Red Line, a species of Ockhamism, instead hold that exactly one of the branching possible histories is already the actual one, so the sentence is determinately true or false right now, without that truth making the battle unavoidable. I've read enough to see why each side thinks the other's position is borderline incoherent, but not enough to reconstruct the arguments for myself.

Could you write me a short dialogue, staged on the docks the night before the battle, between a supervaluationist strategist and an admiral sympathetic to the Thin Red Line, both serving on the same ship, who keep interrupting their pre-battle preparations to argue about whether it is already true that they will be fighting at dawn? I'd like each of them to press the other's characteristic weak point rather than simply restate their own view: the admiral should have to answer the charge that positing one privileged actual future among otherwise-open branches either does no real explanatory work or quietly reintroduces the very settledness the view was meant to avoid, while the strategist should have to answer the charge that denying any truth value to "there will be a battle" while still affirming the truth of "there will be a battle or there will not" is an unstable halfway house between classical and non-classical logic. Please don't let either of them win too easily -- I'd like to come away about as genuinely torn as I am right now.
