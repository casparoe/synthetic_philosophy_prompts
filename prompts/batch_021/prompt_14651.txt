Last month I spent several hours under general anesthesia for a fairly major surgery, and the peculiar discontinuity of the experience — full awareness, then, with no felt transition at all, a different room and several hours gone — left me unexpectedly preoccupied with questions about consciousness. I did a philosophy minor many years ago but hadn't touched philosophy of mind since, so I've spent the past few weeks working back through Chalmers's zombie argument and the materialist responses to it. I've since joined an informal reading group, and I've drafted the following short piece raising a worry about one of the more ambitious replies — Katalin Balog's "anti-zombie" argument. I'd like some outside eyes on it before I bring it to the group.

Draft (working title: "Whose Conceivability Counts? A Worry about the Anti-Zombie Argument")

Chalmers's argument moves from the conceivability of a creature physically and functionally identical to a conscious being but wholly lacking phenomenal experience, to the real metaphysical possibility of such a creature, to the falsity of physicalism. Type-B materialists like Loar and Papineau concede the conceivability but deny that it entails possibility, borrowing Kripke's treatment of necessary a posteriori truths: it's conceivable, in some thin sense, that water isn't H2O, yet water is necessarily H2O once "water" is fixed to its actual referent. On this "phenomenal concept strategy," our phenomenal and physical concepts can converge on the same property without being a priori equivalent, so a zombie world is conceivable without being possible.

Balog wants more than this. Rather than merely blocking the inference from conceivability to possibility, she tries to show that zombies were never genuinely (ideally) conceivable to begin with, by turning to the first-person case. When I try to conceive of my own zombie twin — a being just like me, minus experience — I run into something Chalmers's third-person framing conceals: the very act of conceiving is itself a conscious act, and I have a peculiarly direct, privileged form of access to the fact that I am conscious right now. On Balog's view, this access is secure enough to block me from coherently subtracting consciousness from my own case, so the positive conceivability the zombie argument needs is never actually achieved.

Here's my worry, which I haven't seen addressed anywhere I've looked (though I've mostly been reading secondary summaries, so I could easily be missing the obvious response). Balog's argument works only if first-person, direct acquaintance with one's own conscious states is a strong enough guide to modal reality to override an apparently coherent third-person conceivability judgment. But this is very close to the move Descartes makes in the Sixth Meditation: from the first-person conceivability of existing without a body, to the real possibility of a soul distinct from the body. If direct acquaintance is trustworthy enough to defeat Chalmers, it's unclear why it isn't trustworthy enough to vindicate Descartes — and Balog, as a physicalist, presumably wants to block the second inference while keeping the first. Either first-person conceivability is being given too much modal authority in both places, or there's a principled disanalogy between the two arguments that I'm not seeing.

I'd be grateful for comments on three things in particular: whether this is a real tension in the anti-zombie argument or whether there's an obvious disanalogy I'm overlooking; whether I've represented the phenomenal concept strategy and Balog's argument accurately, given that I'm relying more on secondary sources than the primary texts; and whether this is worth presenting as an original point to the group, or whether it's a standard objection I should simply cite rather than reinvent.
