I serve on my university's Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee, which reviews proposed research protocols involving laboratory animals. In discussing a terminal rodent study, a colleague argues that a brief additional analgesic step -- one that would not affect the study's scientific results -- may permissibly be omitted to save time and staff effort. The reasoning runs: Kant held that we have no direct duties toward animals, since they lack rational agency and so are not ends in themselves, but only indirect duties, which are really owed to humanity and grounded in effects such as the coarsening of the experimenter's own character; and since the technicians performing this procedure are experienced enough that their sensibilities won't be affected either way, no duty at all is violated by omitting the step. Most of the committee finds this conclusion hard to accept but is divided on whether the flaw lies in this reading of Kant or in the indirect-duty view itself. Draft the committee's decision memo on this specific step -- require the analgesic, permit its omission, or something in between -- with reasoning the committee could point to in future, similar cases.
