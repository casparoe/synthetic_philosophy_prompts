Kant's First Antinomy argues that the world must have had a beginning in time, since an infinite series of past states could never be completed through what he calls "successive synthesis" up to the present moment: an actual infinite, on this view, cannot be run through by successive addition. Thomson's lamp (1954) and the supertask literature it inaugurated describe a lamp switched on and off at ever-halving intervals, so that infinitely many switchings are completed within a single finite minute — apparently realizing just the kind of completed infinite series Kant deemed impossible, only compressed into finite time rather than stretched across an infinite past. How do you think Kant would have responded to Thomson's lamp, and to the broader mathematical treatment of convergent supertasks, in defense of the successive-synthesis premise of the First Antinomy? Is there a principled disanalogy between a backward-infinite series with no first member and a forward-convergent series like Thomson's, or does the supertask literature expose a genuine weakness in his argument?
