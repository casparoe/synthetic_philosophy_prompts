The question set for students was the following: "Section 47 of the (hypothetical) Intimate Privacy Act provides: 'Any person who knowingly distributes, without the consent of the person depicted, any photograph or video recording that depicts an identifiable person in a state of nudity or engaged in sexual conduct is guilty of unlawful disclosure of private images, a Class A misdemeanor.' Three years after the Act took effect, a man used AI video-generation software to produce a realistic video showing his ex-partner — a real, identifiable person — engaged in sexual conduct that never actually occurred, building it entirely from public photographs of her face; she was never filmed or photographed in any state of undress. He then sent the video to several of her coworkers. He is charged under Section 47. Using both textualist and purposivist methods of statutory interpretation, analyze whether Section 47 reaches this conduct, and state which approach you find more persuasive and why."

Here is the student's answer, reproduced in full: "Textualism instructs courts to interpret a statute according to the ordinary public meaning its words would have carried at the time of enactment, without importing purposes or legislative history that the text itself does not support. The operative word in Section 47 is 'recording.' In ordinary usage, a recording is a fixation of something that actually happened — one records an event, a conversation, a performance — but one cannot 'record' an event that never took place. Because the defendant's video is entirely synthetic and depicts an act that never occurred, a textualist would conclude it is not a 'video recording' within the meaning of Section 47, so the statute does not reach it. Indeed, because the legislature that wrote Section 47 could not have anticipated video generated by artificial intelligence, a faithful textualist must confine the statute to the kinds of images lawmakers actually had in view when they legislated, which were genuine photographs and films; if the legislature wants to reach AI-generated fakes, it has to amend the statute, since courts applying this method should not stretch the text to reach an unanticipated case no matter how sympathetic the victim.

Purposivism, by contrast, asks what mischief the legislature meant to remedy and reads the statute to serve that end even where the literal words are an imperfect fit. The evident purpose of Section 47 is to protect people from having intimate depictions of themselves circulated without consent, and that harm to dignity and reputation is identical whether the depiction is a real recording or a synthetic one — if anything it is worse here, since the victim never consented to being filmed in any form. A purposivist judge would therefore read 'video recording' to include a realistic synthetic depiction, since excluding it would produce a result plainly at odds with the statute's aim. This is exactly the interpretive move Justice Scalia made in Holy Trinity Church v. United States, where the Court set aside a statute's literal text in favor of its evident purpose.

On balance, I find the purposivist reading more persuasive, since a technical reading that lets the defendant escape liability merely because his method of invading the victim's privacy was novel defeats the entire point of the statute."
