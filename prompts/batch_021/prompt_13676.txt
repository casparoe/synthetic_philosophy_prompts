A friend of mine—a philosophy grad student working through Quine's minor essays for a seminar paper—sent me a copy of "On the Nature of Moral Values," the short piece Quine wrote for the 1978 Frankena/Stevenson/Brandt Festschrift and apparently the only thing he ever published devoted entirely to ethics. We ended up arguing about how to read it for the better part of an evening. The essay draws an elaborate analogy between the structure of empirical science, where a holistic web of belief meets experience only at its periphery through observation sentences keyed to shared sensory stimulation, and the structure of moral value, where innate affective responses (Quine's examples include a wincing reflex and a child's internalized attachment to a parent's approval) play a role structurally similar to observation sentences, with general ethical principles playing the role of theory. Quine, however, seems to conclude that the analogy ultimately breaks in science's favor: because moral "observations" don't command anything like the cross-subject agreement that sensory observation sentences do, ethics ends up on comparatively shaky ground, closer to a matter of individual or cultural upbringing than to anything resembling objective check.

My friend thought this conclusion drops straight out of Quine's general empiricism once the analogy is taken seriously. I wasn't so sure, and this is the part we never resolved: the operative standard in Quine's naturalism elsewhere isn't cross-subject convergence but indispensability to our best overall theory—we're ontologically committed to whatever that theory needs to quantify over ("to be is to be the value of a bound variable"), and Quine is perfectly willing to grant reality to classes, numbers, and unobservable particles that no one perceives directly and that plenty of competent theorists disagree about, so long as they earn their keep. If moral value is at least as load-bearing and indispensable to how we actually organize and act on our total picture of the world, then Quine's own standards would seem to license treating it as real in whatever thin, immanent sense he treats numbers or electrons as real—which would make the convergence gap look more like evidence that ethics is an immature or badly taught discipline than like a principled reason to withhold ontological credit.

I'd like you to argue my side of this as rigorously as you can: that a consistent application of Quine's own naturalistic criteria for ontological commitment should have pushed him toward treating moral value as a genuine, indispensable posit, rather than toward the more deflationary picture he actually seems to favor in the essay. Please don't just wave away the disanalogy—engage seriously with the strongest reasons someone might have for thinking intersubjective convergence is doing independent, non-negotiable work in Quine's epistemology, rather than being an incidental feature of the particular sciences he happened to be generalizing from—and tell me what you think a fully consistent Quinean naturalist should actually conclude about the objectivity of moral value.
