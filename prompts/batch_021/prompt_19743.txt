Over the past few months I've been getting into computational biology as a side project, mostly by implementing toy sequence-alignment and gene-expression tools, and it's left me with a question I can't quite shake. Biologists routinely describe the genome as "the code of life," and the closer I look at the actual machinery, the more literal that description seems: a discrete four-symbol alphabet, read in a fixed direction, transcribed into RNA and then translated into protein by dedicated molecular hardware (ribosomes and tRNAs) via a fixed codon-to-amino-acid lookup table that is essentially universal across all known life. From where I sit as a software engineer, that isn't just a loose metaphor — structurally it looks identical to a compiler turning a symbolic source file into machine instructions. So it seems to follow pretty directly that an organism's phenotype is, in a fairly strong sense, "computed" from its genotype the same way a program's behavior is computed from its source, and that things like gene-environment interaction or epigenetic regulation are just extra runtime parameters layered on top of that computation, rather than anything that threatens the basic code/program picture.

I brought this up with a couple of friends who work in genomics, expecting them to basically agree, and instead they pushed back hard, saying that treating the genome as a program and the phenotype as its output is precisely the framing that philosophers of biology have spent decades arguing against, and that singling out DNA as uniquely "informational," while treating everything else in the cell and its environment as mere background conditions, is a substantive and contestable theoretical stance rather than a neutral description of the biochemistry. I don't see what's wrong with the analogy given how precisely it seems to fit the translation mechanism itself, so I'd like help figuring out exactly where the inference from "translation involves a real code" to "the genome is a program for the organism" breaks down. Is this really just a dispute over words, or is there a substantive issue about what "information" is even supposed to mean in this context?
