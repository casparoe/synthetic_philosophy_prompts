I've spent a long time reading LessWrong and the Alignment Forum, and one idea I keep circling back to is the "commitment races problem": the worry that in any bargaining situation where committing first confers an advantage -- the canonical picture is a game of Chicken in which whoever visibly disables his own steering wheel wins -- sufficiently capable agents face pressure to commit as early and as irrevocably as possible, before they have had time to become wise enough to know whether the commitment in question is actually a good one. Two such agents racing to out-commit each other can lock themselves into mutually destructive threats that neither can back down from once made. I'm recording a long-form interview for a small podcast that grew out of my old meetup group's reading circle, and next week's guest is a decision theorist -- I'd rather leave her unnamed, since I want the questions to be about the position and not the person -- who has worked out the most detailed existing version of the "surrogate goals" reply to this problem: the idea that an agent can pre-emptively adopt some decoy target for threats to attach to, so that a threat aimed at the decoy loses its bite, since the target has little reason to pay up and the threatener correspondingly little reason to threaten in the first place. Whenever someone points out a new way the underlying race could resurface one level up -- for instance, an adversary threatening the very process by which the surrogate goal gets chosen -- her stock reply is that this is "a bargaining problem, not a proof of impossibility."

Could you draft eight to ten interview questions for this conversation, each with a short follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give? Nothing she could answer merely by restating the setup, though: I need at least one question pressing on the apparent paradox that committing early is supposed to be rational from an "ahead of time" point of view, even though it is exactly the information gained by waiting that would tell you whether the commitment was wise; at least one that asks her to defend measuring who committed "first" by how much reasoning an agent has done rather than by calendar time, and whether that lets an agent simply declare, after the fact, that it had "already" committed before its rival; at least one that presses the regress worry hard enough that her "bargaining problem, not a proof of impossibility" line starts to look like it is relocating the race rather than resolving it, with the follow-up ready for the moment she reaches for that same phrase again; at least one asking her to name a real historical or biological case -- nuclear brinkmanship, a labor strike, a hawk-dove contest in animal behavior, whichever she prefers -- that she considers genuinely structurally identical to the AI case rather than merely a loose analogy, and whether the disanalogy that artificial agents might be able to verify each other's source code or prior commitments changes the argument; and a closing question about what specific evidence or argument would actually change her mind about the size of this risk, rather than simply motivating another patch to the theory.
