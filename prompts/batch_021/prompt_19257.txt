I never studied philosophy formally, but a while back I went down a Wikipedia rabbit hole about why so many everyday rules feel completely made up and yet nobody ever breaks them—things like which side of the road everyone drives on. That's how I first ran into David Lewis's book Convention, or really just summaries of it, since the actual text is heavy going for someone without a background in logic. As far as I can piece together, Lewis's idea is that a "convention" is the solution to what he calls a coordination problem: there are at least two ways a group could act that would work out fine for everyone as long as everyone expected everyone else to do the same thing, and the group settles on one of them, which then perpetuates itself purely through precedent and the fact that everyone expects everyone else to keep it up—but it only counts as a genuine convention, on Lewis's account, if some other way of acting would have worked just as well, so that the one people actually landed on is arbitrary rather than forced on them. I've been telling anyone who will listen about this ever since, including my friends Mara and Colin, which is presumably why, a couple of weekends ago, an ordinary bit of queue-jumping turned into a proper argument between the two of them.

We were waiting for a table at a busy brunch place when a guy walked straight past the whole line and got seated because he knew the host. After he left, Mara and Colin spent a good fifteen minutes arguing about whether queueing even counts as a "Lewis convention" in the first place, and it went roughly like this. (1) Mara said queueing is a textbook case: like driving on the right, it's arbitrary, it solves the coordination problem of who gets served in what order without anyone having to negotiate, and it keeps going purely because everyone expects everyone else to hold their place in line. (2) Colin said that's not the same structure at all: driving on the right works because deviating is bad for the deviator too, whereas the guy who walked to the front did great for himself, at everyone else's expense—that's not a coordination equilibrium in Lewis's sense, where conforming already has to be your own best move given what others are doing; queueing survives on glares and awkward confrontations, not on it being independently rational to comply. (3) Mara answered that once you count the risk of a glare or a confrontation as part of what you stand to gain or lose, conforming still comes out as everyone's best response given what everyone else is doing, so it's still a coordination equilibrium—Colin is just refusing to count the social cost as part of the situation. (4) Colin replied that this move proves too much: if any social pressure at all can be folded into "the payoffs" after the fact, then literally any norm whatsoever, including ones we'd normally call moral obligations rather than mere conventions, could be redescribed as a coordination equilibrium, and Lewis's entire distinction between conventions and other kinds of norms collapses into nothing.

Neither of them budged, and I don't know this material well enough to referee it myself. Could you go through the exchange move by move and say who has the stronger argument at each of the four steps, rather than just declaring an overall winner? I'd also like to know whether either of them is simply missing something obvious—for instance, a thought that occurred to me afterward, which neither of them raised: doesn't Lewis's account also require that some other regularity could have worked just as well as the one that actually caught on, and if first-come-first-served is really the uniquely fairest way to allocate scarce spots in a line, rather than just one arbitrary option among equally good alternatives, wouldn't that undercut Mara's opening claim on completely different grounds than anything Colin said?
