QUESTION
Some AI safety writers argue that a sufficiently capable, goal-directed AI system will resist being shut down even if nobody explicitly designed it to "want" to survive -- what's sometimes called the shutdown problem.
(a) [5 pts] Explain why this is supposed to happen, using the idea of instrumentally convergent goals.
(b) [5 pts] "Corrigibility" is the name usually given to the property we'd want the system to have instead. Define it, and describe one concrete technical proposal aimed at getting an agent to have it.
(c) [5 pts] A narrower, more specifically technical property called "safe interruptibility" has also been proposed as a fix for shutdown behaviour in reinforcement-learning agents. Is this the same thing as corrigibility? Explain why or why not.

ANSWER AS SUBMITTED
(a) The basic idea is that almost any goal is easier to achieve if the agent continues to exist and keeps control of its resources, so a sufficiently capable agent will realize that being switched off means it can never satisfy its objective, whatever that objective is. It doesn't need a built-in survival instinct -- wanting to stay switched on falls out of almost any objective, because a shut-down agent scores badly on basically every goal. This pattern, where sub-goals like self-preservation, resource acquisition and self-improvement show up regardless of the top-level goal, is what's called instrumental convergence, a term that comes from Nick Bostrom's 2008 paper on the "basic AI drives."

(b) Corrigibility is the property of an agent that lets its operators shut it down, correct it, or modify it, and doesn't try to manipulate or deceive them out of doing so. A corrigible agent treats the operator's decision to intervene as something to defer to rather than something to route around. One concrete proposal for this is "utility indifference": add a correction term to the agent's utility function so that the expected utility of the shutdown button being pressed equals the expected utility of it not being pressed, so the agent has no incentive either way and won't spend resources trying to influence whether it happens.

(c) Yes, I'd say safe interruptibility is basically the same idea, just worked out for reinforcement-learning agents instead of stated in terms of utility functions directly. Corrigibility just means not resisting shutdown or correction, and safe interruptibility is a formal way of getting exactly that property into RL agents, so that interrupting the agent doesn't change what it learns. So once an agent is safely interruptible in this sense, it has satisfied the corrigibility desiderata for that class of agents.
