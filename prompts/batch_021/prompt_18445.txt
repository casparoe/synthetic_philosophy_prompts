My niece, who is finishing a doctorate in philosophy on practical reasons, is marrying her partner next month, and she has asked me -- her uncle, a retired physicist who came to philosophy only in retirement -- to give the toast. While preparing, so as not to embarrass myself in front of her department, I kept returning to Bernard Williams's distinction between internal and external reasons, and to how squarely a wedding vow sits on top of it: is "I will" binding only for as long as the desire behind it lasts, or does saying it create an obligation that survives the desire's disappearance? Roughly half the guests will be her fellow philosophers, who will notice at once if I flatten the distinction; the other half are family who have never heard of internalism and only want to be moved. Please write me a five-minute toast in which this tension does the real work of the speech rather than sitting in it as decoration, and which still lands as love rather than as a seminar, without pretending the difficulty resolves as neatly as a wedding toast would like.
