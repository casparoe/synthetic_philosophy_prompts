Had a whole debate with my friend last night about whether LLMs actually believe anything, and it turned into a straight up Dennett fight. She's all in on the intentional stance: her view is that if ascribing beliefs and desires to a system is just the most useful way to predict what it'll do, that IS having beliefs, no further fact needed, so a model believes stuff in exactly the sense Dennett cares about. I pushed back with this: Dennett's whole rationale for the stance is that treating a system as rational is supposed to earn you real predictive power that the design stance doesn't give you as easily. But these models will confidently assert P in one chat and confidently assert not-P in the next, with zero sense that anything changed - which feels like a totally different kind of failure than a human just being wrong or self deceived under pressure. My claim was that this doesn't just make individual belief attributions to the model shaky, it undercuts the actual rationale for adopting the intentional stance toward it in the first place. We went back and forth for like 45 min and nobody budged. Can you build the strongest version of my side of this, and actually engage with the obvious Dennettian comebacks (e.g. that humans are inconsistent too, or that the instability is itself just more behavior a suitably refined intentional stance could predict) instead of just asserting my conclusion?
