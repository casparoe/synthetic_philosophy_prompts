Question 3 of a graduate seminar exam on philosophy of mind reads as follows:

"(a) Reconstruct Jaegwon Kim's causal exclusion argument against nonreductive physicalism, making its key premises explicit. (b) Nonreductive physicalists have sometimes appealed to the multiple realizability of mental properties as a way of resisting the argument's threat of mental epiphenomenalism. Explain this line of response and assess whether it succeeds. (c) State briefly how Kim himself proposes to resolve the difficulty, noting which mental properties, if any, he takes to escape the threat of epiphenomenalism." (25 points: 10/10/5)

One student's answer is reproduced in full below.

"(a) Kim's argument targets nonreductive physicalism, the view that mental properties are distinct from, but wholly dependent on, physical properties. The argument turns on four claims. Distinctness: a mental property M is not type-identical to the physical property P that realizes it on a given occasion -- this is what makes the view nonreductive rather than a type-identity theory. Supervenience: whenever M is instantiated, it is instantiated in virtue of the instantiation of some physical realizer P. Causal Closure of the Physical: every physical event that has a cause at time t has a sufficient physical cause at t. And the Exclusion Principle: no single event can have more than one sufficient cause at a given time, barring genuine overdetermination. Now suppose M causes a physical effect P*. Since P is, by Closure, already sufficient for P*, and M is not identical to P, we appear to have two distinct sufficient causes of P*. Given Exclusion, this cannot be tolerated as ordinary overdetermination, since it would recur systematically rather than being a coincidence like two bullets striking a heart simultaneously. So either M does not really cause P* -- mental properties are epiphenomenal -- or M causes P* only by causing P, a 'downward causation' that Kim thinks is unintelligible for a merely supervenient property. The stakes are high: as Davidson's dictum has it, to be real is to have causal powers, so an epiphenomenal mental property is a significant embarrassment for physicalism.

(b) A response nonreductive physicalists have found attractive appeals to the very multiple realizability that motivated the view in the first place. Because a mental property like pain can be realized by heterogeneous physical properties across different systems, M cannot be identical to any one physical realizer P; and once M and P are not identical, the 'overdetermination' Kim describes is not the objectionable kind, since M's multiple realizability shows that M's causal contribution does not simply duplicate P's, but is pitched at a different, more abstract level that is not in competition with P for producing the same effect. On this response, multiple realizability is exactly what defuses the exclusion worry: it is because mental properties are multiply realizable that they can be causally relevant without being causally redundant, so nonreductive physicalism can retain both the irreducibility and the causal efficacy of the mental.

(c) Kim's own proposal is to accept functional reduction for mental properties that admit of a functional definition. A property like believing that p, or the functional side of pain -- its role in producing withdrawal behavior, reports, and further inference -- can be reconceived as a second-order property of having some physical property that occupies a certain causal role. So understood, the property is not a distinct competitor to its physical realizer but is reduced to it, and its causal efficacy is simply inherited from the realizer. What resists this treatment, on Kim's view, is qualia: the intrinsic, felt quality of an experience, as opposed to its causal role, cannot plausibly be given a functional definition, so qualia remain distinct from their physical bases and are not rescued by reduction. Kim therefore concludes that physicalism is vindicated for the functional side of the mind, but that qualia are left epiphenomenal -- hence the title of his book, Physicalism, or Something Near Enough."
