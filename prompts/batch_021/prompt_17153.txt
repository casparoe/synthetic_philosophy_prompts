"Luis de Molina, a 16th century Jesuit, tried to reconcile human free will with God's providence by dividing God's knowledge into three logical moments. First, natural knowledge: knowledge of all necessary truths and all merely possible states of affairs, prior to any decision on God's part to create. This is the same in every possible world, since it doesn't depend on what God decides. Second, middle knowledge: knowledge of all the true counterfactuals of creaturely freedom, i.e. what any possible free creature would freely choose to do in any possible circumstance. This is contingent, since the creature could have chosen otherwise, but it's still prior to God's decree to create, because God takes these truths into account when deciding which world to actualize. Third, after God freely decrees to create a particular world, he has free knowledge: knowledge of everything that will actually happen in the world he's chosen.

Middle knowledge is what lets Molinists explain how God can providentially govern a world of free creatures without determining their choices: God simply looks at all the possible worlds available to him through his middle knowledge and picks the best one to create.

This framework is also central to Plantinga's free will defense. His key move is transworld depravity: its possible that every free creature would go wrong at least once no matter which circumstances God put them in, so it wasn't within God's power to create a world with free creatures and no sin at all. This shows the existence of evil is logically compatible with an omnipotent, wholly good God.

The main objection to middle knowledge is the grounding objection, first leveled by the Dominican theologian Domingo Banez against Molina during the De Auxiliis controversy: counterfactuals of freedom can't be true prior to God's decree, because there's nothing to make them true, the creature in question doesn't exist yet and God's will hasn't settled anything either. If the objection succeeds, Molinism collapses into either open theism or a Banezian picture of predetermination. I think it's a serious problem but not fatal, since Molinists like Thomas Flint have argued that not every truth needs an external grounding in the way the objection assumes."
