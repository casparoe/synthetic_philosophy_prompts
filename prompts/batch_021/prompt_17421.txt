In my day job I work on machine learning systems, and I've recently been reading about causal inference (Judea Pearl's work on causal graphs and the do-calculus, mainly) to understand why so much of classical statistics trains people to avoid causal language in favor of purely correlational claims. Several of the historical overviews I read trace this reticence back to Hume's argument that we never actually perceive a "necessary connection" between cause and effect — only a constant conjunction of the two, plus a mental habit of expecting the second upon observing the first. Curious, I went further back and found that the medieval theologian Al-Ghazālī had made an argument with a strikingly similar structure against the Aristotelian philosophers of his day, centuries before Hume — most memorably via his example of fire and cotton, where he insists we only ever observe burning accompanying contact with fire, never any perceivable necessity that would rule out, say, God producing one without the other.

What I can't quite square is that these two arguments, which seem to share the same core skeptical premise (we never perceive necessity, only regular co-occurrence), get put to completely opposite uses. Ghazālī's version is the standard basis given for occasionalism, on which God is the only genuine causal agent and the "laws of nature" are just His habitual practice, suspendable at will in miracles (though I gather scholars disagree about how fully committed Ghazālī himself was to occasionalism as opposed to a more modest "we can't rationally rule out miracles" claim). Hume's version, by contrast, grounds about as thoroughly naturalistic and anti-miraculous a theory of causation as one could ask for — Hume is separately famous for arguing that we should essentially never believe testimony of a miracle. Could you compare the two arguments in some depth and locate exactly where they part ways: what extra premise or background commitment does each side import that isn't already contained in the shared observation that necessity is never perceived? And judging each argument on its own terms rather than by whether you find its conclusion congenial — does either side actually earn its further conclusion (Ghazālī's occasionalism, or Hume's fully mind-and-God-independent regularity account) from the shared skeptical starting point alone, or does each smuggle in something more?
