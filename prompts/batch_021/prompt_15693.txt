Over the past month or so I've fallen into something of a rabbit hole reading about "supertasks" — the idea of completing infinitely many steps within a finite stretch of time. The example that really lodged itself in my head is Thomson's lamp: you flip a switch on, wait thirty seconds, flip it off, wait fifteen seconds, flip it on again, and so on, halving the wait each time, so that by the end of a single minute the switch has been flipped infinitely many times. The question is whether the lamp is on or off once that minute has elapsed.

What I hadn't appreciated at first is that this was never meant as a mere brain-teaser: Thomson used it in the 1950s to argue that finishing a supertask like this is not just physically unachievable but strictly incoherent, since there appears to be no consistent answer as to the lamp's final state. I later read that a philosopher named Benacerraf (if I have the name right) pushed back on this a few years afterward, on the grounds that the rule governing the lamp only fixes its state immediately after each flip, and simply has nothing to say about the state exactly at the one-minute mark, since no single flip is its immediate predecessor — meaning the lamp's final state is left open by the story rather than genuinely contradictory. Digging further, I also came across a couple of related scenarios that seem to complicate this reply: one involving a vase that has balls added to and removed from it infinitely often, and a nastier one involving a whole line-up of assassins (or, in one telling, gods), each poised to act at their own moment strictly before some deadline, where there doesn't seem to be any single leftover instant to hold responsible for the trouble.

I would like you to write a dialogue between two people who hold these opposing positions: one who maintains something close to Thomson's original view, that cases like this really do show supertasks to be logically impossible rather than merely impractical, and another who maintains something like the reply above, that the appearance of contradiction always traces back to a mistaken assumption about what the rules were ever meant to settle. I don't want this to be a one-sided contest — I'd like the first speaker to have a genuine rejoinder to the "it's just an unsettled boundary condition" move, rather than simply restating the lamp case (the assassins/gods scenario might be relevant here, if it helps, precisely because there's no obvious boundary instant to point to), and I'd like the second speaker to have to actually grapple with that rejoinder rather than falling back on their original line. Both of them should be arguing in good faith and at their best, so that it's genuinely unclear, at least at the outset, who has the better of it.
