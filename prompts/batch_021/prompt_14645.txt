I've spent years reading rationalist blogs and forums, and a disagreement from a small meetup reading group -- we'd been working through David Lewis's papers on objective chance -- has been nagging at me since I couldn't settle it myself. We'd reached the Principal Principle, roughly the thesis that your credence in an outcome should equal its known objective chance, when two members, Priya and Sanjay, got into it.

(1) Priya said the principle just is what it means to treat something as a chance: if you know a coin's chance of heads is 0.5, rationality requires credence 0.5, full stop, and anything else is a mistake.

(2) Sanjay objected that this can't be right, since Lewis himself restricted the principle to "admissible" evidence: if a flawless clairvoyant truly tells you the coin will land heads, your credence should rise well above 0.5 even though the chance is still 0.5, so chance alone doesn't fix credence.

(3) Priya replied that "admissible" then just means "whatever evidence I already think should win," which makes the whole principle circular, since you can't specify in advance which evidence is allowed to trump chance without presupposing the answer.

(4) Sanjay said no: Lewis gave admissibility real content -- ordinary historical facts up to the time in question are admissible, and it's only exotic stuff like clairvoyance or time travel that gets excluded, so the line isn't ad hoc.

(5) Priya then invoked the "undermining" problem: since Lewis's chances are Humean, grounded in patterns spanning all of history, facts about the future can bear on what the chance at a given time even was, which blurs exactly the past/future line Sanjay just drew, and is why Lewis himself had to revise the principle in 1994.

(6) Sanjay granted that undermining forced a revision, but said this only shows admissibility needed refining, not abandoning, and pointed out that a propensity theorist who rejects Humean chance never faces undermining at all -- so the problem indicts a particular metaphysics of chance, not the Principal Principle itself.

Neither was willing to concede.
