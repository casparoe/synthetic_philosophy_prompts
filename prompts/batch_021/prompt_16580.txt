I cover science and ideas for a living, and I've spent the last few weeks reporting a feature on the evolution of the mammalian ear. One detail keeps tripping me up as I try to write about it clearly: the malleus and incus, the two tiny bones nearest the eardrum, are the very same bones — the articular and quadrate — that formed the jaw joint in our synapsid ancestors, before that role was taken over by the dentary-squamosal joint that all mammals use to chew today. Paleontologists point to transitional fossils such as Morganucodon and Diarthrognathus (literally 'two-jointed jaw'), which appear to show both jaw joints operating side by side, as evidence for just how gradual this handover was. When I drafted the sentence "at some point, these bones acquired the function of transmitting sound," a philosopher of biology I'd contacted for background stopped me and said it quietly presupposes an answer to an unresolved debate about what a biological 'function' even is.

She pointed me to two rival accounts I'd like your help sorting out. The etiological (or 'selected effects') theory, originating with Larry Wright and later sharpened by philosophers like Karen Neander, holds that a trait's function is the effect that explains why natural selection kept producing it — functions, on this view, are inherently backward-looking and tied to a history of selection. The causal-role theory, due to Robert Cummins, drops the history requirement: a trait's function is whatever it currently contributes to some capacity of the organism that we're trying to explain, regardless of how the trait came to be there. Applied to my case, the etiological theory seems to say the proto-ossicles could not yet have had the function of hearing at the moment they first happened to transmit a useful vibration, since that effect hadn't yet had the chance to be selected for — the function would only 'switch on' retroactively, once enough subsequent generations of selection on hearing had accumulated. The causal-role theory, by contrast, seems to say the bones had the function the instant they made any contribution to detecting sound, transitional status notwithstanding.

I'd like you to compare the two accounts head-to-head on this specific case rather than just summarizing each in general terms: which theory gives the more defensible verdict about when, if ever, the transitional ossicles first counted as having the function of transmitting sound? And is whichever account comes out looking worse here stuck with a genuine bullet to bite, or does it have a good reply available? Please justify your answer rather than restating both positions and declining to choose.
