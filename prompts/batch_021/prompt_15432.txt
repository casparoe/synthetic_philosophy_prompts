I am drafting a novel whose narrator is a former actuary who has drifted into amateur epistemology, and in the course of researching her voice I have been sitting in on a small monthly reading group where a handful of friends work through papers in formal epistemology purely for pleasure. At last month's meeting, two of the regulars, Dev and Marta, spent most of the evening arguing about van Fraassen's Reflection Principle, and I wrote down the exchange afterward because I want to adapt a version of it for the book. I can follow each move individually, but I cannot tell who is actually getting the better of the argument, so I would like your help adjudicating it properly.

The dispute grew out of a case Dev raised about himself. He is about to attend a weekend seminar run by a cryptocurrency promoter whose methods he already knows in detail: the promoter reliably moves something like nine in ten initially skeptical attendees to a credence of about 0.9 in the claim that his featured coin will multiply tenfold within the year, using scarcity framing and curated testimonials rather than anything resembling evidence. Dev's own credence going in is a skeptical 0.05, but he is close to certain -- call it 0.95 -- that he will walk out at 0.9 regardless, simply because that is what happens to almost everyone who sits through the pitch. The exchange, in order, went like this:

(1) Dev said Reflection, taken at face value, requires his current credence in the coin claim -- conditional on the supposition that his credence tomorrow will be 0.9 -- to itself already be 0.9, and since he is nearly certain that supposition will come true, the principle seems to commit him, tonight, to shifting his unconditional credence sharply upward before he has heard a word of the pitch. He called this an unacceptable result: simply knowing in advance that he is about to be worked over should not do the manipulator's job for him.

(2) Marta answered that this is not really a case Reflection was ever meant to govern. The principle's own justification, she said, treats your future opinion the way you would treat a trusted expert, and that kind of deference only makes sense when you lack specific, standing reasons to distrust the process that will produce the future opinion. Since Dev can already name exactly why his credence will move and already regards that mechanism as worthless, his post-seminar self is not the kind of future self Reflection was ever meant to defer to.

(3) Dev replied that this fix saves Reflection only by hollowing it out. If "applies only when you already trust the shift" is built in as a silent proviso, the principle never tells you anything you did not already believe: it stops being an independent constraint underwritten by a Dutch-book argument and becomes a restatement of "defer to your future opinions when you already think you should." The interesting uses of Reflection, he said -- trusting a future self who has seen evidence you have not, or a future expert consensus you cannot yet anticipate -- are exactly the cases where you cannot yet see why your opinion will change but are told rationality requires deferring anyway; excluding the seminar case by fiat does not defend the principle so much as concede that it fails on contact with it.

(4) Marta said Dev was collapsing a distinction he needed to keep. Being "almost certain you will end up at 0.9" is not one bare fact that means the same thing in every case; here it comes bundled with a further fact Dev already possesses right now, namely that the shift will be produced by a process with no evidential bearing on the coin claim. That further fact is itself part of his current information, and a coherent Bayesian is entitled to let it screen off the naive inference Reflection would otherwise license. Far from being an ad hoc exclusion, she said, this is precisely what separates manipulation cases from the cases -- deferring to a more experienced future self, or to evidence you have not yet seen -- that made Reflection worth taking seriously in the first place.
