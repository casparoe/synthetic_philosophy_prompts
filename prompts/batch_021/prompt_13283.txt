I spent my career as a particle physicist, and since retiring I've gotten hooked on philosophy, currently working through some epistemology. I just ran into the lottery paradox and the preface paradox and they hit uncomfortably close to home. In particle physics we worry a lot about the "look-elsewhere effect": if you scan enough bins or channels, some of them will cross the significance threshold just by chance, so any single result can be individually well-supported even though you know, as a matter of course, that some fraction of all the "discoveries" floating around out there are bound to be flukes. That feels a lot like the preface paradox, and the lottery paradox seems to be pointing at the same tension from a different angle. So, what are the most important implications of these two paradoxes for how someone in my position should think about a big pile of individually well justified beliefs? Do I have to give up believing each result on its own, give up believing that they cant all be true together, or is there some other way out thats not just a dodge? And do the two paradoxes actually teach the same lesson here, or do they pull apart in some way that matters?
