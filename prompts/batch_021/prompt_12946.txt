Kurt Gödel's modal ontological argument, as reconstructed from his notes by Dana Scott, defines a "God-like" being as one that possesses every positive property, then uses further axioms about essence and necessary existence to conclude that such a being must exist not merely contingently but necessarily. Given those axioms, the derivation is generally agreed to be logically valid. In a seminar on philosophy of religion, though, I learned that Howard Sobel showed the very same axioms entail "modal collapse": the result that every actually true proposition turns out to be a necessary truth, so that there are no genuinely contingent facts at all, not even the most mundane ones. Our instructor treated this as essentially closing the case against Gödel's proof, and the class moved straight on to Plantinga's version, which felt like a hasty dismissal given how intricate the underlying derivation actually is.

I'd like you to make the case that this dismissal is too hasty. Please argue that the modal collapse result does not show that Gödel's ontological argument fails, or at least not for the reason usually given. Start by reconstructing, precisely enough for a reader unfamiliar with the details to follow, how collapse is actually derived from the axioms governing positivity, essence, and necessary existence; most informal discussions of this objection assert the conclusion without walking through why it follows. Then mount the strongest defense you can of the argument against this charge, whether by showing how the offending axiom(s) could be weakened or replaced without losing the intended conclusion, by restricting the class of properties allowed to count as positive or to figure in a being's essence, or by arguing that a committed defender of the proof could simply accept the necessitarian consequences as a cost worth paying. You do not need to defend the conclusion that God exists; the target is narrower and more technical: showing that modal collapse specifically is not the knockdown objection it is usually taken to be.
