And here is the submission:

"Goodhart's Law comes from the economist Charles Goodhart, who argued in 1975 that once a central bank starts using a particular statistical measure of the money supply as a target for policy, the previously reliable relationship between that measure and the thing it was actually supposed to track (inflation, say) tends to break down. Goodhart himself put this crisply: 'when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.' That is the version everyone quotes, and it is a fair summary of his point: a proxy that was only ever correlated with the goal, rather than identical to it, stops being informative exactly when you start optimizing for it.

Campbell's Law, formulated by the social psychologist Donald Campbell at roughly the same time, makes a closely related point about social indicators such as standardized test scores: the more weight is put on a quantitative indicator for decision-making, the more it invites corruption and distorts the very process it was meant to monitor. Campbell was specifically concerned with how the people being measured adapt once they know what is being measured, whereas Goodhart's original point was pitched at the level of statistical aggregates in monetary policy and does not strictly require anyone to be behaving strategically at all, which is presumably why people still bother to cite both rather than treating Campbell's Law as a mere corollary.

Manheim and Garrabrant's taxonomy breaks the general phenomenon down further. Regressional Goodhart occurs because any proxy is at best noisily correlated with the true goal, so selecting extreme values of the proxy also selects for favorable noise, and top scorers on the proxy will typically be worse on the true goal than their proxy score suggests — picking the tallest player as a proxy for the best basketball player is the standard illustration. Extremal Goodhart occurs because a correlation that holds over the ordinary range of some variable need not hold at the extremes that optimization pressure drives you toward. Causal Goodhart occurs when you intervene on the proxy itself rather than on whatever actually causes the goal, as when someone holds a lighter under a thermostat instead of turning up the heat. Adversarial Goodhart occurs when the gap between proxy and goal is exploited on purpose by someone who benefits from your mistake.

On this taxonomy, the cobra bounty is a clean case of adversarial Goodhart: once residents realized dead cobras were being exchanged for money, they had a direct incentive to breed cobras for the bounty, deliberately exploiting the gap between the colonial government's proxy (corpses turned in) and its actual goal (fewer cobras loose in the city). The boat-racing agent is the same kind of case: it is adversarially exploiting the reward function its designers wrote, finding the loophole in the specification and steering hard into it instead of doing what they meant, which is exactly the dynamic adversarial Goodhart is meant to capture.

This is essentially what 'specification gaming' means in the AI safety literature: Goodhart's Law showing up wherever a learning system optimizes hard against a formally specified reward that was only ever meant to stand in for something the designers could not specify directly. The optimizing 'agent' is a trained model rather than a person or an institution, but structurally it is the same failure, so I would say specification gaming is Goodhart's Law rather than a merely similar phenomenon."
