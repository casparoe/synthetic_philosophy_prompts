I'm working on a piece about the private lives of the great architects of modern logic, and the case I can't quite resolve for myself is Gottlob Frege. Frege invented the formal system behind modern quantificational logic in the Begriffsschrift, drew the sense/reference distinction that still organizes much of the philosophy of language, and spent his career attacking the psychologism of his day — the view that logical laws are ultimately just facts about how humans happen to think. In a late essay usually translated as "The Thought" (1918), he goes further still, locating the objective content of judgments in what he calls a "third realm," strictly separate from both the physical world and anyone's private mental life, precisely so that logic and arithmetic can never be hostage to psychology, culture, or historical moment.

What I keep getting stuck on is a diary Frege kept in 1924, the last full year of his life, found among his papers and not available to English-language readers until decades after his death. In it he attacks Weimar parliamentary democracy, Catholics, socialists, the French, and above all Jews, including musings that Jews should be deprived of political rights or, failing that, driven out of the country entirely. Michael Dummett, who probably did more than any other twentieth-century philosopher to install Frege as the founding father of analytic philosophy, and who was himself an outspoken campaigner against racism in Britain, has written about how disorienting it was to discover this about a thinker whose work he'd spent decades defending.

The Frege scholars I've spoken with for this piece seem to split into two camps once the diary comes up. One camp treats it as a purely biographical scandal with no bearing on the philosophy: you assess Begriffsschrift and "On Sense and Reference" on the page, the same way you'd assess a theorem regardless of the mathematician's marriage or politics. The other camp thinks that's too quick specifically in Frege's case, because the very temperament on display in the diary — zero tolerance for blurred boundaries, contempt for compromise, an all-or-nothing insistence that things are either strictly objective and fixed or worthless — looks suspiciously like the temperament behind his lifelong refusal to let logic or number be anything less than mind-independent and non-negotiable. Write me an essay that actually adjudicates this rather than splitting the difference. Distinguish the different things "bears on his philosophy" could mean — relevance to whether his arguments are sound, relevance to explaining why he held the views he held, relevance to how we should present or teach him now — and defend a specific, stated position on each, engaging seriously with the strongest form of whichever view you end up rejecting.
