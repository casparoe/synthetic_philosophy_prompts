Write a dialogue between an epistemicist about vagueness, who holds that predicates like "red" have perfectly sharp but unknowable extensions, and a supervaluationist, who holds that a sentence containing a vague predicate is true only if it holds under every admissible way of making that predicate precise. The two are looking together at a strip of 100 numbered colour swatches that shade in minute, continuous steps from a swatch both agree is red (swatch 1) to one both agree is orange (swatch 100), and are trying to settle whether there is some swatch n such that n counts as red while n+1 does not. The epistemicist should press the objection from higher-order vagueness: since "definitely red" is itself a vague predicate, doesn't invoking truth-in-all-admissible-interpretations simply relocate, one level up, the very sharp cut-off that supervaluationism was meant to dissolve? The supervaluationist should press back on whether the epistemicist's margin-for-error account of our ignorance actually explains why one particular swatch is the true cut-off, or whether it merely redescribes the same mystery in epistemic terms. Each philosopher should engage directly with the specific objection just raised rather than restate their opening position, and the dialogue should end with the disagreement still unresolved.
