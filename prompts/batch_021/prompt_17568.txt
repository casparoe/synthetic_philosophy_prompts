At work I spend a lot of time thinking about distributed systems, and one of my favorite pieces of CS folklore has always been the Two Generals Problem: two generals, each camped on a hill with their own army, can defeat the enemy force in the valley between them only if they attack at exactly the same time, but they can communicate only via messengers who might be captured -- and it's a clean impossibility result that no protocol, however many rounds of acknowledgment it uses, can give both generals certainty that the other will actually attack. I mentioned this to a friend doing a PhD in economics, and she told me game theorists have essentially the same setup under a different name -- Ariel Rubinstein's "electronic mail game" -- but that they draw a sharper conclusion from it than we usually draw in CS, one I found genuinely unsettling once I worked through it.

In Rubinstein's version, two players' computers automatically exchange confirmation messages about whether a rare, mutually profitable but risky coordination opportunity has arisen; each message has some small chance of getting lost, so the exchange eventually just stops, leaving both sides with a large but finite number of confirmed exchanges instead of certainty. Once you work out the equilibrium of the resulting game -- or so I gather from the lecture notes and blog posts I've been reading, since none of this is my field -- the unique Nash equilibrium has both players playing it safe forever, regardless of how many hundreds of confirmations successfully went through, even though, if the two sides had literal, infinite-order common knowledge of the opportunity instead, coordinating on it would obviously be the equilibrium they'd land on. So the claim isn't the comparatively modest one that you can never be fully certain; it's that arbitrarily high finite mutual knowledge and true common knowledge are not even approximate substitutes for purposes of rational coordination, no matter how many acknowledgments pile up.

That struck me as a genuinely strong philosophical claim about what common knowledge even is, but reading further I found that some game theorists treat the whole thing as a fragile artifact of Rubinstein's specific setup: switch from his discrete confirmation-counting structure to a "global games" style model, where players instead receive continuous, slightly noisy private signals about the state of the world, and the equilibrium gets pinned down by risk-dominance instead, with players coordinating more and more reliably as their information improves -- no cliff, just a smooth curve. I can't tell whether that's a genuine rebuttal of the lesson people draw from the email game, or a different model that quietly changes the question and then declares the original paradox solved. Could you write me an argument -- an actual argument with a conclusion you're willing to commit to, not a balanced survey -- defending a specific claim: that Rubinstein's result is philosophically load-bearing rather than a knife-edge artifact. Specifically, I'd like you to defend the view that finite mutual knowledge, however high, is categorically insufficient for rational risky coordination, and that the global-games rebuttal succeeds only by changing the informational setup rather than by rescuing the intuitive idea that enough confirmations should work just as well as genuine common knowledge. Please engage honestly with why the global-games response is tempting before explaining why it ultimately fails, rather than dismissing it out of hand.
