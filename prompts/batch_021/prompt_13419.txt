Question: In Part V of the Discourse on Method, Descartes proposes two tests for telling genuine thought from mere mechanism and uses them to argue that non-human animals lack minds. When Henry More later pressed him on the moral cost of this view, Descartes clarified exactly what he was, and was not, prepared to deny animals. State the two tests and explain what each is meant to rule out, then explain the concession Descartes does and does not make to More. Does his considered position entail that animals cannot suffer?

Student answer: Descartes' argument turns on two tests introduced in the Discourse. The first is linguistic: a real thinker can arrange words to respond appropriately to whatever is said in its presence, on any topic, whereas even trained animals such as parrots or magpies can only produce learned sounds without adapting them to new circumstances. The second is behavioural: reason is a "universal instrument" that can cope with any situation, whereas a machine needs a particular disposition of organs for each particular action, so it is not credible that a machine could be furnished with enough different organs to act in every contingency of life the way a person can. Since human brains are considerably more intricate than those of any animal, Descartes takes this anatomical gap to explain why animals fail both tests: a simpler organ can only sustain a narrower repertoire of behaviour, exactly as we observe.

Pressed by More on the cruelty this seems to license, Descartes did not retreat. He is on record as having nailed live dogs to boards in the course of his own investigations, dismissing their cries as nothing more than the noise of an internal spring being struck, with no sensation behind them, much as a clock makes noise without feeling anything. On this basis, his considered position is that thought and sensation both require an immaterial soul that animals simply lack, so his view straightforwardly entails that animals cannot suffer.
