I'm taking a graduate seminar on formal epistemology, and during last week's session on the Sleeping Beauty problem, two classmates had a disagreement the professor didn't have time to referee. For context: Beauty is told the full experimental protocol in advance, then put to sleep on Sunday; a fair coin is tossed; on heads she is woken once, Monday, and the experiment ends; on tails she is woken Monday and Tuesday, with her memory of the Monday waking erased so that Tuesday feels subjectively identical to Monday. Each time she wakes, not knowing the day or the coin's outcome, she is asked for her credence that it landed heads. The two classmates, whom I'll call Ines and Daniel, argued specifically about whether the standard betting argument for "thirdism" (credence 1/3) actually settles anything, and I couldn't tell who was getting the better of it.

(1) Ines opened with the classic argument: if Beauty holds credence 1/2 and evaluates each waking's bet on its own, she'll call an even-money bet on heads fair — stake $1, get $2 back (net +$1) on heads, nothing back on tails. But accepting that bet on every waking loses money on average over many repetitions of the whole procedure, since the loss gets charged twice whenever tails comes up. The rate that actually breaks even in the long run is 2-to-1 against heads (stake $1, get $3 back on heads, nothing on tails) — exactly what a per-waking credence of 1/3 calls fair. So, she concluded, rationality requires 1/3, not 1/2.

(2) Daniel replied that this only embarrasses a careless halfer. Because Beauty can't tell her wakings apart, whatever she decides on one waking she automatically decides on the other, so under tails the consequences of a single decision are cashed out twice. A halfer who takes that seriously — weighting the tails branch twice when tallying expected value, since it's realized twice — finds that credence 1/2 recommends the identical 2-to-1 rate Ines derived. So the betting argument doesn't discriminate between the two credences at all; it only catches a halfer who forgets that tails-decisions come in pairs.

(3) Ines pushed back that Daniel's fix isn't neutral bookkeeping. If he's entitled to say a single decision "counts twice" under tails because its consequences occur twice, she didn't see why he wasn't equally committed to saying that there are twice as many moments of it being true that this is her, right now, wondering about the coin — which is just the thirder's original reason for weighting tails twice as heavily. He wanted the doubling on the decision-theoretic side of the ledger while refusing it on the credal side.

(4) Daniel answered that the two doublings are not the same fact, and one doesn't license the other. How many times a fixed policy gets carried out is settled by the experimental protocol alone, and would hold even if Beauty somehow retained her memories and knew perfectly well what day it was; how confident an amnesiac Beauty should be about which day she's in is a further, separate question about how to individuate her own uncertainty, and nothing about payoffs being multiplied answers that question by itself. He added that if it did, a fixed recording that happens to be replayed twice under one condition and once under another would have to make the recorded voice itself more confident which condition held, which seems wrong, since the number of replays is fixed by something the speaker has no access to and stands in no evidential relation to.

Neither of them budged, and I don't know this literature well enough to referee it myself. Could you go through the exchange move by move — (1) through (4) — and say who has the better of it at each step, rather than just declaring an overall winner? I'd also be interested in whether either of them is relying on a hidden assumption that the other should have challenged but didn't.
