A friend and I spent most of dinner last night arguing about AI risk arguments, and we finally landed on something we both agreed on, which is unusual enough for the two of us that I'd like a third opinion before I trust it. What we agreed was this: once you accept both the orthogonality thesis and the instrumental convergence thesis, the specific content of a superintelligent agent's final goal stops mattering very much for predicting what it will actually do. Orthogonality just tells you the goal could be anything; convergence tells you that whatever it is, the agent will predictably pursue the same handful of instrumental strategies — self-preservation, resource acquisition, resistance to having its goals altered — so the downstream behavior looks basically the same no matter what final goal you plug in.

Given that, I've been trying to figure out why so much of the alignment literature is preoccupied with value specification — the painstaking work of getting an AI's terminal goals to match human values — when it seems like the more tractable, goal-agnostic problem of containment and corrigibility should be doing most of the actual safety work, since it doesn't depend on anyone successfully solving the much harder problem of what values to install in the first place. Is there a good reason the field is weighted the way it is, or is the emphasis on values more a matter of philosophical inheritance from Bostrom and company than something the two theses jointly imply?

I should mention that my friend is a philosophy grad student and I'm coming at this from a machine learning background, so it's entirely possible one of us is smuggling in a premise the other doesn't share, but neither of us could locate exactly where over the course of the argument. If you can see the gap, I'd rather you say so plainly than split the difference between our two positions.
