So I've been down a Frank Ramsey rabbit hole for a piece I'm writing on underrated early-20th-century thinkers, and a philosopher-of-language friend keeps sending me papers that all trace back to one footnote the guy tossed off before he died at 26 in 1930. In "General Propositions and Causality" Ramsey basically says: to figure out how confident you should be in "if p, then q," hypothetically add p to your stock of beliefs and see how confident that leaves you in q. That's the whole "Ramsey test." Decades later Ernest Adams turned it into the formal claim that the probability of an indicative conditional just IS the conditional probability P(q|p), and Robert Stalnaker tried to build a possible-worlds semantics where "if p then q" is a genuine true-or-false proposition that STILL satisfies that same equation. Then in 1976 David Lewis dropped his triviality result, which (roughly) shows you can't have it both ways: no proposition's unconditional probability can track the matching conditional probability across a rich enough space of credence functions, except in totally degenerate cases. My friend says most people who cite the proof these days couldn't actualy reconstruct it, which, guilty as charged until this week.

Anyway, here's my real question: Ramsey obviously never lived to see any of this play out, so what do you think he'd have made of Lewis's result if he had? I'm less interested in a "well he was a pragmatist about probability so he'd probably just shrug" answer than in something that grapples with the actual mechanics of his position. Given his betting-based operationalism about degrees of belief AND his redundancy-theory instincts about truth (from "Facts and Propositions"), do you think he'd have happily joined Adams and later Dorothy Edgington on the "indicative conditionals just aren't truth-apt" side, would he have thought the whole way the problem gets posed is confused in a way that dissolves it rather than answers it (very on-brand for the guy who ran around with Wittgenstein), or is there some third move available given what he actually wrote that the later literature never really tried? Pick a lane and actually defend it, don't just hedge evenly across all three — a few hundred words of real argument is worth way more to me than a survey of options I could've written myself.
