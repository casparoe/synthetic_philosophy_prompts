The question, worth 20 points, read as follows: David Armstrong, Fred Dretske, and Michael Tooley each independently proposed that laws of nature consist in a relation of natural necessitation, N(F,G), holding between universals. (a) Explain the objection, sometimes called the "inference problem," that critics such as van Fraassen have raised against this proposal. [6 points] (b) Would a dispositional essentialist about properties (for instance, Bird) regard this as a problem for their own view? Explain why or why not. [7 points] (c) Lewis's Best System Analysis is often presented as the leading Humean alternative to necessitarian theories of laws. Does it face a problem structurally similar to the inference problem? Defend your answer. [7 points]

Here, verbatim, is the student's answer:

(a) The inference problem targets the DTA (Dretske–Tooley–Armstrong) necessitation theory of laws. On this view, a law that all Fs are G obtains just in case there is a second-order relation of natural necessitation, N(F,G), between the universals F-ness and G-ness, and this second-order fact is supposed to necessitate the first-order regularity that all particular Fs are Gs. The objection is that simply naming this relation "necessitation" and stipulating that N(F,G) entails the regularity does not actually explain why it should: nothing in our antecedent grasp of universals and the relations that can hold between them guarantees that N(F,G) carries this entailment, so Armstrong appears to have posited a primitive connection and then asserted, without further elucidation, that it does the inferential work needed. The worry, in short, is that positing N(F,G) does not solve the problem of what ties laws to their instances so much as relocate it and give it a new name.

(b) A dispositional essentialist would say this problem does not arise for their own view, because the tie between a property and its nomic role is not a further, independent relation that needs its own bridging principle: to have negative charge just is, in part, to be disposed to repel other negatively charged things, so there is no separate fact, over and above the property's being instantiated, that has to be connected to the regularity by brute stipulation. Because the dispositional character is essential to the property rather than externally conferred on it, the laws describing how such properties behave turn out to be logically necessary rather than the contingent matters Armstrong took them to be, which is exactly why dispositional essentialists such as Bird present their view as more economical than his.

(c) Lewis's Best System Analysis avoids this particular problem because it is not a necessitarian theory at all: it identifies the laws with whatever regularities appear as axioms or theorems of the best — simplest and strongest — systematization of the actual particular facts, without positing any further relation between universals that then has to be connected back to the regularities it is meant to underwrite. This comes at a price, though. Since Lewis, like Hume, ultimately identifies a law with nothing more than a certain kind of true regularity among the particular facts, his view runs into the very problem that originally motivated Armstrong to introduce necessitation in the first place: a theory on which laws just are true regularities has no principled way of ruling in the genuine regularities of physics while ruling out accidental ones, such as the well-known case of all gold spheres being less than a mile in diameter. So while Lewis's Humeanism escapes the inference problem in its original form, it does so only by collapsing back into the more basic difficulty that necessitation was introduced to solve.
