I am preparing a graduate seminar on early twentieth-century British ethics, and this week we are working through the closing chapter of G. E. Moore's *Principia Ethica* (1903), "The Ideal." I want to spend real time on the passage in which Moore names the two supreme goods, since it is the textual anchor for everything the Bloomsbury Group later made of him. The passage reads:

"By far the most valuable things, which we know or can imagine, are certain states of consciousness, which may be roughly described as the pleasures of human intercourse and the enjoyment of beautiful objects. No one, probably, who has asked himself the question, has ever doubted that personal affection and the appreciation of what is beautiful in Art or Nature, are good in themselves; nor, if we consider strictly what things are worth having purely for their own sakes, does it appear probable that any one will think that anything else has nearly so great a value as the things which are included under these two heads."

Earlier in the book, two methodological devices do a great deal of work that I think bears directly on this passage: the method of isolation, by which the intrinsic value of anything is to be judged by imagining it existing quite alone, stripped of every accompaniment and effect; and the principle of organic unities, according to which the value of a whole need bear no fixed proportion to the sum of the values of its parts. Later in the same chapter, Moore goes on to insist that neither the mere cognition of a beautiful or admirable object, nor a feeling of admiration or affection unaccompanied by such cognition, amounts to much of anything by itself, so that the consciousness of beauty and the beautiful object each acquire their great value only in the specific combination he has in mind. That claim looks like a straightforward application of organic unities: the whole is worth vastly more than the sum of its parts.

What I would like help thinking through is whether this application sits well with the isolation test Moore relies on elsewhere to establish that a thing has intrinsic value in the first place. If a part's value can genuinely depend on the whole in which it is embedded, in the strong sense the organic unity doctrine requires, it is unclear what isolating a candidate good is supposed to reveal, since isolating something just is removing it from the very context that, on this doctrine, partly constitutes its value. Is Moore simply running two incompatible pictures of value together here, or is there a principled way of reading the isolation test so that it survives contact with organic unities—perhaps by restricting isolation to a narrower, more modest role than the one it is usually credited with? I would welcome a careful reconstruction of how these two pieces of Moore's apparatus are meant to cohere, since the commentaries I have consulted tend to invoke one or the other without ever confronting the tension head-on.
