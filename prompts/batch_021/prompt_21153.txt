I'm a second-year philosophy PhD student currently preparing a qualifying paper on evidential decision theory (EDT), and I've been working through Gibbard and Harper's classic discussion alongside more recent treatments by Arif Ahmed and others. Newcomb's Problem finally clicked for me once I stopped worrying about causal influence and just asked which act constitutes better news: since one-boxing is strongly correlated with the predictor having already filled the opaque box, an evidential agent should one-box, and this is widely treated as at least a defensible, if not outright correct, verdict.

When I got to "Death in Damascus" in the same literature, it struck me as the very same predictor structure in different clothing. Death, like Newcomb's demon, has already formed an accurate prediction of where the agent will go, so whichever city is better news—more strongly correlated, given the agent's evidence, with Death being elsewhere—is simply the city an evidential agent should choose, by exactly the reasoning that's supposed to vindicate one-boxing. So I was puzzled to see Gibbard and Harper deploy this very example to argue that rational choice requires a ratifiable act, a constraint evidential reasoning can apparently fail to satisfy, and to find it still routinely cited as a serious difficulty for EDT rather than as a second illustration of the same one-boxing logic that Newcomb's case is supposed to vindicate.

Since the underlying structure looks identical to me—perfect predictor, evidentially significant act, no causal back-channel from choice to prediction—why do decision theorists treat these two cases so differently? Is there some feature of Death in Damascus that actually breaks the analogy with Newcomb's Problem, or is the standard treatment of it as a special problem for EDT more a matter of theorists being generally uneasy about evidential reasoning, with this particular example just bringing that unease to the surface?
