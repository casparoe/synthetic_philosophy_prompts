Grice's Cooperative Principle, together with the four maxims (Quantity, Quality, Relation, Manner), is supposed to explain how a hearer recovers meaning that goes beyond what a speaker strictly says. Consider the two exchanges below.

Exchange 1
A: "Can you make book club on Friday?"
B: "I've got a work deadline that day."

Exchange 2
A: "Did all the guests enjoy the dinner?"
B: "Some of them did."

(a) [5 pts] For each exchange, state the implicature carried by B's reply and name the maxim primarily responsible for generating it.
(b) [5 pts] For each exchange, classify the implicature as conversational or conventional, and, if conversational, as generalized or particularized. Justify each classification.
(c) [5 pts] For each exchange, apply the cancellability (defeasibility) test to show that what you identified in (a) is a genuine implicature rather than part of the truth-conditional content of B's utterance.
(d) [5 pts] State one respect in which conventional implicature differs, in its defining theoretical properties, from conversational implicature.

And here's the answer I've been assigned to grade, copied exactly as they submitted it:

(a) In Exchange 1, B is implicating that she can't come to book club on Friday. "I've got a work deadline that day" isn't a direct answer to a yes/no question, so it only counts as a relevant contribution if we take it to be a reason she can't come -- that's the maxim of Relation doing the work. In Exchange 2, B is implicating that not all of the guests enjoyed the dinner. If all of them had, B could just as easily have given the more informative answer "yes, all of them did," so by choosing the weaker "some" instead, she implicates that the stronger claim isn't true. This is generated by the first maxim of Quantity -- make your contribution as informative as is required.

(b) Both of these are conversational implicatures rather than conventional ones, since neither is tied to the conventional meaning of a specific word the way "but" conventionally signals a contrast. Exchange 1 is a particularized conversational implicature -- there's nothing about "I've got a work deadline that day" that implicates an inability to attend things in general, so the inference only goes through relative to this specific question. Exchange 2 is a generalized conversational implicature, since "some" implicates "not all" by default, in more or less any context, without needing the kind of special background assumptions Exchange 1 needs. Because it goes through automatically like that, the "not all" inference is really coming from the conventional meaning of "some" itself rather than from reasoning about cooperativeness, which is what makes it the default reading in a way particularized implicatures aren't.

(c) For Exchange 1, B could continue "...but I'll try to swing by after" without contradicting herself, which shows the "she's not coming" implicature is cancellable and therefore not entailed. For Exchange 2, someone could likewise say "some of them did -- in fact, all of them did" without contradiction, which shows "some" doesn't entail "not all." The best explanation for this is that "some" is actually ambiguous between an "at least some, possibly all" reading and a stronger "some but not all" reading, and context just selects which reading is the relevant one here.

(d) Conventional implicature differs from conversational implicature in that it's attached directly to the conventional meaning of a particular expression instead of being worked out from the Cooperative Principle plus context. Grice's own example is "therefore" in "He is an Englishman; he is, therefore, brave" -- the word conventionally implicates that his bravery follows from his being English, without that being part of what's strictly said, i.e. without touching the truth conditions of the sentence. Since it's just attached to the word by convention, conventional implicature, unlike conversational implicature, isn't cancellable.
