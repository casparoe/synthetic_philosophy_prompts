I've been a science reporter for about six years now, and there's an argument I keep having with my editor that neither of us has ever really pinned down. Whenever we cover a big multi-author paper - a physics collaboration result, an early trial for some new drug, whatever - he insists I track down a second expert who wasn't involved in the work to "independently confirm" the claim before we run anything. Sounds reasonable, except I'm not a physicist or a statistician myself, so when that second person tells me the methodology checks out, im still just taking their word for it too. I have no way to personally check their reasoning any better than I could check the original authors'. So the "independent verification" step doesn't actually get us off the hook of trusting somebody's say-so, it just adds another link to the chain, and I'm honestly not sure what that extra link is buying us.

A friend of mine who reports for a rival outlet - we got into this over drinks after a conference panel a few weeks back - thinks I'm overthinking it. Her rule is that a single on-record, credible source is fine to run with unless something about their story doesn't add up, like inconsistent details or an obvious motive to spin things, and that demanding extra corroboration for everything basically treats every source as guilty of lying until proven otherwise. I keep going back and forth on which of us has the better instinct here, and it's been bugging me way more then a routine editorial policy question probably should. Is there an actual difference between our two positions, philosophically speaking, and is one of them more defensible, or are we both missing some better way to think about when someone else's word is enough on its own?
