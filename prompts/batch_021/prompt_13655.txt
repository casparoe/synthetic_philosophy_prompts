Over dinner last week, a friend and I spent nearly two hours arguing about Gottlob Frege and got nowhere. She thinks the antisemitic and anti-parliamentary views Frege recorded in his 1924 diary should bear on how the discipline honors him today; I think his technical achievements in logic and the philosophy of language stand on their own, independent of his private politics. Neither of us gave much ground, and as it happens the question is no longer just academic for me.

I sit on the committee that administers my department's Frege Prize, awarded biennially for the best graduate essay in logic or philosophy of language. A group of graduate students has petitioned to rename it, citing the diary, which even Michael Dummett -- who did more than anyone to establish Frege's centrality to analytic philosophy -- later wrote about with evident discomfort once it came to light. Some committee members are sympathetic but worry about where that reasoning stops, since almost every historical figure worth honoring held some view we would now reject; others point out that, unlike Heidegger's case, no one has shown any real connection between Frege's logicism and his politics, and think that absence matters. We have no standing policy for petitions like this.

Draft the decision memo I will circulate before the committee votes. It should recommend one concrete outcome -- keep the name, rename it, or adopt some intermediate measure -- propose a criterion the committee could reuse for comparable cases, and take the strongest version of the losing argument seriously rather than dismiss it in passing.
