The question, worth 20 of the exam's 100 points, reads as follows: "Galen Strawson has argued that no one is ever truly (or 'ultimately') morally responsible for what they do. (a) Reconstruct his Basic Argument step by step, making clear where the regress comes from. (b) Strawson insists that the argument's force does not depend on whether determinism is true. Explain why he thinks this, and evaluate the following objection: 'If indeterminism is true, then at least some of what an agent does is not fixed by antecedent causes; so at least sometimes the agent, rather than fate, is the source of the action, and we get genuine responsibility after all.' (c) Strawson connects his conclusion to a remark about the impossibility of being causa sui. Whose remark is this, and what work is it doing in his argument?"

Here is the student's answer in full: "Strawson's Basic Argument is meant to show that no one is ever truly, or 'ultimately,' morally responsible for what they do, where 'true' responsibility is the strong, desert-implying kind that would make sense of someone genuinely deserving punishment or reward in the way ideas of heaven and hell presuppose. The argument can be reconstructed as follows. First, how we act flows from how we are: from our beliefs, desires, and character. Second, if we are to be truly responsible for an action, we must be truly responsible for being the way we are, at least in the mental respects from which the action issued. Third, we cannot be truly responsible for being the way we are, because that would require having intentionally made ourselves that way; but any such act of self-formation would itself have had to flow from some prior nature, and we are only responsible for that prior nature if we made ourselves into it too, and so on without end. Since the regress never reaches a starting point for which we are responsible, we are never truly responsible for our nature, and so never truly responsible for our actions. In effect, true responsibility would require being causa sui, cause of oneself, and Strawson borrows this idea from Sartre's remark that we are 'condemned to be free' and must in effect create ourselves out of nothing — a feat Strawson thinks is strictly impossible, so the demand it sets is one nothing could ever satisfy.

Strawson is explicit that this argument does not depend on determinism: the regress in step three arises just from asking what it would take to be responsible for one's own nature, and that question can be asked whether or not the universe is deterministic. This is what makes the Basic Argument more interesting than a simple hard-determinist argument that just points to the causal history of our actions. That said, I think the indeterminist objection in the prompt is actually the strongest response available: if some part of what I do is genuinely undetermined, rather than an automatic playing-out of a character I never chose, then there is a point at which the outcome is not merely inherited from antecedents outside my control, and it seems fair to hold me responsible for what I do at exactly that point, even if I cannot take credit for the character I started with. So while Strawson is right that determinism per se is not doing the crucial work in his argument, I don't think he's right that indeterminism fails to help; a modest, local kind of responsibility for undetermined choices survives even if 'ultimate' responsibility for one's whole character does not."
