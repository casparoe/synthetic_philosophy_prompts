I've been sitting in on a graduate seminar in social epistemology this term, and the session on conspiracy theories ran well past its scheduled end because several of us got stuck on something more basic than any of the specific cases on the syllabus. The disagreement splits, roughly, into two camps. Generalists hold that being a conspiracy theory is itself epistemically loaded: such theories characteristically explain away disconfirming evidence as further proof of the cover-up (their self-sealing quality, as it's sometimes put), they require positing coordinated secrecy among an implausibly large and ever-growing cast of people, and these structural features license a standing presumption of suspicion against the whole category, prior to and independent of any particular theory's evidence. Particularists respond that this is guilt by genre: history contains plenty of confirmed conspiracies — Watergate, COINTELPRO, the tobacco industry's long cover-up of its own internal research, MKULTRA — that exhibited exactly the same suspicious structural features right up until the moment the documents surfaced, so no informative prior can be read off from category membership alone; every claim still has to be weighed on its own merits.

Could you write me a dialogue between a generalist and a particularist about conspiracy theories — both professional epistemologists, equally sharp, and don't let either one become a strawman for the other — who are asked, after giving back-to-back talks at a conference, to spend the coffee break hashing out whether they actually disagree about anything beyond terminology? Rather than let them stay at the level of abstract principle, I'd like them to keep returning to one concrete case: MKULTRA. Someone who believed, around 1970, that the CIA was running unwitting mind-control experiments on American and Canadian civilians held a belief that, by the generalist's own diagnostic criteria, should have looked irrational — self-sealing, reliant on an implausible scale of coordinated secrecy, contradicted by flat official denial — and yet it was substantially true, as the agency's own surviving records would go on to confirm a few years later. I want each side to genuinely work through what that case does to their position — the generalist should have more to say than a shrug, and the particularist shouldn't just declare victory and change the subject — and if they can't fully resolve the disagreement by the end, that's a perfectly honest place to leave the dialogue.
