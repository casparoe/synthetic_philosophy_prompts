The question he was set reads: "Drawing on David Lewis's 'The Paradoxes of Time Travel,' explain (a) the distinction between personal time and external time and why Lewis needs it; (b) how Lewis's analysis of two senses of 'can' is meant to dissolve the apparent contradiction in the grandfather paradox; and (c) how this compares to Paul Horwich's discussion of the same scenario in Asymmetries in Time, specifically on whether Tim's repeated failures require any special explanation."

Here is what he handed me: "Lewis points out that a time traveler effectively has two clocks running on him at once. Personal time is the sequence of Tim's experiences from breakfast to boarding the machine to stepping out the other end, and it always runs forward for him exactly the way our lives normally do. External time is the ordinary calendar time of the world, and what makes this time travel rather than just a very long walk is that external time can run backward relative to personal time: Tim's personal clock ticks forward through the whole trip even while the external calendar runs back decades. Lewis needs the distinction so he can say the journey itself is perfectly consistent, since nothing before Tim's departure has to un-happen for him to arrive earlier. The grandfather paradox is harder, and Lewis's fix is to say that 'can' is relative to which facts about the situation we hold fixed. Held fixed only the facts about Tim's skill with a gun and Grandfather's being defenseless, Tim can kill him, in the ordinary sense that anyone fully equipped to do something can do it. Held fixed the wider facts of the one actual history, including the fact that Grandfather lived to father Tim's parent, Tim cannot kill him, since that would be a contradiction. Whenever Tim actually pulls the trigger, some accident always gets in the way, and he ends up on the branch of history where Grandfather survives, as though each failed attempt quietly shunted him onto the one timeline consistent with his own existence. There's no real conflict once you see that both 'can' claims are true relative to different held-fixed facts, so the paradox doesn't show that time travel is logically impossible, only that this particular attempt is guaranteed to fail. This is basically the same point Horwich makes in his own discussion of the case: Horwich agrees that the near-misses that stop Tim are perfectly ordinary bad luck of the sort that turns up all the time and doesn't call for any special explanation, so on this point the two of them are in complete agreement."
