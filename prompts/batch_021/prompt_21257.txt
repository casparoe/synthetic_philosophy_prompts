I'm a retired physicist, and since I stopped working I've spent the last year or two reading way more philosophy than physics -- and I've gotten completely stuck on this argument about Kelly betting that I cant let go of. Here's the setup as I understand it: when your wealth compounds multiplicatively over repeated bets, the "ergodicity economics" crowd (Ole Peters and that lot) say the objectively correct thing to maximize isn't expected value at all but the long-run time-average growth rate -- which just amounts to full Kelly betting -- and that this doesn't even require positing a utility function, it's forced on you by the dynamics themselves. But Samuelson spent decades insisting this is a straightforward fallacy -- he was apparently so annoyed by the whole thing that he once wrote an entire paper trashing the "maximize expected log wealth" school using nothing but one-syllable words -- and his point was that "you'll almost surely end up ahead eventually" just isn't the same claim as "maximizes your expected utility," unless your utility happens to be exactly logarithmic.

Could you write me a dialogue between a Kelly-betting/geometric-rationality advocate and an old-school expected-utility theorist really hashing this out? I don't want them talking past each other -- the Kelly side has to answer why someone whose utility isn't log should care about growth rate at all, and the expected-utility side has to answer what gives them the right to just posit a utility function rather than take the dynamics as they come. Make them equally sharp, nobody's strawmanned. If you can plausibly set the whole thing at a card table somewhere, even better.
