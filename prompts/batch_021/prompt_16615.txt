So I'm doing a bioethics PhD, and in my proseminar we've been working through the Boorse/Daniels "normal function" literature (the biostatistical theory of health, Just Health, the From Chance to Choice stuff) alongside real gene-editing cases, and it kicked off a whole argument with my cohort about the He Jiankui case — the 2018 CRISPR twins, where he knocked out CCR5 in the embryos to try to make them HIV-resistant. I ended up arguing that what actually makes that case wrong, over and above the obviously botched consent process and lack of oversight, is that it crosses the therapy/enhancement line: the embryos didn't have HIV and there wasn't any actual disease being corrected, so knocking out CCR5 pushes them beyond normal species-typical immune function into enhancement, which is exactly the kind of intervention Daniels' model says isn't owed to anyone as a matter of health-related justice (unlike restoring someone to normal function, which is). To back this up I gave my cohort a hypothetical: if He had used the literal same technique on an embryo that actually carried a disease allele, like the sickle-cell mutation, just to correct it back to the normal allele, that's textbook therapy, and so it would've been basically fine, modulo the consent stuff, which I think is a totally seperate problem from the enhancement question.

My friend Priya (also in the program) says I'm making the therapy/enhancement line do way more work than it can bear, but every time I ask her to spell out what's actually wrong with my reasoning she just says "its more complicated" and changes the subject, which drives me nuts. Is she onto something real, or is she just being cagey? Like, assuming we could even get precise about what counts as 'species-typical' function for something like immune resistance, is 'restores vs exceeds' really doing the moral work I'm assigning it in a case like this — and if not, what exactly is it missing?
