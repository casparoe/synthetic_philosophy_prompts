QUESTION: (a) State the Gibbard-Satterthwaite theorem precisely: what has to hold of the domain and of the social choice function for the conclusion to follow, and what exactly does the conclusion say? (b) Briefly explain how the theorem relates to Arrow's Impossibility Theorem. (c) A classmate claims: "Instant-runoff voting isn't a dictatorship, and every candidate can win under some ballot profile, so by Gibbard-Satterthwaite it has to be manipulable by some voter in some election." Is the classmate right? Defend your answer, and if you think IRV is manipulable, give one concrete profile (sincere strict rankings over three candidates is enough) where some voter or bloc gets a strictly better outcome by voting insincerely than by voting sincerely.

STUDENT ANSWER:

(a) The theorem says that once there are three or more possible outcomes and voters can hold any strict ranking over them (unrestricted domain), any social choice function that is Pareto efficient and non-dictatorial must be manipulable: there's some profile of true preferences and some voter who does strictly better, by their own true preferences, submitting an insincere ranking than by voting sincerely. Two alternatives are the special case that escapes this -- majority rule with two alternatives is non-dictatorial and still strategy-proof, which is why the "three or more" part is doing real work.

(b) This connects directly to Arrow's Impossibility Theorem, which says no social welfare function aggregating individual rankings into a social ranking can satisfy unrestricted domain, the Pareto principle, independence of irrelevant alternatives, and non-dictatorship all at once, once there are three or more alternatives. The standard way to get Gibbard-Satterthwaite is actually as a corollary of Arrow: you show a strategy-proof, onto, non-dictatorial social choice function would give you a social welfare function satisfying all of Arrow's conditions, which Arrow's theorem says can't exist, so the social choice function can't exist either. So really they're the same underlying impossibility, just dressed up for social choice functions instead of social welfare functions.

(c) The classmate is right. IRV isn't a dictatorship and every candidate can win under some profile, so it satisfies the theorem's conditions, and Gibbard-Satterthwaite tells us there's a profile where someone benefits from misreporting.

Here's a concrete case. Three candidates, A, B, C, 100 voters:
35: A > B > C
33: B > C > A
32: C > B > A

Nobody has a majority (35/33/32), so C is eliminated first for having the fewest first place votes, and its 32 ballots move to their second choice, B. Final round: A 35, B 65 -- B wins.

Now suppose the 32 C > B > A voters, realizing C can't win, insincerely rank B first: B > C > A. B then gets 33 + 32 = 65 votes outright and wins in round one -- same outcome as before, so that move doesn't actually change anything. The cleaner case is the 35-voter A > B > C bloc: seeing A has no path to victory, they insincerely rank B first instead of A, voting B > A > C. That pushes B to 33 + 35 = 68 votes in round one, an outright majority, so B wins immediately. This shows the bloc can force their preferred outcome by not "wasting" their vote on A -- exactly the kind of insincere, outcome-changing vote the theorem predicts must exist somewhere.

So the classmate's claim holds up, both in the abstract and with an actual example.
