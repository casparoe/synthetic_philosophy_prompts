I spent part of last spring working on a piece about how far "top-down" effects really reach into perception, which took me to a small, invitation-only workshop on the philosophy and psychophysics of perception — the kind of gathering where a philosopher of mind and a vision-science postdoc end up sharing a table at the pub afterward. The argument I ended up scribbling notes on all evening wasn't from the panel itself but from that pub table, between two of the more talkative attendees: Ingrid, who works on the philosophy of perception, and Devraj, who runs a psychophysics lab. Both had just sat through a talk revisiting the well-known finding, associated with Emily Balcetis and David Dunning, that people who want or need something — a bottle of water when they're thirsty, say, or a gift card worth more money — will judge that object to be physically closer to them than an equally distant but undesired object. The speaker had presented this as one of the stronger pieces of evidence that perception itself, and not merely judgment or memory, can be "cognitively penetrated" by our desires.

Their disagreement went roughly like this. (1) Ingrid said the finding is exactly what should unsettle the classical modularity picture inherited from Fodor and Pylyshyn, on which early and mid-level vision is walled off from belief and desire: a paradigmatically "high-level" state, wanting a drink, is changing a paradigmatically "low-level" perceptual variable, judged distance, and that just is cognitive penetration. (2) Devraj replied that the inference moves too fast, invoking the methodological critique associated with Chaz Firestone and Brian Scholl: the "distance" being measured in these studies is always a verbal report or a motor estimate collected well after the initial glance, so the effect could easily be sitting in the response stage — participants half-guessing what's expected of them, or adjusting a judgment for reasons that have nothing to do with what they actually saw — rather than in the percept itself, and when similar paradigms are tightened to remove that kind of contamination, the effect tends to shrink or vanish, exactly as you'd expect if it had never been perceptual at all. (3) Ingrid countered that taken seriously, this objection is unfalsifiable: since we only ever have access to perceptual content through some report or action, a critic can always say "that might just be judgment," whatever test is run, so demanding immunity to every conceivable confound before a case counts is a standard that would make cognitive penetration undetectable even if it were real. (4) Devraj answered that the criticism isn't unfalsifiable in principle: Firestone and Scholl themselves specify what better evidence would look like — converging measures that don't share the same demand characteristics, or effects that survive when subjects have no idea what's being tested — and the fact that effect sizes specifically shrink under exactly those tighter controls is itself evidence for a response-level story, not merely an absence of proof for a perceptual one.
