Having spent my career in physics, I've been filling my retirement with philosophy of science and its history, and I recently reread John von Neumann's 1955 essay "Can We Survive Technology?" What struck me most this time was not his now-familiar point about nuclear weapons, but his broader diagnosis: that technologies of speed and communication had effectively exhausted the earth's size as a natural buffer against catastrophe, leaving humanity in a permanent condition of crisis management, with no final fix available, only a sequence of improvised, temporary stabilizations. He was pointedly unimpressed by two commonly proposed remedies for this state of affairs — outright prohibition of dangerous technologies, and reliance on human wisdom or self-restraint — arguing instead for new administrative and organizational arrangements. Interestingly, the same essay anticipates the coming possibility of deliberate climate and weather control, and suggests, if I recall correctly, that such a capacity would itself become a source of friction between nations. Given this diagnosis, and his clear preference among remedies, how do you think von Neumann would have assessed today's debates over solar geoengineering governance — for instance, calls for an international moratorium on stratospheric aerosol injection research? Would he most likely have welcomed such a moratorium as a sensible stopgap, or dismissed it as exactly the sort of well-meant but toothless restraint he thought inadequate to the deeper problem?
