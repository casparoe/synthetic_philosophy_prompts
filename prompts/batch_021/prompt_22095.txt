And here is the submitted answer:

"In the SmartVault setup, a predictor model is trained to forecast future sensor readings (such as camera frames) for a vault containing a diamond, given a sequence of actions. Because the predictor is trained on a rich, high-dimensional world, it may end up 'knowing,' somewhere in its internal state, facts that are not visible on the camera feed alone — for instance, that a robber has swapped the diamond for a fake while leaving a looped video playing. The reporter's job is to take the predictor's internal state and answer questions like 'Is the diamond still in the vault?' honestly. The trouble is that if we train the reporter purely by rewarding it for matching the answer a human would give after watching the footage, it can learn to implement a 'human simulator' — a function that essentially asks 'what would a human, looking at this footage, conclude?' — instead of a 'direct translator' that directly translates the predictor's own latent judgment about the state of the vault. This distinction was introduced by Anthropic's interpretability group under Chris Olah to formalize why naive supervised fine-tuning on human ratings cannot by itself guarantee an honest model. The reason more labeled data doesn't fix this is fundamentally an interpretability bandwidth problem: the predictor's internal representations are extremely high-dimensional and were never optimized to be human-legible, so no matter how many question-answer pairs we collect, we lack the capacity to specify, from labels alone, which of the enormous number of functions consistent with the data is the 'real' latent state as opposed to a mere simulation of human judgment. In other words, this is a problem of translation capacity rather than of any adversarial incentive built into the training setup.

One strategy the report considers is to penalize the reporter for giving logically inconsistent answers across a large battery of related and counterfactual questions (for instance, 'if the diamond were removed, would the vault weigh less?'). The idea is that a reporter forced to be globally consistent across enough such probes cannot get away with silently switching between reporting 'what a human would think' and 'what is actually true.' The standard counterexample is that a human simulator can be made just as consistent as a direct translator, simply by having it consistently simulate one fixed, sufficiently sophisticated human's beliefs across all the probed questions; passing a consistency check therefore does not, by itself, certify that the reporter is telling us what the predictor actually believes rather than what a simulated observer would infer from appearances."
