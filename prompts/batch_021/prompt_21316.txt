I've been part of a small rationalist-adjacent reading group for a few years, the kind of crowd that had absorbed the folk version of Aumann's agreement theorem from LessWrong and Overcoming Bias posts long before anyone actually sat down with the two-page 1976 paper itself. We finally did that last month, and it set off a genuinely heated argument between two of the regulars -- I'll call them Julian and Renata -- that ran through most of the drinks after the meeting and that I haven't been able to referee to my own satisfaction. I've reconstructed the exchange below as faithfully as I can, and I'd like a more careful, move-by-move verdict than I can give myself.

1. Julian opened by claiming that the theorem is a formal vindication of the idea that "agreeing to disagree" is always a symptom of irrationality: if two Bayesians' posterior probabilities for some event are common knowledge between them, those posteriors must be identical, so two people who have genuinely talked a question through, and each still knows exactly where the other stands, cannot both be rational if they still disagree.
2. Renata objected that this smuggles in the common prior assumption -- the requirement that the two agents start from the very same prior over the relevant state space before conditioning on their private information -- and that nothing in Bayesian rationality as such forces two agents to share a prior. Two impeccable Bayesians with different priors can satisfy every axiom of rational updating and still disagree indefinitely, common knowledge of posteriors notwithstanding.
3. Julian replied that the common-prior objection is weaker than it looks, because (he claimed, and I later found this really does trace back to an actual exchange Aumann had with a critic) any apparent difference in priors can be redescribed as a difference in information: if the state space is enriched to include everything causally upstream of a person's beliefs -- upbringing, genetics, idiosyncratic life history -- then two people who seem to start from different priors are really just two people who haven't yet pooled all their private information about a shared, more fundamental starting point. On this view, the common prior assumption costs you almost nothing extra.
4. Renata answered that this move doesn't dissolve the assumption, it just relocates it: to make the trick work, you still have to assume that two agents given that same enriched information would converge on one and the same prior over it, which is exactly the disputed claim, restated one level up. She added that a consistent Bayesian subjectivist treats coherence -- avoiding Dutch books, updating strictly by conditionalization -- as the only rational constraint there is, and nothing in that picture rules out two people whose priors would still differ even given identical total information.
5. Julian countered that if priors can differ without any rational basis for preferring one over another, then "rational" stops ruling anything out -- any belief becomes rational as long as it's updated correctly -- and that this is too high a price to pay just to preserve the possibility of blameless disagreement.
