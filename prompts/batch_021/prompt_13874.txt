After thirty years as a health physicist studying radiation exposure, I retired three years ago and have since taken up moral philosophy, currently working through Elizabeth Anscombe. I sit on my university's Honorary Degrees Committee, and a nomination before us uncomfortably echoes the case behind her 1956 pamphlet opposing Oxford's honorary degree for Truman. Our nominee, a retired general, is credited with ending the Torvalen civil war by destroying the capital's only water-treatment plant; a leaked planning memo shows his staff had projected that the resulting cholera outbreak would break the government's will within weeks -- which it did, killing some thirty thousand civilians. He maintains the plant was a legitimate military target and the epidemic a foreseen, unintended side effect. Half the committee calls this textbook double effect; the other half thinks the memo settles what he actually intended, and invokes Anscombe against him. Please draft the short memo I will circulate before the vote: recommend whether to proceed with, suspend, or withdraw the nomination, give the "legitimate target" defense its strongest form, and engage Anscombe's own criteria for distinguishing intention from foresight rather than resting on the label "double effect" itself.
