Outside of my day job as a backend engineer, I've maintained a moderately popular open-source project -- a data-serialization library with a small but active contributor base -- for about six years. Lately, the five of us who act as maintainers have spent two governance calls stuck on a proposed change to our code of conduct that I still haven't been able to think my way through. At the moment, the code of conduct only governs behavior in project spaces: our issue tracker, our chat server, and the one conference meetup we run each year. The proposal on the table would let us remove a maintainer or a frequent contributor for conduct entirely outside those spaces -- in their personal life, on their own social media, at unrelated events -- provided a clear supermajority of our regular contributors would find that conduct seriously objectionable, even when it never touches the project, a user, or a fellow contributor in any concrete way.

The maintainer proposing the change argues that a volunteer project like ours runs entirely on mutual trust and goodwill rather than employment contracts, so tolerating conduct that most contributors find morally repugnant will, in her view, quietly corrode that trust and eventually drive people away, even though no single incident would ever show up as a reportable harm. Another maintainer thinks this gets things backwards: he argues our authority should extend only as far as protecting the project and the people in it from concrete harm, and that once we start sanctioning people for conduct or beliefs a majority merely finds distasteful, we've handed every future majority a license to purge whoever it currently disapproves of, with talk of "community trust" doing the work that used to require an actual victim.

I don't have any background in ethics or political theory -- I've read a few other projects' write-ups of similar disputes, but they mostly just assert a conclusion rather than argue for one, which hasn't been much use. Before we put this to a vote, I'd like help working out whether one of these two positions has a genuine philosophical advantage over the other, or whether it's really just a values trade-off with no fact of the matter, and whether the strongest version of each side has an adequate answer to the other side's central objection.
