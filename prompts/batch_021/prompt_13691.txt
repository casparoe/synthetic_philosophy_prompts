In his 1926 essay "Criteria of Negro Art," originally delivered as a conference address before appearing in The Crisis, W. E. B. Du Bois insisted that art made by and about Black Americans could never simply be art for art's sake: beauty had to be harnessed to truth and to the struggle for justice, so that, in his own words, "all Art is propaganda and ever must be." Yet Du Bois was also famously uneasy with younger Harlem Renaissance writers who claimed the freedom to depict the grittier side of Black urban life exactly as they saw it, since he worried that unflattering truths, however honestly rendered, would simply be absorbed back into the racist stereotypes he was trying to dismantle. His standard, in other words, was never just "show the truth," but "show the truth in a way that serves the race."

I'm writing a term paper for a seminar on the Harlem Renaissance, and my professor has asked us to apply Du Bois's own framework, rather than a generic notion of "positive representation," to a phenomenon he did not live to see: the Blaxploitation cycle of the early-to-mid 1970s (Sweet Sweetback's Baadasssss Song, Shaft, Super Fly, and their many successors). These films gave Black actors their first sustained run of unapologetic, defiant leading roles in American commercial cinema and were adored by Black urban audiences, even as many of the cycle's biggest hits were financed and distributed by white-owned Hollywood studios eager to exploit a newly discovered market, and even as the NAACP and allied civil rights organizations denounced the genre for glorifying pimps, drug dealers, and vigilante violence.

Working through those criteria directly, rather than jumping straight to a verdict on representation, how do you think Du Bois would have judged the Blaxploitation films as propaganda, in his own honorific sense of that word? I'm especially interested in which of his commitments would have pulled him toward something like endorsement, which would have pulled him toward condemnation, and which pull you think would ultimately have prevailed for him, and why.
