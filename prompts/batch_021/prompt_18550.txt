So a couple weeks ago my friend Dana and I got into a whole thing after our colonial lit seminar covered Sor Juana Inés de la Cruz's "Respuesta a Sor Filotea de la Cruz" (1691), and we never actually resolved it, so I want a third opinion. Dana's take is that the Respuesta is basically a straightforward proto-feminist manifesto — Sor Juana arguing, in general terms, that women deserve real access to learning. My take is that once you actually follow the specific arguments instead of just the famous inspirational bits (the cutting-her-hair story, "I study only in order to know less," etc.), the case she's building is way narrower and more tactical than "women deserve education," and it doesnt obviously generalize the way Dana wants it to.

The passage we kept circling back to comes a bit later in the letter. Right before it, she brings up a theologian named Arce, who weighs Paul's line that "mulieres in ecclesiis taceant" (women should keep silent in the churches) against Paul's other instruction, in his letter to Titus, that older women should be "bene docentes" (teaching well) — and Arce concludes that women may study, write, and teach privately, so long as they're unusually virtuous, mature, and learned. She then quotes a letter of St. Jerome's to a Roman woman named Leta, telling Leta to personally tutor her own small daughter in scripture. Right after that, Sor Juana writes (this is public domain, so here's the original, with my own rough translation underneath — apologies for any rough edges):

"¡Oh cuántos daños se excusaran en nuestra república si las ancianas fueran doctas como Leta, y que supieran enseñar como manda San Pablo y mi Padre San Jerónimo! Y no que por defecto de esto y la suma flojedad en que han dado en dejar a las pobres mujeres, si algunos padres desean doctrinar más de lo ordinario a sus hijas, les fuerza la necesidad y falta de ancianas sabias, a llevar maestros hombres a enseñar a leer, escribir y contar, a tocar y otras habilidades, de que no pocos daños resultan, como se experimentan cada día en lastimosos ejemplos de desiguales consorcios, porque con la inmediación del trato y la comunicación del tiempo, suele hacerse fácil lo que no se pensó ser posible. Por lo cual, muchos quieren más dejar bárbaras e incultas a sus hijas que no exponerlas a tan notorio peligro como la familiaridad con los hombres, lo cual se excusara si hubiera ancianas doctas, como quiere San Pablo, y de unas en otras fuese sucediendo el magisterio como sucede en el de hacer labores y lo demás que es costumbre."

("Oh, how many harms would be avoided in our society if old women were as learned as Leta, and knew how to teach as Saint Paul and my father Saint Jerome command! Instead, for want of this, and through the sheer laxity into which poor women have been allowed to fall, when parents want to give their daughters more than the ordinary schooling, necessity and the lack of learned old women forces them to bring in male tutors to teach reading, writing, arithmetic, music, and other skills — and no small harm results from that, as we see every day in unhappy cases of ill-matched liaisons, since through closeness of contact and the passage of time, what once seemed impossible often becomes easy. That's why many parents would rather leave their daughters uncivilized and unlettered than expose them to so notorious a danger as familiarity with men — a danger that would be avoided if there were learned old women, as Saint Paul wishes it, so that the task of teaching could pass down from one to another, the way it does with needlework and other customary skills.")

Can you help me actually pull apart the logic here and maybe adjudicate between me and Dana? Specifically: is "women deserve education" the real thesis, with all the chaperone/decorum stuff just rhetorical packaging to make it swallowable for a bishop, or is protecting girls' virtue from male tutors actually the point, with education being merely instrumental to that? Is there something knowingly ironic in how she credits this whole arrangement to Paul twice ("como manda San Pablo," "como quiere San Pablo"), given that Paul is exactly who her critics keep quoting against her? And given that even her "solution" only licenses teaching for women who are unusually virtuous and erudite, not women generally, doesn't that suggest she's really defending an exception for extraordinary women like herself rather than making any general claim about women's education? I'd like an actual close reading, not the standard "Sor Juana, early feminist icon" gloss.
