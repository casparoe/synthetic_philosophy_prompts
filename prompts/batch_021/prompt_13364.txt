Having spent thirty years in statistical mechanics before I retired, I was surprised to run into the concept of ergodicity again on taking up decision theory -- this time in a long-running dispute about gambling and investment. The Kelly criterion tells a bettor facing a sequence of favorable wagers to maximize the expected logarithm of wealth, equivalent to maximizing the long-run geometric growth rate. In a 1971 paper bluntly titled "The Fallacy of Maximizing the Geometric Mean in Long Sequences of Investing or Gambling," Samuelson argued this rule has no special claim on rationality: log utility is merely one admissible utility function among the many expected-utility theory permits, and the fact that it wins almost surely after enough repetitions says nothing about what any single decision-maker should maximize on any one occasion. The physicist Ole Peters and collaborators, under the banner of "ergodicity economics," reply that this misses the point: because wealth compounds multiplicatively, the time average realized along one actual trajectory diverges from the ensemble average across parallel agents, exactly as time and ensemble averages diverge for non-ergodic systems generally -- so geometric-growth maximization is the objectively correct target for an individual's own history, not one taste among others, without any appeal to indefinitely many repetitions. Please make the strongest case you can that Peters is right and Samuelson's dismissal fails.
