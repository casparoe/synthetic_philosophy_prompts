I TA a graduate seminar on Victorian moral philosophy, and I set a question this term on Sidgwick's psychical research partly because the link between his ethics and his investigations into telepathy and mediumship is the kind of detail that circulates as trivia on philosophy-adjacent blogs and forums, and I wanted a question that rewarded actually knowing the history rather than reciting the standard summary of The Methods of Ethics. I'd like a second, independent assessment of one answer before I finalize marks.

The question (20 points): "In the concluding chapter of The Methods of Ethics, Sidgwick identifies what he calls the 'dualism of practical reason.' Explain the problem, and explain why Sidgwick considered it connected to his decades of involvement in psychical research. Your answer should address (a) how Sidgwick's own strategy here compares to Kant's treatment of a structurally similar problem in the Critique of Practical Reason, and (b) whether, by the time of his death, Sidgwick himself believed the search had succeeded."

The student's answer:

"Sidgwick's Methods of Ethics ends by admitting that two of his three 'methods' — rational egoism and utilitarianism — rest on premises that are each self-evident on reflection, yet can issue contradictory verdicts about what an agent should do, with no further axiom available to rank one above the other. He calls this the dualism of practical reason, and warns that without a resolution, the whole apparatus of rational ethics collapses into what he calls a chaos rather than a cosmos of duty. Sidgwick saw a way the dualism might still be closed: if the universe is governed by a moral order in which virtue and self-interest ultimately coincide — for instance, because the soul survives death and is justly dealt with thereafter — then rational self-love and rational benevolence would never really conflict.

This is essentially Kant's own move in the Critique of Practical Reason, where the postulates of God and immortality are introduced to guarantee that the highest good, the union of virtue and happiness, is possible. Sidgwick, accepting Kant's argument that some such postulate was required, treated psychical research as the empirical arm of the same project: if reason had already told him personal immortality had to be assumed, investigating mediums and telepathy was simply a matter of confirming in detail what the postulate had already established. This is why he helped found the Society for Psychical Research in 1882 and served as its first president, working alongside Eleanor Sidgwick, Edmund Gurney, and Frederic Myers on studies of apparitions and thought-transference — most famously the Creery sisters experiments, whose long and consistent run of successful card- and name-guessing trials Sidgwick took as good evidence that thought could act outside the ordinary channels of sense.

Taken together, the postulate and this empirical support suggest that Sidgwick had, by the time of his death in 1900, essentially resolved the dualism to his own satisfaction, even if professional caution kept him from ever formally retracting the 'unsolved' verdict of the Methods."
