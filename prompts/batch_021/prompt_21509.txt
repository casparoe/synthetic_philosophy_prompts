In a philosophy of action reading group, two people got into a running disagreement over a classic Frankfurt-style case, and I'd like a neutral read on who's actually right. The setup: the neurologist Black wants Jones to vote for a certain measure, and has planted a device that monitors Jones's brain, ready to force the vote through if Jones ever shows signs of deciding otherwise. As it happens, Jones decides on his own, for his own reasons, to vote for it, so the device never triggers.

(1) Naomi said Jones is clearly responsible for his vote, since he cast it entirely on his own — but that he couldn't have done otherwise, since any move in the other direction would have been overridden. So the case refutes the Principle of Alternative Possibilities (PAP): responsibility doesn't actually require the ability to do otherwise.

(2) Felix objected that Jones does have an alternative, just a thin one: he could at least have begun to show signs of leaning the other way, even though the device would have snuffed that out before it became an actual contrary vote. That flicker is still an alternative possibility — just relocated — and it's enough to save the principle.

(3) Naomi replied that nobody's responsibility hangs on some sub-personal blip they're not even aware of and can't control. If a flicker like that is all PAP needs, the principle is saved in name only, since some such flicker is presumably available in just about any deterministic case you'd care to describe.

(4) Felix came back with a harder problem: for the device to know when to intervene, some sign in Jones's brain has to reliably predict, in advance, which way he's leaning. If that sign guarantees the outcome, the case has quietly assumed determinism from the outset — hardly a fair test of PAP for anyone who thinks free will needs indeterminism. And if the sign doesn't guarantee the outcome, Jones could show it and still decide otherwise at the last moment, so the device might fail to catch him in time — meaning he really could have done otherwise.

Please go through the exchange move by move and say who has the better of it at each of these four points, not just who comes out ahead overall, and flag anything relevant that neither of them raises. Then give your own verdict: does a case built like this actually show that moral responsibility doesn't require alternative possibilities, or does Felix's last point (or something like it) block that conclusion unless Naomi has more to say?
