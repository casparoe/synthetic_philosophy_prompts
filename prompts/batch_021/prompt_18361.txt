So I've been working on a longer piece about AI alignment and keep running into Eliezer Yudkowsky's old "coherent extrapolated volition" idea — basically the notion that instead of programming a superintelligent AI with our current, messy preferences, you'd want it to figure out what we'd want if we knew more, thought faster, and were more the people we wish we were, and then have it act on that instead. A philosopher friend told me over drinks a few weeks back that this is basically just repackaged "idealized preference" / "ideal advisor" theory of the good, the kind of thing Peter Railton was doing back in the 80s, and that Yudkowsky didnt really add anything philosophically new, just AI flavoring on an old dish. I wasnt totally convinced but I don't know the Railton-adjacent literature (Sidgwick, Brandt, Harsanyi's "true preferences" stuff, etc.) well enough to push back properly, and it's been bugging me since.

Could you actually compare these two ideas for me properly? Specifically I'm curious about two things: (1) is CEV doing anything philosophically distinct from earlier idealized-preference/full-information accounts of a person's good, or is my friend basically right that its only real innovation is the delivery mechanism (an AI actually implementing it) rather than the underlying theory of value, and (2) the part of CEV that has to somehow combine millions of different people's extrapolated volitions into one coherent output — is that a genuinely new problem, or just the old interpersonal-aggregation/social-choice problem turning up again in a new costume? I'd rather you take a real stance on both questions than give me a diplomatic "well, there are similarities and differences" non-answer.
