I'm a retired physicist -- spent about thirty-five years doing condensed-matter work before I hung up the lab coat -- and for the last year or so I've fallen into a philosophy rabbit hole almost by accident, mostly through a little discussion group that meets at the library on Tuesdays. A few of us started a shoestring podcast out of it (maybe two dozen listeners, half of them our own spouses), and next month's guest is a philosopher of physics who has built a good chunk of her career defending the claim that certain "infinity machine" thought experiments -- the kind with infinitely many balls of shrinking size colliding in a finite stretch of time, so that the very last ball in the sequence can start moving on its own with nothing ever having hit it -- aren't just cute paradoxes but genuine proof that classical, Newtonian mechanics is indeterministic once you allow completed infinities. (I want to say the fellow who first dreamed up this kind of machine has a Spanish surname, Perez-something, but don't quote me on that.)

As an old physicist this needles me in a way I haven't managed to explain properly to my book club: letting a ball start moving from a dead stop, with no energy or momentum having gone into it from anywhere, feels like it should count as a reductio against the setup, not an interesting discovery about determinism -- but apparently that's exactly backwards from how she reads it. I don't want the interview to turn into forty minutes of relitigating Thomson's lamp, though somebody in the audience always brings it up so I suppose we need one question there too. Could you draft eight or nine questions for me that really lean on the physics side of this? I'd like at least one that forces her to address the energy and momentum bookkeeping head-on instead of letting her just call it "surprising but consistent," one that makes her say plainly whether her argument does more work than Benacerraf's old reply to Thomson or is just that same point wearing a lab coat, one on whether bringing in relativistic cases like Malament-Hogarth spacetimes actually helps her argument or is a distraction from a claim that's suppose to be purely Newtonian, and one asking whether any of this would even matter for real physics if it turns out spacetime is discrete at the Planck scale. For each of those, throw in a quick follow-up keyed to whatever dodge she's most likely to reach for.
