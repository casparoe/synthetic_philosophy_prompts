I spent nearly forty years as an experimental physicist, mostly doing laser spectroscopy of alkali and Rydberg atoms, before retiring six years ago. Since then I have been reading my way through philosophy of science, prompted by nothing more than curiosity about what philosophers make of theory change, something I lived through more than once over the course of my career. I have become rather absorbed by the debate around structural realism, particularly John Worrall's 1989 paper proposing it as a way of reconciling scientific realism with the fact that so many earlier, empirically successful theories turned out to be radically wrong about the underlying entities they posited, and James Ladyman and Don Ross's considerably more radical ontic version in *Every Thing Must Go*. Below is a short argument I have drafted for a philosophy discussion circle I now attend at the local university, and I would be grateful for comments before I present it to people whose training in the subject is rather more solid than mine.

My example is the hydrogen spectrum rather than the usual case built around Fresnel's equations for light. Balmer and Rydberg fitted an empirical formula to the observed spectral lines in the 1880s, decades before anyone had a workable model of the atom. Bohr's 1913 model of electrons in quantized circular orbits then derived that same formula from first principles and even predicted the numerical value of the Rydberg constant from more basic quantities, rightly remembered as one of the great early triumphs of quantum theory. Sommerfeld subsequently refined the model with elliptical orbits, a second quantum number, and a relativistic correction for the electron's varying speed, in order to capture the fine structure of the lines. Then, within little more than a decade, the entire picture of orbiting electrons was abandoned. Schrödinger's wave mechanics describes the electron by a wavefunction with no trajectory whatsoever, and yet it reproduces exactly the same energy-level formula and the same quantum numbers, now reinterpreted as labels of a wavefunction rather than of an orbit; once spin is added and the equation made relativistic, it even reproduces essentially the same fine structure Sommerfeld had patched in by hand. Even in my own research on alkali atoms I relied constantly on quantum-defect theory, in essence a Rydberg formula corrected by an empirical fudge factor introduced long before anyone could derive it, and only later justified from scattering theory within full quantum mechanics. What survives across every one of these upheavals is not any claim about what an electron really is, but a tight set of formal relations among energy levels, quantum numbers, and observed frequencies. That, as far as I can tell, is exactly Worrall's point about Fresnel's equations surviving the move from an elastic-solid ether to Maxwellian electromagnetism, except the atomic case strikes me as more dramatic still, since here the very medium once said to be vibrating disappears from the picture entirely, rather than merely changing its properties.

Two worries keep me from being fully convinced by my own argument, and these are what I would most value your view on. First, the whole thing sounds uncomfortably close to Bohr's own correspondence principle, which I was taught simply as a heuristic constraint on theory-building, and which I have since learned a philosopher named Heinz Post generalized into something he called a General Correspondence Principle governing theory change quite generally; I cannot tell whether Worrall's structural realism adds a genuine metaphysical thesis on top of this or merely restates it in more realist-sounding language. Second, I have come across an objection that was originally aimed at Bertrand Russell's very similar claims about structure and is now regularly redirected at Worrall, attributed to the mathematician M. H. A. Newman: a theory which claims only that the world has "some structure or other" matching its equations says next to nothing, since almost any sufficiently large domain can be assigned relations realizing any structure one likes. I cannot decide whether the specificity of an actual formula like Rydberg's protects my example from this worry, or whether I am simply being naive about it. I would welcome your thoughts on three things: whether I have represented Worrall's position and Newman's objection accurately; whether the hydrogen case is really analogous to the Fresnel one, or whether I am forcing the comparison; and, on reflection, whether the whole story supports epistemic structural realism, the more radical ontic version, or neither.
