I'm a backend engineer, and a few weeks back I got nerd-sniped by a discussion at work about how to represent conflicting facts in a reconciliation service we run: it ingests identity/compliance signals from several third-party providers, and every so often two providers flatly disagree about the same boolean fact for the same customer (provider A says "is-US-person: true," provider B says "is-US-person: false," with no obvious way to adjudicate). A colleague mentioned that this is basically the use case Nuel Belnap had in mind when he proposed a four-valued logic in "How a Computer Should Think" (values true, false, both, and neither), so that a system can hold "both" for a fact without the whole knowledge base exploding into triviality. That sent me down a rabbit hole into paraconsistent logic generally, and from there into Graham Priest's dialetheism, i.e., the much stronger claim that some contradictions (the Liar sentence being the classic case) aren't just useful things for a database to represent, but are actually, literally, true.

I've drafted some notes for a blog post aimed at other engineers, and the core argument I want feedback on is this: our reconciliation service's four-valued scheme is just sensible epistemic bookkeeping about untrustworthy, conflicting sources — it doesn't commit us to thinking the world itself is contradictory, only that our evidence about it is. Dialetheism is a completely different and much riskier bet, since it says the contradiction is out there in the semantics, not just in our data pipeline, and it has to earn its keep on the Liar and similar cases alone, with no help from the engineering use case. My notes then argue that dialetheism doesn't obviously even win on its home turf: Priest's preferred logic LP has to treat "both" as a designated value to make the Liar's negation come out true, but that same move seems to break Modus Ponens for the ordinary conditional (there are assignments where A and "if A then B" both come out designated while B doesn't) — which strikes me as a strange price to pay just to "solve" the Liar. I also gather Curry's paradox is a related headache, since it can be derived without using negation at all, so merely tolerating true contradictions doesn't obviously stop it, and you apparently need a separate move like restricting contraction.

Could you look over this line of reasoning and tell me where it's naive, or where I'm collapsing distinctions a philosopher would keep separate? In particular I'd like your take on: (1) whether the "epistemic bookkeeping vs. genuine metaphysical glut" distinction I'm leaning on is really the right way to cut things, or whether there's a more serious argument running from the engineering usefulness of paraconsistency to dialetheism that I'm dismissing too quickly; (2) whether the Modus Ponens failure in LP is as damaging as I'm treating it, given that Priest has responses to this that I don't know well; and (3) how the Curry point relates to the Modus Ponens point — are these the same underlying problem wearing two hats, or genuinely separate difficulties a dialetheist has to solve one at a time? Please be direct if you think the whole framing is off.
