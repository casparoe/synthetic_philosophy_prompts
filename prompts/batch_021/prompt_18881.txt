Last month a diagnostic-support tool flagged one of my working diagnoses as statistically likely to be wrong, citing outcomes data on clinicians who share my specialty, experience, and time-of-shift. I trusted my own read anyway, kept the diagnosis, and turned out to be right — though I still can't tell whether that was good reasoning or dumb luck. I run a small talk series for our residency program on the philosophy of clinical judgment, and next month's guest defends the view that epistemic akrasia — keeping a first-order judgment even after acquiring statistical higher-order evidence, of just the kind I encountered, that one's reasoning on cases like it is unreliable — can be fully rational, contrary to the enkratic principle. Draft seven or eight interview questions that go beyond a restatement of the view: at least one pressing the Moore's-paradox-style worry about believing P while believing the evidence tells against P; one asking her to distinguish principled steadfastness from plain dogmatism, since the same move seems to license dismissing any well-documented statistical evidence of one's own bias; and one on whether the view survives third-order evidence — evidence that one's resistance to the tool's flag is itself self-serving — without licensing resistance all the way up. Add a follow-up to each, keyed to the answer she's most likely to give.
