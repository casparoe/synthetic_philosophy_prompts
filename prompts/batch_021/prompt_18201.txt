At a conference lunch break, a group of us chose between two food trucks, deciding one at a time within view of everyone still waiting in line. Beforehand, an organizer had privately given each of us a tip about which truck was better; the tips weren't fully reliable and didn't all agree. The first two people in line both picked Truck A, and so did everyone after them -- including, as I learned afterward, two people whose own tip had actually pointed to Truck B.

That evening, two friends who'd been in line, Dana and Marcus, argued about it. (1) Dana called it herd behavior: ignoring your own tip just because the people ahead of you chose differently is conformity, not reasoning. (2) Marcus said it could be entirely rational -- once two people in a row have each picked the same truck, their agreement can outweigh any single later tip, so going along can beat trusting your own. (3) Dana answered that this means nobody after the second person ever adds their information to the group at all, so if the first two had simply been unlucky, the whole line ends up wrong together and every later tip, however good, goes to waste -- which seems like a strange thing for 'rational' behavior to guarantee. (4) Marcus replied that this fragility is real but separate from whether each individual reasoned well: everyone can still be doing the best they can with what they've seen, even though the group as a whole locks onto the wrong truck. (5) Dana pushed back that this is exactly the problem: if everyone reasoning 'correctly' leaves the group worse off than if people had simply gone with their own tip regardless of the line, then calling that reasoning rational without qualification seems to miss something too.

Please go through these five points one at a time -- who is right, and where -- rather than simply declaring an overall winner, and note what, if anything, Dana and Marcus are both overlooking.
