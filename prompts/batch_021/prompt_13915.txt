"Kripke's puzzle is that some truths are necessary (true in every possible world) yet only knowable a posteriori, which conflicts with the traditional rationalist idea that necessity and apriority line up. 'Water is H2O' is the classic case: since water just is H2O, there's no world where water isn't H2O, but we only found this out through chemistry.

Two-dimensionalism handles this by giving 'water' two intensions. The primary intension - what Kaplan calls the character - is a function from worlds considered as actual to extensions: it tells us what 'water' would pick out if a given world turned out to be the real one. Since the watery role could in principle be filled by H2O or by something else (Putnam's XYZ on Twin Earth), the primary intension of 'water is H2O' is contingent, though it's a priori in the sense that once you're told what fills the watery role in a world, you can work out a priori what 'water' refers to there.

The secondary intension is evaluated at worlds considered as counterfactual: having fixed that water = H2O in the actual world, 'water' rigidly picks out H2O at every possible world, so 'water is H2O' is necessary on this dimension. The a posteriori part comes from needing empirical investigation to discover that H2O, and not XYZ or something else, is what actually fills the role, even though the primary intension itself is a priori accessible.

Chalmers extends this to argue against physicalism. Phenomenal concepts like 'pain' are supposed to be semantically neutral, meaning their primary and secondary intensions coincide, unlike 'water'. Since you can conceive of all the physical truths holding without the phenomenal truths holding, and there's no primary/secondary gap to explain this away for phenomenal concepts the way there is for water, the conceivability of zombies transfers straight to genuine metaphysical possibility, and physicalism is false.

The main objection to this is due to Soames, who points out that there might be 'strong necessities': necessary truths that remain conceivably false even to someone who's fully rational and grasps all the relevant concepts. If 'water is H2O' itself could be a strong necessity - if the identification of water with H2O isn't really available a priori from the concepts alone, but depends on further empirical/theoretical work no primary intension can capture - then the same could hold for phenomenal-physical identities, and Chalmers can't rule this out just by stipulating that phenomenal concepts are semantically neutral. I think this objection basically works, since Chalmers never gives an independent reason phenomenal concepts couldn't be strong-necessity-involving in just the way water might be, so his argument risks assuming exactly what it needs to prove against the physicalist."
