For my Just War Theory seminar we had to read Anscombe's pamphlet "Mr Truman's Degree" (the one where she says Oxford giving Truman an honorary degree basically amounts to honoring mass murder, because of the atomic bombs), and afterward two people in my discussion group got into an argument about it that never really got resolved. Neither of them is me, I was just watching this go down. Here's how it went:

(1) Sam said Anscombe's argument doesn't actually work: Truman's intention was to end the war fast and avoid an invasion that would've killed way more people on both sides, and the civilian deaths, however predictable, were foreseen side effects, not the intended means. So it's just an (extremely large) double-effect case, not "choosing to kill the innocent as a means," and calling it murder is wrong.

(2) Priya said that gets the case backwards -- the cities weren't hit that way because civilian death was an unfortunate byproduct of destroying something else. The mass civilian deaths were supposed to be the shock that forced surrender. When the mechanism by which your plan works is "kill enough people fast enough that the government gives up," the killing isn't a side effect of the plan succeeding, its HOW the plan succeeds. So its intended, full stop.

(3) Sam said if that's the standard, intention basically swallows every foreseeable effect the plan causally depends on, and then almost every WW2 bombing campaign, plus the blockade of Germany, plus arguably Japan's own conduct, all count as "intentional murder of civilians" too, since they worked (to the extent they worked) by grinding civilians down. That seems like it proves too much to still be a useful moral category.

(4) Priya said Anscombe would probably just accept that -- the pamphlet isn't really only about Truman, its about how "shorten the war" reasoning got used to wave away exactly this kind of killing everywhere, and she wants to name that even if it convicts nearly everyone.

(5) Sam wasn't happy with that -- he said if literally no one comes out clean, "murder" stops doing any real moral work, it just becomes a fancy way of saying "war is bad." He wants an actual principled version of the argument that gets Truman specifically, without dragging in every belligerent in the war.

I couldn't settle it so I said I'd ask you. Can you go through moves (1)-(5) and say who's got the better of it at each step (not just who wins overall), flag anything you think both of them are missing, and then take a shot at Sam's question in (5): is there a way of reading Anscombe's actual argument that convicts Truman without convicting basically everybody?
