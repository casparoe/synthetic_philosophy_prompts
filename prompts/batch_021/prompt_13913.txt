I am in the final stages of a novel—a long, research-heavy piece of historical fiction—and have arrived at the small ceremonial task of writing the author's preface: the part where one thanks one's sources and adds the customary hedge that, notwithstanding years spent in archives, any errors that remain are the author's alone. I described this ritual over dinner to a friend of mine, a philosopher by training who can't resist finding paradoxes in ordinary life, and she informed me, with some delight, that I was about to commit "the preface paradox": I am justified, claim by claim, in believing every sentence in my book, and yet, in writing that hedge, I am also justified in believing that at least one of those sentences is false. She then insisted this is really just the lottery paradox wearing a different coat—there, you are justified in believing of each individual ticket that it will lose, while also being certain that some ticket will win—and that any respectable solution to one paradox is, ipso facto, a solution to the other.

I found myself resisting this claim, though I could not immediately say why, beyond a vague sense that a lottery is a single, purpose-built device for manufacturing exactly one winner out of otherwise interchangeable tickets, whereas my book is not the output of any such device at all: each claim in it was arrived at separately, checked against its own particular piece of evidence, and would be false, if it is false, for its own local reasons rather than because some general mechanism assigns exactly one falsehood per book. I would like your help finding out whether that hunch survives real philosophical pressure. Please argue for the position that the lottery paradox and the preface paradox are not, despite their popular pairing in the literature, two instances of one and the same underlying puzzle, and that a solution which dissolves the contradiction in one case need not dissolve it in the other, engaging seriously with the strongest form of my friend's "same paradox, different costume" view—the fact that both cases seem to violate the very same closure or agglomeration principle governing justified belief—rather than a weaker version of it. Please close by telling me what, if anything, your answer implies about whether I am still entitled to write the standard disclaimer in my own preface.
