I'm designing a graduate seminar session that puts Blaise Pascal's reflections on justice and force in the Pensées into dialogue with twentieth-century theories of nonviolent resistance, and I want to sharpen one discussion question before putting it to students. Pascal's well-known fragment on the topic holds, in effect, that justice without force is powerless while force without justice is mere tyranny, and that since we cannot make justice strong, we have settled for calling whatever is strong "just"; elsewhere in the Pensées he pairs this with a conservative insistence that established custom deserves general obedience, since exposing its arbitrary origins to ordinary people risks a disorder worse than the injustice of living under it. Suppose Pascal were confronted with the strategy of civil disobedience later articulated by Thoreau, Gandhi, and King: deliberately breaking an unjust law while renouncing force altogether, so that the state's own violent response does the work of stripping the law of legitimacy in the eyes of onlookers. Arguing strictly from Pascal's own premises, would he most likely side with civil authority against such demonstrators as agents of disorder, or could he recognize in their strategy a way of exposing, without inciting chaos, the very groundlessness of authority he elsewhere concedes? I'd like your reasoning about which strands of his thought pull in each direction, and which ought to win out, rather than a flat verdict.
