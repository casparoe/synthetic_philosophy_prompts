I've spent the past few weeks reporting a feature on the quieter, second-order side of the replication crisis: not the famous failed replications themselves, but the arguments that break out inside research teams when a reanalysis of the very same data produces two incompatible verdicts and nobody can locate the bug. Sitting in on one such team, I watched two of its statisticians, Naomi and Felix, get into a dispute I haven't been able to stop turning over, because underneath the statistics it turned out to be a genuinely open philosophical question about what you're entitled to believe about your own believing.

Here is roughly how it went. Naomi ran the team's preregistered pipeline on a contested finding and got a significant effect; Felix ran what was meant to be the identical pipeline and got nothing. A full day of walking through each other's code line by line turned up no point where the two analyses actually diverged. On its own, that might have stayed a simple, familiar kind of stalemate — meta-scientists have found that a dozen skilled analysts given the same data and question will often reach a dozen different, individually defensible answers. But then Felix pulled up the team's shared log of past reanalyses and noticed that, over two years, Naomi's individual reanalyses had turned up significant effects the team's official pipeline had missed more often than chance would predict, and specifically in cases where she had been personally invested in the hypothesis. (1) Felix argued that Naomi should now substantially lower her confidence in her own result, not because anyone had located an error in it, but precisely because a biased process wouldn't feel biased from the inside, and this was documented, specific evidence about her, not a generic accusation. (2) Naomi answered that this put her in an impossible position: if she lowered her confidence without finding any actual flaw in the math, she would end up believing the data supported the effect while also believing she had no business believing that. She recognized this as an instance of what the team's resident philosopher of science — brought in after an earlier methods dispute — had called "epistemic akrasia," and it struck her as a paradox rather than a rational resting point, so something had to be wrong with the demand itself. (3) Felix replied that this wasn't a paradox at all, just an update: once she genuinely absorbed the track-record evidence, her considered view of the effect should simply shift to something more modest, full stop. There would be no leftover belief for her to be conflicted about, because the old, more confident belief had been replaced, not held alongside a doubt about itself. (4) Naomi countered that this proves too much: if a documented pattern about her past behavior can override a specific, freshly checked analysis with no limit in principle, then any sufficiently well-recorded accusation of bias could make her distrust her own arithmetic indefinitely, on any question — leaving her first-order judgment permanently hostage to whoever keeps the best statistics on her. (5) Felix granted that the evidence has to clear some bar to do that kind of work, but maintained that a two-year pattern drawn from the team's own records clears it easily, and that Naomi's confidence that she'd checked her code carefully enough was itself exactly the sort of self-assessment the evidence gave her reason to distrust.

Neither of them budged, and I don't think I'm qualified to referee it myself. Could you go through the exchange move by move — I've numbered the five above — and say, for each, whether it succeeds, partly succeeds, or fails, and why? I would also like to know whether you think one of them has simply made a mistake somewhere in this back-and-forth, or whether Naomi and Felix have instead run up against a genuine, unresolved tension in how evidence about our own reliability is supposed to bear on what we go on believing.
