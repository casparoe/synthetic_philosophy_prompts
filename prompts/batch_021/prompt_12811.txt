I'm working on a piece about prediction and forecasting -- superforecasters, prediction markets, that whole world -- and a decision theorist I interviewed for background sent me down a total rabbit hole with an old thought experiment called "Death in Damascus." I already knew the Somerset Maugham fable everyone quotes, the servant who flees to Samarra and finds Death waiting for him there anyway, but aparently Gibbard and Harper turned it into something meaner back in the 70s. In their version the man is choosing, right now, between staying in Damascus or fleeing to Aleppo, and Death is an honest-to-god perfect predictor who's already headed for whichever city the man will actually end up in. My source's point was that this makes it impossible for the man to land on a stable choice: whatever city he settles on is, by construction, exactly the one Death picked, so the moment he'd notice that and want to switch, the switch is already accounted for too. He called it a case with no "ratifiable" choice, which was apparently a whole headache for people trying to defend evidential decision theory, and he mentioned -- though I didn't fully follow why -- that even deciding by coin flip doesnt get the man off the hook.

That structure's been rattling around in my head all week, and I think it'd make a genuinely good short story -- not just a costume-drama retelling of the Maugham version everyone already knows. Could you write one, maybe 800-1200 words, built specifically around the Gibbard/Harper mechanics: the part where it's the man's own deliberation, not some fate fixed in advance, that keeps sealing his doom no matter how he tries to outsmart it? You can keep the ancient marketplace setting or transplant it somewhere else entirely (a modern city, an algorithmic casino, whatever serves the story) -- I just don't want the philosophical teeth of the original to get lost in translation. Ideally the reader should feel, by the end, exactly why there's no clever trick, randomizing included, that lets him wriggle free. And honestly, it doesn't need a tidy resolution -- given the puzzle, maybe it shouldn't have one.
