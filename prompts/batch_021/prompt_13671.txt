I'm taking a seminar this term, cross-listed between Computer Science and Philosophy, called "Fairness and Accountability in Automated Decision-Making." For the final project, each of us has to imagine ourselves as a member of a real-world institutional body and produce the memo that body would actually need in order to act. We spent two weeks of the course on the impossibility results associated with Chouldechova and with Kleinberg, Mullainathan, and Raghavan, which show that once two groups have different base rates for the outcome being predicted, a decision rule generally cannot be simultaneously well-calibrated within each group and equalized in its error rates across groups. Most of my classmates are writing their memos about the standard COMPAS-style criminal justice examples from the readings, so I put together my own case study and would like my memo to actually resolve it rather than just restate what's already in the papers.

Here is the case. Imagine I sit on the Model Risk and Fair Lending Committee of a mid-sized credit union that recently replaced manual underwriting for personal loans with a machine-learning model. An audit commissioned ahead of a fair-lending exam scheduled in six weeks found that the model is equally well-calibrated for both long-tenured members and recently arrived members with thin U.S. credit files: a predicted default probability of, say, 20% corresponds to an actual default rate near 20% in either group. But because thin-file applicants default at a higher base rate overall, among applicants who would not have defaulted, thin-file applicants are denied at roughly two-and-a-half times the rate of long-tenured applicants; symmetrically, among applicants who would have defaulted, long-tenured applicants are incorrectly approved at a noticeably higher rate than thin-file applicants. The committee is deadlocked. The Chief Risk Officer wants to leave the single calibrated model untouched, on the grounds that giving two applicants with identical predicted risk different decisions because of their membership tenure is itself a form of discrimination. The Compliance Officer wants group-specific approval thresholds that would satisfy something close to equalized odds — equal false-denial rates for creditworthy applicants across the two groups — even though this breaks calibration and means the "same" score would no longer mean the same thing for both groups. (A third proposal, to abandon risk-based scoring here altogether and simply equalize overall approval rates, was raised and tabled by the chair as inconsistent with the credit union's safety-and-soundness obligations, but a couple of members keep bringing it back up.)

Could you draft the memo I'd circulate to the committee ahead of next week's vote? I'd like it to recommend one concrete policy rather than a vague call to "balance the values at stake," to specify how that policy would be implemented and monitored during the six weeks before the exam, and to engage seriously enough with whichever side it overrules that a sharp member of that side would recognize their actual objection was addressed rather than brushed aside.
