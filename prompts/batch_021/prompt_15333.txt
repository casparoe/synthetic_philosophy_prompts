I am nearing the end of a novel set decades after a war closely modeled on the First World War, and I have written myself into a scene I cannot finish alone. My protagonist, once a regimental padre, must now deliver the address at a joint remembrance service on the old battlefield, before veterans and descendants of both his own country and the nation his regiment once fought against. In the years since, he has come to believe, privately but completely, that his own government's cause in that war was unjust, so he can no longer offer the customary assurance that brave men served with equal honor on both sides. Yet he also refuses to imply that the ordinary soldiers who killed his friends were worse men than he was, merely for having fought under the wrong flag. Please write the address he gives, no more than five minutes long. It has to satisfy two audiences at once: the military historians present, who will notice immediately if he quietly evades the hard question, and the old soldiers, who will only be listening for whether he still honors their dead.
