Over the past few weeks, my partner and I have been going through preconception carrier screening before trying to conceive, and the results came back showing that I carry a recessive mutation linked to a serious metabolic disorder. The genetic counselor who explained this to us was thoughtful and clear, but I noticed she leaned hard on one particular metaphor throughout the conversation: my DNA has a "typo," a "spelling mistake," a "glitch in the instructions." It's a natural way to make the science feel accessible, and I doubt she meant anything philosophically loaded by it, but the phrase has stuck with me in a way I didn't expect. I did just enough undergraduate biology to remember that there's at least one place where "code" is a precise, technical term rather than a friendly metaphor: the mapping from three-letter codons to amino acids really is arbitrary and rule-governed, the way an alphabet or a cipher is, which is presumably part of why biologists ended up calling the codon table "the genetic code" in the first place (Schrödinger, tellingly, was already describing chromosomes in terms of a hereditary "code-script" decades before anyone had worked out what the underlying mechanism even was). But when my counselor said my DNA had a typo "in it," she wasn't talking about the codon table at all. She meant something much broader, and I've come to suspect much shakier: that the sequence itself specifies, in something like the way a program specifies a computation, how an entire trait or disorder comes about, when in fact a great deal of what happens between a mutated sequence and an affected child depends on cellular machinery, maternal environment, and regulatory processes that are nowhere written in the DNA sequence itself.

I would like an essay from you that treats this as the genuinely open controversy it appears to be, rather than settling it by fiat in either direction. Please lay out the strongest case that DNA really does constitute a code, or carries genuine information about the organism in some sense stronger than mere correlation, alongside the strongest case that this is a seductive but ultimately misleading metaphor that smuggles in far more than the molecular facts actually support. Then reach a verdict and defend it, rather than leaving me with "both sides have a point." A qualified conclusion is entirely acceptable — for instance, "code-talk holds up at the level of the codon table but not at the level of traits and disorders," if that is where the arguments lead you — as long as you commit to a specific position and argue for it, since I would genuinely like to know how much weight to put on this metaphor the next time I have to explain to a curious relative what it actually means that I am, as my counselor put it, a carrier of a "misprint."
