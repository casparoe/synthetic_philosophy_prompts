So I spent about thirty-five years as an experimental physicist (mostly condensed matter, a bit of optics near the end), and since retiring Ive gotten weirdly hooked on philosophy of physics. It started with one of those general-relativity-anniversary popular books a while back, snowballed into me actually working through Earman and Norton and a few Stanford Encyclopedia entries, and now I bring half-formed questions to a local philosophy discussion group every other Tuesday. Lately I've been stuck on the hole argument. I think I've got the basic mechanics down: take a solution of Einstein's field equations, pick a "hole" region with no matter in it, apply a diffeomorphism that's the identity everywhere outside the hole but does something nontrivial inside it, and you get a second, mathematically distinct solution that agrees with the first on absolutely everything outside the hole -- same initial data, same boundary conditions. Earman and Norton's point is that a "manifold substantivalist," someone who thinks spacetime points are real individuated things rather than just a way of talking about relations between bodies, is committed to treating these two diffeomorphic models as genuinely different possible worlds, and that hands them a radical indeterminism -- nothing outside the hole fixes what happens inside it.

What I can't sort out, even after two passes through the secondary literature, is how much trouble this is actually supposed to be for the substantivalist. Some people say you can go "sophisticated" -- accept what Earman and Norton call Leibniz equivalence (diffeomorphic models are the very same physical situation, just redescribed) and give up haecceitism about spacetime points, while still insisting points are real -- and the whole problem evaporates. Other people say that move is exactly the trick Leibniz used against Newton's absolute space with his shift argument, so either it was always cheap and the substantivalist is no worse off than before, or there's a genuine disanalogy between a rigid global shift of the whole universe and a diffeomorphism that can do absolutely anything inside an arbitrarily tiny hole, and that disanalogy is precisely why the hole argument is supposed to hurt more. Could you write me a proper essay on this: lay out the strongest version of the hole argument against manifold substantivalism, the sophisticated-substantivalist escape route, the best pushback against that escape, and the Leibniz-shift comparison -- and then actually tell me who's ahead. My discussion group has a bad habit of ending every session on "well, reasonable people disagree," and I'd like an essay that doesn't chicken out that way. If your honest answer is conditional (substantivalism survives, but only at some further cost, say), that's a perfectly good answer too, just spell the condition out.
