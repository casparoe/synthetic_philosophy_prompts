Quick one from my decision theory seminar that's been bugging me. We got onto the topic of "acausal threats" — the idea, popular in rationalist/EA circles, that an agent you have no causal contact with (a future superintelligence, something in a causally separate branch of the multiverse, whatever) could still extort you right now by threatening to simulate and hurt copies of you unless you comply, purely on the strength of its being able to predict/model you perfectly. One of my students brought up "surrogate goals" as the standard proposed fix: the target deliberately adopts some cheap, arbitrary goal and commits to treating threats against it as if they mattered, so that a rational extorter redirects its threat there instead of at whatever the target actually values.

Here's what I can't get past, though: if the extorter is powerful enough to simulate the target accurately enough to run this whole scenario in the first place, wouldn't it also see straight through the fact that the target's "caring" about the surrogate goal is manufactured, and that it's going to evaporate the second it's actually tested — basically the same failure mode as the hitchiker in Parfit's famous case, who secretly plans to stiff the driver once he's safely in town? A good enough driver wouldn't pick that hitchhiker up, so why would a good enough extorter ever be taken in by a surrogate goal? It feels like decision theorists are smuggling in exactly the kind of unverifiable sincerity that these scenarios are supposed to rule out from the start. Am I missing something, or is this a genuine hole in the surrogate goals idea?
