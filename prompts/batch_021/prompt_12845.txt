Over the past several months I have been teaching myself about dispositional properties, mostly by following footnotes out of a Stanford Encyclopedia article, and I have become a bit fixated on the pair of puzzles usually discussed under the headings of finkish dispositions and masking: the live wire connected to a device that senses an approaching hand and cuts the current a split second before contact, and the poison that never kills because an antidote is swallowed in the very same instant, so that the disposition to kill is still there even though nothing ever manifests it. I have no formal training in philosophy, so before I take the argument below any further I would like an honest outside opinion on whether it actually holds up or whether I have just dressed up a confusion in borrowed vocabulary.

The case I keep testing this on is my uncle. As a child he had a reputation as a coward: he backed down from every schoolyard confrontation and never once stood his ground against the kids who pushed him around. As an adult, during a house fire, he ran back into the building without visible hesitation to pull a neighbor's child out of a smoke-filled room. One reading is that the disposition to be courageous was present the whole time and that something else, social anxiety in front of watching peers, say, or simply the low stakes of playground status, masked it, the way bubble wrap masks a glass's fragility while leaving it exactly as breakable as before. The rival reading is that there was no standing disposition at all, and that the emergency itself is what installed something like courage in him for those few minutes, much as the sensor in the wire case installs conductivity only once a hand draws near. I lean toward the masking story, partly because it lets me say something to situationist psychologists like John Doris, who argue from experiments on how sensitive behavior is to circumstance that people lack robust, cross-situational character traits altogether: my suggestion is that what looks like the absence of a trait is really just masking that happens to be unusually widespread and effective, rather than evidence that no underlying disposition exists.

I mentioned the gist of this to a friend who is finishing a philosophy PhD, and she called the situationism point "promising but underdeveloped," which was encouraging but not something I could actually act on. So, more concretely, I would like your comments on three things: whether my uncle's case is genuinely better described as masking than finking, or whether I am forcing a distinction built for wires and poisons onto behavior it was never designed to handle, with some third description fitting better; whether the closing move against Doris earns its keep or just relocates the disagreement, given that a disposition which is always so conveniently masked starts to sound unfalsifiable; and whether there is already a cleaner version of this example worked out in the literature that does more carefully what I am only groping toward. I would rather learn where this breaks now than keep building on it.
