I'm reporting a "science of happiness" feature — hedonic adaptation, the whole positive-psychology beat — and along the way I re-read Nozick's old experience-machine argument: the one where almost nobody says they'd plug into a machine guaranteeing a lifetime of perfect simulated pleasure. That pretty much settles it that raw pleasant experience isn't actually the thing people value most, right? Which has given me an angle for a somewhat snarky sidebar: if philosophers already closed the book on hedonism about well-being back in the 70s with one thought experiment, why does an entire research field (plus a huge chunk of the self-help industry) still get funded while basically treating "maximize positive affect" as the goal of a good life? Feels like they either havent gotten the memo or they're quietly sidestepping a pretty devastating objection.

A philosopher friend, when I ran the pitch by her at a dinner party, made a face and muttered something about a follow-up study — grad-student work, I think out of Duke — that reran the thought experiment by telling people they'd already been living in the machine their whole life and asking if they'd switch to "base reality," and got a much messier result than the original. She called it "a side point" and got pulled into another conversation before she finished explaining, so I never chased it down. Before I file this, can you fact-check the framing for me — is "the experience machine refuted hedonism, so positive psychology is standing on sand" actually a fair way to put it, and if not, what's the more careful version of that story I should be telling readers?
