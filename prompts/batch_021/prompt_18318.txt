I work as a software engineer and have no formal background in philosophy, but I recently fell down a rabbit hole reading about Thomas Hobbes's strange relationship with mathematics, including his decades-long public feud with the mathematician John Wallis after Hobbes repeatedly and wrongly insisted he had squared the circle. What caught my attention wasn't the error itself, but his underlying reason for treating geometry as the one truly certain science: he held that we can have complete, causal knowledge of geometric figures only because we construct them ourselves out of simple motions, and he even faulted Euclid's own definitions for not being properly causal, insisting that a line must be defined as the path traced by a moving point. Natural science, by contrast, he considered permanently second-rate, since we aren't the makers of nature the way we are of geometric figures, and so can only guess at nature's causes.

Given that specific argument, how do you think Hobbes would have reacted to the 19th-century discovery that Euclid's parallel postulate can be swapped for alternatives, producing perfectly consistent "non-Euclidean" geometries whose theorems disagree with Euclid's — for instance, on whether a triangle's angles sum to, more than, or less than 180 degrees? Does his own logic commit him to treating all of these as equally certain sciences, since each is just as constructible from clear, motion-based definitions? Or is there a way for him, consistent with his materialism, to still insist that only one of them is the true geometry of the physical space we actually inhabit?
