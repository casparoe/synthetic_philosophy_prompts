Last night a friend and I spent hours arguing about Jeffrey conditionalization, and neither of us managed to convince the other. The point of contention: when an agent updates on uncertain evidence via probability kinematics rather than by learning some proposition with outright certainty, the resulting credences are in general sensitive to the order in which the uncertain inputs arrive — shifting credence first on the partition {E, ¬E} and then on {F, ¬F} need not yield the same posterior as shifting in the reverse order, even when the rigidity condition holds at every step and, intuitively, the same total evidence has been absorbed either way. My friend takes this order-sensitivity to be a decisive strike against treating Jeffrey's rule as being just as rationally mandatory as strict conditionalization is usually taken to be: on her view, an ideally rational agent's opinions shouldn't hinge on the mere sequence in which uncertain observations happen to arrive. I think she's moving too fast. Please argue for my position: that the failure of commutativity is not a mark against Jeffrey conditionalization, and may even be exactly what we should expect from any adequate rule for updating on uncertain evidence. In making the case, take a stand on whether the dynamic-coherence Dutch Book argument that Brian Skyrms and others have offered for Jeffrey's rule also vindicates its order-sensitivity, and explain what an agent would actually have to give up in order to secure order-independent updates instead.
