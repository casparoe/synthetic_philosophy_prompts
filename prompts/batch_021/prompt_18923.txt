I spent most of my career as an experimental particle physicist, and since retiring three years ago I have been auditing philosophy of science courses at a university near me, largely to finally examine the foundations of the statistical machinery I relied on for decades without much philosophical scrutiny. Two weeks ago the seminar covered Reichenbach's reference class problem, and I left the session thinking I had already understood the issue, since it struck me as essentially a more technical restatement of the base-rate fallacy that Kahneman and Tversky made famous with examples like their taxicab problem, in which people asked to judge whether a cab involved in an accident was more likely blue or green neglect the known citywide frequency of the two colors in favor of an eyewitness's imperfect identification. On my reading, the reference class problem simply evaporates once a reasoner is disciplined enough to use the correct, most specific base rate available in a given case, leaving nothing for philosophers to argue about beyond the practical question of how to train people out of the bias.

I aired this view over dinner with an old colleague who now works in biostatistics, and she insisted I had the relationship backwards. Reichenbach's puzzle, she said, is not a claim about any failure of reasoning, and it does not disappear even for an agent who never neglects a base rate, because the difficulty is that there is no uniquely correct base rate, or reference class, to use in the first place: a single case belongs simultaneously to indefinitely many true reference classes, each with a different associated frequency, and nothing in the case itself picks out one of them as privileged. She added, to my greater surprise, that philosophers such as Alan Hájek have argued this problem survives more or less intact even for Bayesians, which I found hard to credit given that I had always understood subjective credences to attach directly to individual propositions rather than to be read off class frequencies at all.

I would like help settling who is closer to right. Please explain precisely how the reference class problem differs from base-rate neglect as a psychological phenomenon, showing why even an ideally rational reasoner with unlimited data and no biases could still fail to resolve it, using something like Reichenbach's own proposal of favoring the narrowest reference class for which reliable statistics exist to illustrate where that resolution runs out; then address my colleague's stronger claim that the trouble persists for Bayesian and propensity interpretations of probability and is not merely an artifact of Reichenbach's frequentism. A worked example from somewhere like medical prognosis or weather forecasting, rather than another pass through the taxicab case, would help me judge whether the distinction she is drawing is as sharp as she thinks.
