I recently moved into a role at my firm where I'm the one who has to defend our valuations to clients and to more senior colleagues, and I keep running into the same unsettling pattern. I'll justify a number by pointing to an assumption — a discount rate, say, or a chosen set of "comparable" companies — and I'm asked what justifies that assumption, and then what justifies my answer to that, and so on, seemingly without end. Occasionally, if I trace the chain back far enough, it loops into itself: a "comparable" company's valuation turns out to rest, a few steps removed, on a figure our own team produced in an earlier report. In practice, everyone just stops somewhere ("that's the market-standard approach," or "that's simply what the data shows") and moves on, but I can't articulate what makes one stopping point legitimate rather than arbitrary, or whether circling back onto one of your own earlier assumptions quietly undermines a valuation or is just unavoidable. My manager tells me I'm overthinking a routine part of the job. Is there a principled way of thinking about what makes a chain of justification like this a sound one, and where, if anywhere, it is actually legitimate to stop?
