I've been reporting a piece on whether the recent vogue for "downward causation" talk among neuroscientists can really be squared with physicalism, and the background reading kept leading me back to Jaegwon Kim's causal exclusion argument. The worry, roughly, is this: if every mental event is realized by, but not identical to, some physical event, and the physical domain is causally closed under its own laws, then whenever a mental event seems to cause a physical effect, that effect already has a physical cause sufficient on its own. Positing the mental event as a second, distinct cause then looks like an implausible case of systematic overdetermination — unless the mental event is simply identified with its physical realizer, which is exactly what non-reductive physicalists refuse to do.

One philosopher I interviewed waved the whole problem away: once you trade Kim's "sufficient cause" picture for a broadly interventionist or counterfactual theory of causation — the kind associated with James Woodward — mental and physical properties stop competing for the same causal role, and the exclusion worry simply evaporates. When I pressed her on the obvious complication, she was less convincing. You can't coherently hold a physical realizer fixed while intervening on the mental property it realizes, since changing the mental property just is, in part, changing the realizer — so it is unclear that any legitimate interventionist counterfactual about the mental property could even be formulated. She called this a solved technical wrinkle, without saying how it had been solved.

I'd like to settle this for the piece myself, rather than report it as an unresolved "some say, others say." Please argue, as forcefully as you can, that switching to an interventionist theory of causation genuinely rescues mental causation from Kim's exclusion argument, and do so by directly resolving the realizer-fixing complication above rather than bracketing it — spell out concretely what a legitimate intervention on a mental property would have to look like, given that the property cannot float free of its physical realizer. Then stress-test your own argument: would the same defense indiscriminately certify causal powers for obviously gerrymandered higher-level properties that nobody thinks are genuinely causal in their own right — the shape of a constellation, say? If it would not, say precisely what keeps mental properties on the right side of that line.
