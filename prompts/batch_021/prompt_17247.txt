My younger sister is finishing a doctorate on G. E. Moore's Principia Ethica, and in a few weeks she is marrying her partner of nine years, a violinist named Thom. She has asked me — the family novelist, hopeless with footnotes — to give the toast, which frightens me more than any deadline ever has. About half the room will be her philosophy department: her supervisor, both examiners, and a small contingent of committed Moore devotees who, she warns me, still treat the closing chapter of Principia Ethica with something close to the reverence the old Bloomsbury set once gave it. The other half is our large, entirely non-philosophical family, who will want to laugh a little and cry a little and have never once heard the phrase "organic unity" and shouldn't have to. My sister has made it very clear, half as a joke and half not, that she will never forgive me if I flatten Moore's actual position into a greeting-card line about friendship and beauty being the good things in life. She wants the real machinery in there somewhere: the claim that a whole can be worth more than the sum of the value of its parts, say, or that strange thought experiment of his about two entirely unwitnessed worlds, one as beautiful and the other as hideous as can be imagined.

Could you write me a five-minute toast that takes Moore's actual, specific argument seriously enough to survive her supervisor's scrutiny, puts at least one real piece of that philosophical machinery to work in saying something true about this particular marriage rather than about marriages in general, and still lands, for my great-aunts and cousins, as a toast rather than a lecture? I would rather it under-explain the philosophy and over-deliver on affection than the other way around, but I don't want it to be wrong about Moore, either.
