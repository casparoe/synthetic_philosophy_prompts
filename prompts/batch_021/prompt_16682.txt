The thing I'm stuck on is the "AI safety via debate" scheme for scalable oversight -- instead of asking a human to directly judge whether a superhuman model's answer to some hard question is correct, you have two copies of the model argue against each other in front of the human judge, the idea being a dishonest debater gets caught out under cross-examination in a way an honest one doesn't. Fine so far. But a couple of the writeups motivate this by leaning on complexity theory -- something about how, with optimal play, these debates could in principle let a computationally weak judge correctly decide questions all the way up at PSPACE, the same way PSPACE has these two-player game characterizations (deciding a quantified boolean formula as a game between an "exists" player and a "forall" player), or like those old interactive-proof results -- Shamir's IP=PSPACE, if I'm remembering the name right -- where a lowly polynomial-time verifier gets talked into correctly believing things way beyond what it could check unaided. I did enough theory of computation in grad school to follow the shape of those results in the abstract, but I don't have good intuition for why any of it is supposed to transfer to two language models arguing about, say, whether some code has a security flaw, in front of an actual human rather than an idealized verifier running a clean protocol.

So: first, can you walk me through, fairly intuitively, what the actual load-bearing similarity is supposed to be between "weak verifier plus adversarial provers" and "human judge plus two debating AI's" -- why does the complexity result give anyone hope here at all? What exactly goes wrong?
