A friend of mine who works in quantitative finance and I spent most of a long dinner arguing about whether the Kelly criterion deserves to be called a rationality norm, or whether it is just a convenient betting heuristic that has been oversold by its enthusiasts. His position was unambiguous: because Kelly betting maximizes the expected logarithm of wealth, it maximizes the long-run, almost-sure growth rate of wealth under repeated favorable bets, whereas any agent who instead maximizes plain expected value each round — say, by staking everything on the highest-expected-value option — will, however good the odds, go broke almost surely given enough repetitions. When I raised Paul Samuelson's old objection — that there is nothing privileged about the logarithm, that maximizing expected log wealth coincides with maximizing expected utility only for an agent whose utility happens to be logarithmic, and that "almost sure" dominance as the number of bets goes to infinity tells us little about what a rational agent facing some large but finite number of bets, and some utility function other than log, should actually do — he dismissed it as a historical footnote, and countered with the newer "ergodicity economics" line: that ordinary expected-value reasoning quietly averages over an ensemble of hypothetical parallel worlds, when any actual agent only ever lives through a single trajectory through time, so that the time-average growth rate a wealth process delivers along that one trajectory (which is exactly what the Kelly criterion optimizes) is the more honest target for rationality to track, at least whenever wealth compounds multiplicatively.

Neither of us budged, and I would like a more careful adjudication than we managed at the table. Write an essay that reconstructs the strongest form of each position: the traditional view, on which Kelly betting is at most a special case of expected-utility maximization, rational only given particular assumptions about utility functions and time horizons, versus the "geometric rationality" view, on which time-average reasoning gives growth-rate maximization a privileged, largely utility-independent claim on rationality for any agent whose wealth evolves multiplicatively. Take Samuelson's objection seriously as a piece of decision theory rather than dismissing it as sour grapes, and take equally seriously how a defender of the ergodicity/geometric-rationality line would try to answer it. Do not end on a non-committal "it depends" — reach and defend an actual verdict about when, if ever, maximizing expected log wealth is the rational thing to do rather than merely one growth-optimal strategy among others, and say what should determine the answer in a given case.
