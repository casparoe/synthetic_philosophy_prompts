My older brother Danny is being sworn in as a U.S. citizen three weeks from now, and the family is throwing him a dinner the same weekend; as the one relative who writes for a living, I've been asked to give the toast. Danny has taken to describing the ceremony, half-joking, as the day he "finally counts" — the day he stops being a permanent guest and becomes, as I can't help hearing it, a full member of the demos rather than merely someone its laws fall on. I want to honor that; the process took him eleven years and he earned every bit of the relief in his voice when he talks about it. But our cousin Marisol, who came over as a small child and has no path to papers, will be sitting three feet from the podium, and nobody in this family has ever treated her as anything less than one of us. I don't want to give a toast that turns out, on inspection, to be a small ceremony of her exclusion dressed up as a celebration of his inclusion.

I've spent the past year reading political philosophy's "boundary problem" for a novel-in-progress about a family not unlike mine, so I know enough to be unsettled by it rather than comforted: Dahl's and Whelan's point that no procedure internal to democracy can fix, without circularity, who belongs to the demos that would have to do the fixing; Goodin's all-affected-interests principle, and how much further it actually reaches than any real electorate once you stop being polite about applying it; and the Abizadeh–Miller argument over whether a state has any unilateral right to draw its own boundary in the first place, with the all-subjected alternative sitting underneath most of what I want to say to Marisol without saying it to her outright — that a claim to belong doesn't wait on a claim to a ballot. None of this makes Danny's night less real. It does mean I can't let the toast imply, even by a warm accident of phrasing, that the line the law just drew for him is the same line that has ever separated who is and isn't ours.

Could you write the toast itself? Four to five minutes read aloud, warm and genuinely a little funny in the places a family toast needs to be, with no jargon and no theorist's name spoken from the podium — my aunts are in that room — but constructed so that someone who does know this literature could point to exactly which move is doing the work in each section. It needs one moment that turns to Marisol without turning her into an object lesson, it has to let Danny's night stay his without correcting him from the podium, and it should close on a line I can actually raise a glass on.
