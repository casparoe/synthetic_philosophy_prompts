I'm a PhD student in philosophy working on the history of early analytic philosophy, and I've drafted the argumentative core of a paper I can't quite decide whether to be embarrassed by. The occasion is Frege's 1924 diary, discovered among his papers and published only decades after his death, in which he records, alongside routine complaints about Weimar politics, a monarchist, anti-parliamentary, and strongly antisemitic outlook. The find unsettled a field that had come to treat Frege as a founder of a tradition many of whose early logicians were Jewish, several of them later persecuted or driven into exile by a regime whose defining antipathies, if not yet its name, the diary already voices. Michael Dummett, who did more than any other English-language philosopher to secure Frege's canonical status, has written candidly of his own dismay on first reading these pages, and of the discipline it took afterward to keep his admiration for the logic separate from any admiration for the man.

What follows is the section I'd like comments on, not the whole paper, just this move.

The standard, and I think basically correct, response is that the diary is irrelevant: the validity of a derivation in the Begriffsschrift, or the coherence of the sense/reference distinction, does not depend on the character of whoever first wrote it down, any more than a proof in Euclid depends on Euclid's temperament. To insist otherwise is to trade on a version of the genetic fallacy. This is also, I think, the hard case for anyone tempted to resist that response, precisely because it lacks the thematic continuity that makes a case like Heidegger's comparatively easy: no one has to squint to see a connection between Heidegger's suspicion of technological enframing and his politics, but no one has ever proposed that concept-script has some elective affinity with authoritarian nationalism.

And yet Frege's output was never merely a formal calculus. It came bundled with a metaphysics, most explicitly in "Der Gedanke," where he defends a "third realm" of thoughts, objective and mind-independent, neither physical nor psychological (the term he actually uses, drittes Reich, predates its later political sense by years and is surely just a coincidence, though not one I can quite stop noticing), offered in service of a lifelong polemic against psychologism and any relativizing of truth to the thinker. My tentative, probably too-cute suggestion is that a single cognitive temperament runs from that polemic to the diary: an impatience with exceptions, a demand that one fully general principle govern every case without remainder. Basic Law V has exactly this shape: it fixes the identity of extensions for every concept whatsoever, with no restriction carved out for the concepts that cause trouble, and its unrestricted generality is exactly what let Russell derive his contradiction from it. The diary's political fantasy has the same shape: a national community rationally and homogeneously ordered, with Jews, and in the same breath Catholics and social democrats, cast as the standing exception, the thing that does not fit and so must be subtracted rather than accommodated by qualifying the rule. I want to be careful here: I am not claiming either commitment entails the other, plenty of committed Platonists and anti-psychologists hold no such politics, only that the same intolerance of remainders shows up at two levels that are otherwise quite disconnected.

I genuinely cannot tell anymore whether that second paragraph is the most interesting thing in the paper or an equivocation dressed up as an argument. Could you tell me, first, whether the Basic Law V analogy does real philosophical work or just trades on a pun between formal-logical and social-political senses of "exception" and "generality"; second, whether there is a more rigorous way to state whatever is right about the intuition, if anything is; and third, whether I am being fair to the separationist position I start from, or quietly stacking the deck against it. I would also welcome any corrections to the historical details, since I am working partly from memory.
