I work on robustness and uncertainty quantification for machine learning systems, and over the past year I've gotten interested in "distributionally robust optimization," where instead of committing to a single probability distribution over some uncertain quantity, you optimize against an entire set of distributions that are all consistent with your evidence (an "ambiguity set," in the jargon). A friend of mine who's finishing a philosophy PhD pointed out over dinner that this is basically a rediscovery of an old move in epistemology called "imprecise credences," and that the lineage goes back, at least in spirit, to John Maynard Keynes's 1921 "A Treatise on Probability," written well over a decade before the economic writing that made him a household name. That sent me down a bit of a rabbit hole.

From what I can piece together, Keynes argued that probability is a logical relation between a body of evidence and a hypothesis, and that this relation isn't always representable by a single real number: sometimes you can only rank two probabilities against each other, and in some cases, he thought, they aren't even comparable at all. He also had a separate notion of the "weight" of an argument — roughly, how much relevant evidence bears on a judgment, as distinct from how probable the judgment is — which struck me as closer to the width of an ambiguity set than to the probability estimate itself. (Amusingly, Frank Knight drew almost the same risk-versus-uncertainty distinction the very same year, apparently without either of them knowing about the other.) Decades later, people like Isaac Levi and Peter Walley turned this kind of idea into real mathematics — sets of probability measures, upper and lower previsions — and decades after that, Adam Elga, in a paper called "Subjective Probabilities Should Be Sharp," argued that any agent whose credences are genuinely imprecise rather than just practically fuzzy can be walked through a sequence of individually reasonable-looking choices that leaves them predictably worse off than a sharp-credence agent would be, which he takes as a reason that rational credence has to bottom out in exact numbers after all.

I'm obviously coming at this as an outsider to philosophy, so correct me if I'm misreading any of the above, but here's what I'd like your take on: how do you think Keynes himself would have responded to an Elga-style sequential-choice argument against imprecise probability? Would he have treated modern credal-set theory as a formal vindication of his own view, or would he have thought it actually domesticates his position into something tamer than he intended, given that he allowed for outright incomparability rather than just an interval of uncertainty around a number? And would you expect him to have simply accepted some degree of dynamic inconsistency as an honest cost of taking real uncertainty seriously — something he was, after all, quite willing to emphasize elsewhere, in his remarks about how little scientific basis we have for our long-term economic expectations — rather than treat it as a decisive reason to retreat to sharp probabilities? I'd like your actual best-guess reconstruction of his position and his reasoning for it, not just a list of considerations on each side.
