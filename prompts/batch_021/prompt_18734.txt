I am reporting a feature on the replication crisis, and while interviewing two researchers for it I found myself the unwilling referee of an argument between them that I still cannot settle on my own.

(1) Naomi's lab had planned to recruit 200 participants for a study testing whether a short planning exercise reduces procrastination on essay deadlines. After 120 participants, an interim look showed p = .03; with funding running low, she and her team stopped there and wrote it up. A reviewer rejected the paper, calling this "optional stopping": peeking at the data and quitting once it looks good inflates the true false-positive rate well above the nominal 5 percent level, and Naomi should have fixed the sample size in advance or used a formal sequential design.

(2) Naomi mentioned this, expecting sympathy, to Theo, a Bayesian statistician she consults. He told her the reviewer's complaint, though standard, reflects a frequentist artifact rather than a genuine flaw: by the likelihood principle, what her 120 data points say about the hypothesis is fixed by their likelihood alone, so a Bayesian analysis of that exact data set gives the same posterior whether she had always planned to stop at 120 or only decided to on the spot. Her reason for stopping, on this view, is simply not part of the evidence.

(3) Naomi pushed back: if the stopping rule is truly irrelevant, a team willing to peek every ten participants could all but guarantee crossing whatever significance threshold it likes, sooner or later, purely by chance -- and that has to bear on whether the result deserves belief.

(4) Theo replied that she was running together two different questions: the long-run behavior of a peeking procedure across hypothetical repetitions of the whole study, and what this particular data set says. A Bayes factor tracked under continued peeking, he added, can only stray past a sufficiently stringent threshold with a correspondingly small probability, however long the peeking continues, so no one is "guaranteed" a false positive; the real mistake, if there is one, is reporting a single Bayes factor as though it settled things when you were secretly prepared to keep going had it come out otherwise.

(5) Naomi's rejoinder was that this may be true of the disciplined analysis Theo has in mind, but that almost nobody in her field actually runs it: in practice, people peek, stop, and report the number as if the sample size had been fixed from the start, so dismissing the stopping-rule objection as a "frequentist artifact" hands ordinary working scientists a rationale for exactly the sloppy practice that produced the replication crisis in the first place.

Neither of them budged, and I could see something to each position. Could you adjudicate the exchange move by move, saying who has the better of it at each numbered step rather than just declaring an overall winner, and flag anything you think both of them are missing? Please close with a one-line verdict, in the form \verdict{Naomi} or \verdict{Theo}, for whichever of them you think had the stronger case overall.
