I don't have any formal background in logic or computer science, but for the past year or so I've spent a lot of my spare time reading old MIRI and LessWrong material on AI self-trust, and there's one argument from that literature I still can't decide what to make of: the so-called "Löbian obstacle" facing self-modifying agents that want to trust their own successors. As I understand it, the underlying mathematical fact is Löb's theorem, which roughly says that a sufficiently powerful, consistent formal system can't get a general reflection principle -- "if I can prove X, then X is true" -- for free: proving that schema for a given X turns out to be possible only if the system could already prove X outright. Yudkowsky and Herreshoff used this to argue that an agent which only wants to take, or to build successors that only take, provably safe actions runs into real trouble: it can't fully certify in advance that a successor running on the very same proof system will only ever act safely, without risking exactly the kind of self-referential inconsistency Löb's theorem warns against. What I haven't been able to work out is how seriously to take this as a genuine limit on AI (or any formal reasoner's) self-trust, given that people in that same literature have proposed ways around it -- Yudkowsky and Herreshoff's own "parametric polymorphism" trick, Christiano and colleagues' probabilistic-logic approach to self-reference, and the more informal notion of "Vingean reflection," where you rely on a smarter agent's conclusions without being able to verify its reasoning step by step.

What I'd like from you is an essay that actually commits to a position on this rather than sitting on the fence. Specifically: does the Löbian obstacle expose a genuine, hard limit on how much any formal reasoner can trust a successor, or a future version of itself, that shares its own reasoning system -- a limit that the proposed fixes only paper over or weaken rather than truly remove -- or is it better understood as a problem generated specifically by insisting on deductive, proof-based certification of safety, so that moving to probabilistic reasoning or Vingean-style trust genuinely dissolves the obstacle instead of just re-describing it in a different formal language? Please set out the strongest case you can for each reading before deciding between them -- "it depends" or "both sides have a point" would be an unsatisfying place to end up; I want to know which way you actually think this leans, and why.
