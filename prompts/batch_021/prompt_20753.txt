So this kicked off in the voting-theory channel of my ACX-adjacent Discord (yes we have one of those, don't judge) after the city here announced it's moving to ranked-choice voting for the next council election, and two regulars, Gabe and Nora, got into it for like forty messages while the rest of us just watched. Trying to reconstruct the core of it: Gabe's opening claim (1) was that RCV basically fixes the strategic-voting problem you get under plurality -- you can rank your honest favorite first, even a long-shot third-party candidate, without worrying you're "spoiling" the race for the candidate you like least, since your vote just transfers down your ballot once your favorite is eliminated. Nora (2) said this oversells it: the Gibbard-Satterthwaite theorem shows literally any voting rule that isnt a dictatorship becomes manipulable once there are three or more candidates who could plausibly win, full stop, so RCV gets no special exemption -- there's always some preference profile where ranking insincerely helps somebody. Gabe (3) granted the theorem but said it's a "there exists some pathological profile" result, not a "this is common" result -- plurality's spoiler problem is trivial to exploit and shows up constantly, whereas the profiles where RCV is manipulable require detailed knowledge of everyone else's rankings that voters basically never have, so theres a real practical gap even without a theoretical one. Nora (4) came back with the 2009 Burlington, VT mayoral election as proof this isn't just hypothetical -- a real, small race where the Condorcet winner (Montroll, who'd have beaten either finalist head-to-head) got eliminated before the final round for having fewer first-choice votes than the two guys who made the runoff, and where people afterward argued the result technically failed monotonicity too. Gabe (5) said that's conflating two different defects: failing to elect the Condorcet winner, or failing monotonicity, is about how a rule aggregates sincere ballots, not proof that any actual voter or bloc had an identifiable, usable strategy to get a better result for themselves in real time. Nora (6) shot back that this is a distinction without much comfort -- a monotonicity failure means some bloc could, in principle, have done better by ranking their favorite lower or a rival higher, which just is the kind of exploitable-in-principle scenario Gibbard-Satterthwaite guarantees exists, and "nobody's found the exploit yet" is a shrinking defense in an era when campaigns already model second-choice preferences for a living.

I don't think either of them is straightforwardly wrong, but I also don't think they're actually disagreeing about the same thing anymore by the end.
