Six months ago my mother was diagnosed with a rare form of kidney cancer that her oncologist believes is connected to years of exposure to an industrial solvent used at the electronics plant where she worked from the late 1970s through the early 1990s. She and several former co-workers are now pursuing a joint claim against the company, and in the materials our lawyer has shared with the families, the epidemiologist retained as an expert witness is careful to distinguish her methodology from "mere correlation" by invoking what she calls an interventionist theory of causation, dropping names like Woodward and Pearl and talking about directed graphs and "do-calculus." Since I would rather actually follow the argument than nod along at the next family meeting, I have spent the last few weeks reading around the subject, starting with Pearl's The Book of Why and then, on the recommendation of a friend who studied philosophy, working through the relevant chapters of Woodward's Making Things Happen.

I think I have grasped the basic idea: on this view, X causes Y just in case some appropriately "surgical" intervention on X — one that changes X without otherwise disturbing the rest of the system — would change Y. What I cannot get past is what looks to me like a fatal problem for applying this framework to a case like my mother's. Nobody can travel back to 1985 and intervene on how much solvent she inhaled; that intervention is not merely difficult, dangerous, or unethical, but flatly impossible now, since the moment is decades gone and cannot be replayed under any conditions. If causation is defined in terms of what would happen under an intervention, and the intervention in question is no longer available even in principle, doesn't that mean the interventionist account simply cannot be what grounds the epidemiologist's claim that the solvent caused my mother's cancer — that whatever she is actually doing with her graphs and statistics has to be something else, dressed up in more fashionable language? And if "intervention" here only ever means something we merely imagine rather than something anyone could ever actually carry out, I am struggling to see what the whole interventionist apparatus adds over the older counterfactual theories of causation it was supposed to improve on. Before I go back to our lawyer with more questions than I started with, I would like to know whether I am missing something about how philosophers actually use the word "intervention."
