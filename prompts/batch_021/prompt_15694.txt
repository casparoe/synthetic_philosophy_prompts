"Wallace argues that hypocrisy undermines the standing to blame because it involves a failure of reciprocity between the blamer and the blamed, rather than because the hypocrite's moral judgment is false or because the hypocrite is equally guilty of the relevant fault.
(a) Reconstruct Wallace's argument for this claim. [7 points]
(b) Fritz and Miller argue that sharing the same fault as one's target ("equal guilt") is neither necessary nor sufficient for being a hypocrite. Using the case of a longtime smoker who has since quit and now urges her teenage son never to start, explain their reasoning for at least one of these two claims. [7 points]
(c) Cohen maintains that a hypocrite's condemnation of, say, terrorism can be true even though the hypocrite lacks the standing to voice it. Explain what work this truth/standing distinction is doing in his argument, and say whether Wallace's account is committed to the same distinction. [6 points]"

And here, submitted as a single continuous response, is the student's answer:

"(a) For Wallace, blaming someone is a way of holding them to a moral demand, and doing so presupposes that the demand applies equally to the blamer. A hypocrite blames another for, say, cutting corners at work while tacitly treating themselves as exempt from that very standard. This is not primarily a problem of accuracy — the blamed conduct really might be wrong — but a problem of fairness: the hypocrite unilaterally arrogates to themselves an exemption they don't extend to others, which violates the reciprocity that Wallace, following the Strawsonian picture of the reactive attitudes, thinks underwrites the whole practice of moral address. So a hypocrite can lack standing to blame even though the person being blamed is genuinely blameworthy.

(b) Fritz and Miller use cases exactly like the reformed smoker to show that equal guilt is not sufficient for hypocrisy: because she sincerely renounces her own past smoking and doesn't exempt her past self from the standard she now enforces, she isn't a hypocrite even though she once did exactly what she now condemns. This same case also shows that equal guilt is not necessary for hypocrisy, since the whole point of introducing it is to detach hypocrisy from the question of whether the blamer ever personally smoked in the first place — what matters is the self-exempting attitude, not the shared biography, and the mother's case is meant to make exactly that point vivid.

(c) Cohen's distinction lets him say that, e.g., a government official who condemns terrorism abroad while quietly sponsoring death squads at home can still be saying something true when he condemns terrorism — the trouble is with his standing to say it, not with the content of what he says. This matters because it stops us from having to choose between 'the hypocrite is right' and 'the hypocrite should be ignored.' Wallace's account is committed to the very same distinction, and for the same reason: since his objection to hypocrisy concerns the fairness of the blamer's position rather than the accuracy of the blamer's judgment, a hypocrite's lack of standing to blame someone for a given wrong lowers the extent to which that person is blameworthy for it, because on Wallace's view blameworthiness is itself partly constituted by someone else's standing to point it out. In that sense his view is a natural extension of Cohen's rather than a rival to it."
