So I've fallen down a random rabbit hole reading about classical Indian epistemology (the Nyaya school), no reason other than its a hobby of mine, and I hit a passage thats messing with my head. Im a backend dev, zero formal philosophy training, so bear with me.

Here's roughly how the Nyaya Sutra's definition of perception (pratyaksha) usually gets translated (sutra 1.1.4, if I'm reading the numbering right):

"Perception is that cognition which is produced by the contact of a sense-organ with its object, and which is not describable by words (avyapadeshya), is unerring (avyabhicari), and is of the nature of definite ascertainment (vyavasayatmaka)."

The "unerring" bit is easy enough. What's tripping me up is that "not describable by words" and "definite ascertainment" are just sitting next to each other in the same sentence like they're obviously compatible. To me those pull in opposite directions -- if a cognition is genuinely pre-verbal, not yet sorted into categories, how is it also "definite" enough to count as knowing something? And if it's already definite about what the thing is, hasn't that already smuggled in concepts, basically words?

I vaguely remember that this connects to some later split into "indeterminate" vs "determinate" perception, and that Buddhist epistemologists were fighting with the Nyaya folks over exactly this territory, but I don't have the toolkit to reconstruct the actual argument myself. Can you unpack what problem this definition is actually trying to solve, and how (if at all) later Nyaya thinkers reconciled the two halves?
