Ok so every year my family does this big Friendsgiving thing and it always ends up in some argument, and this year it was about my uncle Ray. Ray's been following this local sports handicapper (guy named Deke, sells picks over text for money) for like two years now, and at the table he said something like "I know Deke's got a real system at this point, he's beaten chance for two straight years, so if the paper runs one of those 'local tout exposed as a fraud' pieces on him next month, I already know going in it's gonna be garbage -- I'm not even gonna read it." That's what set my cousin Devon and my sister in law Claire off, and I just sat there eating pie while they went back and forth for like twenty minutes.

Devon's first move was that there's nothing wrong with what Ray said: if Ray genuinely knows Deke is reliable (and two years of beating chance is a lot), then it just follows that any future "debunking" piece has to be flawed, because a real debunking of someone who's actually reliable can't exist -- so refusing to reopen a question you already know the answer to isn't dogmatism, it's just what knowing something means. Claire said that can't be right: by that logic anyone who's overconfident about anything gets to wave off disagreement the exact same way, and plenty of overconfident people are just wrong, so "I know it, so I don't need to look" can't be a good policy in general, whether or not Ray happens to be right about Deke. Devon came back that the comparison's unfair, because this only works if you genuinely know the thing, not just feel sure of it -- someone who's merely overconfident doesn't really know their belief is true, so their case isn't parallel to Ray's, and maybe Ray really does have the real thing here given two years of data. Claire's last point was that this "real knowledge vs. just feeling sure" distinction is exactly the problem, not a way out of it -- nobody gets an internal signal telling them which one they've got, it feels the same from the inside whether you actually know or you're just deluded, so as advice for an actual person at an actual table, "once you know something you can write off future evidence in advance" tells the correct uncle and the deluded uncle to do the identical thing, meaning the distinction Devon leaned on isn't doing any real work.

I looked around online afterward (never went to college, just couldn't let this go) and apparently this is close to something called the "paradox of dogmatism," which made me feel less crazy but didn't tell me who actually had the better of it move by move.
