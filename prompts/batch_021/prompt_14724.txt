For six years before I published my first novel, I taught in a philosophy department, including an undergraduate seminar on classification in the human sciences that leaned heavily on Ian Hacking. I still think about that material more than I probably should: the novel I'm drafting now follows a clinical psychologist who starts to suspect that her own diagnostic categories are shaping the very patients she is supposed to be neutrally describing, so I've been rereading Hacking anyway for research. Partly for that reason, and partly out of old loyalty, I agreed to help a former colleague grade a backlog of take-home midterms for that same seminar while she's on medical leave this term. There's one answer I'd like a second opinion on before I submit a grade, since the distinction it turns on is one I remember finding genuinely slippery the first time I taught it myself, and I'd rather not let my own blind spots decide someone's mark.

Here is the question as it was set, worth twenty points: "Ian Hacking distinguishes 'interactive kinds,' which are subject to a 'looping effect,' from the 'indifferent kinds' of the natural sciences. Using the case of multiple personality disorder / dissociative identity disorder as it developed in North America from the late 1970s through the 1990s: (a) explain the mechanism of the looping effect in your own words, identifying at least two distinct channels by which the classification fed back onto the people classified; (b) explain why Hacking resists the conclusion that this history shows the disorder to be 'merely a social construction' or 'not real'; (c) state and evaluate one serious objection to the looping-effects framework."

And here is the student's answer: "Hacking's point is that human kinds are not like natural kinds such as gold or quarks, which he calls indifferent kinds: a rock does not change its behavior on being classified as igneous, but a person does change on being classified as, say, having multiple personality disorder. In the MPD case, the loop ran through at least two channels. First, popularized case histories like Sybil gave both patients and the public a script for what 'having multiple personalities' looked like, so people in genuine distress increasingly interpreted and presented their experience through that script. Second, clinicians who believed the disorder was radically underdiagnosed used techniques such as hypnosis and repeated, leading interviews to actively search out alters, and the resulting case reports fed back into the diagnostic literature, which is part of why the disorder went from a handful of documented cases before 1980 to thousands by the early 1990s, and why DSM-III first codified it as its own category in 1980, later relabeled dissociative identity disorder in DSM-IV in 1994. Given all this, Hacking's real conclusion is that MPD/DID was not a natural disease entity waiting to be discovered but essentially a role that suggestible patients learned to perform for their therapists, which is exactly why he counts as a social constructionist about psychiatric categories: the classification did not track an independent condition, it manufactured one. This is basically the same insight as Merton's self-fulfilling prophecy applied to psychiatry, and it also fits Scheff's labeling theory, from which Hacking borrows the general mechanism. As an objection, one could argue that even indifferent kinds are not fully static either, since biological taxonomies are constantly revised as species boundaries are redrawn; if natural-kind classification can also change over time, the interactive/indifferent line starts to look like one of degree rather than kind, and Hacking does not have a principled way to block this."

Could you grade this the way the instructor would, on the twenty-point scale, with a letter-grade equivalent, working through (a), (b), and (c) in turn? I'd like the assessment backed by a short rubric filled in as JSON — say, mechanism and channels, the realism/anti-realism point, the objection and its evaluation, and command of Hacking's terminology and history, each out of five points with a one- or two-sentence justification — followed by a few sentences of overall comments. Please be specific about which claims are correct, which are subtly off and why, rather than just conveying a general impression, since I want to be able to defend every point taken off if the student comes to office hours.
