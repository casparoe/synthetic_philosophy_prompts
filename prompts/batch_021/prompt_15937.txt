So I've been messing around building a little ranked-choice voting tool for my apartment building's HOA (don't ask) and fell down a wiki hole on Arrow's impossibility theorem. Every explainer I can find just says "this proves no voting system can ever be truly fair, wild right?" and then moves on, which bugs me cause it feels too easy. I noticed the impossibility result seems to lean really hard on the "unrestricted domain" axiom -- basically that voters can have literally any preferences whatsoever -- and that Duncan Black's median voter theorem shows that if you restrict to "single-peaked" preferences (everyone ranking options along one left-right-ish spectrum), majority rule dodges the whole result: transitive, non-dictatorial, respects Pareto, all of it. Can you make the strongest possible case that this basically defangs the popular "Arrow's theorem proves fair democracy is a myth" takes, at least for real elections, since real voters preferences are usually roughly single-peaked along some dominant ideological axis anyway? And then preempt the obvious comeback -- that real political issues aren't actually one-dimensional (economy vs social issues vs foreign policy etc) -- does that wreck the whole defense, or is there a way to save it?
