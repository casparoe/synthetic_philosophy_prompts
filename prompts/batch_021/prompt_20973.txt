Been going down a bit of a rabbit hole on Thomas Hobbes and his strange relationship with geometry. Guy thought it was basically the only real science humanity had ever nailed down, precisely because we construct the figures ourselves through motion, so we get to know their causes with total certainty. And yet he spent something like the last 25 years of his life insisting he'd squared the circle, getting publicly demolished by John Wallis in pamphlet after pamphlet, and never backed down once, dying still convinced everyone else was too dim or too jealous to see he was right.

As someone who spent forty-odd years doing actual lab science, that kind of stubbornness in the face of a clean refutation kind of fascinates me. So here's my question: Lindemann proved in 1882 that pi is transcendental, meaning squaring the circle isn't just hard, its provably impossible with compass and straightedge, end of story. If you could've shown Hobbes that proof, what do you reckon he'd have done with it? Does his own theory of geometric knowledge -- certainty through construction, everything reducible to bodies in motion -- actually give him a way to accept it, or would it more or less force him to reject or reinterpret the result? Was his stubbornness here just ego, or is it baked into his philosophy of maths itself?
