For the past month I've been reporting a feature on the small, mathematically inclined corner of AI safety research that worries less about today's chatbots misbehaving than about whether a highly capable system could ever build a successor smart enough to replace it and be justified in trusting that successor. Talking it through by phone with a researcher who works on formal approaches to this problem, I kept hearing her invoke "the Löbian obstacle," a term she traced to a 2013 MIRI paper by Eliezer Yudkowsky and Marcello Herreshoff on what they called "tiling" agents. She sketched the underlying logic for me: Löb's theorem shows that a consistent formal system cannot, for an arbitrary statement P, treat "whatever I can prove is true" as a free assumption, because if the system could prove that particular instance of the schema, it could already prove P outright, so the schema never offers a shortcut to trusting a conclusion the system hasn't independently reached. She added that this is a close cousin of Gödel's second incompleteness theorem, which already bars a consistent system from proving its own consistency, and that researchers have since found only partial ways around the obstacle: bounded or "parametric" versions of Löb's theorem, probabilistic rather than proof-based accounts of self-trust, and schemes in which an agent vouches for a successor only as being safe for some fixed number of reasoning steps rather than forever.

I followed the logic well enough, but I would like help turning it into something a general readership would find illuminating rather than merely correct. Please describe the most important implications of the Löbian obstacle to self-trusting agents. I am especially interested in what it does and does not show about a system's ability to build or certify a successor it should trust; whether the workarounds researchers have devised are principled resolutions or just disciplined ways of living with an unresolved tension; and whether the obstacle points to something more general about the limits facing any reasoner, artificial or human, an individual mind or an institution, that tries to formally underwrite the reliability of its own future judgment.
