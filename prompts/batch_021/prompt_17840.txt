I work as a software engineer on the infrastructure side of a mid-sized ML team, and "paradigm shift" gets thrown around constantly in our internal talks and Slack threads. At a recent all-hands, someone described the field's trajectory -- from hand-engineered features, to end-to-end deep learning, to today's large language models -- as "three back-to-back Kuhnian paradigm shifts," and everyone just nodded along. That comment sent me back to actually read most of Kuhn's *Structure of Scientific Revolutions* over a couple of weekends, and some secondary commentary alongside it, where I learned that Kuhn himself later split the notoriously overloaded term "paradigm" into the narrower "disciplinary matrix" and "exemplars" in the 1969 postscript. That made me even more suspicious of how loosely the word gets used at work.

So here's what I'd like your help with: suppose Kuhn were somehow shown the history of AI research from the symbolic-AI era, through the rise of connectionism, to the current scaling-driven LLM boom. Using his actual apparatus rather than the watered-down office version of "paradigm shift," how do you think he'd assess it? I'm especially interested in three things. First, whether he'd even grant that AI research had one reigning paradigm at any point before deep learning's recent dominance, or whether he'd describe the long symbolic-vs-connectionist rivalry as closer to the "pre-paradigm" state he attributes to fields like electrical research before Franklin -- competing schools sharing no common ground, rather than one paradigm being overthrown by another. Second, whether the standing disputes between symbolic-AI and deep-learning researchers look like genuine incommensurability to him: do the two camps actually fail to share standards for what counts as a solved problem or a satisfying explanation, and perhaps even mean different things by words like "representation," "learning," and "understanding" -- or is this just an unusually heated ordinary disagreement that doesn't rise to that level? Third, whether he'd classify the current scaling-and-more-data era as normal science quietly extending an already-settled connectionist paradigm, or whether he'd read the now-familiar complaints about hallucination, brittle reasoning, and lack of grounding as exactly the sort of accumulating anomalies that, on his model, tend to precede a crisis and an eventual revolution.

I'd rather you stay honest about the places where a framework built for mature natural sciences like physics and chemistry might not transfer cleanly onto a fast-moving applied field like AI, rather than forcing the analogy to fit just because the vocabulary is tempting.
