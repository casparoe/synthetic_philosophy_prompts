I'm taking a graduate seminar on modality and meaning that has spent the last few weeks working through the aftermath of Kripke's *Naming and Necessity*, and this week's readings paired Stalnaker's "Assertion" (1978) with several chapters of Chalmers on two-dimensional semantics. It left a few of us arguing in section about how much these two "two-dimensionalisms" actually have in common. As far as I can tell, Stalnaker's diagonalization is meant as a broadly pragmatic, metasemantic device — it explains phenomena like why asserting an a priori truth can still convey substantive information in a given context, without committing him to the claim that ordinary speakers grasp some world-independent "primary" content for a term like "water" in advance of learning any empirical facts. Chalmers and Jackson, by contrast, treat the primary intension as a genuine, cognitively significant component of meaning that competent speakers have a priori access to, and they lean on that stronger commitment to argue that every necessary a posteriori truth is merely superficially, rather than deeply, necessary. Our professor also mentioned, without fully explaining it, that Stalnaker is willing to tolerate so-called "strong necessities" in a way Chalmers's picture cannot afford to — and that this apparently matters for whether the whole apparatus can still do the anti-Kripkean work Chalmers wants from it.

For a short paper, I need to compare these two versions of two-dimensionalism directly instead of treating "2-D semantics" as one unified framework, which is exactly the mistake my professor keeps flagging in our drafts. Could you lay out precisely where Stalnaker's metasemantic two-dimensionalism and Chalmers's (and Jackson's) epistemic two-dimensionalism actually diverge, especially on whether primary intensions are psychologically real objects of a priori grasp or merely a bookkeeping device for context-dependence? I'd also like you to explain the "strong necessities" point clearly enough that I actually understand why it matters, and then assess which conception is more defensible as an account of natural-language meaning — including whether Stalnaker's more modest picture can still supply what Chalmers needs for his anti-materialist uses of the framework, such as the claim that the conceivability of zombies remains metaphysically significant despite Kripke's necessary a posteriori truths. Please reach an actual verdict and justify it, rather than presenting the two views as equally reasonable alternatives.
