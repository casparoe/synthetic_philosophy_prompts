Hey - I've been noodling on an idea for a short paper on accuracy-first epistemology and wanted a gut check before I sink more time into it. Here's the rough shape of the argument:

Part of what's supposed to make accuracy-first epistemology exciting is that it derives the core Bayesian norms - having coherent credences, and updating them by conditionalization - from a single currency, closeness to truth, instead of a pile of separately motivated axioms. And Joyce's classic argument for probabilism really does deliver something strong: it's a pure dominance argument. Whatever credence function you happen to have, if its incoherent, there's a coherent rival that beats it on accuracy in EVERY possible world, no matter which one turns out to be actual. You don't have to already trust any particular probability assignment for that argument to have bite.

But the classic argument for conditionalization (Greaves and Wallace's) isn't like that at all - it's an expected accuracy argument. Conditionalizing is shown to maximize expected accuracy relative to your own prior. That's a much thinner kind of vindication, because it only tells you conditionalizing is best by your own lights, so it can't get any grip on someone who doesn't already trust the prior thats doing the expecting. Which means the two "halves" of Bayesianism that accuracy-first epistemology is supposed to jointly ground actually rest on justifications of pretty different strength - one a no-matter-what dominance result, the other a merely-by-your-own-lights expected utility result. And I don't think this gets fixed by the newer attempts at a genuine dominance argument for conditionalization (Briggs and Pettigrew, if I'm remembering the name right) - my sense is those only go through given extra assumptions about scoring whole updating plans rather than individual credal states at a time, which smells like it's smuggling in something expectation-like through the back door.

Is this an actual crack in the unification story, or am I missing how it's already been handled? And is it even novel enough to be worth writing up on its own, rather than a footnote to something people already know? Would love comments, especially on whether the reasoning holds up and what I should be reading if this ground's already been covered.
