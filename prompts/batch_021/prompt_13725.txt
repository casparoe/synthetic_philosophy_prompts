I am deep into a novel whose protagonist is a professional painting conservator, and I have run into a scene I do not want to write until I understand the philosophy underneath it properly. She is cleaning a small seventeenth-century still life whose attribution has been quietly disputed for years, and partway through the work she becomes convinced that a second, painted-over face is emerging beneath the surface: a pentimento that an earlier infrared reflectography scan was too ambiguous either to confirm or to rule out. I want it to be a genuinely open question for the reader whether she is actually seeing this face — perceiving something a less trained or less invested eye simply could not — or whether her professional stake in proving the painting a forgery (a hidden face beneath it would settle the matter) is merely shaping what she believes and reports, while her visual experience remains exactly what anyone looking at that canvas would have. A friend of mine who works in vision science tells me this is essentially a live, unresolved dispute in philosophy of mind, filed under the "cognitive penetrability" of perception, and I would rather get the philosophy right than fall back on the vague "perception is subjective" gesture that so much fiction settles for.

What I would like from you is an essay, not a survey — something that actually stakes out and defends a position rather than presenting both sides and shrugging. Please take seriously the case that perception is informationally encapsulated from belief and desire, in the spirit of Fodor's modularity thesis and Pylyshyn's arguments for the impenetrability of "early" vision, and weigh it properly against the case, associated with philosophers such as Susanna Siegel, that at least some high-level contents — natural-kind properties, causal structure, the fruits of recognitional training — can be woven directly into experience by what the perceiver believes, fears, or has learned to expect. I would also like you to engage with the more deflationary rejoinder, of the kind pressed by psychologists like Firestone and Scholl, that most purported demonstrations of "top-down" effects on perception really only show effects on attention, memory, or after-the-fact judgment, leaving perceptual content itself untouched; and with the further complication that even a durable, expertise-driven change of the sort my conservator has undergone over a career might be better described as perceptual learning that retunes an encapsulated system, rather than as penetration of it in the moment.

Please do not hedge your way down to "reasonable people disagree." Tell me which view you actually think is right, and say so plainly enough that I can use the answer: specifically, whether it would be intellectually honest for me to write my conservator as truly seeing a face that an untrained eye could never have picked out, or whether the honest version of the scene has her seeing precisely what anyone would see and merely believing, wrongly or rightly, something more.
