Karl Popper notoriously argued, in several of his writings from the 1970s including *Objective Knowledge* and his intellectual autobiography *Unended Quest*, that the Darwinian theory of natural selection is not a strictly testable scientific theory but rather a "metaphysical research programme." His worry was that because fitness is standardly defined in terms of differential survival and reproduction, the claim that the fittest survive threatens to collapse into the empty tautology that those who survive, survive. The charge was widely read as an attack on evolutionary biology's status as a genuine science and drew sustained criticism from biologists and philosophers of biology alike. In 1978, Popper published a paper in which he stated that he had changed his mind about the theory's testability and wished to recant his earlier position — yet commentators remain divided on what exactly this recantation amounted to. Some treat it as a substantial and largely unqualified reversal; others argue that the concession was considerably narrower than it looked, limited mainly to acknowledging that particular, low-level evolutionary hypotheses (such as predictions about how a specific trait should spread under a specified selection pressure) are testable, while Popper continued elsewhere to treat the overarching Darwinian schema as a metaphysical framework that organizes research rather than a falsifiable theory in its own right.

This disagreement about how to read the recantation came up recently in a philosophy-of-science reading group I take part in, and I would like a carefully argued outside opinion on it. Please write an essay that takes a position on two connected questions: first, whether Popper's original tautology-based objection was ever a fair criticism of natural selection as biologists actually formulate and use the concept of fitness, rather than of a loose slogan like "survival of the fittest"; and second, whether Popper's 1978 statement is best read as a genuine, substantial recantation or as a much more limited concession that leaves the core of his original worry intact. Weigh the strongest considerations on each side — you may want to draw on how modern evolutionary biology's more quantitative and predictive successes (for instance, in population genetics or in directly observed cases of selection in the wild) bear on the tautology charge — but the essay should not end on the fence: state clearly which reading of the recantation you find correct and why, and give an overall verdict on whether Popper's worry about the testability of natural selection retains any force today.
