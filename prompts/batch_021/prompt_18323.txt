So in my philosophy of infinity seminar this week we covered Thomson's lamp, and what's stuck with me is that Thomson himself didn't just mean it as a fun puzzle -- he actually thought it proves that completing an infinite sequence of tasks in finite time is flat out logically impossible, since there's no coherent answer to whether the lamp ends up on or off. But apparently Benacerraf wrote a whole reply saying that's too quick, that the rules of the scenario just never fix what state the lamp is in at the limit moment, so its not a contradiction, more like an underdetermined case, kind of like how a function can just fail to have a value at a point even though it's well-behaved everywhere around it. A friend in the class thinks Benacerraf basically wins by default, but that feels too fast to me, and our professor only gestured at the back-and-forth without walking us through the actual moves, so I don't fully see why the indeterminacy response is supposed to work rather than just being a dodge.

Could you write me a dialogue between two philosophers who each hold one of those positions -- one arguing the lamp shows supertasks are logically impossible, the other arguing it's logically fine and the "paradox" is just us demanding an answer the setup was never rich enough to give? I want real engagement rather than both of them just restating their opener: have the impossibility side press the indeterminacy side on why we should be happy with a perfectly well-posed yes/no question ("is the lamp on right now") getting no determinate answer at all, and have the indeterminacy side hit back with something like the Grandi's series point -- that 1-1+1-1+... has partial sums flipping between 1 and 0 forever with no ordinary limit, which is structurally the same situation as the switch -- and maybe bring in the Ross-Littlewood balls-in-a-vase case, where the "obviously correct" naive answer turns out to depend entirely on details you wouldn't have thought mattered. If it fits naturally, let one of them flag that even granting logical coherence, actually building the lamp is a separate physical question (infinite energy, switching speed approaching a limit, etc) so that doesn't get mixed up with the logical dispute. Keep it fairly tight, a couple pages, and don't let either one just concede at the end -- I'd rather they finish having sharpened where the real disagreement sits than have a tidy winner.
