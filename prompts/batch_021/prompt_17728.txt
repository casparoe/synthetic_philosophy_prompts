I've been asked to give a toast at the wedding of two friends from graduate school, and I want to do the material justice rather than just wave at it for a laugh. The bride is a social psychologist who works in the situationist tradition: her research program leans heavily on findings like Milgram's obedience studies and the Good Samaritan experiment, in which seminary students in a hurry walked past a man slumped and groaning in a doorway, suggesting that most of what we call "character" is really just behavior tracking incidental features of a situation. The groom is a philosopher who works on virtue ethics and has spent his career arguing, against her and people like her, that there are genuinely stable character traits that explain why some people keep their footing when a situation turns ugly and others don't. As far as I can tell, they started dating after an argument at a conference that neither of them has ever really conceded, and the bit has become one of the more charming fixtures of their relationship. I did my doctorate alongside both of them in an unrelated field and seem to have been chosen to give this toast precisely because I'm neutral territory.

I'd like the toast to be built around that ongoing argument rather than treating it as a bit to get through before the sincere part starts. In particular, I think there's something real to be said about how a wedding vow is itself a strange move in their debate: a promise to be loving and steady across whatever situations marriage produces sounds like a bet on stable character, except that getting married is also, transparently, a way of choosing your future situations -- who you live with, who is watching, what your ordinary days look like -- rather than trusting character to hold up unassisted. I'd like the speech to find something true and moving in that tension instead of just declaring one side the winner. Roughly half the room will be psychologists and philosophers who will notice immediately if the situationist critique or the virtue-ethics responses to it get flattened into a cliché; the other half is family who have listened to this couple bicker affectionately for years without ever quite following what it's about. Could you write me something in the four-to-five-minute range that works for both halves of the room?
