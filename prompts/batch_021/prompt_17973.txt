As a software engineer, I've been reading about Bertrand Russell's theory of descriptions, having realized that it's essentially an early formal treatment of what happens when a referring expression fails to pick out anything -- something that felt oddly familiar from handling null references in code. I follow the basic analysis: "the F is G" unpacks into an existence claim, a uniqueness claim, and a predication, so that "the present King of France is bald" comes out false rather than meaningless, which is central to how Russell defends the law of excluded middle for such sentences. Where I get stuck is the further move he makes to handle negation, as in "the present King of France is not bald" -- his distinction between the "primary" and "secondary" occurrence of the description within the sentence. Could you explain what separates these two readings, why Russell holds that ordinary language is genuinely ambiguous between them, and why the choice between the two changes whether the sentence comes out true or false?
