I'm working on a feature about the philosophical foundations of money, and I'd like to dramatize a genuine scholarly disagreement through a concrete case rather than competing abstractions. Write a dialogue between two monetary theorists debating the meaning of the giant stone disks, known as "rai" or "fei," once used as money on the Micronesian island of Yap. One is an economist in the Mengerian tradition, who holds that money is at root a commodity that emerges spontaneously out of barter as traders converge on whichever good is most saleable, requiring no state or communal sanction to function as money. The other is an economic anthropologist in the credit-theory/chartalist tradition associated with Innes and Graeber, who holds that money was never really a traded commodity at all, but a socially instituted ledger for tracking debts and obligations. Have them argue specifically over a case retold by Milton Friedman: a Yap family whose especially fine fei stone sank while being transported and came to rest, unseen by anyone, on the sea floor — yet whose value, and the family's ownership of it, no islander ever thought to dispute. Each should say what the episode really proves about the nature of money, and challenge the other's reading of it directly rather than simply restating their own theory.
