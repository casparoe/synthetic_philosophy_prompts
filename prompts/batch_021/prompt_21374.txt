I'm outlining a historical novel set in London during the closing years of the 1770s. It centres on a young Scottish clerk, Alan, newly arrived at the counting-house of his uncle Hector, a 'nabob' who made his fortune in the East India Company's Bengal trade and holds cynical, mercenary views on the American war then underway. I want a set-piece dinner scene in which Alan, having lately worked his way through Adam Smith's Wealth of Nations, invokes Smith to defend the imperial project against his uncle, while Hector dismisses the argument as fashionable economics dressed up as principle.

The trouble is that a friend from my writing group, who studied economic history, warned me I might be flattening Smith into a caricature, and I would rather settle the question before I draft the scene than after. My working assumption has been that Smith, as the great theorist of commercial society and the gains from specialisation and exchange, would hand Alan a ready-made brief for empire: colonies, on this reading, are simply free trade and the division of labour extended across oceans, so a good Smithian ought to see the British Empire as a natural, if imperfect, expression of his own economic principles. Before I put a version of that speech in Alan's mouth, I would welcome help checking it against what Smith actually argues about colonies, chartered monopolies like the East India Company, and the case of the American colonies in the Wealth of Nations. Would a well-read young man of 1778 who took Smith seriously really come away primed to cheer for empire, or am I about to give my character an argument that Smith himself would have made short work of?
