A few weeks ago, during a team meeting, a colleague of mine was blamed for a mistake that had actually resulted from a decision made well above her pay grade, and I said nothing, even though I have always thought of myself as someone who speaks up when something is unfair. The incident has stayed with me, and it sent me back to some old notes on moral character, which is how I found myself comparing two bodies of reading I had always kept in separate mental drawers. Years ago, in a religious studies module, I learned Joseph Fletcher's situation ethics, where he sets out his own position, which he explicitly labels "situationism," against legalism on one side and antinomianism on the other, holding that the right act is whichever one love requires given the concrete particulars of the situation rather than a rule fixed in advance. More recently, prompted by my own bout of self-examination, I have been reading about the "situationist critique" of virtue ethics associated with Gilbert Harman and John Doris, which draws on experiments like Milgram's obedience studies and the "Good Samaritan" experiment with seminary students to argue that traits like honesty and courage are far less stable across situations than Aristotelian virtue ethics assumes.

The parallel struck me as too neat to be a coincidence: in both cases, a general, fixed framework for evaluating conduct (moral rules in one case, stable character traits in the other) is displaced in favor of attending closely to the particulars, and Fletcher was already using the word "situationism" for his own view well before Harman and Doris started describing their position as "situationist." I have more or less concluded that the social-psychological critique of virtue ethics is really just Fletcher's situationism transplanted from rule-based Christian ethics onto trait-based Aristotelian ethics, and that reading Fletcher closely would give me the conceptual foundations I need before tackling Harman and Doris properly. Before I commit to that reading plan, I would value your view on whether that lineage is actually right, or whether I am projecting a connection between two traditions that do not, in fact, bear on each other in the way I am assuming.
