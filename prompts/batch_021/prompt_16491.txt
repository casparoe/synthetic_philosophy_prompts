The Special Composition Question, first posed by van Inwagen in Material Beings, asks: given some objects (the xs), under what conditions do they compose something further (a y)? There are three broad kinds of answer. Nihilism says the xs never compose anything, so strictly speaking there are no tables, rocks or chairs, only mereological simples arranged in various ways. Universalism (defended by David Lewis) says any xs whatsoever compose something, no matter how scattered or unrelated, so there's a single object made of my left shoe and the Eiffel Tower. Van Inwagen rejects both of these and instead defends Organicism: the xs compose something if and only if their activity constitutes a life. So on his view there are no tables or chairs, just simples arranged table-wise, but there are organisms, because a life is a self-sustaining, well-individuated event that some simples can jointly participate in.

Lewis's argument for universalism is that any restricted answer to the SCQ has to draw a line somewhere between cases where composition happens and cases where it doesn't, and that line will always be vague — you can imagine slowly pushing two objects together, or slowly assembling a life out of non-living matter, and there won't be one exact instant where composition suddenly kicks in. Since it can never be a vague matter whether or not something exists, Lewis concludes composition must be unrestricted: it always happens, which proves universalism.

Van Inwagen is aware that this threatens his own view just as much as any other restricted answer, since 'constituting a life' looks just as vague as any other candidate boundary — it's not obvious exactly when a fertilized egg becomes an organism, for example. His response is to argue that 'life' is actually a much sharper, better defined concept than critics assume, because biology gives us a precise, non-vague criterion for when a self-sustained metabolic event is occurring, so there's no real borderline area for his view to worry about. Because of this, Organicism escapes the argument from vagueness in a way that other restricted answers (like 'composition happens whenever things are touching') cannot, which is why van Inwagen thinks his answer beats out both nihilism and universalism.
