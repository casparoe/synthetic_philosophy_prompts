So this happened after my phil-of-mind seminar last week and I still havent decided who won. We'd just covered higher-order theories of consciousness -- the Rosenthal-style view where a state is conscious just in case there's a suitable higher-order thought (HOT) representing you as being in it -- and two of my students, Nina and Theo, kept going at it in the hallway afterward.

Theo's opening move: if a HOT can misfire -- represent you as being in pain when there's no first-order pain state there at all -- then by the theory's own lights you should end up consciously feeling pain anyway, since its supposed to just be the content of the HOT that fixes the conscious character. But there's nothing there to actually hurt. So the theory doesn't explain phenomenal consciousness, it conjures it from nothing and calls that an explanation.

Nina said that's not a flaw, it's basically predicted: confabulation and things like Anton's syndrome already show our sense of what we're experiencing can come completely unmoored from what's actually happening first-order. The theory gets that right, it doesn't stumble into it.

Theo replied that those are cases of false belief about perceiving (you believe, wrongly, that you can see), not cases of an actual felt quality with nothing behind it -- Nina's move quietly treats "seeming to have an experience" and "having one" as the same thing, which is exactly what's in dispute.

Nina shot back that this is Theo smuggling in the idea that felt quality is some extra ingredient over and above any representational fact, and that's precisely the assumption a reductive theory like this one is entitled to reject -- he can't just assert it as a counterexample.

Then Theo went for the flip side: the theory also says a first-order state can be as intense as you like -- pain, fear, whatever -- and still not be conscious at all, not even dimly, if no HOT happens to target it. That seems to badly understate what sheer intensity can do on its own.

Nina bit that bullet too, pointing to blindsight and inattentional cases as proof that a state's sheer intensity or richness isn't enough by itself -- consciousness tracks the HOT, not the strength of the underlying signal.

I go back and forth on this one and don't think either of them clearly lost. Could you go through the exchange move by move and say who's got the better of it at each step -- not just an overall winner -- and flag anything you think both of them are still missing?
