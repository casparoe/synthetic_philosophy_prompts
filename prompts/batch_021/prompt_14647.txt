My aunt is retiring after thirty-nine years running the small member-owned credit union she started out of the back of our family's old hardware store. Whenever anyone asked why she refused to add the high-interest "quick cash" products every other lender in town eventually carried, she would just say, "money doesn't have babies." It was only a few years ago, working through Aristotle's Politics and Nicomachean Ethics on my own, that I realized she had essentially reinvented his argument against usury -- that lending at interest is the most unnatural way of getting wealth, since money is only ever a convention for measuring people's needs against one another, not something that can breed like livestock -- and that she had quietly run the whole place on his older distinction between managing resources toward genuine need and the separate, limitless business of making money simply to make more of it. I have been asked to give the toast at her retirement dinner: about half the room will be credit union colleagues and regulators, including one regional director with an economics degree who will almost certainly catch what I am doing, and the other half will be members she once gave car loans to and relatives who have never read a word of philosophy in their lives. Please write a five-minute toast built around her "money doesn't have babies" line that both halves of the room can enjoy -- warm and genuinely funny rather than a lecture -- and that never once praises her in the terms she spent her career refusing, such as growth or returns, since the whole point of how she ran things was that money was never supposed to be the point.
