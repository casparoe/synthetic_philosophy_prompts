I sit on the internal multi-agent safety review committee at the AI lab where I work, and I need to circulate a decision memo before we meet on Friday to vote on a proposal from our automated-negotiation team. For a couple of years I've followed the open-source game theory writing that circulates on LessWrong and out of places like MIRI and the Center on Long-Term Risk mostly as an intellectual curiosity: Tennenholtz's original program equilibrium result and its folk theorem -- once players submit programs that can read each other's code, rather than simply choosing actions, almost any individually rational payoff profile can be sustained as an equilibrium via strategies like "cooperate if your code matches mine, otherwise defect" -- and the later "robust cooperation" work built on provability logic, where FairBot and PrudentBot use Löb's theorem to escape a self-referential loop that naive mutual simulation can't resolve, partly in order to answer the complaint that plain code-matching equilibria are absurdly fragile, collapsing the moment the opponent's program differs by so much as a renamed variable. It's strange to now be asked to evaluate a real version of this instead of a thought experiment.

The proposal itself: for a trial quarter, our contracting agent would submit its literal source code to a shared verifier before each negotiation over supply contracts with a partner firm's agent, and condition its opening offer and concession schedule on what the verifier can establish about the counterparty's program, rather than on revealed bids alone. Sandbox results look good -- close to the full cooperative surplus, exactly as the folk theorem would predict. But one committee member, who is in fact the person who first got me reading this literature, is pushing hard on the objection that the whole scheme rests on an idealization the theory quietly assumes away: a real verifier cannot fully simulate an arbitrary submitted program without risking non-termination, so in practice it has to fall back on bounded proof search or static checks, and a sufficiently motivated counterparty could ship a policy that looks cooperative to that bounded check while carrying a defection clause the check never reaches. She wants us to reject the pilot outright and keep our current sealed-bid protocol indefinitely.

Please draft the committee's decision memo. I need one clear recommendation -- approve, approve with specific conditions and a concrete rollout-and-review timeline, or reject -- and the reasoning needs to actually engage with the verification objection on its merits rather than dispatching it with something like "we'll add more testing." If you recommend approval, say specifically what safeguards would have to be in place and by when; if you recommend rejection, say what, if anything, would change the committee's mind later.
