In a reading group on the philosophy of spacetime, we have been working through Earman and Norton's 1987 reconstruction of Einstein's "hole argument," and before I write up my notes for next week's session I want to make sure I have drawn the right moral from it. As I understand the argument, if one is a manifold substantivalist -- someone who holds that spacetime points are individuals with their own identity, over and above the metric and matter fields defined on them -- then a diffeomorphism that acts as the identity everywhere outside some matter-free region (the "hole") but differs smoothly inside it generates a second solution to Einstein's field equations. This second solution agrees with the first on every bit of data outside the hole and disagrees inside it. Since nothing outside the hole can tell you which of the two futures you are actually in, this seems to show that general relativity itself is a physically indeterministic theory, in roughly the same sense that quantum mechanics is indeterministic prior to measurement.

If that is the right way to put it, though, I am puzzled by how untroubled working relativists seem to be by it. Numerical relativists simulating binary black hole mergers, for instance, talk as though the Einstein equations fix a unique future evolution from Cauchy data on a spacelike slice, and nobody treats this as requiring some further completion of GR by hidden variables, in the way indeterminism in quantum mechanics motivated hidden-variable programs. So which is it: is general relativity a genuinely indeterministic physical theory that practitioners are simply choosing to ignore for practical purposes, or have I misunderstood what the hole argument actually establishes?
