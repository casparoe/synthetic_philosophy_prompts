I've belonged for a few years to a small, LessWrong-adjacent calibration-and-forecasting group that meets monthly to run Fermi estimates and afterward score everyone's confidence intervals against reality. At the last meeting, one member, Leah, spent a while working through a multi-step estimate (something like the number of working beekeepers in our metro area), checked her arithmetic twice, and gave an 80% confidence interval she felt good about. Another member, who logs everyone's long-run calibration, then pointed out — accurately — that on this specific category of multi-step demographic estimate, Leah's stated 80% intervals have contained the true value only about 45% of the time over two years of recorded results, even though she is close to perfectly calibrated on every other category the group tracks. Nothing about how she worked through the problem that night felt any different to her than usual. What happened next turned into a real argument between two other regulars, Owen and Ines, and since I think both of them are onto something, I'd like an outside adjudication.

Owen said Leah is obligated to widen her interval substantially, regardless of how sound the derivation looks to her on reflection, because the track record is itself strong evidence that something about her reasoning on this category is quietly broken, even though she has no introspective access to what it is. Ines objected that if Leah reruns every step of that night's derivation and still can't locate an error, deferring to the base rate just produces the very state that's supposed to be irrational: believing the estimate on its merits while simultaneously believing she shouldn't — textbook epistemic akrasia, and Owen's advice walks her straight into it. Owen replied that avoiding that state doesn't require ignoring the track record; it requires that Leah's actual, occurrent confidence shift to match the data, so that there is no mismatch left to be akratic about — if her gut still insists on the old number afterward, the gut is what's irrational, not the deference. Ines came back that this cannot be the general policy, since then anyone could talk anyone out of any belief just by citing an unflattering statistic about "people like you, on problems like this," and that Owen's view gives no principled way to separate manipulative higher-order evidence from the real thing. Owen's closing point was that manipulative cases are exactly the ones that don't survive scrutiny of the purported track record itself, and that two years of logged, scored predictions is not that.

Could you go through this move by move rather than handing down an overall winner — where exactly is Owen right or wrong, where is Ines right or wrong, and does Owen's final distinction actually get him out of the exploitability problem Ines raised, or does it just relocate the same question, namely how Leah is supposed to tell, in the moment, a rigorous track record from one that merely presents itself as rigorous? Please also flag anything important you think both of them are missing, including whether Leah might simply be stuck in unavoidable epistemic akrasia no matter which way she goes.
