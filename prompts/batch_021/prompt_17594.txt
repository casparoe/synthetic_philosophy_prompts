Question: Explain how Frederick Douglass's view of the Constitution's relationship to slavery changed between the early 1840s and the early 1850s. Be sure to address (a) what Garrisonian abolitionists thought was wrong with the document and which clauses they pointed to, (b) what pushed Douglass to break from that position, and (c) how Douglass's later view differed from Garrison's.

Student answer: Up through the 1840s, Douglass was a loyal follower of William Lloyd Garrison and the American Anti-Slavery Society, and he shared their view that the Constitution was rotten at its core -- in fact its Douglass who coined the phrase "a covenant with death, and an agreement with hell" for it, pointing to three clauses in particular: the three-fifths clause, which counted enslaved people as three-fifths of a person specifically so as to cap how much power slave states could hold in Congress; the slave-trade clause, which shielded the Atlantic slave trade from federal interference until 1808; and the fugitive-slave clause, which required escaped slaves to be returned to their owners. Garrison's answer to all this was to publicly burn copies of the Constitution and call for the North to secede rather than stay yoked to slaveholders -- "no union with slaveholders."

Douglass broke from this position around 1851, largely because of his friendship with Gerrit Smith and his reading of Lysander Spooner's argument that slavery was actually unconstitutional. From then on he insisted the Constitution should be read the way any legal text is read, by its actual language -- and since the words "slave" and "slavery" appear nowhere in it, it can't be said to sanction slavery at all; any pro-slavery reading has to be smuggled in from outside the document. This is the view he defends in "What to the Slave Is the Fourth of July?" (1852), where despite his fury at the nation's hypocrisy he still calls the Constitution, read in light of its purposes, a glorious liberty document. So where Garrison treated the document's silence on slavery as a fig leaf over a pro-slavery deal cut by the framers, Douglass came to treat that same silence as proof that slavery was a betrayal of the founding law rather then something built into it.
