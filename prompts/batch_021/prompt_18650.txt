I'm a PhD student in philosophy of science, currently drafting a dissertation chapter on scientific realism, and I've run into a technical knot I can't seem to argue my way out of. Popper introduced the notion of verisimilitude (truth-likeness) in *Conjectures and Refutations* because he needed some way of saying that Einstein's theories, though presumably false in the strict sense, come closer to the truth than Newton's — otherwise falsificationism makes every episode of theory-change look like the replacement of one falsehood by another, with no real cognitive progress involved. His formal proposal defines a theory's truth-content and falsity-content as the true and false members, respectively, of its class of logical consequences, and says that one theory is more verisimilar than another when its truth-content is a superset of the other's and its falsity-content is a subset (or vice versa, for the opposite ranking). What stopped me cold is the 1974 result, arrived at independently by Tichý and Miller, showing that on Popper's own definition a false theory can never come out more verisimilar than another false theory: the ordering only ever delivers a verdict when one of the two theories being compared is actually true, which is exactly the case his whole apparatus was supposed to be unnecessary for. That looks to me less like a technical hiccup than a proof that the qualitative project, as Popper conceived it, cannot do the job it was built to do.

Naturally, that hasn't stopped people from trying to rebuild the concept on different foundations. Niiniluoto's and Oddie's similarity-based accounts, for instance, replace Popper's content-inclusion ordering with some measure of resemblance between a theory's models and the way the world actually is. But these seem to trade one problem for another I find just as uncomfortable: the rankings they produce depend on essentially arbitrary choices about which properties count as "basic" for measuring similarity, so that redescribing the same facts in a different vocabulary can reverse a verisimilitude ordering. My advisor's response is that this is all reason enough to retire the concept and let realists ground talk of scientific progress in something more modest, like Laudan-style problem-solving effectiveness or bare predictive success. That feels to me like giving up on something close to the heart of scientific realism — the idea that later theories are approximately true in some sense that outruns mere track record.

Could you write me an essay that actually takes a side on this? I would like it to explain why Popper's original definition fails in the way Tichý and Miller showed, assess whether the similarity-based successors genuinely put verisimilitude back on its feet or merely relocate the same difficulties one level up, and then commit to a real verdict — either that some workable, non-arbitrary notion of truthlikeness is available after all, or that the realist has to give up on cashing out "approximate truth" in formal terms and find some other way to explain scientific progress. I would much rather have an essay that argues for one of those two positions and defends it than one that lists considerations on both sides and shrugs.
