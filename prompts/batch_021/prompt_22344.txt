I'm taking a seminar this semester on the history of political and economic thought, and this week's session is devoted to Book IV of Adam Smith's Wealth of Nations, specifically the long chapter "Of Colonies." Our professor asked us to work through the section, late in Part III of that chapter, where Smith entertains a proposal for uniting Britain and the American colonies under a single Parliament, with colonial representation apportioned according to how much the colonies contribute in tax revenue, rather than continuing the existing arrangement of monopoly and taxation without representation. I've gotten stuck on the paragraph where he spells out what that proposal would eventually lead to, and I'd appreciate some help working through it. The passage reads:

"The people on the other side of the water are afraid lest their distance from the seat of government might expose them to many oppressions. But their representatives in Parliament, of which the number ought from the first to be considerable, would easily be able to protect them from all oppression. The distance could not much weaken the dependency of the representative upon the constituent, and the former would still feel that he owed his seat in Parliament, and all the consequences which he derived from it, to the good will of the latter. It would be the interest of the former, therefore, to cultivate that good will by complaining, with all the authority of a member of the legislature, of every outrage which any civil or military officer might be guilty of in those remote parts of the empire. The distance of America from the seat of government, besides, the natives of that country might flatter themselves, with some appearance of reason too, would not be of very long continuance. Such has hitherto been the rapid progress of that country in wealth, population, and improvement, that in the course of little more than a century, perhaps, the produce of American might exceed that of British taxation. The seat of the empire would then naturally remove itself to that part of the empire which contributed most to the general defence and support of the whole."

Two things about this passage are giving me trouble. First, purely as a matter of grammar, I can't get the fifth sentence to parse: "the natives of that country might flatter themselves, with some appearance of reason too" seems to be some kind of inserted clause, but I'd like to see the main clause set out plainly, with its subject and verb identified, once that insertion is set aside; I'm also not sure whether "the produce of American" in the next sentence is just shorthand for "American taxation" (mentioned a few sentences earlier in the same part of the chapter) or whether something has dropped out of the text. Second, and this is what I actually want to write about in my response paper, I'm unsure how seriously to take the conclusion itself. A few pages earlier in the same chapter, describing the existing arrangement rather than his own proposed union, Smith says that Britain's claim to empire in America "has hitherto existed in imagination only," calls the whole thing a "golden dream" that the country's rulers should either "realise" or "awake from," and recommends simply abandoning the colonies if they cannot be made to pay their own way. Given that surrounding skepticism, is the endpoint of the representation scheme here – the seat of empire migrating across the Atlantic within a century – a genuine prediction Smith wants his readers to take seriously and plan around, or is it functioning more like a reductio, a deliberately unappealing consequence meant to make his real recommendation (give up the colonies) look sensible by comparison? A couple of classmates and I went back and forth on this after class without settling it, so I'd value a close reading of the passage itself rather than a general summary of Smith's views on colonial policy.
