Two friends of mine, both regulars at a small reading group on animal ethics that I run, got into a real argument after watching a nature-documentary scene in which a lion pride kills a wildebeest calf. I don't have a settled view of my own on this and would like an outside adjudication of how the exchange actually went, move by move, rather than just who "won."

(1) Nadia said the scene was a reminder of how much suffering nature ordinarily contains -- starvation, disease, parasitism, and predation dwarf, in sheer scale, anything that humans inflict on animals. She said that if we could ever intervene safely and cheaply to reduce this suffering, we would be obligated to, on the same grounds that obligate us to pull a drowning child from a pond at little cost to ourselves.

(2) Marcus replied that this treats suffering as the only currency that matters, and that it misses what's valuable about wild systems as such: a landscape of contracepted, vaccinated, predator-suppressed animals would be a garden, not a wilderness. He added that the intervention might not even lower suffering by Nadia's own lights, since wherever predation is suppressed, populations tend to be thinned instead by starvation or disease -- often more slowly, and by many measures more painfully.

(3) Nadia granted the empirical worry but said it only counsels caution about clumsy interventions, not the deeper claim. Suppose some future intervention really did lower total suffering with no such rebound: Marcus's objection would still apply, which shows that his real objection isn't about suffering at all -- it's about protecting "the wild" as a further value that can override the significance of how an actual, sentient calf experiences its own death. Treating a collective that cannot itself suffer as though it outweighs the suffering of its members, she said, is exactly what gives this kind of holism a bad name.

(4) Marcus replied that he isn't ranking the ecosystem's good above the calf's suffering so much as denying that the suffering can be neatly separated from the relations -- hunting, being hunted, competing, going hungry -- that made the calf the kind of animal it was. Remove those relations, he said, and you haven't simply spared it a bad death; you've changed what it would have meant to be that animal at all. He also noted that Nadia's "no rebound effects" clause is doing enormous work for a scenario nobody has actually specified.

Please go through the exchange move by move and say who has the better of it at each step, and note anything you think both of them are overlooking. End your reply with a verdict on its own line, in exactly this format: \answer{Nadia} or \answer{Marcus}, whichever of them you think is more right overall.
