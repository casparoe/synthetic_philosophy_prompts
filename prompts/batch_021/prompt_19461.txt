I've spent years following the more statistics-minded end of the rationalist blogosphere (Andrew Gelman's blog, the Ioannidis-style meta-science literature, old Slate Star Codex posts on power and publication bias), and I now TA a research-methods course for psychology graduate students. I'd like a second, careful opinion on how to grade one student's response before I enter final marks — I want the grading to hold up to that level of scrutiny rather than just checking for the right buzzwords.

Here is the question as posed to students:

"Suppose that in a certain subfield of psychology, only 10% of the hypotheses researchers formally test turn out to reflect a real, non-zero effect; the other 90% are actually null. Studies in this subfield are conducted at the conventional significance threshold of α = 0.05 and have, on average, 40% statistical power to detect a true effect when one is present (this level of power is not unusual for small psychology studies). A new study in this subfield comes back statistically significant, with p = 0.04.

(a) What is the probability that this particular significant result reflects a genuine effect rather than a false positive? Show your work.
(b) Explain precisely why this number differs from both the p-value itself and from "one minus the p-value." State, in your own words, what a p-value is and is not.
(c) A classmate argues that this scenario shows frequentist statistics is simply mistaken and that Bayesian statistics avoids the problem. Do you agree? What role does the 10% figure play in the calculation, and could a committed frequentist make any principled use of that kind of figure?
(d) Briefly connect this to the replication crisis."

And here is the student's submitted answer:

"(a) We want P(real effect | significant). By Bayes' rule, this equals true positives divided by all positives. True positives, as a share of all hypotheses tested: 0.10 × 0.40 = 0.04. False positives: since the false-positive rate is set by α, this is 0.05 × 0.10 = 0.005. So P(real | significant) = 0.04 / (0.04 + 0.005) ≈ 0.89. So even though only 10% of tested hypotheses are true, a "significant" result in this field is correct about 89% of the time.

(b) This differs from the p-value because a p-value only tells you P(data this extreme or more extreme | null true), not P(null true | data) — it says nothing on its own about how likely the null or the alternative is. It's also not "one minus the p-value," which would just be 0.96 here and isn't a meaningful quantity at all; the p-value doesn't work that way.

(c) I don't think this really vindicates Bayesianism over frequentism specifically. The 10% figure is essentially a prior probability, and a frequentist has no principled way to incorporate a prior into an analysis at all, since frequentist statistics is defined entirely in terms of long-run error rates and explicitly forbids assigning probabilities to hypotheses. So the calculation in part (a) only really makes sense from a Bayesian standpoint, even though α and power are themselves ordinary frequentist quantities.

(d) This helps explain the replication crisis because it shows that even when every individual study is conducted and reported completely honestly at p < 0.05, a surprisingly large share of "significant" results in a low-base-rate, low-power field can still be false positives — so it's no surprise that many of them fail to replicate."
