A few months back, two engineers on my team independently made the same judgment call under a tight deadline: each pushed a config change straight to production without waiting on a second reviewer, since the change looked trivial. One of them got lucky and nothing happened. The other's change collided badly with a caching layer nobody had touched in years, and we had a four-hour outage that cost us a visible client. In the postmortem, the second engineer was treated as having shown a real lapse in judgment, while the first engineer's literally identical shortcut never even came up. That asymmetry bothered me enough that I mentioned it to a friend with a philosophy background, and she told me I'd basically stumbled onto "moral luck," pointing me to Thomas Nagel's and Bernard Williams's well-known companion essays on the topic from the same 1976 symposium.

I've since read a summary of Nagel's distinction between resultant, circumstantial, constitutive, and (I think it's called) causal luck, and I think I understand Williams's idea of "agent-regret" — the thought that the lorry driver who non-negligently runs over a child owes the world something that an equally careful driver who simply doesn't get unlucky does not, even though neither driver behaved differently. What surprised me is that this isn't universally accepted as a real phenomenon at all: apparently some philosophers hold what I've seen called the "control principle" (I gather this is associated with Michael Zimmerman, among others) and argue that genuine culpability can only track what was under an agent's control at the time of acting, so any case that looks like moral luck must really be something else in disguise — for instance, just us acquiring better evidence about a person's underlying carelessness once we see the bad outcome, rather than any actual difference in how blameworthy they were all along.

Since I don't have a philosophy background (I'm a backend engineer by trade), I don't trust myself to adjudicate this by reading position statements in isolation — I'd like to see the actual disagreement play out. Could you write a dialogue between two philosophers of roughly equal skill: one who defends the reality of moral luck in the Nagel/Williams vein, and one who defends the control principle and denies that any of this amounts to genuine moral luck? I'd like them to work through a two-driver case (equally reckless drivers, only one of whom happens to hit a pedestrian) in real detail, have the control-principle defender directly address whether "it's just better evidence" is doing legitimate philosophical work or is really a dodge, and have the moral-luck defender explain what, if anything, agent-regret is supposed to prove if it isn't itself just a confused feeling we ought to talk ourselves out of. I want them to genuinely engage with each other's strongest points and concede ground where they have to, rather than each side just restating its own view past the other.
