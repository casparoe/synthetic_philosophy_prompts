Kind of a niche one but ive been down a rabbit hole on Anscombe's old pamphlet 'Mr Truman's Degree' (the one she wrote protesting Oxford giving Truman an honorary degree). Her whole case that Hiroshima and Nagasaki were flat out murder rests on double effect: the dead civilians weren't foreseen collateral from hitting a military target, they *were* the means -- thats how you terrorize a government into surrendering -- so no amount of 'it saved more lives than an invasion would've cost' arithmetic gets Truman off the hook.

Except this is basically just the classic terror-bomber vs strategic-bomber case from the double effect literature, and the standard gripe with that case is that 'intended as means' vs 'merely foreseen side effect' is suspiciously easy to fudge through redescription -- you can describe the bomb's aim as 'destroy Japan's capacity and will to keep fighting' just as easily -- foreseen civilian deaths as a mere byproduct -- which is the same exact move that lets a 'legit' strategic bombing of a munitions factory or a naval blockade count as permissible.

Please argue that this redescription/closeness problem actually sinks Anscombe's argument on its own terms: that anyone who accepts double effect generally has no principled way to stop the Truman case being redescribed into the 'permissible' bucket, so her murder charge either has to condemn a bunch of otherwise-sanctioned WW2 bombing and blockade tactics too, or it quietly needs some extra premise beyond intention-vs-foresight thats doing the real work. try to actually stay inside her framework rather than just falling back on 'consequentialism is true anyway, so who cares'.
