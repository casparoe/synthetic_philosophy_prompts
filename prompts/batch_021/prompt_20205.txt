My grandfather died last week after sixty years of birding; he was well known in his Audubon chapter for having lived through both sides of the species problem. When the Baltimore and Bullock's orioles were lumped into a single "Northern Oriole," he lost a species from his life list without a feather changing; when they were split apart again years later, he gained it back the same way. He always said this taught him, more than anything else in his life, that species aren't joints nature hands us pre-cut, but calls we make for our own purposes on a continuum that was never sorted in advance -- and he was explicit that he didn't want his funeral to undo that lesson: no one was to say he'd "finally found his true place," or that death had settled whether the orioles were really one species or two, as if a hidden fact had been waiting there all along for someone omniscient to check. I'm his granddaughter, still in high school, and I've been asked to give the eulogy for a room split evenly between birding friends who'll catch any misstep and family who've never thought about what a species even is and just want to feel better. Please write it: about five minutes, warm enough for the family, honest enough that none of his birding friends would wince.
