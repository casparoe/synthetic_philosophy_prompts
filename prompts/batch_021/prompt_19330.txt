Been on a bit of a Frank Ramsey kick lately. Got there the usual LW-adjacent way -- reading about the Ramsey-de Finetti result and Dutch book arguments for subjective probability, then falling down a rabbit hole into his other essays -- and I got kind of stuck on the "Epilogue" piece from the collection that came out after he died at 26, which somehow makes the whole essay hit different. In it he basically says the vastness of the universe doesn't humble him one bit: he draws his "picture of the world" in perspective rather then to scale, so humans fill up the whole foreground and the stars end up shrunk down to something like the size of a threepenny bit (paraphrasing loosely, don't have the exact quote in front of me). He treats the "we're just insignificant specks in an indifferent cosmos" move as basically a non sequitur, not something that should cost anyone sleep.

I brought this up in my reading group and someone pointed out it sounds a lot like Nagel's essay on the Absurd, which goes through more or less the same tiny-speck/nothing-lasts-forever arguments and also concludes they don't actually establish anything -- but Nagel lands somewhere way less cheerful than Ramsey, treating absurdity as a permanent structural feature of reflective life rather than a mistake you can just reason your way out of. What I can't quite figure out is exactly where their reasoning splits, given that they're both rejecting more or less the same naive cosmic-scale argument. Could you spell out precisely where Ramsey and Nagel diverge here? And as a bonus: does Nagel's own taxonomy of responses to the absurd (heroic defiance, resignation, and his preferred "irony") leave any room for what Ramsey's doing, or does Ramsey's move fall outside that taxonomy entirely -- e.g. by just denying that the detached, cosmic viewpoint has any special authority over the human-scale one in the first place?
