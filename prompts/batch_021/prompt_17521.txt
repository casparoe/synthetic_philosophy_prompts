I belong to a small reading group that grew out of the old Overcoming Bias and Less Wrong blogs, and this year we have been working through classic decision-theoretic puzzles one meeting at a time. Last month's session on the two-envelope paradox produced a disagreement between two members, Raj and Mei, that the rest of us never managed to settle, and I would like a more careful adjudication than we could give it ourselves.

(1) Raj opened with what he called the standard dissolution of the paradox: the tempting argument for switching treats the other envelope as containing either 2X or X/2 with equal probability, but that fifty-fifty split would have to hold for literally every value of X you might observe, which requires a prior over the smaller amount that is flat everywhere — and no genuine probability distribution can be flat over an unbounded range, since it would then fail to integrate to one. Once you see that this illegitimate assumption is doing all the work, he said, the urge to switch disappears. (2) Mei objected that this cannot be the whole story, since the feeling of paradox survives under priors that are perfectly legitimate. Suppose it is common knowledge that the smaller amount was drawn uniformly from $1 to $100: see $60 in your envelope, and ordinary conditioning really does say the other envelope is equally likely to hold $30 or $120, so switching genuinely has the higher expected value given what you observed, with no improper prior in sight. (3) Raj replied that this actually vindicates his view rather than refuting it, once you look at the whole distribution rather than one convenient value of X: that same prior guarantees that whenever you see more than $100, you know for certain that you are holding the larger envelope, and switching is guaranteed to cost you half your money — a full quarter of all outcomes, not a rounding error, whose losses exactly cancel the apparent gains elsewhere. The sense that switching always looks good survives only because people quietly decline to imagine landing in the region that would refute it. (4) Mei's closing move was to grant Raj's arithmetic for that example while denying that it generalizes: any bounded prior has some cutoff that supplies his cancelling region, but there exist perfectly proper, unbounded priors, with no such cutoff, for which the numbers favor switching for literally every value you could observe. The catch, she said, is that any prior with that property has infinite mean — which is precisely why treating 'expected value recommends switching' as decisive was never safe to begin with, for reasons closely related to why the St. Petersburg game is a poor bet despite its infinite expectation. So the real moral, in her view, was never proper priors versus improper ones; it was the more basic unreliability of expected-value reasoning once unbounded quantities are in play, which makes Raj's cancellation story a feature of the bounded special case rather than the general resolution he took it to be.

Neither of them would budge, and I could not tell who was right, or whether they were each right about different parts of it.
