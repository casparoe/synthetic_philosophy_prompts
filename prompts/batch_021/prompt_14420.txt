I'm a software engineer, and my instinct on first hearing about the "statue and the clay" puzzle in metaphysics was that it must reduce to something like the distinction between reference equality and value equality in code: just a question of which equality relation you have in mind. Reading further, I've gathered that most philosophers working on material constitution think that response is too quick — there's a real modal argument, via Leibniz's Law, for saying the statue and the lump of clay are numerically distinct despite being made of exactly the same matter arranged the same way, plus a further "grounding problem" about what could possibly make two exactly co-located, microphysically identical things differ in their persistence conditions. Write a dialogue set in a sculptor's studio, where a lump of clay that has just been shaped into a statue is about to be squashed back into a ball. It should be between two equally capable philosophers: one a constitution theorist who holds that the statue and the clay are distinct, coincident objects, and the other someone who denies this and maintains there has only ever been one object. Have them press each other's strongest arguments rather than talk past each other, and make sure the second philosopher actually explains, rather than just asserts, how a one-object view accommodates the fact that the clay would survive the squashing while the statue would not.
