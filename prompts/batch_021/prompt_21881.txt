"Lewis's theory says C causes E if E counterfactually depends on C: if C hadn't happened, E wouldn't have happened. This gets spelled out with possible worlds - you look at the closest world where C doesn't occur and check whether E still occurs there. It's basically a formalized version of Hume's constant conjunction idea, just restated in terms of possible worlds instead of regularities. Woodward's theory says almost the same thing, except instead of possible worlds he uses interventions: C causes E if intervening on C (in Pearl's do-calculus sense) changes E while holding everything else fixed. Because Woodward cashes this out in terms of precise, ideal interventions rather than vague similarity between worlds, his account is a fully reductive theory that doesn't presuppose any causal notions of its own, which is a real advantage over Lewis's approach.

Take the two-assasin case: assassin A shoots and kills the victim, while backup assassin B is standing by and would've shot if A had missed, but A doesn't miss so B never fires. Since the victim's death counterfactually depends on A's shot - if A hadn't shot, the victim wouldn't have died - Lewis's theory straightforwardly and correctly picks out A as the cause, no extra machinery required."
