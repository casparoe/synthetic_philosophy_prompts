Me and my friend Theo got into it for like two hours last night over this decision theory thing called "Death in Damascus" - you know, the one based on that old fable where a guy meets Death in the market, panics, and flees to another city, except Death is basically a perfect predictor so it just shows up wherever the guy ends up anyway. Theo's take was that causal decision theory handles this fine: you work out the odds Death is already in each city (causally prior to your choice) and go from there. I kept arguing that's not even the hard part - the nasty bit is that neither "stay" nor "flee" ends up ratifiable, since the second you actually commit to one, your credence about where Death is shifts to match your own choice, so whatever you just picked immediately looks like the wrong call in hindsight. We went down a whole rabbit hole about whether flipping a coin is the only way out of that (since a predictor presumably can't out-guess a genuinely random process), and whether that's a real solution or just a fancier way of dodging the question, and never actually landed anywhere.

Anyway, I'd love it if you wrote me a short story built around that specific wrinkle, not just another moody retelling of the old "appointment in Samarra" fable that everyone's already read a thousand times. Pick whatever setting you want - ancient Syria is fine, but honestly I'd find it more interesting if you did something totally different, like a chess grandmaster up against an opponent who's read their entire psych file, or a submarine crew against a sonar operator who predicts evasive maneuvers before they're made, whatever works dramatically for you. The one thing I really want preserved is the actual shape of the problem: the protagonist working through why the "safe" choice keeps dissolving in their hands the instant they commit to it, and grappling with whether randomizing the decision is a genuine escape or just a different flavor of self-deception. Bonus points if that comes through in the action and thoughts of the story itself rather than a character just lecturing the reader about decision theory.
