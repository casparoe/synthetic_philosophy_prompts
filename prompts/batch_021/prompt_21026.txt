I spent my career as a field geologist and only took up philosophy after retiring, through a discussion group that meets every other Thursday. Something from our last meeting has stayed with me.

A friend of mine, an ethical vegan who thinks eating meat is seriously wrong, is also a keen and untroubled recreational poker player. Her running partner is the reverse: an easy conscience about meat, but a firm conviction that even purely recreational gambling is exploitative. So the two struck a bargain -- he would go vegetarian for six months, she would give up poker for the same stretch -- each changing the habit the other objected to, while remaining entirely unmoved about their own.

When she told the group, two other members, Halvard and Ines, got into a back-and-forth I couldn't referee myself.

(1) Halvard called it a neat piece of "moral trade": each reduced what they themselves regard as wrongdoing, at minimal cost to themselves -- comparative advantage applied to conscience rather than goods.
(2) Ines said that just dresses up something uglier: structurally, each was saying "I'll keep doing what you think is wrong unless you give me something in return," which is uncomfortably close to extortion.
(3) Halvard replied that, unlike extortion, the arrangement was reciprocal -- both sides gave something up and got something back -- and that we already accept costly incentives (fines, subsidies, praise) as legitimate ways of changing morally relevant behavior, so an incentive's mere presence can't be the problem.
(4) Ines granted the reciprocity point but said her deeper worry survives it: if such deals became common, the winners would be whoever can most convincingly manufacture or escalate conviction about something arbitrary, since conviction is the currency being traded -- rewarding performative outrage rather than correct moral views.

Could you go through their exchange move by move, saying who has the better of it at each step, and flag anything you think both of them are missing about how this sort of arrangement is supposed to work? Close with a one-line verdict, in the form \verdict{Halvard} or \verdict{Ines}, whichever you judge to have the stronger overall position.
