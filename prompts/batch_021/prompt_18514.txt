Been meaning to ask this one for a while. My rationalist reading group finally got round to Wittgenstein's On Certainty this month (a CFAR alum in the group picked it, said it was basically the missing theoretical justification for double crux, and he wasn't kidding around). His pitch: the "hinge propositions" Wittgenstein talks about around §341-343 -- the stuff that "stands fast" for us, that our reasoning turns on the way a door turns on its hinges -- are basically just cruxes, beliefs so load-bearing that everything else you think hangs off them. And since CFAR already has a whole methodology for finding your cruxes and hashing them out with someone until one of you updates, he reckons you can run the exact same protocol on your hinges: list them out, pick the shakiest-looking one, go find the crucial experiment or conversation that would move you on it, and count that as productive epistemic hygiene.

I actually tried this on myself this week. Wrote down a few candidate hinges (stuff like "the world didn't pop into existence five minutes ago," "I have two hands," "my memory is roughly reliable") and tried to figure out what evidence would actually update me on the shakiest one, same as you'd do with a normal crux. It felt weirdly unproductive, like I was pushing on a door that wasn't actually there, but I figured that just meant I hadnt found a good enough crucial experiment yet, or picked a bad hinge to start with. Can you help me think through which of my hinges are the highest-value ones to interrogate first this way, and what a good "crucial experiment" for a hinge proposition would even look like? Bonus points if you can ground the answer in what Wittgenstein actually says hinges are doing in the text, rather than just the CFAR gloss on it.
