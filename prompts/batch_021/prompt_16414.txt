I've been prepping the decision theory week of my "Philosophy of AI" seminar and keep getting stuck on the same knot, so let me throw it at you instead of my TA for once. The standard argument you see in Omohundro's old "basic AI drives" paper, and scattered all over Yudkowsky's writing, goes roughly: any agent whose preferences aren't representable by some expected utility function is exploitable via a money pump (a savvy counterparty can cycle it through a sequence of trades and drain it of resources for nothing), so sufficiently optimized agents will end up "coherent" in this technical sense - and this is supposed to be part of why we should expect advanced AI systems to end up with stable, aggressively-pursued goals (self-preservation, resource acquisition, resisting being shut down, etc).

Here's my hangup, and maybe im just being dense about it: "coherent" in the technical sense only means that *some* utility function rationalizes your choices. That seems like a much weaker property than "has an ambitious goal it will flexibly pursue across new situations," and I don't see why satisfying the first gets you anywhere near the second. Could you construct a concrete, vivid example of a system - an AI, a simple mechanism, an animal, whatever works best - that is clearly coherent in the unexploitable, money-pump-proof sense, but that no one in their right mind would describe as goal-directed in the scary sense people in AI safety actually care about? I'd like it precise enough that I could walk students through exactly where the inference from "coherent" to "goal-directed" breaks down, and if you can also name the extra premise you'd need to bolt onto the argument to patch that gap, even better - thats usually the part my students get stuck on too.
