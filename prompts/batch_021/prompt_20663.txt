For the past several months, my team at work has been building interpretability tooling on top of an open-weight language model. The core of it is a sparse autoencoder trained on residual stream activations, which decomposes the model's internal activity into a large dictionary of sparse "features" that we then try to label and monitor. This project has produced a running disagreement between two of my colleagues that I haven't been able to resolve, and that has started to feel less like an engineering dispute and more like a genuinely philosophical one.

One colleague treats the features our autoencoder surfaces as real, pre-existing components of the network's computation: the sort of thing you can independently verify, since the same circuits (induction heads being the standard example) turn up again and again across independently trained models, and since ablating or patching a given feature reliably produces the behavioral change you'd expect if it really were doing the causal work we attribute to it. My other colleague thinks this overstates what the method has shown. She points out that raising or lowering the dictionary size or the sparsity penalty changes which features come out the other end, that a single feature can be made to split into ever more fine-grained sub-features as the dictionary grows with no principled place to stop, and that all of this makes "these are the representations the network uses" sound like a much stronger claim than the evidence supports -- closer to "this is a useful compression of the network's behavior" dressed up as a discovery.

I don't have any formal training in philosophy, but a friend of mine who does described this to me as basically a rerun of the scientific realism versus instrumentalism debate, just moved from physics to machine learning. I'd like you to write the dialogue my colleagues would have if they sat down and actually hashed this out, cast as a realist and an instrumentalist about neural network representations. Please make sure the two sides are evenly matched and genuinely respond to each other's strongest points rather than talking past one another: the realist needs a real answer to the dictionary-relativity and feature-splitting worries, and the instrumentalist needs a real answer to why universality and successful causal interventions aren't exactly what we'd expect to find if the features were real. And if, by the end, it turns out the two of them are actually using "real" or "representation" in subtly different senses rather than disagreeing about the facts, I'd like that to come out on the page rather than have one side simply declared the winner.
