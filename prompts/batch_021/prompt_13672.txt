I recently came across Moritz Schlick's essay "On the Meaning of Life" in an old anthology on the subject, and it struck me as an odd piece for him to have written, given that he's otherwise remembered mainly as the founder of the Vienna Circle and a fairly hard-line verificationist. In the essay, he draws a sharp distinction between activities that are mere means to some further end — call this toil, undertaken for the sake of wages, survival, or some other external payoff — and activities whose purpose lies entirely within themselves, the paradigm case being play. On his view, meaning in a life tracks the second category almost exclusively: a life is meaningful to the extent that it is filled with genuinely autotelic, playful activity. This is why he thinks children, who are almost wholly absorbed in play, have the fullest access to meaning, and why he's fairly pessimistic about adult "civilized" existence, which he sees as organized overwhelmingly around toil. He doesn't leave things there, though: he seems to hold out hope that as productive technology improves, the portion of life given over to toil could shrink and the portion given over to play could grow, and not just for a leisured few.

Two developments that come up constantly on the rationalist and rationalist-adjacent blogs and forums I read strike me as natural test cases for this framework, even though Schlick obviously never encountered either. The first is the culture of relentless self-optimization — quantified-self tracking, gamified habit apps, "scheduled fun," and the like — whose explicit aim is to make more of life feel like play, but whose method is to subject leisure itself to exactly the metric-driven, instrumental mindset that Schlick associates with toil. The second is the live discussion in these same circles about a possible post-AGI economy, in which automation eliminates most toil and something like a universal basic income funds a life of mass leisure for most people — which sounds, on its face, like Schlick's technological hope realized more or less literally.

Working from Schlick's actual criteria for what makes an activity count as meaningful play rather than disguised toil, how do you think he would judge these two cases? Would he see self-optimization culture as self-undermining almost by construction — toil wearing play's clothing — or is there some way it could still satisfy his criteria? And would he have welcomed an automation-driven leisure society as a vindication of his essay, or is there a specific worry, grounded in his own distinction rather than in generic "money can't buy happiness" sentiments, about whether merely removing toil is enough to produce the kind of meaning he has in mind? I'd like your best reconstruction of his likely position, engaging closely with the structure of his actual argument rather than with meaning-of-life truisms in general.
