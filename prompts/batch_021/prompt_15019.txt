Practice question (20 points): Explain how the sorites paradox is generated for a predicate like "heap." Then explain (a) Timothy Williamson's epistemicist solution, including his appeal to the margin-for-error principle, and (b) how a supervaluationist treats the paradox's major premise instead. Finally, assess whether either view succeeds in avoiding commitment to an arbitrary sharp boundary, and at what cost.

My classmate's answer:

"The sorites paradox arises from an argument that looks sound but produces an unacceptable conclusion. Consider a heap of 100,000 grains of sand: intuitively, if n grains form a heap, then removing a single grain still leaves n−1 grains that form a heap. Repeating this principle down the entire series, using nothing more than repeated applications of modus ponens, forces the conclusion that a single remaining grain is a heap. Since the starting premise is true and the inference pattern is valid, yet the conclusion is absurd, something has to give: either the initial premise, the universal generalization licensing each step, or the validity of the argument form itself.

Williamson's epistemicist response rejects the universal generalization: 'heap' has a perfectly precise extension fixed by the totality of our dispositions to use the word, so some exact number of grains, say 5,412, marks the true cutoff between heaps and non-heaps. What we lack is knowledge of where the boundary falls, not a determinate fact about it. Williamson explains this permanent ignorance through the margin-for-error principle: knowing that n grains make a heap while n−1 do not would require a method of belief formation reliable enough to get the right answer across all the very similar cases that share our evidence, and since the meaning of 'heap' is itself fixed anew, however slightly, by each fresh act of usage, no belief about a specific cutoff can stay safely true across that margin. The boundary is real, but no amount of additional observation of how we talk could ever locate it.

A supervaluationist takes a different approach: 'heap' is semantically indeterminate between many equally admissible ways of making it precise ('precisifications'), and a sentence is true ('super-true') just when it comes out true on every precisification, false when it comes out false on all of them, and neither true nor false otherwise. Since each precisification is itself a classical, fully sharp interpretation, each one contains some transition point, but because different precisifications place that point differently, no particular instance of the major premise's conditional is true on all of them, so no specific instance is false either. By locating the failure of the premise in this way, rather than in any single conditional, the supervaluationist avoids ever committing to a sharp boundary in the language itself, unlike the epistemicist, who must insist one exists even though it can't be named.

On balance, supervaluationism seems the more attractive view. Both agree that the sorites reasoning fails at the universal premise rather than at any individual step, which already shows the paradox does not force us to accept the absurd conclusion. But supervaluationism pays a smaller metaphysical price: rather than positing a fact about the world that is forever hidden from us, it treats the appearance of a precise boundary as an artifact of our failure to make a vague predicate fully precise."
