I'm several chapters into a historical novel built around a formal philosophical disputation, a vāda, that I've invented for a fictional North Indian royal court in the early medieval period. My protagonist is a young Naiyāyika logician who has been given the unenviable task of defending her school's list of four pramāṇas against a visiting monk trained in the pramāṇa tradition of Dignāga and Dharmakīrti, whose lineage recognizes only perception and inference as genuine sources of knowledge. The scene I keep putting off concerns upamāna, comparison or analogy, which her tradition treats as a fully independent fourth pramāṇa, but which the monk insists can be folded into perception and inference without remainder. Both characters reach for the same illustration, inherited from Vātsyāyana's commentary on the Nyāya-sūtra: a traveler is told that a gavaya, a wild ox he has never seen, resembles a cow, and later, coming across the animal in a forest, correctly applies the word to it for the first time.

Where the two characters part ways is over what that moment of recognition actually is. As I understand the monk's position, nothing happens there that perception, memory, and an ordinary inference from resemblance to applicability could not jointly explain, which makes positing an entirely separate faculty of knowledge explanatorily idle. I've read enough secondary literature to know that Naiyāyikas rejected this reduction, but most accounts I've found simply report that they did so, without reconstructing the reasoning in enough detail for me to trust it in my protagonist's mouth. I'd rather not have her win the debate by asserting her school's conclusion more forcefully than her opponent asserts his.

Could you write out, as continuous argumentative prose rather than a summary of the historical positions, the strongest case available to a Naiyāyika for why the cognition produced by upamāna resists reduction to perception, memory, and inference? I'd particularly like the argument to say something precise about what upamāna is supposed to deliver that an inference could not, and to address whether the monk's proposed inferential reconstruction could even satisfy Nyāya's own requirement of invariable concomitance (vyāpti) between reason and conclusion. Something in the range of a page would suit the scene, and a closing sentence or two distilling the argument's logical skeleton would help me confirm that I've followed it correctly before I start drafting her lines.
