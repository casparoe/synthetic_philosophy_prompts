I spent most of my working life as an experimental physicist, running Bell‑inequality tests in a quantum‑optics lab (including a couple of the loophole‑closing experiments of the mid‑2010s), and it is only since retiring that I have had the leisure to read philosophy properly. A reading group I recently joined has been slowly working through Luis de Molina's attempt to reconcile divine providence with libertarian free will by positing "middle knowledge": God's knowledge, logically prior to any decision about what to create, of every true counterfactual of creaturely freedom, that is, every proposition of the form "were agent S placed in circumstances C, S would freely choose A." Frankly, the theological stakes — providence, grace, the problem of evil — interest me less than the purely metaphysical puzzle the position raises, which strikes me as a question about truthmakers and modality dressed up in doctrinal clothing. What has occupied me in particular over the past few weeks is the grounding objection: the charge that such counterfactuals cannot be true, because nothing available could make them true. They are not necessary truths, since a libertarian‑free agent could always have chosen otherwise in the very same circumstances; and they cannot be grounded in any actual choice either, since, by the theory's own lights, they are true prior to and independent of God's decision to create anyone at all. I noticed, for what it is worth, a family resemblance to a dispute I know far better — whether unperformed measurements have determinate outcomes before any system and detector actually interact, an assumption physicists call "counterfactual definiteness" — though I hold the parallel loosely, since a photon's dispositions and an agent's free choices may lack determinate truth values, if indeed they do, for quite different reasons.

I would like an essay from you that takes a genuine stand on this rather than merely surveying the terrain. Please lay out the grounding objection in its strongest form, drawing on Robert Adams' original statement of it and the sharper versions later critics such as Hasker gave it; then set out the most serious Molinist rejoinders — for instance, the response associated with Plantinga and Flint that counterfactuals of freedom are simply true without any further truthmaker, and Craig's challenge to the truthmaker principle the objection presupposes — together with the difficulties these rejoinders face in turn, such as whether they saddle creatures with an unsettling sort of counterfactual power over what God does, or whether calling a counterfactual "brute" simply relabels the very problem it was meant to dissolve. Please end by telling me plainly, and defending, whether you think the grounding objection succeeds in showing that middle knowledge — as Molina's contemporary defenders understand it — is untenable, or whether the Molinist has adequate resources to withstand it, perhaps in some suitably revised form.
