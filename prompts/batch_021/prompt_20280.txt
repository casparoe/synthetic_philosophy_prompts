A friend of mine, who is midway through a dissertation in metaethics, and I got into a long argument over dinner last week about buck-passing accounts of value -- the family of views, associated with Scanlon but tracing back to Ewing and Brentano's talk of "fitting" attitudes, on which for something to be good just is for there to be reasons to favor it (to desire it, admire it, pursue it, and so on). I raised the standard objection that such analyses run into the wrong-kind-of-reasons problem: in Rabinowicz and Rønnow-Rasmussen's well-known example, a demon who will torture you unless you admire him clearly gives you a reason to admire him, yet nothing about the demon thereby becomes admirable. My friend's response was that this is a solved problem -- you simply restrict the analysis to reasons of "the right kind," cashed out as reasons grounded in the object's own features (its cruelty, its wit, whatever they may be) rather than in the causal upshots of holding the attitude (namely, avoiding torture) -- and that this restriction is not circular, because it is stated in terms of where a reason comes from, rather than in terms of value itself.

I was not persuaded, but I struggled to pin down exactly what was wrong with that response, and we ended up going in circles for the rest of the evening. What I would like from you is a careful reconstruction of why this "object-given versus state-given" move is widely regarded as failing to fully dissolve the circularity worry -- that is, why sorting reasons into the right kind by appeal to their source, rather than their content, is suspected of smuggling back in an independent, unreduced notion of value-relevance instead of genuinely deriving it from facts about reasons alone. If there is a way of drawing the object-given/state-given line that avoids this worry without simply stipulating it away, I would like to understand what that would look like.

As a secondary matter, I would also value your view on whether this is structurally the very same difficulty that arises for evidentialism about reasons for belief, where Pascal's-wager-type cases give you overwhelming practical reason to believe something with no bearing on whether it is true. Is the belief case simply a special instance of the general wrong-kind-of-reasons problem, or do features peculiar to belief, as opposed to admiration, desire, or other pro-attitudes, make the two problems, and their candidate solutions, come apart in interesting ways?
