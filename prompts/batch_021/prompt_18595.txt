I spent the better part of four decades running an analytical chemistry lab before retiring, and in the years since I have been trying to make up for a philosophical education I never had time for. Lately I have been working through Kripke's *Naming and Necessity*, and the material in the third lecture on natural kind terms has rather taken hold of me: the claim that a statement like "gold has the atomic number 79" or "water is H2O" is metaphysically necessary, even though it could only ever have been discovered a posteriori, by doing the actual chemistry. As someone who took it for granted, across an entire career, that this sort of empirical finding was about as contingent a fact as one could ask for, I found the argument genuinely disorienting, in a good way.

A friend in my reading group, who knows far more of the history than I do, pointed out that I had stumbled into a very old dispute without realizing it. Locke, in Book III of *An Essay Concerning Human Understanding*, had already drawn a sharp line between the nominal essence of a substance such as gold — the cluster of observable properties (colour, weight, malleableness, fusibility, and so on) by which we sort it into a kind — and its real essence, the hidden "internal constitution" that actually produces those properties, which he understood in broadly corpuscularian terms after Boyle. Crucially, Locke doubted that beings with senses as crude as ours could ever gain real cognitive access to that internal constitution, and he thought our species and kind terms therefore answer only to the nominal essence, which carries an inescapable element of human convention.

What I would most like help thinking through is how Locke's actual position bears on the Kripke-Putnam picture, now that we have the chemistry Locke lacked. Would he have regarded modern atomic theory and structural chemistry as having finally accomplished what he suspected human faculties probably could not — namely, penetrating to the real essence of gold or water — and so come to accept that "gold is the element with atomic number 79" states more than a convenient nominal classification? Or would his deeper doubts, about both the limits of unaided human cognition and the partly conventional "workmanship of the understanding" involved in any sorting into kinds, lead him to resist that conclusion even in the light of the actual science, on the grounds that our best chemical theory is still only a very refined nominal essence, forever open to revision? And, separately, since Locke ties the signification of a general term to the idea a speaker associates with it, does his framework have any resources at all for Kripke's move of letting a term designate a kind's essence rigidly, via a causal chain running back to paradigm samples, without the speaker's idea capturing that essence — or would that whole manoeuvre, and with it the very notion of a truth that is necessary yet knowable only a posteriori, have struck him as unintelligible on his own terms? I would be grateful for your best reconstruction of how the argument would have gone.
