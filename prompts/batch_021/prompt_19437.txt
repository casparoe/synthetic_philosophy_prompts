Had a fun one after my epistemology seminar last week: two of my PhD students, Nadia and Theo, stayed behind arguing about Williamson's anti-luminosity argument (the "feeling cold over the course of a day" case) and never actually resolved it, so I'd like a neutral outside read, since I've spent too long inside Williamson's head to trust my own judgment here.

(1) Nadia opened by saying the whole argument is basically a Sorites paradox in a lab coat: slice time finely enough that adjacent moments are indiscriminable, then act surprised theres no sharp point where you stop knowing you're cold. That's vagueness doing its usual thing, not some deep discovery about introspection.

(2) Theo said that can't be the whole story, since the margin-for-error premise is about the reliability of your discriminating capacities, not semantic vagueness -- swap in a precise, non-vague magnitude (the room is exactly 15.000°C or it isn't) tracked by a slightly noisy thermometer-reading disposition, and you get the identical result with no vagueness anywhere in sight.

(3) Nadia replied that you still need an unbroken run of arbitrarily close, pairwise-indiscriminable cases to get the induction going, so that's just a Sorites series wearing a different label, whatever you call the premise that generates it.

(4) Theo answered that the diagnosis is genuinely different even though the setup looks similar: ordinary vagueness gives you a merely epistemic moral (there's a sharp fact, we just can't locate it), while Williamson is after something stronger -- that no state tracked by any fallible process can be luminous, sharp cutoff or not, because luminosity would need your evidence to move in perfect lockstep with the state, margin-free, and nothing fallible can do that.

They then got sidetracked onto whether the KK principle failing is just anti-luminosity again with "knowing that P" plugged in as the state (Nadia's view: free, no extra work needed) or a genuinely separate result needing its own margin-for-error premise about access to your own knowledge (Theo's view).
