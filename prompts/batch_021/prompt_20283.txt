Two friends of mine, Marcus and Elena, have been arguing for weeks about something that happened during last spring's college basketball tournament, and I haven't been able to settle it for them. Before one of the games, a mutual friend, Sam, texted our group chat about the heavy underdog: "They're done. Book it." His confidence rested on a well-regarded forecasting model that gave the favorite a 96% chance of winning. The underdog won. Over dinner afterward, the argument went like this:

(1) Marcus said Sam had nothing to answer for: he was going on the best evidence anyone had access to, and forming a confident prediction on excellent evidence isn't shown to be a mistake just because the unlikely 4% came in.

(2) Elena said that's not the issue. Sam didn't say "they're very likely done" or "I'd bet heavily against them" -- he flatly declared them done, as though the matter were settled. It wasn't settled, and what he said turned out to be false. Having excellent evidence explains why he believed it; it doesn't make the flat claim true, and truth is what should be at issue.

(3) Marcus replied that this standard proves too much. Forecasters say "it'll rain tomorrow," doctors tell patients a treatment "will work," and we all say the sun "will rise," none of which is certain either, and we don't think an occasional miss shows the speaker was out of line to have said it so plainly. If flat statements about the future required certainty, almost nothing we ordinarily say about what's coming would be permissible.

(4) Elena answered that the sunrise case isn't a fair comparison, since no one treats that as remotely open, whereas everyone -- Sam included -- treated this game's outcome as genuinely up for grabs beforehand, which is exactly why it was worth watching. So the real question isn't whether ordinary talk can absorb some risk of error (of course it can), but whether Sam's specific, hedge-free way of putting it represented the outcome as more settled than he, or anyone, actually took it to be at the time.

Neither will budge. Could you adjudicate move by move?
