I'm a software engineer on the motion-planning team at a warehouse robotics company, and our internal "Responsible AI" reading group recently spent a session on the idea of a "responsibility gap" in AI ethics. As I understood the argument, once a robot's behavior is driven by a learning system rather than hand-coded rules, its actions can become genuinely unpredictable even to the engineers who built and trained it. Since moral responsibility normally requires that an agent could reasonably have foreseen and controlled an outcome, the argument goes that none of the usual candidates — the individual engineers, the company, or the robot itself, which isn't a moral agent in any case — straightforwardly qualifies as "responsible" when the system ends up causing harm. Responsibility, in other words, falls into a gap between all the parties we'd normally look to.

Our discussion afterward landed on a conclusion that's now quietly shaping how our safety team prioritizes its roadmap: if serious philosophers accept that nobody can be held responsible once a system is adaptive enough, it seems to follow that a court would have just as much trouble pinning legal liability on the company specifically if one of our robots ever injured a warehouse worker, since you can't sue an entity for an outcome that, by hypothesis, nobody is responsible for. A few people on the team are now using this to argue that we can deprioritize the more expensive parts of our incident-liability and insurance planning.

I'd like to raise this at our next planning meeting, but I don't have any formal philosophy background, so I want to get the underlying argument right before I do. Is it actually valid to move from "there's a responsibility gap" at the moral or philosophical level to "the company has reduced legal exposure," or does that conflate two different senses of responsibility? And separately, for cases where a genuine gap really does seem to exist, what's the field's best answer to it: is it something that gets closed as robots and their training methods improve, or is it more a permanent structural feature of deploying autonomous systems that has to be managed institutionally rather than solved?
