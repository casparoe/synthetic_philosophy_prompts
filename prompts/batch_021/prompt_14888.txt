Ok so I fell down a rabbit hole this week reading old LW threads on Bayesian updating and ended up at the Judy Benjamin problem, which I'd somehow never run into before despite thinking I'd seen every classic probability puzzle by now. Quick recap in case its useful: van Fraassen set this up in some early-80s paper (something like "a problem for relative information minimizers," don't quote me on the exact title) where a soldier, Judy Benjamin, parachutes into a region split into Red and Blue territory, each of which is itself split into an area held by Headquarters Company and one held by Second Company -- so four equally likely cells going in. She gets a garbled radio message telling her, in effect, "if you're in Red territory, the odds are 3:1 you're in the Headquarters area" -- a conditional, not a flat statement about where she actually is. The weird part is that if you update using the standard move of minimizing relative information (basically maxent relative to your prior, subject to that one constraint), her probability of being in Red at all shifts away from 1/2, even though the message never compared Red to Blue, only the split within Red. People don't treat this as just a fun brain-teaser -- it's considered a genuine problem case for that whole family of "least-informative update" rules.

This got me wondering what Keynes -- the Treatise on Probability Keynes, not the "in the long run we're all dead" Keynes -- would've made of it. He spent a big chunk of that 1921 book on exactly this family of moves: he thought the Principle of Indifference (assign equal probability when you have no reason to favor one option over another) was fine in restricted cases but generates contradictions once you notice that the same state of ignorance can be carved into alternatives in incompatible ways, and he worked out fairly specific conditions for when it's actually licensed. He also had this separate notion of the "weight" of an argument, layered on top of the probability number itself, which as far as I can tell has no analogue anywhere in the maxent framework. He obviously never heard of Judy Benjamin -- the guy died in '46, decades before van Fraassen wrote this up -- but he clearly had a technical, worked-out position on the exact kind of inference the puzzle turns on.

So here's what I'm actually asking: using his real arguments from the Treatise, not the cartoon version of Keynes as just "the uncertainty guy," can you reconstruct what he'd probably say is going wrong (or isn't going wrong) in the Judy Benjamin case? Specifically I want to know whether his restrictions on the Principle of Indifference would flag the Red/Blue shift as exactly the kind of illegitimate move he spent all those pages warning about, and whether "weight of argument" gives him a principled reason to distrust relative-information-minimization as a general way of updating on conditionals -- as opposed to him just shrugging and saying ordinary probability theory doesn't apply to cases like this at all.
