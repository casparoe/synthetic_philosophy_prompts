Last weekend I hosted a game night for a few friends from my old graduate program. After dinner, Owen, who is finishing a PhD in decision theory, decided to demonstrate the classic "dollar auction," which he said he'd once used to teach undergraduates about escalation of commitment: whoever bids highest for an actual $20 bill wins it and pays that bid, while whoever ends up second-highest also has to pay their own last bid and gets nothing at all. Six of us started bidding in $1 increments, and, true to the game's reputation, everyone else dropped out well before the bidding reached $20, leaving Owen and our friend Renata as the last two bidders. The two of them kept going long past that point, and by the time Renata "won" with a bid of $34 (Owen's last bid, and what he ended up paying for nothing, was $33), the table had gone quiet in a slightly horrified way. What followed was a genuinely long argument between the two of them, which I ended up moderating without ever really resolving, and I'd like an outside opinion on it, since I don't think either of them was simply wrong.

The exchange went roughly as follows. (1) Owen opened by calling the whole thing a textbook sunk-cost fallacy on both their parts: once the bidding passed $20, continuing could only be justified by money already committed, and rational agents are supposed to ignore sunk costs. (2) Renata pushed back that this mislabels what happened: neither of them had actually paid anything at any point before the very end, so their bids were not sunk in the relevant sense, since whether that money ever left their pockets depended entirely on what they did next; given where Owen stood each time it was her turn, outbidding him by a dollar really was the loss-minimizing option available to her. (3) Owen granted the technical point but said it changed nothing important: the real mistake was not any particular bid near the end but the failure, back around $2 or $3, to privately commit in advance to a hard cap, rather than re-deciding at every increment once momentum and irritation had taken over. (4) Renata replied that demanding a precommitment like that is easy in hindsight but unreasonable in the moment, since neither of them knew how far the other was willing to go; testing Owen's resolve one dollar at a time was the only way to find that out, and she could just as easily have folded early and ended up the one regretting it, if Owen had turned out to be the one willing to stop first. (5) Owen answered that this is exactly why crediting or blaming either of them individually misses the point: the rule that the second-highest bidder still pays guarantees that whichever of them is marginally more stubborn ends up "winning" a bad prize, so nothing about Renata's final bid was actually smart, only less unlucky than the alternative. (6) Renata closed by saying that doing better than the real alternative you actually faced still counts as the better decision, and that Owen would be defending his own bids just as forcefully as she is defending hers if the last increment had gone the other way.
