I'm a retired epidemiologist (35-ish years of building risk models and chasing outbreaks) and these days I fill my mornings reading old epistemolgy papers instead of case reports, which is a strange trade to have made but here we are. Last week I fell down a rabbit hole on something called the "dogmatism paradox" -- I gather it traces back to some unpublished lectures Kripke gave in the mid-60s, later picked up in print by Gilbert Harman and others, though I wouldn't swear to getting the history exactly right. The gist as I understand it: if I really know that p is true, then right now, in advance, I already know that any future evidence that turns up against p has to be misleading evidence (since p is true, and evidence against a truth is by definition misleading). So knowing p seems to commit me to being justified in discounting whatever counter-evidence shows up later, before I've even seen it -- which sounds exactly like kind of thing we'd normally call dogmatic, even though nothing about the reasoning looks obviously invalid.

That rang a bell from a stats-for-epidemiologists course decades ago, where I first heard of the "preface paradox" (Makinson, I believe, from roughly that same era): an author can be perfectly justified in believing every individual claim in her book, while also being justified, via a line in the preface, in believing that the book contains at least one error somewhere -- because books that long just never come out completely clean. On first pass these two feel like close cousins: both are cases where a bunch of locally impeccable, individually justified beliefs seem to add up to something that smells inconsistent or irrational once you step back and look at the whole picture. But I suspect that's a lazy pattern-match on my part.

And since I spent a good chunk of my career quietly discounting "outlier" data points that didn't fit an already well-supported model, I'm curious which of the two paradoxes you think better captures that particular flavor of everyday scientific overconfidence.
