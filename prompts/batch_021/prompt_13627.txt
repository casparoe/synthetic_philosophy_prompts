I am in the final year of a philosophy PhD, and my dissertation defends Lewis's Humean, Best-System account of laws of nature: a regularity counts as a law just in case it is a theorem of the deductive system that best balances simplicity and strength, with nothing beyond the total pattern of local, intrinsic qualities across spacetime — the "Humean mosaic" — to ground it. This semester I audited a graduate quantum foundations seminar to shore up my physics background, and it has left me unsettled: entanglement turns out to be one of Tim Maudlin's central arguments against Humeanism about laws, since entangled systems seem to instantiate physical relations that don't supervene on the intrinsic states of their parts — meaning the mosaic Lewis needs isn't even there at the fundamental level. Lewis himself, in "Humean Supervenience Debugged," all but concedes the point, defending himself only by refusing to take metaphysical lessons from present-day quantum mechanics.

My advisor insists the objection is old news, fully defused either by "wavefunction realist" moves that relocate the mosaic to a point in a very high-dimensional configuration space, or by more radical "Super-Humean" proposals (associated with Michael Esfeld and collaborators) that drop the wavefunction from the fundamental ontology entirely and treat it as nothing but a compact summary of actual particle trajectories. I am no longer sure I believe her, and this has stopped being an academic exercise, since my final chapter depends on the answer.

Please argue that neither strategy actually rescues the Best System Account from Maudlin's objection: that each saves Humeanism in name only, by quietly abandoning the very locality that made the thesis substantive in the first place. Engage with at least one of the two rescue strategies in genuine technical detail rather than gesturing at the debate.
