Otto Neurath died in 1945, decades before anyone spoke of "infographics," "dashboards," or emoji, yet his Isotype project (the "International System of Typographic Picture Education") rested on specific design commitments: quantities were to be shown by repeating a fixed unit icon rather than by resizing a single icon, since resizing distorts perceived proportion, and pictures were meant only to supplement verbal and symbolic language, given Neurath's insistence that pictorial statistics could display comparisons and distributions but not negation, causation, or general theoretical claims. Bearing these commitments in mind, together with his broader logical-empiricist wariness of rhetorically loaded language and his hope that pictorial statistics could convey quantitative knowledge across barriers of literacy and language, how would Neurath likely have judged today's data-visualization culture — in particular, the now-common use of proportionally sized bubbles and icons in infographics and dashboards, and the rise of emoji as a supposedly universal pictorial idiom? Would he have regarded either as a natural extension of Isotype's aims or as a betrayal of its guiding principles, and on what specific grounds?
