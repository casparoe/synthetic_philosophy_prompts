I'm taking a graduate algebra course right now and Zorn's Lemma has turned into this recurring magic wand: every existence proof (every vector space has a basis, every proper ideal sits inside some maximal ideal, every field has an algebraic closure, etc.) just says "by Zorn's Lemma" and moves on, and nobody in the room ever blinks. Out of curiosity I looked into what Zorn's Lemma actually is under the hood, and it turns out its provably equivalent (over ZF) to both the Axiom of Choice and the Well-Ordering Theorem, i.e. the claim that literally any set, including the real numbers, can be well-ordered (I gather it's sometimes credited to Kuratowski too, who apparently had a version of it over a decade before Zorn did). The well-ordering claim is the one people's intuitions actually revolt against -- nobody can even gesture at what a well-ordering of R would look like -- and I ran across some mathematician's joke, I think it was Jerry Bona, along the lines of "the Axiom of Choice is obviously true, the well-ordering theorem is obviously false, and nobody can decide about Zorn's Lemma."

That's the part that's been bugging me. If the three are all logically equivalent, then treating Zorn's Lemma as an unremarkable technical convenience while treating "the reals can be well-ordered" as borderline absurd seems like it should be flatly inconsistent -- like being 95% confident in a claim while being 20% confident in something you already know is logically the very same claim, just because it's dressed up differently. But nobody in my class, including the professor, seems bothered by this. Everyone just keeps reaching for Zorn's Lemma like it's obviously fine.

So here's what I actually want from you: argue for the position that this comfortable, unreflective everyday use of Zorn's Lemma is NOT justified -- that once you know the equivalence, "every poset in which every chain has an upper bound contains a maximal element" deserves exactly as much suspicion as "the reals can be well-ordered," and that mathematicians' calm about the former is just habituation from years of textbook use, not any real insight. Don't give me a wishy-washy "it's all a matter of perspective" answer; actually commit to the position and push it as far as it'll go, including dealing head-on with the obvious comeback that logically equivalent statements can differ hugely in how transparent their equivalence is to a non-ideal reasoner, so differing confidence levels aren't automatically irrational. And if you can, say something about why the miscalibration (if that's what it is) survives even among mathematicians who could rattle off the equivalence proof from memory.
