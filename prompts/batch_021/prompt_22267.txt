Since retiring from a career in experimental physics, I have been working through classic texts in the philosophy of mind, and I recently reread Turing's 1950 paper "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" more carefully than I did as a student. I was struck by his treatment of the last of the nine objections he considers, the "Argument from Extra-Sensory Perception." Where he dismisses the theological and "heads in the sand" objections out of hand, he instead concedes that the statistical case for telepathy is, in his own words, "quite a strong one," and tries to rescue the imitation game by confining both parties to a "telepathy-proof room." He even worries, separately, that a psychokinetic interrogator might bias a machine's internal random-number generator and so defeat the test from outside.

What are the most important implications of this episode for how much weight the imitation game can bear as a criterion for machine thought, and for the prospects of any purely behavioral or operational test of mentality more generally? I am especially interested in whether Turing's willingness to treat ESP as a live empirical threat, rather than as pseudoscience to be dismissed, reveals a tension with the mechanistic picture of mind that seems to motivate the rest of the paper.
