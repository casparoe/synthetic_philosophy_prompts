For the past year I have been writing a novel narrated by a paintings conservator — a woman in her sixties who has spent four decades cleaning and retouching Old Master canvases in a provincial museum — and I have run into a problem of phenomenology that I would like some philosophical help clarifying. In the novel she insists, against her more skeptical young apprentice, that this training has not merely sharpened her judgments about a canvas but has altered what she actually sees when she looks at one: where the apprentice sees an undifferentiated passage of brown shadow, she sees, immediately and without any conscious inference, the separate campaigns of glaze, the retouched losses, the later overpaint. Wanting to give her claim more backbone than mere authorial assertion, I have spent the last few weeks reading through the philosophical literature on the "cognitive penetration" of perception — the debate over whether beliefs, concepts, and expectations can genuinely alter the content of perceptual experience itself, rather than only the judgments we form on the basis of it afterward. Fodor and Pylyshyn's insistence that low-level vision is essentially encapsulated, with attention doing all the apparent work, sits uneasily against philosophers like Siegel, who argue that expertise, hostility, or expectation really can change what is presented to consciousness, and not merely how that presentation is subsequently described.

What struck me, reading around this material rather later at night than I should admit, is how closely it echoes an older argument I remember from a philosophy-of-science course years ago: the claim, associated with Hanson and later Kuhn, that observation is irreducibly "theory-laden" — that Tycho Brahe and Kepler, watching the same dawn, do not simply interpret an identical visual given in two different ways but, in some substantive sense, see different things, because each carries a different cosmology into the act of looking. The two debates seem hardly ever to cite one another, and yet they appear to be wagering on the same basic possibility: that there is no theory-neutral, cognitively sealed-off layer of "raw" seeing sitting underneath our concepts, waiting to be interpreted.

I would like you to compare these two ideas properly, purely for my own understanding rather than for anything that will end up on the page. Are cognitive penetration and theory-ladenness really the same underlying thesis wearing different disciplinary clothing, or do they make importantly different claims once you look closely at what each actually requires? In particular, could Kuhn's picture of paradigm-relative observation survive even if Fodor and Pylyshyn turned out to be right that low-level visual experience is strictly impenetrable, so that only attention, categorization, and judgment shift across paradigms — or does the Kuhnian story quietly need something like penetration in the strong, experience-altering sense in order to do the work he wants it to do? I would be grateful for your own assessment of which of the two literatures has the more defensible case that something happens at the level of experience itself, and not merely at the level of interpretation after the fact.
