I spent almost forty years as an observational astronomer - mostly stellar spectroscopy, some cosmology near the end - and only got serious about philosophy after I retired a few years back. My wife jokes that the philosophy reading group I joined was supposed to be the gentle hobby, easier on the blood pressure than fieldwork ever was, and instead I come home more worked up than I ever did over a broken spectrograph. Lately we've been slowly working through the Bayesian confirmation theory literature, and I've gotten completely stuck on what's apparently called the "problem of old evidence." The setup is almost insultingly simple once you see it: if E is some fact I already know for certain, so P(E)=1, then conditionalizing on E can't move my credence in any hypothesis H at all, since P(H|E) just collapses back to P(H) - so on the plain vanilla Bayesian story, evidence you already had in hand can never confirm anything, ever. And yet the standard example, the one every physicist knows, is Mercurys perihelion precession: an anomaly that had been sitting right there in the astronomical data for decades before Einstein came along, and that every physicist I've ever met still treats as a genuine, serious confirmation of general relativity - maybe the best early one there was. So something in that simple story has to give.

From what I can tell there have been a few serious attempts to fix this since Glymour first pressed the point, back around 1980 if I'm remembering right. One move, which I gather traces to a paper by Garber (with a couple of others proposing something similar around the same time), denies that real agents are logically omniscient, so that discovering H entails E counts as new information even when E itself is old news. Howsons move instead asks what your credence in H would counterfactually have been if you'd never learned E in the first place, and uses that as the real baseline for confirmation. And then there's Worrall's more heretical answer, which just drops Bayesian confirmation altogether in favor of "use-novelty" - the idea that what actually matters is whether E was used to build H, never mind when E was discovered. I honestly can't tell whether any of these dissolve the puzzle or just shove the lump under a different rug. Could you write me an essay that lays the problem out properly, weighs these responses against each other on their actual merits, and then commits to a verdict instead of trailing off into "reasonable people disagree"? A conditional verdict is fine if that's genuinely where the argument leads - something like "Bayesianism survives if you grant the logical-omniscience move, otherwise you need Worrall's rescue from outside" - but I want an actual position defended, the way I'd have had to defend a result to a skeptical referee.
