I'm working on a feature about whether belief, desire, and emotion can genuinely penetrate perceptual experience itself, rather than just the judgments people form afterward, and I'd like the strongest version of this disagreement, not the popularized one. The standard exhibits for cognitive penetration — a softball that looks larger to a batter on a hitting streak, a hill that looks steeper to someone who is tired or carrying a heavy pack, a desired object that looks closer than an equally distant but undesired one — are precisely what Chaz Firestone and Brian Scholl target, arguing that these effects trace back to response bias, memory, or shifts of attention once what subjects say or do is separated from what they actually see. Defenders of penetration don't simply deny this: some argue that dismissing an effect as "merely attentional" begs the question, since attention is itself routinely deployed according to the subject's goals and expectations, and that a thesis which survives every counterexample by invoking some further, less contaminated measure starts to look unfalsifiable. Write a dialogue between two equally capable researchers, one a skeptic about cognitive penetration and one a defender of it, who work through a single one of these cases in real depth rather than trading slogans. Have the skeptic state plainly what result would change their mind, have the defender answer the unfalsifiability charge on its merits, and have them close by addressing what actually turns on the outcome: whether, as Susanna Siegel has argued, an experience already shaped by what one believes can still do any work in justifying that belief.
