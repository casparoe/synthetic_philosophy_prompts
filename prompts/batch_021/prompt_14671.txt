Two friends of mine, both regulars in EA-adjacent discussions of wild-animal welfare, argued recently about Robert Nozick's "utilitarianism for animals, Kantianism for people" proposal from Anarchy, State, and Utopia -- a view he floated but never fully endorsed, under which humans get strict side-constraints against being sacrificed for aggregate benefit, while animal welfare is simply maximized, with individual animals traded off against each other whenever the total benefit exceeds the cost.

1. Theo argued the split is principled rather than arbitrary: side-constraints are meant to track something like the capacity for autonomous, self-directed agency, and since animals plausibly lack this while humans have it, protecting only humans from aggregation isn't ad hoc.

2. Naomi replied that this can't be the real basis, since infants and humans with severe cognitive impairments lack that capacity too, yet nobody -- Nozick included -- thinks harvesting their organs for the greater good would be fine; so the view still owes an account of why protection tracks species membership rather than the very capacity it's supposedly grounded in.

3. Theo answered that the rationale can operate one level up: protection tracks being the kind of being that normally has the relevant capacities, not whether a given individual actually does -- which is why humans as a kind count and chickens as a kind don't.

4. Naomi countered that the "kind" move doesn't rescue the theory from its sharper problem: as stated, it permits inflicting arbitrarily severe suffering on one animal whenever the aggregate benefit to others is even slightly larger, with no stated floor -- which just relocates exactly the trade side-constraints exist to forbid onto a population the theory has chosen not to protect.
