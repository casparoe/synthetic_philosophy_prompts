Two colleagues of mine got into a running disagreement after last week's department reading group over a third colleague, Ines, who won't even open a forthcoming paper that purports to refute the central result of her dissertation. (1) Leah said Ines was being dogmatic: whatever her confidence, intellectual honesty requires reading a serious challenge before dismissing it. (2) Owen replied that Ines has spent six years stress-testing that result, and at some point she really does know it's true — and if she knows it, she already knows that any apparent counter-evidence must be misleading somehow, whether or not she can yet say how. (3) Leah objected that this can't be right, since it would mean that knowing something entitles you to disregard literally any future evidence against it, however good, which would make careful people like Ines indistinguishable from someone who simply never updates on anything. (4) Owen granted that the conclusion sounds bad, but said Leah hadn't located the actual mistake: grant that Ines knows her result, and the rest seems to follow just from what knowledge is, whatever we think of how it sounds. (5) Leah then suggested that this shows we should be stingier with the word "know" — reserving it for claims that no future evidence could ever unseat. (6) Owen answered that this concedes too much, since we still want to say working scientists know plenty of things while remaining genuinely open to revision, so the trouble must be located in the inference itself, not in our willingness to apply "know."

Please go through their exchange move by move and say who has the better of it at each point — not just who wins overall — and then, in a few sentences, say what you think both of them are missing.
