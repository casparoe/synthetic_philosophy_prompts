I've been rereading Leibniz's correspondence with Arnauld together with the closing pages of the Theodicy for a seminar on early modern metaphysics, and one issue keeps nagging at me. Against Arnauld's worry that the doctrine of complete individual concepts (first laid out in the Discourse on Metaphysics) makes God's choices unfree, Leibniz answers that a hypothetical Adam who never sinned would not be our Adam behaving differently, but simply a distinct possible person; he dramatizes the same point later in the Theodicy, where the goddess Pallas leads Theodorus through a palace of countless rooms, each containing a different possible Sextus Tarquinius living out a different life, of which God actualizes only one. Several commentators on Leibniz's modal metaphysics have argued that this "superessentialism" about individuals amounts, in substance, to a counterpart-theoretic account of cross-world identity avant la lettre, long before David Lewis gave the position its canonical twentieth-century formulation.

I'd like a considered guess, reasoning as strictly as possible from Leibniz's own commitments, at how he would have adjudicated the actual dispute between Lewis's counterpart theory — on which ordinary individuals are world-bound, so that cross-world talk about them is really talk about resembling but numerically distinct counterparts — and the rival view, associated with Kripke and later Plantinga, on which one and the same individual can be rigidly designated as existing, and differing in its properties, in more than one possible world. Does the doctrine that an individual's complete concept fixes every predicate ever true of it commit Leibniz to something close to Lewis's side of that dispute, or is there room in his system for genuine transworld identity despite full essentialism about properties? And how do you think he would respond to Kripke's "Humphrey objection" — the complaint that counterpart theory leaves an individual with no real stake in what happens to his counterparts elsewhere, since it is never strictly he who fares better or worse? Finally, and as a separate question from the identity issue, would Leibniz have accepted anything like Lewis's further thesis that non-actual worlds are exactly as concrete as ours, given that his own unrealized possibilities seem to exist only as ideas in the divine understanding rather than as parallel concrete universes?
