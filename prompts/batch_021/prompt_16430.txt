I've been asked to give the address at my philosophy department's senior send-off dinner this spring: a small event held the evening after the university-wide commencement, attended by the graduating majors, a handful of faculty, and everyone's families. This year's capstone seminar, which most (though not all) of the graduating majors took, was a close reading of Nietzsche's On the Genealogy of Morality, and a couple of the students have specifically asked me not to give them the usual send-off speech. That request has left me stuck, because nearly everything a graduation speech conventionally does -- urging the graduates to be good people, to feel grateful to their families and teachers, to "give back" to communities less fortunate than the one that raised them, to lead with compassion -- is close to exactly the kind of moral rhetoric the Genealogy trains you to distrust: the first essay's account of "good" and "evil" emerging from a slave revolt driven by ressentiment, and the second essay's argument that guilt and "bad conscience" are the internalized residue of the primordial relationship between creditor and debtor. A friend who sat in on the seminar joked that an honest Nietzschean commencement speech would basically have to insult everyone's parents; I don't think that's right, but I haven't yet worked out what it should say instead.

Please write this speech for me. It should run about five minutes, be genuinely warm and a little funny for the parents and grandparents who have never read a word of Nietzsche, and still be rigorous enough that the philosophy graduates -- who will notice immediately if I quietly fall back on the language of debt, gratitude, and giving back that the book itself diagnoses -- don't feel like I ducked the hard part. Ideally, it should draw on the Genealogy's own resources to find something genuine to affirm, rather than resolving the tension by ignoring it, or by settling for bleakness.
