I'm a second-year philosophy PhD student currently drafting a seminar paper on triviality results for indicative conditionals, and there is one issue I keep getting stuck on that I would like to work through properly rather than take on authority. Ernest Adams's original probabilistic account of indicative conditionals held, in a fairly modest and restricted way, that the assertability of a simple conditional "if A, then C" tracks the conditional probability P(C|A); crucially, Adams never committed himself to the claim that such conditionals express propositions with stable, context-independent truth conditions, and he explicitly confined the thesis to simple, non-embedded occurrences. Robert Stalnaker, by contrast, offered a genuinely truth-conditional semantics for conditionals, built around a selection function over possible worlds, and maintained that the resulting propositions should still satisfy essentially the same equation between a conditional's probability and the corresponding conditional probability — with no comparable restriction against embedded or compound occurrences.

David Lewis then showed, in "Probabilities of Conditionals and Conditional Probabilities" (1976) and its 1986 sequel, that no non-trivial proposition can have its probability equal to the relevant conditional probability across a sufficiently rich class of credence functions closed under conditionalization. My advisor mentioned, somewhat in passing, that this result is usually read as devastating for Stalnaker's project while leaving Adams comparatively unscathed, and that this asymmetry is a large part of why so many philosophers of language ended up gravitating toward non-truth-conditional or "suppositional" treatments of indicative "if." I would rather understand in detail why that asymmetry is supposed to hold than simply repeat it in my own paper without having worked it through myself.

Could you compare Adams's and Stalnaker's theses closely enough to spell out exactly what each is, and is not, committed to; explain why Lewis's triviality result bears so differently on the two of them; and then tell me which of the two positions — Adams's restricted, non-propositional thesis, or Stalnaker's fully propositional one — you find more defensible once the triviality results are taken into account?
