I teach decision theory in my university's philosophy department and serve, as the resident philosopher, on my affiliated hospital's Clinical AI Governance Committee. I've been asked to draft the memo the committee will vote on next week, covering the rollout protocol for a new deterioration-risk algorithm that currently reports a single calibrated probability of decline within twelve hours against a fixed alert threshold. Our data science lead wants to replace this point estimate with an imprecise probability — a range reflecting genuine uncertainty for patients whose vitals are sparse or atypical, rather than a false precision the data don't support — but the committee is split on what to do when a patient's range straddles the threshold. One natural decision rule (act on whatever maximizes expected benefit for at least one distribution in the range) and another (act on whatever does best in the worst case) can recommend different things for the same patient, or fail to settle on a single recommendation at all. Some members are also unsettled that ordering a confirmatory test can sometimes widen a patient's range rather than narrow it — a documented feature of reasoning under this kind of uncertainty known as dilation, not a bug, though they want to know whether it undermines the whole approach. Nursing leadership, meanwhile, warns that any recommendation this indecisive will simply be ignored at the bedside. Draft the memo: recommend a point estimate or a range, specify the decision rule and escalation protocol that should govern borderline cases, set an implementation timeline, and give the actionability worry its due rather than dismissing it as mere unfamiliarity with the new format.
