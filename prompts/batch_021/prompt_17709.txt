I'm a software engineer who got into Borges through "The Garden of Forking Paths" and assumed his view of time was basically that the future branches like a tree, not unlike branching version control. Then I read the essay "A New Refutation of Time," where he does something stranger: building on Berkeley's denial of matter and Hume's account of the self as nothing but a bundle of perceptions, he argues that a single perception recurring exactly — within one life, or across two different people — is enough to destroy the linearity of time, since nothing but an assumed timeline distinguishes the first occurrence from the second. I can follow Berkeley and Hume individually, but not how a merely repeated perception is supposed to undermine succession in general, rather than just being a strange edge case. And the essay seems to walk itself back at the end, conceding right after the line "And yet, and yet..." that our fate is terrible precisely because it's irreversible. Could you reconstruct the missing logical steps from "no persisting self" to "a repeated perception destroys linear time," and explain what work that closing reversal does to the argument overall?
