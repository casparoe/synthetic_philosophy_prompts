My grandma died twelve days ago at eighty-six, and I've somehow ended up being the one giving the hesped (the eulogy, I had to look up the spelling) at her funeral this Sunday, which is terrifying because she thought about this stuff way more carefully than I ever have. She grew up in a loudly secular, borderline anti-religious house and was basically a High-Holidays-and-that's-it Jew for most of her adult life, but in her late sixties she started auditing philosophy classes at the community college near her just to keep busy, and got completely hooked on a Hilary Putnam book on Jewish philosophy -- Jewish Philosophy as a Guide to Life, I think it's called, about Rosenzweig, Buber, Levinas and Wittgenstein. Its been sitting on her nightstand for a decade, a third of the pages dog-eared and covered in her handwriting, and I've been reading through it since she died, and it is NOT light material. It completely changed how she lived, though: she started davening, keeping much stricter kosher, going to shul every week, the whole thing.

Here's my problem. She was adamant, to me specifically and more than once, that none of this meant she'd decided there's a Being out there whose existence is a plain fact of the matter -- something settleable, as she put it (paraphrasing Putnam, I think, not her own line), from a "God's-eye view" -- and she'd get genuinely annoyed if you implied that. But she was just as annoyed at my uncle's version of the story, which is that she "just liked the ritual and the community" and didn't really believe any of it deep down -- she thought that was the exact same mistake in the other direction, the one Putnam spends half that book arguing against, because for her the relationship and the obligation were the real content, not decoration sitting on top of a secret atheist. So now I have to write a eulogy for a room that's half her old philosophy-audit-class friends, who will absolutely clock it if I get this wrong, and half our very traditional shul community and cousins who could not tell you what "metaphysical realism" means if their life depended on it. My roommate's advice was to just Google eulogy templates, which, no. It has to actually work as a eulogy -- warm, comforting, something you could say out loud at a funeral without people staring at their shoes -- but it can't quietly cheat toward either "she's up in heaven now watching over us" or "well, she didn't really believe any of it," because those are precisely the two things she told me, in so many words, not to let anyone say about her. Can you write it? Four or five minutes out loud is probably the right length.
