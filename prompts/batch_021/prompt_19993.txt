Okay, this is a weird one, but I could use some help thinking it through properly. I sit on the ethics committee for my state's independent used-car dealers association - I run a hardware store and got asked to join years back as one of the two non-dealer "public" members, so I'm not exactly qualified for any of this, I just show up. Most complaints we get are boring (guy didn't do a promised oil change, whatever), but one's been stuck in my head for two weeks now. A dealer had a pickup with big letters on the windshield sticker saying "CARFAX VERIFIED - ZERO ACCIDENTS!", sold it, and three months later the buyer's mechanic found it had clearly taken on serious water damage in a storm at some point, and that the previous owner had paid to fix it out of pocket instead of filing an insurance claim, so none of it ever showed up on any report. The dealer's defense is that he only ever repeated exactly what the Carfax said and never personally claimed anything about the truck himself; the buyer says nobody puts "ZERO ACCIDENTS" in huge letters unless they want you to believe the truck has a clean history, full stop, and that's obviously what its for. We genuinely don't even know if the dealer knew about the water damage himself - he says he just flipped it off a trade-in and glanced at the report, the buyer's mechanic says the damage should've been obvious to anybody who ever pulled the back seats out. Half the committee thinks that doesn't matter one bit, the other half thinks it's basically the whole question.

What got me going down a rabbit hole is that one of the dealers, defending his buddy, said something like "there's a difference between lying to someone and just not stopping them from fooling themselves." I started reading about this on my own and it turns out there's an actual philosopher - Grice, I think his name was - who wrote about the gap between what a sentence technically says and everything it ends up communicating just by being said in that particular situation. It mapped onto our case way better than our actual bylaws do, which just ban "deceptive advertising" without ever saying what that means. I have to send the committee a written recommendation before we vote next Thursday - dismiss the complaint, reprimand the dealer, make him refund part of the price, whatever the reasoning actually supports - and I don't want it to just lay out both sides and shrug, which is all I've managed to do in my own head for two weeks. Can you write that memo for me? It needs to land on an actual answer, it has to take the dealer's "I never said anything false" defense seriously instead of waving it off, and it needs to be written so the rest of the committee - a few dealers, a retired mechanic, and some chamber-of-commerce types - can follow the reasoning without any background in this stuff.
