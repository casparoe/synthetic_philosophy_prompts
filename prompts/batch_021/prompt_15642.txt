In my school's Classics elective, we spent a week on ancient Greek attitudes toward wine, and out of curiosity I ended up reading further into Plato's Laws than the excerpts we were actually assigned. I was surprised to find that the Athenian Stranger, in Books I and II, does not simply condemn drunkenness the way I had assumed a philosopher would; instead, he argues that a wine party, provided it is properly regulated by law and overseen by a sober elder who keeps order, can serve as a kind of test that exposes a person's underlying character, since the pleasure of wine loosens inhibitions much as fear exposes cowardice. He even suggests that deliberately practicing self-restraint while under wine's influence can help train the soul's capacity for moderation, so that a well-run drinking party ends up looking less like a vice to be tolerated and more like a genuine pedagogical tool.

For a different assignment on Aristotle, I noticed that the Nicomachean Ethics puts drunkenness to a rather different use. When Aristotle tries to explain how someone can act against their own better judgment (akrasia), he compares that person to someone asleep, mad, or drunk, who may be able to produce all the right words about what they should do without truly possessing or properly exercising that knowledge, the way an actor might recite a role, or a student might repeat a lesson just memorized. Here drunkenness seems to illustrate a gap between having knowledge and using it, rather than serving as a device for testing or training character.

I'd like help figuring out how seriously these two pictures actually conflict, since they came up in two separate assignments and neither of my teachers has drawn the connection for me. Could you compare Plato's account of supervised drunkenness as a diagnostic and pedagogical device for cultivating self-mastery with Aristotle's use of drunkenness as an analogy for the akratic person's peculiar relationship to their own knowledge? In particular, are Plato and Aristotle really making competing claims about what wine does to reason and character, or are they simply answering different questions that happen to both invoke drunkenness? Please don't stop at listing similarities and differences: tell me, and justify, which of the two views strikes you as the more philosophically coherent account of the relationship between intoxication and self-control.
