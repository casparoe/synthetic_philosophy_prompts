Okay this is going to sound like a weird combo but hear me out. I got really into that whole stoic/minimalist self-help wave thats everywhere right now -- the "want less," declutter-your-desires, detach-from-outcomes stuff, digital minimalism, people doing FIRE so they can retire at 35 and go find inner peace, all of it. Genuinely helped me alot for a while. Then a few months back I fell down a Wikipedia hole that started with Voltaire and ended up on Émilie du Châtelet, the woman who translated Newton's Principia into French, did serious physics of her own on the side, gambled constantly and sometimes lost huge amounts of money doing it, and had a couple of love affairs that apparently everyone at court knew about. Turns out she also wrote a whole essay on happiness -- I think it's called the Discourse on Happiness in English -- and her whole argument is basically the opposite of the stoic thing. From what I can tell, she thought you actually need strong passions, and even a decent amount of illusion or self-flattery, to be happy, and that people who kill off their own desires and try to live like some unbothered sage aren't reaching some higher state, they're just setting themselves up for boredom.

So heres my actual question: if you somehow sat Émilie du Châtelet down in front of all of this -- the meditation apps, the "detach from outcomes" content, the minimalist declutter-your-life books, guys retiring at 35 to go chase inner peace -- what do you think she'd genuinely say about it? Would she just call it the same mistake she was already arguing against back in the 1740s, just dressed up in new packaging, or is there some version of "non-attachment" she might actually be fine with, maybe because it's not quite what the old Stoics meant by it? I don't want a wishy-washy "she'd probably have mixed feelings" type answer -- I want you to actually argue it out using what she specifically believed, not just generic Enlightenment-guy-on-happiness vibes.
