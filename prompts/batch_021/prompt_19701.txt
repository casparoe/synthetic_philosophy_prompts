I'm taking a formal epistemology seminar this semester, and a disagreement broke out in my study group last week that I still haven't been able to sort out, even after our lecture on peer disagreement the following day. For one of our problem sets, we each had to independently work out a posterior probability for a fairly elaborate scenario (something like: given a base rate for a condition, an initial test with a known false-positive rate, and a second, less reliable confirmatory test that came back borderline, what credence should you assign to the hypothesis being true). Two people in my group, Elena and Marcus, are both strong students who've gotten essentially identical grades on every assignment so far, but they arrived at noticeably different final numbers — something like 0.29 versus 0.46. We spent close to an hour going through both derivations line by line on the whiteboard, and nobody could find a step in either one that was clearly mistaken; both looked locally valid, and the gap seemed to come from a genuinely ambiguous judgment call in translating the word problem into numbers, not from an arithmetic slip.

What I actually want help with isn't the original problem, but the argument Elena and Marcus then had about what to do given that they couldn't resolve it. (1) Elena said that since they were obviously epistemic peers on this particular question — same coursework, same track record, same information — the rational move for each of them was to treat the fact of disagreement itself as evidence and shift toward something like the average of their two numbers, rather than each just sticking with their own. (2) Marcus responded that he'd re-derived his answer five separate times and felt no less confident in it after seeing Elena's derivation than before; general peerhood on past problem sets, he said, doesn't obligate him to treat this specific derivation as a coin flip when he genuinely can't locate anything wrong with it. (3) Elena replied that "I checked it again and it still seems right to me" can't be what breaks the tie, since that feeling is exactly as available to whichever of them actually made the error as to the one who didn't; unless he had some further reason to think he was the one more likely to be correct — something other than his own reasoning still seeming sound to him — he wasn't entitled to just hold his ground. (4) Marcus countered that Elena's own principle looked self-undermining: he'd noticed, from a footnote in one of our readings, that professional epistemologists are themselves considerably divided over whether her "move toward the middle" view is even correct, so if she genuinely accepted that disagreement among careful peers should pull your credence toward theirs, she ought to be a lot less confident in that view than she was acting — and it seemed inconsistent for her to demand he conciliate on the probability problem while refusing to conciliate on the very principle she was using to demand it.

Could you go through their exchange move by move, (1) through (4), and say who has the better of the argument at each stage, rather than just declaring an overall winner? I'd also be curious whether there's some consideration both of them are overlooking that would actually break the impasse — particularly around the self-undermining objection Marcus raises in (4), which is where our study group's conversation just died out without anyone being satisfied.
