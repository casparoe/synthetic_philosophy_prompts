Been arguing with my friend Theo for like two hours last night about Lewis's *Convention* and neither of us backed down, so I want a neutral judge. Backstory: Theo's been on a Brian Skyrms kick (the stag-hunt and signaling-game stuff, evolution of conventions via simple reinforcement learning / replicator dynamics, that whole tradition, plus some Peyton Young on stochastically stable equilibria) and their claim is that this basically breaks Lewis's analysis. Lewis's definition of convention leans hard on common knowledge and this iterated "I expect that you expect that I expect you'll conform" structure — that's the machinery he needs to dodge the regress problem and to make convention do real philosophical work (it's also tied up with his whole project of defending a Gricean, use-based theory of meaning against Quine-ish skepticism that "convention" can even be given a non-circular analysis). But in the evolutionary models — including, ironically, signaling games, which Lewis himself introduces in the same book as a paradigm case of convention — you get populations settling into a stable, arbitrary, self-perpetuating regularity that satisfies Lewis's own arbitrariness/alternative-regularity clause, without any agent ever doing anything like representing higher-order expectations, let alone common knowledge in his technical sense. So Theo's line is: either common knowledge isn't actually necessary for convention, or you have to water it down to something so thin and dispositional that it's not doing any explanatory work, meaning Lewis's whole apparatus was overkill to begin with.

My pushback was that this conflates the causal/historical story of how a regularity arose with the question of what makes it a convention *now*, and that Lewis can just say the common-knowledge condition holds synchronically for the current population regardless of the diachronic process (reinforcement learning, drift, whatever) that got them there. Theo thinks that's basically a cop-out, since it makes the common-knowledge clause unfalsifiable against the actual cognitive states of agents in these models, who are often just simple learners with nothing resembling a representational "expects" state. We never resolved it and I'm tired of going in circles, so: can you actually settle this properly, engaging with the real structure of Lewis's definition (the coordination-equilibrium and arbitrariness conditions, and how he tries to get common knowledge off the ground without an infinite explicit regress), not just the one-line textbook gloss? Does the existence of these evolutionary/dynamic conventions genuinely threaten Lewis's account, or is there a principled way to keep the common-knowledge requirement while accommodating them — and if Theo's basically right, what's the smallest fix to the definition that would patch it?
