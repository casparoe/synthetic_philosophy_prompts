So a coworker talked me into joining this informal "great books" reading group at work, and we're grinding through Hobbes's Leviathan right now, which is... a lot, not gonna lie. I got stuck on a bit in chapter 4 where he's talking about names and definitions and then suddenly veers into geometry. Here's the exact passage:

"Seeing then that truth consisteth in the right ordering of names in our affirmations, a man that seeketh precise truth had need to remember what every name he uses stands for, and to place it accordingly; or else he will find himself entangled in words, as a bird in lime-twigs; the more he struggles, the more belimed. And therefore in geometry, which is the only science that it hath pleased God hitherto to bestow on mankind, men begin at settling the significations of their words; which settling of significations they call definitions, and place them in the beginning of their reckoning."

I get the surface-level reading (start from clear definitions or you'll trip over your own words), and not gonna lie the "reasoning is just reckoning" vibe he's got going kind of reminds me of arithmetic/computation, which is fun to me as a programmer. But a few things are bugging me that I can't find a good explanation for anywhere. Why does geometry specifically get singled out as THE science that's escaped this word-trap, when presumably every field uses definitions of some kind? Is there something about Hobbes's nominalism (truth being about how you string names together, not about things themselves) that makes geometrical definitions uniquely trustworthy in a way that, say, definitions in physics or theology supposedly aren't? I also vaguely remember reading that this connects to his bigger ambition of doing civil philosophy/politics "geometrically" too (definitions first, then strict deductions, supposedly like Leviathan itself is built), which seems like a wild thing to attempt for something as messy as politics - do people who study this seriously think he actually pulls it off anywhere, or does the whole thing fall apart once you loose the thread a bit? And as sort of a fun wrinkle: isn't this the same guy who spent years insisting he'd squared the circle and got completely dismantled by Wallis over it? Curious whether historians see that episode as undercutting this confident talk about geometry, or as a basically separate, unrelated story.
