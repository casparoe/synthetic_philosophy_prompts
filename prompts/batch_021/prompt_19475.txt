Okay so I've fallen down a bit of a Spinoza rabbit hole lately (zero philosophy background here, I just got curious after a youtube video on his whole "God equals Nature" idea and ended up finding a free translation of the Theological-Political Treatise online). I got to the chapter where he lays out what he means by "natural right" and it's kind of messing with my head. From what I can tell he's saying a thing's right is literally just equal to its power - he's even got this example about how a big fish has exactly as much "right" to eat a little fish as it has the power to actually catch and eat it, no morality attached at all, its purely descriptive. So as far as I can tell, for Spinoza "right" and "power" are basically the same word for the same thing, might makes right, no asterisks. I tried explaining the fish bit to my partner and even I couldn't make it sound like anything other than "the strong get to do whatever they want."

But then every summary of his politics I come across says he was this early defender of democracy, that he thought it was the most "natural" and best form of government, better then monarchy or aristocracy. And that's the part I can't square. If right is just however much power you actually have, shouldn't whoever ends up with the most raw power - a king, a warlord, whoever can out-muscle everybody else - automatically have the greatest right to rule, since that's literally what having more power means in his own system? It feels like starting from "might makes right" should lead you straight to justifying strongman rule, not "let's put it to a vote." Is he being inconsistent here, basically sneaking in a modern democratic intuition that his own premise doesn't actually support, or is there some specific move in his argument that actually gets you from "right equals power" all the way to "therefore democracy" without quietly dropping the might-makes-right part the moment it's inconvenient?
