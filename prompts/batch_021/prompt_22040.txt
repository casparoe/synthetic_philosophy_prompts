A friend and I spent most of a long dinner arguing about Jeremy Bentham's private, largely unpublished case against punishing consensual sexual acts between men — then a capital offense under England's sodomy laws. My friend's objection was that Bentham's felicific calculus is supposed to sum pleasure and pain wherever they occur, indifferent to their source; and if enough of his contemporaries felt genuine, intense disgust merely at such acts being tolerated, that aggregated disgust-pain could in principle outweigh the pleasure of the acts themselves, so that a consistent hedonist would have to recommend continued suppression rather than tolerance. I countered that Bentham's explicit rejection, elsewhere in his work, of what he called the "principle of sympathy and antipathy" as a legitimate ground for legislation gives him the resources to exclude that kind of disgust from the calculation before it is ever aggregated. Neither of us could convince the other. Write an essay that settles the dispute: does Bentham's mature utilitarian system, taken strictly on its own hedonistic terms, actually justify excluding aggregated antipathy-pain from the calculus, or does blocking that aggregation require him to import something — an implicit liberty or harm principle, say — that isn't really hedonistic bookkeeping at all? Weigh the strongest considerations on both sides, and close with a clear, defended verdict rather than a neutral survey.
