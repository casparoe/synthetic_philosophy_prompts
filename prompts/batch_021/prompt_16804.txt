In my seminar on Wittgenstein and the philosophy of language this term, two classmates, Nadia and Theo, got into an argument after class that a few of us listened to without joining in. The dispute was over whether Kripke's "skeptical solution" to the rule-following paradox actually escapes the very problem it is a response to.

Nadia opened by arguing that the skeptical solution succeeds precisely because it changes the question. Instead of asking what fact makes it true that I mean plus rather than quus by "+", we should ask only what entitles the community to treat my answers as correct. Once meaning-talk is governed by assertability conditions rather than truth conditions, there is no further fact left to demand, and the paradox loses its grip.

Theo objected that this just relocates the problem Kripke raised against dispositionalism. Individual dispositions failed to fix meaning partly because they are finite and error-prone: ask someone to add numbers large enough and their disposition runs out or misfires, yet we do not conclude they meant something other than plus. A community, however, is only a larger set of finite, error-prone dispositions, and on a case that outruns everyone's collective track record, the community has nothing determinate to converge on either. So the community view does not supply the missing fact; it just makes the finitude problem harder to notice.

Nadia replied that this objection only bites if the community view is meant to fix an extension the way dispositions were supposed to, and that is exactly the truth-conditional picture the skeptical solution rejects. The community is not a bigger calculator whose output determines the right answer; shared practices of correction and teaching are what give "correct" and "incorrect" any sense in the first place, since a solitary individual has no one to correct them. That is a claim about what makes normative talk intelligible, not a hidden fact about extension in disguise.

Theo pushed back: when the community does converge on a hard case, is that convergence authoritative or not? If it is, we need an account of why collective agreement, rather than individual disposition, has this rule-constituting power, and that account risks being just as arbitrary as the dispositionalism Kripke dismissed. If it is not authoritative, the community could be wrong en masse, which means some standard of correctness floats free of what the community actually does, which is exactly the independent fact the skeptical solution was supposed to let us do without.

Could you go through this exchange move by move and say who has the better of the argument at each point, not just overall? I would also like to know whether there is an asymmetry between the individual and communal cases that both of them are missing.
