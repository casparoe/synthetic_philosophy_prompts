I have spent the past few weeks reading through some of the more contentious entries of the Encyclopédie for a piece on the philosophes' internal quarrels, and I keep returning to the pamphlet war that Jean d'Alembert set off, seemingly by accident, with his 1757 article on Geneva. Tucked into an entry that was largely a backhanded compliment to the city's pastors — d'Alembert suggested their private theological views were considerably more liberal than their public Calvinism let on, which caused its own scandal — was a further proposal that Geneva lift its ban on public theatre. Rousseau's reply, the 1758 Lettre à d'Alembert, is the text I actually want help with, because the one-line gloss it usually receives ("Rousseau thought plays were immoral") misses nearly everything that makes the argument interesting.

What strikes me on a closer read is how many distinct, only loosely connected claims Rousseau bundles together under the heading of an attack on theatre. There is the argument that drama is structurally incapable of reforming a people's morals, since a playwright who fails to flatter an audience's existing tastes simply will not be produced — which raises the immediate question of how something powerless to improve morals can nonetheless be blamed for corrupting them. There is a separate argument that the profession of acting is itself a kind of institutionalized self-loss, the actor's whole skill consisting in impersonating what he is not. There is a striking piece of literary criticism claiming that a play as seemingly harmless as Molière's Misanthrope actually trains its audience to laugh at the sincere, awkward Alceste and to admire the socially fluent, complaisant Philinte. And there is the claim that one and the same institution could be nearly harmless in a large, already-dissipated monarchy like Paris while being genuinely ruinous for a small, still-egalitarian republic like Geneva. D'Alembert, for his part, seems to have thought he was making a fairly unremarkable Enlightenment case for refining manners through pleasure, and was startled by the force of the reaction.

Could you write a dialogue between Rousseau and d'Alembert that takes all of this seriously rather than flattening it into "theatre bad" versus "theatre good"? Stage it as a direct exchange — feel free to take whatever dramatic license the timeline requires — rather than two speeches stitched together. I would like d'Alembert to press hard on the apparent tension between "theatre cannot reform morals" and "theatre corrupts morals," and to defend the civilizing value of theatre for a public that, after all, is not asking to be turned into Spartans; and I would like Rousseau to answer that specific challenge on its own terms, rather than simply restating his conclusion, while also making room for the participatory civic festivals he proposes as Geneva's alternative to a playhouse. Neither side should be allowed to win by default.
