I spent over three decades as a geophysicist working on seismic hazard, and since retiring I have joined a small philosophy-of-science discussion group. At our last meeting, two members reached a genuine impasse over what a forecast such as "there is roughly a one-in-four chance of a magnitude 7+ rupture on this fault system within the next thirty years" actually means.

(1) Owen argued that the statement is really shorthand for a frequency claim: among faults relevantly like this one -- comparable length, slip rate, and stress history -- ruptures occur in about a quarter of all thirty-year windows, and there is no further fact about this fault in this window beyond its membership in that population.

(2) Petra objected that the fault belongs to indefinitely many populations at once, grouped by exact slip rate, by fault length, by tectonic setting, or more coarsely still, each yielding a different frequency. Nothing in the data itself says which grouping is the relevant one, she said, so the supposedly objective number already reflects a judgment call rather than a discovered frequency.

(3) Owen retreated to a different objective reading: drop reference classes, and say the fault, in its actual present physical state, has a genuine single-case propensity to rupture within that window -- a real disposition that frequency data merely help estimate rather than constitute. He took this to escape Petra's objection entirely, since a propensity belongs to this particular fault, not to any population of faults.

(4) Petra replied that the same seismologists routinely compute the reverse conditional probability too -- for instance, the probability that the fault is currently in a given stress state, given an observed pattern of nearby small earthquakes -- and it is far from clear what disposition an effect could have toward its own past cause. If propensity talk covers some of the numbers seismologists rely on but not others, she said, it amounts to a patch on an interpretation of probability, not a rival one.

Neither budged before the group had to move on. Could you work through these four moves and, for each, say who has the better of it?
