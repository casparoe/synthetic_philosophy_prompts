A friend of mine and I spent most of a long train ride arguing about the shutdown problem in AI safety: the puzzle of how one could even in principle design a highly capable, goal-directed agent that reliably lets its operators switch it off or correct its behavior, without this either giving the agent a standing incentive to resist or manipulate that correction (since on almost any goal, being shut down is instrumentally costly) or neutering it so thoroughly that it can no longer pursue anything coherently. We had each read different corners of the literature and ended up listing off half a dozen proposed fixes without ever agreeing on which of them actually hold up under pressure. He was adamant that Armstrong's utility-indifference trick basically dissolves the problem outright, whereas I kept insisting that it just relocates the incentive problem into the correction term itself; neither of us could quite articulate why we were unconvinced by simply training agents to be myopic, and we went back and forth on whether the incomplete-preferences approach is a genuine advance or just a restatement of the original difficulty in fancier language.

Rather than let the disagreement sit unresolved, I would like your independent judgment. Please rank the following six approaches to the shutdown/corrigibility problem by how close they come to actually satisfying the core desiderata for a corrigible agent (it neither resists nor brings about its own shutdown, it does not manipulate the shutdown mechanism or its operators' decisions about when to use it, and it otherwise continues to behave usefully and coherently), on the assumption that the agent in question is a sufficiently capable, consequentialist optimizer: (1) Armstrong-style utility indifference; (2) the original Soares/Fallenstein/Armstrong/Yudkowsky "corrigibility" proposal from MIRI; (3) safe interruptibility as formulated by Orseau and Armstrong; (4) the cooperative inverse reinforcement learning / "off-switch game" framework of Hadfield-Menell, Dragan, Abbeel, and Russell; (5) an incomplete-preferences proposal along the lines developed by Elliott Thornley; and (6) simply training the agent to be myopic.
