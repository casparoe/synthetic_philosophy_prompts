For National History Day this year, I chose a topic on Isaac Newton's theological motivations for his physics, and I've gotten stuck on a puzzle that further reading hasn't resolved. In his early 1690s letters to Richard Bentley, Newton argues that the tidy structure of the solar system — every planet orbiting the sun in the same direction, in nearly circular orbits, all roughly in the same plane, quite unlike the wildly eccentric orbits of comets — was too improbable to result from unguided natural causes, and therefore pointed to a deliberate, intelligent cause. But in the long concluding query of the Opticks (Query 31, in its final form), he goes further, suggesting that the mutual gravitational pulling of the planets and comets on one another would gradually introduce irregularities that could accumulate over time, so that the system's regularity would eventually break down and need God to step back in and set it right again. Leibniz seized on exactly this in his correspondence with Samuel Clarke, mocking the idea of a God who has to keep returning to fix his own machine — to Leibniz, a truly perfect craftsman would have built something that ran forever without adjustment, and Newton's God looked more like a mediocre watchmaker.

Here's what I can't stop thinking about: while researching further, I came across a popular-science book (I believe it was called Newton's Clock: Chaos in the Solar System) that traces exactly this story forward. Decades after Newton's death, Lagrange and Laplace used more refined perturbation theory to show that the very irregularities Newton worried about, particularly the long-puzzling drift in Jupiter and Saturn's motion, are actually periodic rather than cumulative, oscillating back and forth rather than piling up in one direction, so that on this specific point no divine correction is ever needed after all. There's even a famous, possibly embellished, story that when Napoleon asked Laplace why God appeared nowhere in his celestial mechanics, Laplace answered that he had no need of that hypothesis. The book then goes on to explain that much more recent chaos theory has shown the solar system's long-term behavior is still fundamentally unpredictable, but for a completely different reason than the one Newton had in mind: not because errors from comets and mutual gravitation pile up and need correcting, but because the system is sensitive to tiny differences in initial conditions in the strict mathematical sense.

Given all three of these later developments, what do you think Newton himself would have said? Would he have quietly conceded the specific stability argument to Leibniz once Lagrange and Laplace did the math, or does his broader theology give him some other way to resist that conclusion? And is the later discovery of chaos in the solar system actually good news for the kind of argument Newton was making in the Opticks, or is it too different a phenomenon to rescue his original point? I raised the chaos theory question with my physics teacher, and she admitted she had never thought about how it connects to Newton's theology, so I would really appreciate an answer that takes both the history and the underlying physics seriously, rather than just concluding that Newton was a religious man who also happened to do science.
