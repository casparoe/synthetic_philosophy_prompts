I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy. As part of my job, I'm building an internal review tool that uses linear probes and a sparse-autoencoder "feature dashboard" to flag hidden-activation directions in our deployed language model that correlate with deceptive or unsafe outputs, so a person can review the response before it reaches users. Reading around the mechanistic interpretability literature, I've found real disagreement over whether a probe- or autoencoder-identified direction is a genuine representation that the model's computation actually depends on, or whether it's better understood as a statistical regularity we're reading into the activations after the fact — a worry sharpened by findings that probes can decode surprisingly rich structure even from randomly initialized, untrained networks, and that ablating a flagged "feature" often doesn't change the model's behavior as much as one would expect if it were truly doing the causal work, since other components can partly compensate.

Treating that realism/anti-realism question as genuinely open rather than settled, please describe its most important implications for how much evidential weight my team should give a flagged feature: whether it reflects something the model is actually doing internally, or merely a pattern that happens to correlate with the behavior we care about.
