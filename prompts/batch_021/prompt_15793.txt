Student's answer: Hart's positivism rests on the separation thesis: whether a rule is law and whether it is just are two different questions, so an unjust statute can still be valid law. On this view, the Nazi-era statute criminalizing remarks against the regime was valid law when the husband was convicted, however wicked its content. Hart argued that the post-war court was right to treat that statute as never having been valid, since a rule so far removed from ordinary decency could not create a genuine legal duty to inform in the first place. Fuller rejects this sharp separation. He argues that law has an "inner morality" — Hart's own term for it is the minimum content of natural law — made up of procedural virtues such as generality, prospectivity, and consistency between announced rules and official action, which any system of rules must largely satisfy simply to count as law at all. Because the Nazi regime flouted these virtues so persistently, Fuller concludes that much of what it called law was not law, so the post-war courts owed it no special deference.
