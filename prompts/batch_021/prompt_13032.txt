Since retiring from a career in experimental physics, I have been working my way through the decision-theory literature, and I recently spent some time with the "Death in Damascus" puzzle usually credited to Gibbard and Harper. The setup struck me as essentially Newcomb's Problem dressed in fatalistic folklore: an infallible predictor, Death, has already gone to whichever city he foresees you fleeing to, so that whichever city you choose, he is there waiting for you. Causal decision theory is generally credited with giving the correct, two-boxing verdict in Newcomb's Problem by having agents ignore the correlation between their act and the predictor's forecast and reason only from the causal consequences of each option, and I assumed the same reasoning simply carries over to the Damascus case and vindicates causal decision theory all over again. I was surprised, then, to find contemporary decision theorists still treating it as a serious unsolved embarrassment for the causal approach rather than as one more feather in its cap. Am I missing some structural difference between the two puzzles, or is this mainly philosophers being reluctant to let go of a question that a working scientist would consider closed?
