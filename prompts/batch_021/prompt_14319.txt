And here's the answer I'm stuck on (grading is blind, so I've just got it labeled Response 7, no name attached):

Luminosity is the idea that whenever a condition actually obtains, the subject is thereby in a position to know, just by reflecting on their situation, that it obtains. The KK principle says that if you know that p, you know that you know that p; it's really just a special case of luminosity where the condition in question is knowing that p. Williamson wants to show that no interesting mental condition is luminous, and since knowing is itself a mental state on his view, this undercuts KK.

The argument goes roughly like this. Imagine tracking someone's sensation of temperature continuously from just after dawn, when it's freezing, to noon, when it's a hot day, in tiny slices of time so that any two adjacent moments feel exactly the same to the subject. Because our discriminatory powers are limited, our beliefs about how we feel aren't perfectly reliable: for a belief that you feel cold to count as knowledge, it isn't enough that you actually feel cold, the belief also has to be safe from error, so you have to feel cold in every neighboring moment that's indiscriminable from the one you're in. Williamson calls this the margin-for-error principle, and it's really the same basic insight that drives the sorites paradox for vague predicates like heap: tiny changes shouldn't be able to flip you from clearly knowing to clearly not knowing.

Now suppose luminosity is true. At the very first moment the subject clearly feels cold, so by luminosity they know it. By margin for error they must also feel cold one instant later, since that moment is indistinguishable from the first. But then by luminosity they know that too, so by margin for error they must feel cold in the next moment, and so on all the way to noon, when they obviously don't feel cold anymore. That's a contradiction, so luminosity is false for this condition, and Williamson argues the same reasoning generalizes to knowledge itself, so KK fails too. The takeaway is supposed to be that no one can ever know their own mental states just by introspecting on them, which is why Williamson thinks the traditional, Cartesian picture of the mind as transparent to itself has to go, and why he prefers an externalist picture on which even your evidence isn't always something you can identify just by looking inward.
