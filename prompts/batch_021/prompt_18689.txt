A few of us from my long-running rationalist meetup got into a drawn-out argument last week over the old naturalism-versus-normativism dispute in the philosophy of medicine — whether "disease" names a value-free biological fact or is normative through and through — and I don't think we resolved much beyond agreeing that Christopher Boorse's biostatistical account is more careful than either of us remembered from the last time it came up on a forum thread. I'd like an essay that does what our conversation failed to do: actually adjudicate the dispute, rather than just restating both positions and calling it a draw.

Here is roughly the shape I'd like it to take. Start from Boorse's naturalism, on which a disease is a statistically species-subnormal contribution of some bodily part or process to survival and reproduction, judged relative to a reference class (age, sex, and the like), with no essential appeal to what anyone values or finds distressing about the condition. Then bring the strongest normativist pressure to bear against this: historical cases such as drapetomania (the supposed nineteenth-century "disease" that made enslaved people flee captivity) and the declassification of homosexuality as a mental disorder, both of which look much more like tracking shifts in values and politics than the discovery of new biological facts, plus the more purely conceptual counterexamples meant to break Boorse's account from the inside — traits that are statistically abnormal yet clearly not pathological, and conditions that would come out statistically "normal" for some population while still seeming to be diseases. I'd also like the essay to take Jerome Wakefield's "harmful dysfunction" hybrid seriously as the obvious compromise — naturalistically-defined dysfunction plus normatively-defined harm — but without letting it off easily: does this hybrid genuinely rescue naturalism, or does it just relocate all the interesting normative work into the harm clause while the dysfunction clause quietly does much less than advertised?

Weigh all of this carefully, but don't land on the fence at the end. I want the essay to commit to an actual verdict on whether disease-ascriptions are, at bottom, normative all the way down, naturalistic once properly refined, or something more precise than either label allows — and then to make that verdict earn its keep by applying it to one live, contemporary dispute of your choosing (say, whether obesity or aging should count as a disease, or the controversy over the DSM-5's removal of the bereavement exclusion for major depressive disorder), rather than resting content with the abstract theory alone.
