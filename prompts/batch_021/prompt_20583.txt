Here's the question: In The Methods of Ethics, Sidgwick describes an unresolved conflict he calls the "dualism of practical reason." (a) Explain what this dualism consists in. (b) Explain how Sidgwick's later involvement in founding the Society for Psychical Research in 1882 can be understood as a continuation of the same underlying philosophical worry. And here's the student's answer: Sidgwick's dualism of practical reason is the tension between doing one's duty and following one's natural inclination toward pleasure — reason tells us to act morally, but our nature pulls us toward self-gratification, and Sidgwick can't find a way to prove that duty always wins out over inclination using reason alone. He thinks this is basically the deepest problem in his whole ethical theory, because without some further guarantee that being moral and being happy actually line up, the whole system of ethics risks collapsing into something arbitrary instead of staying a rational whole. This is why Sidgwick helped found the SPR in 1882 (he was its first president). He'd resigned his Trinity fellowship back in 1869 because he could no longer in good conscience subscribe to the Thirty-Nine Articles, so by the 1880s he had no orthodox religious grounds left for thinking there's a moral order to the universe that rewards virtue — he needed some other, empirical route to the same conclusion. What he was hoping psychical research would turn up was scientific evidence of personal survival after bodily death, which would suggest the universe is set up so the moral life gets rewarded after all, patching the gap in his ethics that pure reason couldn't close on its own. Along with Myers and Gurney he ran years of experiments on thought-transference and apparitions, and by the time he died in 1900 he was satisfied that the Society's mediums had finally furnished the proof he'd been looking for, giving his ethics the metaphysical backing it needed.
