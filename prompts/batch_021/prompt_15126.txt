As a longtime reader of the rationalist blogosphere, I've never quite shaken Eliezer Yudkowsky's thought experiment "Pascal's Mugging," in which a stranger claims to be able to grant or inflict a payoff so vast that even a vanishingly small credence in the claim, once run through an expected-value calculation, seems to demand you hand over your wallet. I'd like you to compare this argument with Pascal's original Wager. Is the Mugging best read as a special, degenerate case of the Wager's argument-form — a tiny probability multiplied against an effectively unbounded payoff, with expected value doing the rest — or does it raise a genuinely distinct problem for decision theory that the Wager alone never forced into view? Address in particular whether the classic "many gods" objection to the Wager, which trades on there being indefinitely many mutually exclusive deities and reward schemes on offer, has any real analogue in the single-mugger case. If it doesn't, say what that asymmetry reveals about whether the deeper flaw lies in theology or in unbounded expected-utility reasoning itself, and defend your verdict rather than simply cataloguing similarities and differences.
