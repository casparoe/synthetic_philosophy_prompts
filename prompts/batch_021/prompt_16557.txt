For the past few meetings, my high school's philosophy club has been working through a short packet on metaethics, and this week's reading introduced me to the "wrong kind of reasons problem," which is apparently a serious headache for "fitting attitude" (or "buck-passing") theories of value. As I understand it, these theories explain what it is for something to be good, admirable, or otherwise valuable by saying that the object has whichever property makes some attitude toward it fitting -- so "X is good" might just mean "it is fitting to desire X." The trouble is a classic counterexample: an evil demon threatens to torture an entire village unless you come to desire (or admire) a perfectly worthless saucer of mud. This certainly seems to give you a reason to desire the mud, yet nobody wants to conclude that the mud has thereby become genuinely desirable -- the reason is of the "wrong kind" because it is generated by your situation rather than by any actual features of the mud that would make desiring it fitting.

At our last meeting, one of the other club members tried extending this to blame, arguing that a boss who threatens to fire you unless you blame a scapegoated coworker for a mistake she didn't make gives you a "wrong kind" reason to blame her. A couple of us weren't convinced this really parallels the demon case, though, since blame (unlike desire) carries public, action-guiding implications that might change what is going on. So here is my request: construct one original example -- not a relabeled version of the demon/mud case, and not the boss/scapegoat case above -- in which the wrong-kind-of-reasons structure arises for a fitting-attitude analysis of some normative property other than goodness, for instance trustworthiness, admirability, or being a fitting object of gratitude or contempt. Please walk through the example carefully: name the attitude and property at stake, identify exactly which consideration counts as the "wrong kind" of reason to adopt that attitude, and explain precisely why that consideration fails to bear on whether the attitude is genuinely fitting, as opposed to merely being made prudent or otherwise advantageous to hold. If working through the case makes you suspect it does not map cleanly onto the demon scenario -- if, say, separating right- from wrong-kind reasons seems to require different resources here -- please flag exactly where and why the analogy strains.
