I sit on the clinical advisory board of a regional grief-support nonprofit -- we run peer groups and a referral network for bereaved families, and I ended up on the board a few years back after I did a series on grief science. We've got a genuinely heated fight going over whether to formally build the new "Prolonged Grief Disorder" diagnosis into our intake screening. It's the one added to the DSM-5-TR back in 2022: at least 12 months since the death for adults, plus a cluster of symptoms like intense yearning and "identity disruption" that's out of proportion to the person's cultural and religious norms. If we adopt it, clients who screen positive get fast-tracked to a licensed grief-therapy track instead of the regular peer groups, and it also means insurance can actually be billed, since right now nothing about ordinary bereavement is reimbursable. Half our clinicians think this is obviously good, evidence-based triage. The other half, including our founder, thinks it betrays the whole premise the org was built on -- that grief is a normal process, not an illness -- and worries a lot about whose norms even count as "normal" duration, since we get referrals from immigrant communities who's mourning customs look nothing like whatever population the DSM committee had in mind. (Someone on the board also pointed out that the WHO's ICD-11 version of this same diagnosis sets the threshold at 6 months, not 12, which didn't exactly reassure the skeptics that there's hard biology under the number.)

I've been down a rabbit hole on philosophy of medicine for an unrelated piece -- Boorse's biostatistical account of disease versus the more normativist, harm-based views like Wakefield's or Nordenfelt's -- and I'm increasingly convinced that debate is doing the real work in our disagreement, even though nobody on the board would put it that way. Could you draft the short memo I'd circulate to the full board before next month's vote? I need it to land on an actual recommendation -- adopt the criteria as written, reject them, or adopt with specific modifications -- not just a survey of both camps, and it has to take the founder's objection seriously on its merits rather than just citing the treatment's efficacy data and calling it settled.
