I have spent a good deal of time on LessWrong and related forums working through the standard Bayesian case for probabilism, and it seems to me that the Dutch book theorem, together with its converse, settles the matter outright: a credence function avoids a guaranteed loss across some suitably constructed set of bets if and only if it is additive and satisfies the Kolmogorov axioms. Imprecise credences—modeled as a set of probability functions, or equivalently as lower and upper previsions with a gap between an agent's buying and selling price for a gamble—are not additive point functions by construction, so doesn't the converse theorem already entail that any agent who adopts them is exploitable? I do not see what remaining work is done by Walley's weaker "avoiding sure loss" condition, since the classical result appears to rule out anything short of a single sharp probability measure. It also puzzles me that people keep constructing further, dynamic-choice versions of the Dutch book argument specifically targeting imprecise credences, as though the atemporal case had not already closed the question. Could you clarify where the scope of the original theorem actually stops applying?
