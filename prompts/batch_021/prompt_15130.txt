I'm currently drafting the final third of a novel built around a machine that sends my protagonist's mind backward into her own body twenty-three years earlier, and a philosopher friend has been needling me at dinner parties about whether my plot actually holds together. My draft currently treats the past as fixed no matter what she does once she's there — closer to the Novikov self-consistency principle from theoretical physics than to the branching-timelines device so many time-travel films rely on — but my friend insists this doesn't really dissolve the grandfather-paradox problem so much as relocate it, and pointed me toward David Lewis's old paper on time travel, with its distinction between a traveler's personal time and the world's external time, and his idea that ability-claims like "she can prevent the accident" are only ever true or false relative to whichever facts about the past we agree to hold fixed.

Rather than have me track down and work through the literature myself, could you lay out the most important implications of this debate for how I should actually construct the back half of the book? I'm less interested in a tour of the various positions than in what each approach — a single fixed timeline, a branching multiverse, or something in between — costs or preserves with respect to two things I care about dramatically: whether my protagonist's choices in the past can be called free in any meaningful sense, and whether the "her" who arrives in the past and the "her" the reader has been following for two hundred pages are still, in any philosophically defensible sense, the same person by the time the loop closes. If one of these approaches is simply more defensible on reflection than the others, I would rather find that out now than after I have written myself into a corner.
