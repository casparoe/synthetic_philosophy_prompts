At work I've been putting together an internal tool for deciding when to stop an A/B test and ship a feature rather than keep collecting data, and in reading up on the statistics behind that decision I ran across a result a former colleague of mine (a philosophy major before she became an engineer) calls "Good's theorem," after the statistician I. J. Good, from a paper apparently titled "On the Principle of Total Evidence." As I understand it, the theorem shows that for a Bayesian decision-maker, the expected value of any piece of cost-free evidence is never negative: looking before you choose can only help you or leave you exactly as well off, never hurt you, provided you update on it correctly and remain free to act exactly as you would have if you'd never seen it. My former colleague treats this as basically settling, once and for all, that "ignorance is bliss" is a comforting myth rather than a genuinely rational stance for anyone who wants to call themselves a good Bayesian.

That claim has been nagging at me, though, because it seems to clash with cases that strike me as obviously sensible rather than confused. My sister recently declined to learn the result of a predictive genetic test for a condition with no available treatment either way, on the grounds that finding out would only make her anxious without changing a single thing she'd do. Along similar lines, I have friends who ask not to be told sports scores before watching a delayed broadcast, and who report being genuinely happier, even in hindsight, for having avoided the spoiler. If Good's theorem really proves that a rational Bayesian can never be made worse off by free information, doesn't it follow that my sister and these friends are all committing a rationality error dressed up as a preference, and that a sufficiently rational agent would always choose to look? I would like to understand whether the theorem actually forces that conclusion, or whether I am stretching it further than it was meant to cover.
