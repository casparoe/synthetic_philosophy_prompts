I'm several months into reporting a longform piece on the philosophical genealogy of "post-truth" culture, and I keep running into an argument that several of my sources make about Richard Rorty. It goes roughly like this: Rorty's antirepresentationalism — his rejection, running from Philosophy and the Mirror of Nature through Contingency, Irony, and Solidarity, of the idea that beliefs and language "mirror" or represent a mind-independent reality — effectively concedes that no mind-independent world is doing any real explanatory work in our theorizing, so that "true" quietly collapses into "whatever a given community currently lets you get away with saying." On this reading, Rorty isn't just a distant ancestor of today's fights over whether scientific findings are "socially constructed"; he's already ceded the field to relativism, however much he protested otherwise.

A philosopher of language I interviewed for the piece pushed back hard on this, insisting that Rorty always maintained a distinction between the world's causal role — exerting pressure on us, constraining which beliefs survive contact with experience — and a representational or justificatory role, which is what he actually denied the world could play. I'll admit the distinction feels thin to me: if Rorty also denies that anything outside of language, some vocabulary or other, can ever adjudicate between rival vocabularies, isn't that just relativism (or, if you push on the metaphysics, idealism) wearing a more careful vocabulary? Before I commit to a characterization for a general readership, I'd like to understand whether that causal/justificatory distinction is doing genuine philosophical work or is more of a semantic escape hatch, and, more broadly, exactly what antirepresentationalism does and doesn't commit Rorty to.
