Hey, I'm drafting a short piece for our department's philosophy-of-math reading group and could use a second pair of eyes before I flesh it out into something longer. The hook is that old joke (usually pinned on Jerry Bona) that the axiom of choice is obviously true, the well-ordering theorem is obviously false, and nobody can tell about Zorn's lemma. Here's roughly the argument so far:

- AC, the well-ordering theorem, and Zorn's lemma are all provably equivalent to each other within ZF -- accept any one and the other two come for free, no extra axioms needed.
- And yet working mathematicians' gut reactions to the three are totally different: AC reads like a harmless closure principle, the well-ordering theorem reads like its promising an explicit well-order of the reals that nobody can actually produce, and Zorn's lemma just reads as bureaucratic poset-shuffling that's hard to have any intuition about at all.
- There's also a nice historical wrinkle (though check me on this): Kuratowski basically had a version of the same maximal-element statement in the early 1920s, and Hausdorff's maximal principle is older still, so "Zorn's" lemma is a bit of a Stigler's-law situation -- Zorn's actual contribution in his 1935 paper was less discovering the statement and more insisting mathematicians use it routinely instead of transfinite induction.
- My payoff point: this is a real problem for any epistemology of math that treats intuitive "obviousness" as evidence of truth (I'm gesturing at Gödel's stuff on set-theoretic intuition being perception-like) -- since apparently obviousness doesn't survive logical equivalence, either most mathematicians are just wrong about two of the three, or "intuitive evidence" in math is way more sensitive to how a claim happens to be formulated than that kind of view lets on.
