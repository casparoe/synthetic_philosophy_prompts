I'm a PhD student in philosophy of logic and language, currently drafting a dissertation chapter on deflationism about truth. I keep running into the conservativeness argument that Stewart Shapiro and Jeffrey Ketland pressed against deflationists in the late 1990s, and while I can recite the formal result, I don't yet feel why it holds or why it's supposed to be as damaging as the literature suggests. My advisor summarized the whole thing in one meeting as "basically Gödel's second theorem wearing a truth-theoretic disguise," which I suspect is roughly right but hasn't actually made anything click for me.

The setup, as I understand it, is this. Deflationists want a theory of truth that is conservative over whatever base theory it gets attached to, since that is supposed to cash out, in a precise and checkable way, the idea that truth is not a substantive, explanatorily potent property but merely a device for disquotation and generalization. The trouble is that a fully compositional, Tarski-style truth theory over Peano Arithmetic stops being conservative the moment induction is allowed to apply to formulas containing "true," rather than just the purely arithmetical ones: the resulting theory proves a reflection principle for PA, and thereby Con(PA), which PA cannot prove of itself by Gödel's second incompleteness theorem. Restrict induction back to the arithmetical vocabulary, and conservativeness is restored -- but only at the cost of being unable to reason inductively about anything expressed using "true."

Could you help me get a gut-level sense of two things? First, purely technically: what is it about letting induction see the truth predicate, specifically, that generates all that extra proof-theoretic strength, such that the non-conservativeness seems to be "located" in that one move rather than in the compositional clauses themselves? Second, philosophically: why should proof-theoretic strength be the right proxy for metaphysical "substantiality" in the first place, such that a gap of this kind is supposed to embarrass the deflationist? I would also value your own considered view, rather than a survey of the literature, on which standard deflationist response -- restricting induction along the lines Field suggests, denying that conservativeness was ever a non-negotiable commitment of deflationism, or simply accepting the extra strength as no real threat -- actually holds up best under pressure.
