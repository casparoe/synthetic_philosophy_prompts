I cover science, data, and ideas for a national newspaper, and much of my recent reporting has traced how algorithmic risk-assessment tools are used in criminal courts. Partly on the strength of that work, a state's newly convened Sentencing Risk-Assessment Advisory Panel has asked me to join as its public member; the group is now finalizing the guidance memo that will accompany its new recidivism-risk instrument. The tool outputs sentences like "this defendant has a 34 percent likelihood of a violent re-arrest within three years," and the panel has discovered, to its own surprise, that its deepest disagreement isn't the now-familiar one about fairness across demographic groups — that dispute has already played out elsewhere — but something more basic: nobody on the panel agrees on what the 34 percent actually means for the one person it is attached to.

Three factions have formed. One wants the memo to gloss the number as a plain frequency: 34 percent of people who match this defendant's measured risk profile go on to reoffend, and courts should be instructed not to read anything further into it. A second wants it described as the defendant's own objective propensity to reoffend, on the grounds that judges will inevitably, and not unreasonably, want a fact about the specific person before them rather than about a class of people that person merely resembles statistically. A third, led by the panel's two staff statisticians, wants the memo to state candidly that the figure is a calibrated subjective credence licensed by the model's training data and nothing more, and that the other two factions are each smuggling in an objectivity the number cannot bear. I described the standoff to a philosopher of science I know socially, who was delighted to point out that the panel had, without quite meaning to, reinvented a long-standing dispute about what probability really is — a fact about groups, a fact about individuals, or a fact about the person doing the judging.

I have to produce the panel's draft memo before its vote in six weeks, and I'd like your help writing a serious version of it — not a survey of the three positions but an actual recommendation. It should adopt one interpretation, or an explicit and workable combination, as the panel's official line, stated in language exact enough to insert directly into the report template judges will read. What I most want is for the memo to be honest about the strongest difficulty facing whatever it recommends: if it leans frequentist, the trouble of privileging one reference class among the many this defendant simultaneously belongs to; if it leans toward propensity, the strangeness of treating a number built from other people's already-known outcomes as if it expressed this particular person's own forward-looking causal disposition; if it leans subjectivist, the discomfort of telling a courtroom that a number entered as expert evidence is, at bottom, someone's calibrated opinion. Please don't hedge the ending: close with the concrete recommendation and the specific sentence or two the panel should vote to adopt.
