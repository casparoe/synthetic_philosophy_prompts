I'm a PhD student in philosophy of physics, and for the past few months our foundations-of-quantum-mechanics reading group has been working slowly through Bell's original papers alongside the Clauser-Horne-Shimony-Holt literature. Something has been bothering me that none of the standard secondary sources seem to address head-on: popular treatments compress "locality" into a single assumption that experiments have refuted, but the actual derivation of the CHSH inequality really rests on two logically separable premises — (i) that each side's outcome is a function only of its own setting and the hidden variable (factorizability), and (ii) that the hidden variable's distribution is statistically independent of which settings will later be chosen (measurement independence, sometimes called the "no-conspiracy" or "free choice" assumption). Every discussion I've read mentions (ii) only to wave it away as obviously innocuous, but nobody actually shows me the mechanics of what denying it would look like, so I can't tell whether the dismissal is principled or just an appeal to intuition about free will.

Could you construct a concrete toy model for the standard two-setting-per-side CHSH scenario that preserves factorizability perfectly and reproduces the quantum-mechanical violation of the inequality purely by correlating the hidden variable's distribution with the experimenters' eventual choice of settings? I would like enough explicit structure — a sample space for the hidden variable, the local response functions on each wing, and the specific correlation with settings that does the work — that I can see exactly where the "conspiracy" is located, rather than just being told in the abstract that such models exist. Once I can see the mechanism clearly, I would also like your help thinking through something I need to take a position on for my dissertation prospectus: is there a principled asymmetry that makes this kind of fine-tuned correlation a worse cost to bear than abandoning factorizable locality outright, as orthodox quantum mechanics effectively does, or is the near-universal preference for protecting measurement independence ultimately just a matter of which failure mode physicists find more tasteful? If it's relevant, I would also be curious whether retrocausal proposals that violate measurement independence through a time-symmetric mechanism, rather than a fine-tuned common cause in the shared past, genuinely escape the conspiracy charge, or whether that's a distinction without much of a difference.
