Last weekend a friend and I got into a long argument over dinner about what money-pump arguments actually prove. He was defending the orthodox line: if an agent's preferences are cyclic (or even just incomplete) over some set of options, a clever counterparty can walk her around a loop of individually acceptable trades until she has paid to return to her starting point, and this exploitability is supposed to be a knockdown demonstration that rationality requires transitive, complete preferences. I countered with the resolute-choice reply that's become standard in the dynamic-choice literature: an agent who commits in advance to the best overall plan and then sticks to it, even at the moment she'd locally prefer to defect, is never actually pumped, so the exploitability looks like a fact about naivety rather than about the underlying preference structure. Neither of us could really settle why "resoluteness" is supposed to bind an agent who, by hypothesis, prefers to switch at exactly the moment she's being asked to hold the line, and we ended up going around in circles almost as neatly as the money pump itself.

As it happens, next week's guest on the decision-theory podcast I co-host is a philosopher who has built much of her career defending resolute choice as the correct diachronic answer to money-pump arguments, including the newer versions of the argument that target incomplete rather than merely intransitive preferences. I don't want the episode to consist of her simply re-explaining the naive/sophisticated/resolute taxonomy to an audience that has already read the papers, so could you draft eight to ten interview questions that push on where I suspect the view is most exposed? I'd like at least one question on the separability worry -- that resolute choice looks like it solves the problem only by smuggling the plan's history into the description of the later option, so that what looked like choosing against her own preference turns out to be true by redefinition; one on whether the fact that a foresighted (sophisticated) chooser must sometimes forgo a good trade at the very first step, purely to avoid a cycle later on, counts as a rationality cost that reintroduces the case for transitivity under another name; one on whether her defense carries over cleanly to the completeness version of the argument, given that an agent with merely incomplete preferences is never actually forced to accept the first trade the way an intransitive agent is; and one on how she blocks the generalization of her view to cases like an addict's resolve or Ulysses at the mast, where "sticking to the plan against later temptation" is not obviously a mark of rationality at all. For each of these, please also note the reply she's most likely to give and a sharper follow-up keyed to that specific reply.
