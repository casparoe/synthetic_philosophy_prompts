A few of my grad students and I run a decision theory reading group, and after last week's session two of the regulars got into it over drinks in a way I haven't been able to stop thinking about. One of the group, Owen, mentioned offhand that he always wants more time on something when the deadline is still months away, but as soon as it's actually close he wants it over with immediately, even at some cost to himself. Someone recast this as the standard textbook reversal: far in advance Owen would take $100 in a year over $90 in eleven months, but once the eleven-month mark actually arrives he flips and grabs the $90 right away rather than wait one more month for the $100. Two other regulars, Rosa and Sam -- the same two who once spent an entire session litigating whether Newcomb's problem is even well-posed -- spent a good fifteen minutes arguing about whether this pattern makes Owen irrational, and both of them were landing real points, so I could use an outside read.

(1) Rosa opened by saying this is a money pump waiting to happen: a trader could sell Owen a switch from the $90-at-eleven-months payout to the $100-at-a-year payout while he's still far off and patient, charging a small fee, then sell him a switch back once he's close and impatient, charging another fee -- and he'd end up with exactly the same $90 at eleven months he could've had for free, just poorer, with nothing in the world having changed in between. That kind of exploitability, she said, is what shows a preference pattern is defective rather than merely quirky. (2) Sam granted that the pump works in principle but said it only bites naive agents who re-decide from scratch each time without noticing their own pattern; someone who sees the reversal coming can just commit at the outset to the option his near-term self will actually stick with, or pay once for a binding commitment device, and then he's never pumped more than once if at all -- so the mere possibility of the pump shows nothing about Owen specifically, only about people who don't see it coming. (3) Rosa's comeback was that needing a commitment device concedes the whole point: the payoffs and dates never change, only how far off "now" is changes, so a reversal that requires outside enforcement to head off is just diachronic inconsistency wearing a prudent disguise. (4) Sam replied that "nothing has changed" is too quick -- what's changed is Owen's distance from the reward, and caring more about proximity as it closes in isn't obviously incoherent, since even a garden-variety exponential discounter does that; the real question is whether Owen's discounting is inconsistent with its own earlier rate, not whether he discounts at all, and maybe the assumption that a person's preferences owe each other agreement across time -- rather than each time-slice just being rational by its own lights, what people sometimes call time-slice rationality -- is one nobody's actually argued for. (5) Rosa's last word, before the food showed up and everyone got distracted, was that if you give up cross-time requirements that easily, you lose any way of explaining why the sequence of Owen's time-slices can be worth strictly less to Owen overall then a policy any single one of those time-slices would itself have endorsed, which seems like exactly the kind of fact a theory of rationality shouldn't have to shrug off.

Could you go through moves (1) through (5) and say, for each, whether it holds up and why, rather than just declaring an overall winner?
