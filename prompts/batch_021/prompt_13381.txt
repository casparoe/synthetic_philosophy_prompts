Three months ago I turned down a substantially higher-paying offer from a management consulting firm in order to stay on at the small education nonprofit where I have worked since graduating, and I mentioned this, with some quiet pride, at a family dinner over the holidays. My brother-in-law, who has a background in economics, was unmoved: he argued that under revealed preference theory, my decision to stay simply reveals that the nonprofit job was, all things considered, what maximized my own utility — salary, meaning, status, guilt-avoidance, or whatever the mix — so there was nothing self-sacrificing about it, and indeed that no choice I could ever make could count as self-sacrificing, since whatever I chose would, by definition, be what I most preferred. I had no good rebuttal at the time, but the exchange has stayed with me, and I have a hazy memory from an undergraduate philosophy-of-economics elective that there is a well-known distinction, which I believe is due to Amartya Sen, between two kinds of behavior that look non-self-interested, only one of which actually creates trouble for an argument like his.

Could you lay out the most important implications of revealed preference theory, and of the standard objections to it, for whether my brother-in-law's argument actually holds up? I would especially like to understand whether there is some principled way, from within or against the revealed-preference framework, of saying that a choice can really be self-sacrificing or altruistic in a sense that is not just true by stipulation, or whether the framework genuinely does collapse the whole distinction between selfish and unselfish action into something empty. I am not looking for reassurance that my decision was obviously virtuous; I would like the actual state of the argument, technical wrinkles included.
