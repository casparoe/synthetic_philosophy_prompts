Question: Harsanyi (1955) argues that if both individual preferences and social preference over lotteries on social states satisfy the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms, and social preference additionally satisfies a Pareto condition relative to the individual preferences, then the social utility function must be an affine combination of the individual utility functions with nonnegative coefficients: W = a_1U_1 + ... + a_nU_n + b. (a) State the theorem's hypotheses and conclusion carefully, being explicit about what it does and does not assume regarding interpersonal comparisons of utility. (b) It is sometimes said that this theorem furnishes a rigorous derivation of utilitarianism from individually plausible axioms. Assess this claim, engaging with at least one substantive objection from the secondary literature.

Student's answer: Harsanyi's setup takes a fixed population of n individuals and a set of social lotteries — probability distributions over a set of social outcomes — over which each individual has a preference ordering, and society has a preference ordering of its own. The first assumption is that every individual's preferences, and society's preferences, satisfy the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms (completeness, transitivity, continuity, and independence), so each can be represented by an expected-utility function; each individual utility function U_i is unique only up to a positive affine transformation, and the same is true of the social utility function W. The second assumption is a Pareto condition: if every individual is indifferent between two lotteries then so is society, and if every individual weakly prefers one lottery to another, with at least one strict preference, then society strictly prefers it too. Given these assumptions, Harsanyi shows that W can be written as a weighted sum of the U_i plus a constant, with all the weights a_i non-negative. Nothing here requires interpersonal comparability in the sense of a common unit or a common zero point for utility; the theorem only pins down each U_i up to its own separate affine transformation, which is part of why the weight attached to one person's utility cannot straightforwardly be compared to the weight attached to another's.

Intuitively, the result falls out of the impartial-observer construction that Harsanyi builds the argument around: society is treated as an impartial spectator who imagines occupying each individual's position with equal probability 1/n, and maximizing expected utility from behind this veil automatically averages the individual utilities together, which is exactly where the linear, weighted-sum form comes from. This is often presented as a formal vindication of utilitarianism, and it's easy to see why: a spectator who is equally likely to be any member of society is, in effect, choosing so as to maximize the average level of well-being in it.

This reading has not gone unchallenged. Sen pointed out that because each U_i is unique only up to its own affine transformation, the weights a_i are not fixed by anything in the axioms, so the very same underlying preferences are compatible with wildly different, non-equal weightings once the individual scales are renormalized, and nothing in the setup tells us which renormalization to prefer. Sen's conclusion is that Harsanyi's proof does not actually go through as stated: once the comparability issue is made explicit, the step from the axioms to a determinate linear aggregation rule has a gap that expected-utility theory alone cannot fill, so the aggregation theorem itself does not hold in full generality. Later commentators such as Weymark have continued to debate how damaging this gap is, but the basic difficulty Sen identified is generally accepted as a real problem for reading Harsanyi's result as a proof of utilitarianism.
