For the past two weeks, my early modern philosophy seminar has been working through Berkeley, and this week our instructor deliberately steered us away from the usual focus on "esse est percipi" and immaterialism toward a much less discussed text: The Querist, his late series of rhetorical queries on the state of the Irish economy. I was struck by how firmly Berkeley denies that money constitutes wealth in itself. Across dozens of these queries, he keeps returning to the idea that gold and silver are merely tickets or counters, used to reckon and transfer a more basic kind of wealth that he locates in the productive "industry" of a nation's inhabitants rather than in any stockpile of bullion. He also seems remarkably open to the notion that well-managed paper credit, issued through something like a national bank, could serve this reckoning function just as well as metal coin, provided the public has sufficient trust in the institution behind it.

A few of us got into a debate after class about how Berkeley would have reacted to cryptocurrencies such as Bitcoin, and I haven't been able to settle it in my own mind. On one reading, Bitcoin vindicates Berkeley's central move, since its value obviously has nothing to do with any intrinsic material property and depends entirely on collective acceptance, much as he argued was already true of coin and paper alike. On another reading, though, Bitcoin's whole design philosophy seems to run against the credit-and-trust model Berkeley favored: it is built specifically to avoid reliance on any managing institution or public authority, and its appeal to many of its proponents lies precisely in reproducing a fixed-supply, quasi-commodity scarcity reminiscent of the very gold fetishism Berkeley spent The Querist arguing against. Which reading do you think Berkeley would have endorsed, and is there a way of using his own arguments to decide between them? I would also be curious whether you think his immaterialism about the physical world more generally, his insistence that a thing's reality lies in its being perceived and used rather than in some hidden material substance, does any real philosophical work here, or whether that would be over-reading a connection between two fairly separate parts of his corpus.
