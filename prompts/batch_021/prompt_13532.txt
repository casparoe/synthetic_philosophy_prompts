I spent almost forty years as an experimental nuclear physicist before I retired, and for the last year or so I've gotten properly hooked on philosophy, mostly philosophy of science stuff (my daughter, who's doing a PhD in the subject, got me started and now I can't stop). Something I read recently about theories of evidence and confirmation dragged up an old dispute from my postdoc days that I never actually settled, and its been bugging me ever since, so I was hoping you could help me think it through properly.

Back then a colleague and I were independently fitting the same batch of noisy decay-rate measurements, maybe forty data points total. I fit mine with a simple two-parameter exponential, which left a small, fairly patternless scatter of leftover error at every point. He fit the identical data with a high-order polynomial that threaded through every single point exactly, zero error anywhere. He argued his curve was strictly the better model of the two because it accounted for 100% of what we'd actually measured, and that mine was just throwing information away. I argued back that his curve wasn't really "explaining" the data so much as just relabeling it point by point in fancier language, but when he pushed me to say what I actually meant by that, all I had was "well, mine's simpler" -- which he said was just aesthetic preference physicists get trained into, not a real argument.

So here's what I'd like help with, thirty-odd years later: was I actually right to trust my curve more, and if so, is there any way to make that rigorous instead of just waving at Occam's razor as a slogan? And separately, if you knew nothing about the two models except how each one was built, is there a principled way to say which deserves more trust for predicting the forty-first point we hadn't measured yet, or does that always just bottom out in background assumptions we're smuggling in from somewhere else?
