Updateless decision theory (UDT) was developed to handle a class of problems in which an agent that updates on new evidence before choosing an action ends up predictably worse off than an agent that simply commits, once and for all, to whatever policy would have looked best before that evidence came in. The sharpest illustration is the "counterfactual mugging": a perfect predictor, Omega, flips a fair coin. If it lands heads, Omega will pay the agent $10,000, but only if Omega has predicted that the agent would, had the coin instead landed tails, hand Omega $100 in return for nothing. If it lands tails, Omega simply appears, truthfully explains all of this, and asks for the $100. An agent that reasons from its actual, fully updated epistemic state — the coin has already landed tails, full stop — can see no way in which paying now brings about any benefit, since the heads-branch and its $10,000 are now permanently counterfactual, and so refuses. An updateless agent pays regardless, on the grounds that the policy of paying was the one that maximized expected value before the coin was flipped, and that being the kind of agent who pays is exactly what would have made it eligible for the $10,000 in the first place. The scenario is a staple of decision-theory discussion in and around AI alignment research precisely because, unlike Newcomb's problem, it drives a wedge between updateless views and essentially every theory — causal, evidential, or otherwise — that chooses by conditioning on the world as it is now known to be.

Write a dialogue between a causal decision theorist and an updateless decision theorist who each, independently, find themselves standing before Omega at the exact moment tails has been announced and the $100 requested. Before answering, each turns to argue with the other about what the rational response is, and each tries to talk the other out of their position. Treat both as equally sophisticated, and have them genuinely engage each other's strongest moves rather than talk past one another. Two possible threads worth working through, though neither is obligatory: what it could even mean to be "the kind of agent who would have paid" when no actual commitment was ever made or promised, and whether a causal decision theorist who was free to redesign their own dispositions in advance of the coin flip would deliberately rewrite themselves into an agent who pays — and if so, what that concedes about the rationality of refusing once the chance to self-modify has passed. Let the dialogue run to several rounds of genuine exchange on each side, and end it not with a capitulation but with each side stating, in a sentence or two, exactly where it now locates the crux of the disagreement.
