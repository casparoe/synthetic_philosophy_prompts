I'm a PhD student in philosophy working on formal epistemology and decision theory, and something that happened to a close friend of mine this year has been nagging at me in a way that my coursework on choice under risk hasn't quite equipped me to resolve. She went through two separate legal disputes with the same former employer, and both her own lawyer and the employer's insurer ended up relying on the same litigation-analytics service, a tool that mines records from thousands of comparable cases to produce fairly precise percentage predictions for how each option would likely play out. I mentioned the case to a few people in my cohort over coffee, and we couldn't agree on how seriously to take it, which is why I'd like an outside opinion.

In the first dispute, over a workplace injury, the insurer offered a guaranteed settlement of $60,000. The analytics service estimated that going to trial instead would give her a 12% chance of a $300,000 award, an 83% chance of the same $60,000, and a 5% chance of walking away with nothing if the claim were thrown out on procedural grounds. She took the guaranteed $60,000 without much hesitation. Some months later, in an unrelated dispute over unpaid overtime with the same employer, there was no settlement on offer at all: she had to choose between pursuing the claim under one statute, which the service gave a 17% chance of yielding $60,000 (83% chance of nothing), or under a different statute, which gave a 12% chance of yielding $300,000 (88% chance of nothing). This time, quite confidently, she picked the 12%-chance-at-$300,000 option over the 17%-chance-at-$60,000 one.

When I told her that I couldn't find any single way of valuing $0, $60,000, and $300,000 that would make both of these choices come out as clearly correct, she said I was thinking about it wrong: she wasn't assigning different worth to money across the two cases, she just couldn't stand giving up a sure thing for a small chance of ending up with nothing, whereas once nothing was already the overwhelmingly likely outcome anyway, she figured she might as well swing for the bigger prize. That sounded to me like it could be a real insight into what she cares about, or it could just be a fluent-sounding rationalization for two gut calls that don't actually cohere. Could you help me think through whether her pair of choices amounts to a genuine failure of rationality, or whether there's a principled way of treating someone with her attitude toward risk as fully rational, and how the major frameworks for thinking about decision-making under risk would each diagnose a person who reasons the way she did?
