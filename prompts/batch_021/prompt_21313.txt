In my epistemic game theory seminar this term, we covered Rubinstein's electronic-mail game as a way of showing that "almost common knowledge" is not simply a slightly weaker cousin of common knowledge. Two players stand to profit from jointly switching to a risky action, but only if a certain state has occurred, and only Player 1 directly observes whether it has; confirmation is then relayed back and forth by automated email, with each message independently lost with some small probability, so the exchange stops after a large but random number of successful deliveries. Because whoever sends the last message can never be sure it arrived, the two players never quite reach common knowledge of how many confirmations went through, however many actually did, and the essentially unique equilibrium has both of them playing it safe regardless, so the profitable coordination is lost almost for certain even after dozens of confirmations succeed. I can follow the backward-induction argument that forces this unraveling step by step, but I can't get an intuitive handle on why the unraveling has to run all the way back to the start rather than stopping once the confirmations feel "high enough" to trust. Could you help me see, at a gut level, why no finite chain of confirmations can substitute for true common knowledge here, and what this suggests more generally about why common knowledge does so much more strategic work than mutual knowledge of any fixed order?
