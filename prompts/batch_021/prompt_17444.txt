So I've been slowly working through Pascal's Pensées for a seminar and got stuck on one of the fragments about justice and force. Here's the passage (Trotter's translation, which is the one my library happened to have):

"Justice, might.—It is right that what is just should be obeyed; it is necessary that what is strongest should be obeyed. Justice without might is helpless; might without justice is tyrannical. Justice without might is gainsaid, because there are always offenders; might without justice is condemned. We must then combine justice and might, and for this end make what is just strong, or what is strong just.

Justice is subject to dispute; might is easily recognised and is not disputed. So we cannot give might to justice, because might has gainsaid justice, and has declared that it is she herself who is just. And thus being unable to make what is just strong, we have made what is strong just."

Me and a couple people in my study group got into it about how to read this. Half the group thinks its basically a Thrasymachus move dressed up in 17th century french -- Pascal's just cynically observing that "justice" ends up meaning whatever the strongest party calls justice, and that's the whole story, no deeper foundation on offer. The other half thinks there's something more specifically Pascalian going on, tied to his whole thing about reason being corrupted after the Fall, so that the gap between what's actually just and what's merely enforced is less a flat cynical claim about power and more a diagnosis of a condition we can't reason our way out of on our own (which would tie into the bigger apologetic argument he's building across the Pensées). I'm also not sure how this connects to the other bit people always bring up where he basically says custom amounts to the whole of equity just because it's accepted -- is that the same move restated, or a genuinely different argument? Can you help me figure out which reading actually fits the text best, or whether Pascal's just being deliberately unresolved here, and maybe say something about how much of this is him borrowing his skepticism about law from Montaigne vs. doing his own thing with it?
