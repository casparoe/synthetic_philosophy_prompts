I teach a graduate seminar on theories of democracy, and something that happened at my brother's Fourth of July cookout has been rattling around in my head every since, partly because it's a cleaner case than anything in the readings I actually assign. My brother Mark sits on the select board of the small lake town where our family has a cottage, and he ended up going at it with his neighbor Lisa, who's a lawyer for what it's worth, over the town's voting rules. Background you need: like a lot of small New England towns, you can only vote at town meeting if you're a registered voter whose primary residence is in town. Seasonal cottage owners -- who make up something like 40% of the tax base but live elsewhere nine or ten months of the year -- don't get a vote no matter how long they've owned property there. This year the town is voting on an ordinance that would sharply cap short-term rentals, which happens to be how alot of seasonal owners afford their property taxes in the first place.

Mark's opening position was that the seasonal owners have nothing to complain about: town meeting is for people who actually live there, full stop, and owning property has never by itself entitled anyone to a vote in how a place is run. Lisa said that gets this particular case backwards, since the ordinance is specifically and almost exclusively about what seasonal owners can do with their own houses -- so cutting exactly the regulated group out of the vote isn't neutral residency policy, it's the majority writing rules for a minority with no way to answer back. Mark's reply was that this can't be the standard, because then any out-of-town landlord anywhere would have a claim to vote wherever their property happens to sit, since some local law always touches them somehow -- you'd never be able to close the rolls. Lisa's comeback was: fine, don't make it "any effect at all," make it laws that are substantially and specifically about your own property and its use, not generic ripple effects -- that line is fuzzy at the edges, sure, but fuzzy isn't the same as fake, and anyway the town can't claim full tax and regulatory power over these people's houses while giving them zero voice in exactly that domain.

I mostly just tried to keep my burger together and stayed out of it, but I'd love your read on it now that I've had time to think it over properly. Could you go through the exchange move by move and say who's got the better argument at each point, rather than just picking an overall winner? I'm especially curious whether Lisa's closing move actually answers Mark's "you'd never be able to close the rolls" worry, or whether it quietly changes the subject to a different (maybe still good) complaint about fairness. And if there's some consideration neither of them raised that should actually settle this, I'd like to hear that too.
