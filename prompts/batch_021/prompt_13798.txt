I'm a PhD student in philosophy working on decision theory, and this term I've been sitting in on a reading group—it draws people from both philosophy and computer science—on strategic bargaining: credible threats, promises, and commitment devices in the Schelling tradition. A situation in my own family has been on my mind precisely because it feels like it should be describable in those terms, and I'd like a second opinion on what's actually going on, since I'm too close to it to think clearly.

My grandmother died a few months ago, and my sister and I are dividing her estate—mainly her house and a modest amount of savings—without our parents around to referee. A cousin who works in dispute mediation told my sister, half-jokingly, that whoever stakes out a "final, non-negotiable" position first, and does so publicly, usually comes out ahead in disputes like this, since walking back a public commitment costs you more than money—it costs you standing within the family. Sure enough, before the two of us had even sat down together, my sister announced at a family dinner that she "needs" the house and won't accept anything less. My own reaction surprised me: I felt a strong urge to immediately stake out an equally firm, equally public counter-claim—on the savings and part of the house—before I had actually worked out what I thought was fair, mainly because I was afraid that every additional hour I spent deliberating was an hour in which her position was hardening into the default outcome. Within a week, we had each committed ourselves, loudly and to different relatives, to incompatible positions, and now neither of us can concede anything without looking weak, so we seem to be heading toward exactly the drawn-out, lawyer-mediated dispute that staking out these positions was supposed to help us avoid.

What strikes me in hindsight is that it wasn't obviously irrational for either of us, individually, to want to commit early and hard—that much is a familiar negotiating tactic—and yet the two of us reasoning that way in parallel produced an outcome that seems worse for both of us than if neither had committed to anything yet. That combination feels like it should have a name and a body of theory behind it, something adjacent to what gets discussed in that reading group, but I can't pin down the structure precisely enough to say whether my sister and I were being reasonable, foolish, or something else entirely, or whether the whole mess was predictable in advance. Is there a recognized account of this kind of situation, and if so, does it suggest we were each behaving rationally, or does the theory point to some mistake in our reasoning—and, practically speaking, is there a way out for two people who are already this dug in, short of one side simply losing?
