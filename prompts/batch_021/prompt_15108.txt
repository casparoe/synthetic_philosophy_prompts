Question: Explain Avicenna's "flying man" thought experiment and state precisely what conclusion it is meant to establish. Then assess the common claim that this argument anticipates Descartes' cogito.

Student answer: Avicenna's flying man argument, found in the Metaphysics of his Shifa, asks us to imagine a man created fully grown but suspended in the air, blindfolded, with his limbs held apart so that they touch neither each other nor anything else, and unable to hear any sound. Because none of his senses give him any information, he has no grounds for affirming the existence of his own hands, feet, or any external body. Avicenna claims that even in this state the man would still affirm his own existence. From this Avicenna concludes that our awareness of ourselves is immediate and not derived from awareness of the body, which he takes to be one piece of his larger case that the soul is a substance distinct from the body. This is essentially the same move Descartes later makes with the cogito: both philosophers strip away all sensory and bodily input in order to isolate a core of self-certainty that survives the most radical doubt, and both conclude that the thinking self can be known with certainty independently of the body. The chief difference between the two arguments is really just historical priority, since Avicenna's version predates Descartes' by roughly six centuries.
