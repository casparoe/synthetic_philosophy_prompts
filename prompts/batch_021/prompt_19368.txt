Hey so I write sci-fi-ish literary fiction, and I've gone down a rabbit hole in population ethics that I think could make a great short story: the nonidentity problem (I think it traces back to Derek Parfit, though don't quote me on that). The gist: a lot of our biggest choices -- when to have a kid, how a society uses up vs conserves its resources -- don't just make some existing person's life better or worse, they determine which person shows up at all. Say a girl has a baby at 14 instead of waiting a few years like everyone begs her to. The kid she actually has isn't a worse-off version of the kid she'd have had later, its a totally different kid -- and if that kid's life, though harder, is still worth living, who exactly got harmed? Not them; the only alternative for that specific kid was never existing. Same weirdness scales up: a society that burns through its resources ends up with different people in it a century on than the careful version of that society would've had, not the same people just worse off. So the wrongness, if there is any, seems to float free of anyone it's actually wrong to.

Could you write me a short story that dramatizes this rather than explains it -- ideally a character who has to sit with the fact that their own existence is downstream of a choice pretty much everyone agrees was reckless or unjust, and has to work out what, if anything, they owe that fact. Don't resolve it too neatly at the end; I want the discomfort still itching after the last line.
