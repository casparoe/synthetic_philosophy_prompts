I've spent a long time reading rationalist and effective-altruist blogs and forums, and one recurring topic there is the family of results in judgment-aggregation theory showing that a group's official answer to a question can come apart from what a majority of its own members individually believe about that very question — the phenomenon usually filed under "discursive dilemma," or in its original legal form, "doctrinal paradox." I recently watched a live instance of this play out in a small community micro-grants fund that grew out of my city's rationalist-adjacent meetup, and it produced a disagreement between two of the three rotating committee members that I'd like a considered outside opinion on. (I help run logistics for the fund but wasn't a voting member this cycle, so I'm reporting this as a bystander.) The fund's charter says a project is approved if and only if (a) a majority of the committee judges it net-positive in expectation, and (b) a majority judges the applicant capable of executing it competently; the two questions are voted on separately and the rule is then applied mechanically to the results — a "premise-based" procedure written into the charter specifically to stop people from voting on gut feeling and reasoning backward to a rationale afterward. This cycle's voters were Naomi, Devon, and Ravi. Naomi judged the project net-positive but the applicant incompetent; Devon judged the applicant competent but the project net-negative; Ravi judged both premises true. So (a) passed 2-1 (Naomi and Ravi) and (b) passed 2-1 (Devon and Ravi), and the charter's rule therefore yielded the verdict: fund. Yet look at each voter's own bottom line, applying the rule to only their own premises: only Ravi's personal verdict was "fund," while Naomi's and Devon's were both "don't fund," since each thought at least one premise had failed. A plain yes/no vote on "should we fund this?" — what the literature calls a conclusion-based procedure — would therefore have gone 2-1 against, the opposite of the charter's official answer, even though every voter's premises and the charter's own rule were applied correctly throughout.

Devon argued afterward, in the fund's group chat, that this shows the premise-based procedure is illegitimate: two of the three people actually deciding this case believe it shouldn't be funded, full stop, and a formula that overrides that isn't a neutral tool but a substitute for the committee's own judgment. Ravi replied that the entire point of voting on (a) and (b) separately is to stop someone from quietly disliking an applicant and then discovering a reason on each sub-question to match; a committee bound in advance to explicit criteria is more accountable than one that simply polls final verdicts. Devon came back that this only relocates the discretion rather than removing it: whoever drafts the application form decides how many premises there are and how finely they are carved, so "principled" premise-based voting is exactly as manipulable as a direct vote, just less honest about where the manipulation happens. Ravi answered that a rubric fixed before anyone has seen a given application cannot be reverse-engineered to fit that application, the way a same-day yes/no vote can be swayed by how the question happens to be framed once everyone already knows the specifics of the case in front of them. Devon's closing point was that none of this touches her actual objection: whatever the rubric's merits in the abstract, in this particular case it produced a decision that a majority of the deciding members individually oppose, and calling that "the committee's decision" rather than "the rule's decision" is a sleight of hand.

Could you work through the exchange move by move — Devon's opening charge, Ravi's accountability defense, Devon's agenda-manipulation rejoinder, Ravi's framing-effects reply, and Devon's closing point — and say who has the better of it at each step, flagging anywhere you think both of them are missing something? I would also value your own view on the more general question underneath all this: is there a principled way to decide when a group should prefer a premise-based procedure to a direct, conclusion-based vote, or does it ultimately come down to which failure mode — manipulable rubrics or manipulable gut votes — one happens to be more worried about?
