Okay so my friend Jay's really into AI alignment and last night he was going on about coherent extrapolated volition like it's obviously the right target for what a future superintelligence should optimize for -- you just extrapolate what we'd want if we knew more, thought faster, and were more the people we wished we were, and go from there. I told him that's basically Railton's old idealized-advisor account of a person's good from 80s metaethics wearing a new hat, and it already has a well known problem: Sobel's objection that once you idealize someone enough (full information, unlimited cognitive powers, etc) they've effectively become a different person, so it's not obvious why THEIR preferences get to be authoritative over what's actually good for the real, non-idealized you -- the idealization severs the very connection that was supposed to make the advisor's verdict relevant. Jay's comeback was that CEV dodges this because it's not idealizing one person in isolation, its extrapolating everybody at once and keeping only the parts where the extrapolated volitions cohere or converge, so no individual's "drift" gets to dictate anything by itself. I think he's just conflating two different problems (aggregating across people vs. one idealized self staying answerable to its actual, unidealized self) but I couldn't pin it down fast enough at the time. Can you build the strongest version of the argument that the coherence/convergence step in CEV does nothing to blunt the Sobel-style worry -- that an extrapolated volition could sail through CEV's convergence test with flying colors and still be exactly as disconnected from real people's actual good as a single Railton-style idealized advisor would be?
