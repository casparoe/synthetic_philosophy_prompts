Here is the student's answer, verbatim:

"Adams' Thesis says that for any probability function Pr and sentences A, C with Pr(A) > 0, the probability of the indicative conditional 'if A, C' equals the conditional probability Pr(C|A) = Pr(A∧C)/Pr(A). This is meant to capture the datum that our confidence in conditionals tracks conditional, not unconditional, probability: asked how likely it is that a fair die landed on six given that it landed on an even number, we say 1/3, not the 2/3 we would get from treating 'if it landed even, it landed six' as the material conditional ¬Even∨Six.

Lewis's proof shows that no proposition can play this role except in a degenerate probability space. Assume the Import-Export-style Factorization principle: Pr(B>C | A) = Pr(C | A∧B) whenever Pr(A∧B) > 0. Suppose Pr(A∧C) > 0 and Pr(A∧¬C) > 0. By the law of total probability, Pr(A>C) = Pr(A>C|C)·Pr(C) + Pr(A>C|¬C)·Pr(¬C). By Factorization, setting the outer conditioning proposition to C, Pr(A>C|C) = Pr(C|A∧C) = 1, and, setting it to ¬C, Pr(A>C|¬C) = Pr(C|A∧¬C) = 0. So Pr(A>C) = Pr(C). But this is absurd: it says the probability of the conditional is just the unconditional probability of the consequent, ignoring the antecedent entirely, which contradicts Adams' Thesis whenever A is evidentially relevant to C — exactly the case we care about. The only way out is to restrict attention to a trivial probability space, one that cannot assign positive probability to A∧C, A∧¬C, and ¬A all at once.

This result is fatal to Stalnaker's semantics for indicative conditionals. Stalnaker wanted 'if A, C' to be true iff C holds at the closest A-world, which makes it an ordinary proposition — a set of possible worlds. By letting the similarity relation that fixes the 'closest' world depend on the conversational context, Stalnaker could let different believers, with different information, express different propositions with the same conditional sentence — which looks like a promising way to dodge Lewis's proof, since Lewis only shows that no single, context-independent recipe for building A>C can satisfy Pr(A>C) = Pr(C|A) across every believer at once. But it is no dodge: Lewis's 1976 argument already establishes that not even a belief-state-relative proposition can satisfy Pr(A>C) = Pr(C|A) for every choice of A and C within one single fixed believer's credences, so Stalnaker's move to context-dependence does not rescue his semantics.

Lewis and Adams/Edgington ultimately draw the same moral from the triviality result. Since conditional credence cannot in general be identified with credence in the truth of a single proposition, Lewis concludes, just as Adams and later Edgington do, that indicative conditionals do not have truth conditions: to accept 'if A, C' is not to believe that some proposition A>C is probably true, but to perform a distinctively conditional act of judgement — supposing A and judging C on that basis, in the spirit of Ramsey's original test. The three of them differ mainly on points of detail, such as how conditionals embed in compounds like negations and disjunctions of conditionals, a problem Lewis himself raises as a difficulty for the suppositional view generally."
