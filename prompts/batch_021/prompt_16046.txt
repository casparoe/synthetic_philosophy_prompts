And here is my friend's answer, reproduced in full:

Millikan's theory says that mental states, like all biological traits, can have a "proper function": a specific effect that a trait's ancestors were selected to produce because they produced it. A frog's fly-snapping mechanism, for instance, has the proper function of catching flies, because over many generations, frogs whose triggering mechanism fired at flies were more likely to survive and reproduce than frogs whose mechanism didn't. This is what lets Millikan solve the disjunction problem: even though the mechanism sometimes fires at BB pellets shot past the frog, the content of the state is still "fly," not "fly-or-BB-pellet," because only flies, and not BB pellets, are part of the mechanism's selective history. When the frog snaps at a pellet, this is simply a misfire relative to Normal conditions, not a change in what the state represents.

Davidson's Swampman case is meant to be a problem for this picture. Swampman is a molecule-for-molecule duplicate of a person that is created by freak chance, for instance when lightning strikes a swamp, and has no evolutionary or causal history at all, even though he is physically identical to the original person and behaves exactly as they would. Because Millikan's proper functions are fixed by history, Swampman's internal states have no proper function and therefore no content, even though he acts for all the world as if he believes and wants things. Millikan's own response, as I understand it, is to accept this consequence rather than treat it as a refutation: the notion of content she is trying to naturalize is inherently historical, so it is simply a consequence of the theory, and not a flaw in it, that a being with no history lacks content in this sense, even though he would presumably still have the same phenomenal experience as the original person, since consciousness itself doesn't depend on having a selection history the way representational content does.

Dretske's theory in Explaining Behavior grounds content in essentially the same way as Millikan's: an internal state has the function of indicating a given condition because, over the organism's evolutionary history, states that reliably indicated that condition were selected for, since they enabled adaptive responses. So although Dretske speaks of "indicator functions" rather than "proper functions," the two theories are really just terminological variants of one another, both cashing out representational content in terms of what natural selection has favored.
