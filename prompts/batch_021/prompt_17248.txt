Here's the question as it was set:

"Ian Hacking argues that many classifications of people are 'interactive kinds' rather than 'indifferent kinds,' and that such classifications are subject to a 'looping effect.' (a) Explain what the looping effect is and why Hacking thinks it marks a difference between classifying people and classifying things in the natural sciences. (b) Illustrate the looping effect using either multiple personality disorder/DID or autism as your case. (c) Some people take looping effects to show that the classification in question is 'merely' a social construction rather than a real kind. Explain what Hacking himself says about this inference."

And here's the student's answer, typos and all:

"Hacking's central idea is that classifying human beings can change the very people being classified, which is not true of classification in the natural sciences. He calls this the looping effect. Natural kinds like quarks or gold are what Hacking calls indifferent kinds: they don't care that we've named and classified them, so classifying a piece of gold as gold doesn't change the gold. But classifying a person as, say, having multiple personality disorder is different -- the person becomes aware of the diagnosis, learns what an MPD patient is supposed to be like, and can start presenting more like the textbook cases; therapists and journalists publicize the category, more patients come forward, and clinicians revise the diagnostic criteria to match what they're now seeing, which then loops back and shapes the next wave of patients. Hacking originally called these 'human kinds' but later preferred 'interactive kinds,' since the important thing isn't that the kind is a kind of human, its that classifying it interacts with what's being classified.

This is basically what happened with multiple personality disorder in North America from the 1970s into the 90s: diagnoses rose enormously after cases like Sybil got popular attention, therapists started using hypnosis to actively look for alters, and the 'profile' of a typical patient shifted over time in step with what clinicians expected to find -- which is really just labeling theory again, the idea that being labeled deviant is what generates the deviant behavior to begin with. You can see looping with autism too: since it became a widely publicized diagnosis, autistic adults have written autobiographies, formed online communities and self-advocacy movements, and pushed back on purely clinical descriptions of autism, which has fed back into how the DSM itself frames the condition (hence the move to 'autism spectrum').

Because the profile of these categories keeps shifting in step with classification and public attention rather than tracking some fixed underlying biological fact, I think Hacking's own upshot is that things like multiple personality disorder aren't real kinds at all -- they're social constructions we've mistaken for objective medical categories, which puts him in basically the same camp as someone like Szasz, who thought mental illness talk was mythical to begin with."
