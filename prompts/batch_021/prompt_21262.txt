Question: Compare epistemicist and supervaluationist treatments of the Sorites paradox. Explain (a) how each view diagnoses the source of the paradox, (b) what each view says about whether predicates like "heap" or "bald" admit sharp boundaries, and (c) whether each view can consistently endorse classical logic, including both bivalence and the law of excluded middle. Illustrate your answer to (c) with reference to the "penumbral connection" constraint on admissible precisifications.

Student answer: Both views are attempts to preserve classical logic in the face of the Sorites, unlike degree-theoretic or many-valued approaches that give up bivalence from the outset.

The epistemicist, following Williamson, holds that predicates like "heap" and "bald" have perfectly sharp boundaries: some exact number of grains, or hairs, separates the heaps from the non-heaps. We simply cannot know where the line falls. This isn't ordinary ignorance, on a par with not knowing the population of a distant city; it's principled and irremediable, because knowledge requires a margin for error. To know that n grains make a heap, a belief would have to be safe from error in the neighboring case of n-1 grains, where the same belief-forming method would yield a false belief, and no belief about an exact cutoff can meet that condition. So the universally quantified Sorites premise is simply false at the (unknowable) cutoff. Because the trouble is purely epistemic, classical semantics is untouched: every sentence, including borderline ones, is true or false, bivalence holds without exception, and excluded middle is just an ordinary tautology.

The supervaluationist denies that there is any fact about exactly where the boundary falls. "Heap" is associated with a range of admissible precisifications, and a sentence is true (supertrue) just in case it comes out true on all of them, false just in case it comes out false on all of them, and neither true nor false when it varies. This yields a similar diagnosis of the paradox to the epistemicist's, but for a different reason: the Sorites premise turns out false on every precisification (each has some cutoff at which it fails), so the premise itself is superfalse — but since a different n serves as the counterexample on different precisifications, no particular instance is ever false. So, like the epistemicist, the supervaluationist agrees that "some cutoff obtains" is true, while denying that any candidate cutoff, such as "exactly 4,000 grains," is true; the latter is neither true nor false, so bivalence fails for it. Since this indeterminacy also infects the disjunction "either n grains is a heap or it isn't" right at the border, excluded middle fails there too, in just the region where we should expect it to.

What keeps this from collapsing into an unprincipled free-for-all is the requirement that admissible precisifications respect what Williamson calls "penumbral connections": for example, no precisification may count someone as bald while counting a person with strictly more hair, and otherwise identical, as not bald. This is also what secures classical inferences among vague predicates specifically: "if Tim is thinner than Tom, and Tom is thin, then Tim is thin" comes out supertrue, since any precisification making the second conjunct true must, by the connection between "thin" and comparative thinness, make the consequent true as well — even though "Tom is thin" and "Tim is thin" may individually be gappy.

On the whole, epistemicism is the more metaphysically costly view, since it posits a fact nobody could ever know, whereas supervaluationism avoids that cost by relativizing truth to precisification instead.
