Ok so I've been falling down a rabbit hole for a piece I'm writing about lottery-based allocation systems -- started out looking at the old H-1B visa lottery and hospitals that randomized ICU beds during covid, and somehow ended up at 1am reading 1960s issues of the Journal of Political Economy, which is not where I expected my Tuesday to go. The jumping off point is Harsanyi's aggregation theorem: if each person's preferences over risky options satisfy the von Neumann-Morgenstern axioms, the social ranking does too, and everyone agrees on Pareto improvements, then (so the theorem says) the social welfare function has to come out as a weighted sum of everybody's individual utility, full stop -- which Harsanyi took as basically deriving utilitarianism straight out of decision theory. But then, if I'm remembering the setup right, Peter Diamond wrote a comment on that exact paper pointing out something that still strikes me as a real problem: say you've got one good that can't be split -- a kidney, a single scholarship, whatever -- and two people who are, by stipulation, totally symmetric: same need, same utility function, same everything. Giving it to person A outright, giving it to person B outright, and flipping a fair coin between them all come out exactly tied in expected utility. So the Harsanyi-style aggregator is stuck being indifferent between them, even though most people's gut says the coin flip is obviously the fairer move and ought to be strictly preferred, not just tied with the alternatives.

What I'd love, if your up for it, is a dialogue that actually sits inside that disagreement instead of just gesturing at it from a distance. Two characters, right at the moment they have to decide how to hand out the one scholarship: one of them a committed Harsanyi-style aggregator who thinks that once expected utility has been correctly computed there's no further fact about "fairness" left for the coin flip to track, the other convinced by the Diamond-style case that a fair procedure carries value that isn't reducible to the expected utility of its outcomes. I want real engagement, not two monologues -- so the aggregator has to say something substantive about where the coin-flip intuition could be coming from if not tracking any real value (mistaken risk-aversion? a disguised taste for procedures that should just get folded back into the utility function?), and the fairness advocate has to explain why Harsanyi's axioms don't already rule out the move they're making, rather than just insisting fairness matters. Neither one should be the obvious loser -- I want to finish the dialogue still genuinely unsure who's got the better of it.
