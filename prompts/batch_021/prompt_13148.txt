A friend and I spent a long evening arguing about Aquinas's position on tyrannicide, and we ended up agreeing on a reading that neither of us could fully defend under further questioning: since Aquinas holds that a ruler who governs for his own advantage rather than the common good has thereby forfeited the legitimacy of his rule, he must be granting ordinary subjects a standing right to kill such a ruler on their own initiative -- which is presumably why he is cited as a forerunner of later Jesuit resistance theorists such as Mariana and Suárez. Yet Aquinas is elsewhere adamant that no private person may inflict death on anyone, however guilty, by his own authority, reserving that power to whoever bears public responsibility for the community's welfare. Why does he treat tyranny as the one case in which this otherwise near-inviolable restriction does not hold?
