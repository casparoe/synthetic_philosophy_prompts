Jain anekāntavāda holds that reality is many-sided and that no single, unqualified assertion can capture the whole truth about anything. Its logical counterpart, syādvāda, prefixes each predication with syāt ("in a certain respect") and systematizes this into the sevenfold schema (saptabhangi): in some respect a thing is, in some respect it is not, in some respect it both is and is not, and in some respect it is inexpressible, together with three further combinations of these. Critics have long pressed a self-refutation dilemma against the doctrine: the claim that every assertion is only conditionally true must itself be either conditionally true, in which case it cannot bind assertions generally, or unconditionally true, in which case the doctrine breaks its own rule and collapses into the very one-sidedness (ekāntavāda) it was meant to overcome. Jain logicians have replied that the seven predications are always indexed to distinct respects -- substance, place, time, and state -- so that "is" and "is not" never attach to the same respect at once, and that anekāntavāda itself functions as a second-order rule governing first-order assertions rather than as one more first-order assertion competing with them.

Write an essay evaluating whether this reply genuinely defuses the self-refutation dilemma or merely relocates it one level up. Defend a specific verdict on what anekāntavāda can coherently amount to -- a substantive logic of predication, a metaphysical thesis about the structure of things, or only a modest epistemic counsel of humility -- and say what, if anything, the doctrine must give up in order to hold that position consistently.
