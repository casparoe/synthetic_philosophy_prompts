I'm at work on a novel set in and around the musical circles of nineteenth-century Vienna, and I've reached a scene I keep putting off because I don't trust myself to get the philosophy right. One of my two leads, a minor composer named Anton, has fallen under the spell of Schopenhauer — not just the familiar slogan that music is a "copy of the will itself" rather than of the world's appearances, but the fuller architecture of the argument in the third book of The World as Will and Representation: the claim that bass, inner voices, and melody correspond to successive grades of the will's objectification (unorganized nature, then the plant and animal kingdoms, then self-conscious human striving), that a suspended dissonance resolving into consonance is a small working model of desire and its satisfaction, and that melody's wandering departures from and eventual return to the keynote trace out the many detours of human willing. His friend Konrad, a critic very much in Eduard Hanslick's mold, thinks all of this is exactly the kind of thing his own side calls "content aesthetics": for him, music's content is nothing but tonally moving forms, and any talk of specific feelings or metaphysical meanings being "in" the music is a category mistake dressed up as insight. An early draft had the two of them simply trade the textbook slogans back and forth, and a friend who teaches music history told me, fairly, that it read like two encyclopedia entries in period dress.

What I'd like from you is a real dialogue between Anton and Konrad, seven hundred to a thousand words or so, set wherever seems natural to you — after a private performance, on the walk home, over the last round at a café that's about to close. I want Anton to have to defend the actual mechanics of the will-analogy against the obvious formalist objections: that pairing bass with inorganic nature and melody with human striving is an allegory imposed on the music from outside, with no principled link to how it's composed or heard, and that if minor keys are supposed to encode pain and major keys pleasure, this runs straight into every buoyant minor-key scherzo and melancholy major-key adagio one can name. And I want Konrad to have to answer Anton's best comeback: that pure form-talk struggles to explain why music seems to move us so much more directly and viscerally than an equally well-proportioned building or a pleasing arabesque, and that chalking this up to mere contingent association rather than to anything essential looks suspiciously convenient. Neither should land a clean knockout or concede more than his position can honestly afford; I'd rather end on real friction than a tidy synthesis, since that seems closer to where the actual disagreement still sits.

If it helps to calibrate register and rigor, you're welcome to treat Anton and Konrad simply as stand-ins for Schopenhauer and Hanslick themselves rather than as characters of my own invention — I care far more about the philosophical content holding up under pressure than about matching my eventual prose style.
