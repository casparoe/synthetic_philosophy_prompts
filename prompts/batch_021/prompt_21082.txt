So I was out with my friend Priya last night (she's a data scientist, has zero patience for what she calls "philosophy major problems") and we got into it for like two hours over the risk-assessment tool our state's parole board uses. My point was that even before you touch the fairness/bias debate everyone always jumps to, there's a more basic issue: to give some specific guy up for parole a risk score, you have to slot him into a reference class — "men 22-30 with two priors," or "men 22-30," or "people with this exact combination of offense, age, and program completion" — and he belongs to endless different classes that would each spit out a different recidivism rate for him. There's no principled fact about which class is the "right" one, and yet the number we hand the board gets treated like it just is his probability of reoffending. Priya thinks this is a purely academic worry — you just pick whatever class the data can support and move on — but I think its closer to that old reference-class problem from probability theory (Reichenbach? I'm fuzzy on the source) and that it actually undermines the pretense that we're assigning a probability to this particular guy, rather than just reporting a fact about some group he happens to fall into.

Turns out this isn't hypothetical for me: I actually sit on my state's parole board, and we're deciding basically this exact question on Thursday — whether to move from our current tool (broad demographic plus criminal-history bands) to a newer one that scores people in much narrower, more "individualized" bands, but which was validated on a smaller sample and has noticeably wider confidence intervals as a result. Half the board sees narrower-and-more-individualized as an obvious upgrade; the other half worries we'd just be trading reliability for a comforting illusion of precision. I have to circulate the decision memo before the meeting, and I don't want it to just rehash the standard disparate-impact talking points that dominate every one of these discussions — I want it to actually reckon with the reference-class issue itself. Can you draft that memo for me? I need a real recommendation (which tool, some hybrid, extra disclosure language, whatever you think is defensible) with a concrete timeframe for rolling it out, and I want it to take the "there's no non-arbitrary reference class, so this number is shakier than it looks" objection seriously rather than wave it off the way Priya did.
