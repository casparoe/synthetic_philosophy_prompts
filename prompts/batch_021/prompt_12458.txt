I'm reporting a long-form piece on the more esoteric corners of AI-safety research, and one idea I can't stop turning over is the notion of an "acausal threat." The worry, as several of my sources describe it, is that a sufficiently capable agent could be coerced without any causal contact whatsoever — no message sent, no interaction of any kind — simply because an adversary can predict, or run a sufficiently accurate simulation of, how the agent would respond under threat, and can act on that prediction alone. (The old "Roko's basilisk" scare that circulated on rationalist forums years ago was one crude, popularized version of this worry.) One proposed defense that keeps coming up in my interviews is the "surrogate goal": an agent expecting to be targeted this way commits, in advance and verifiably, to caring enormously about some cheap, otherwise-irrelevant variable, on the theory that a rational extortionist will then find it more efficient to threaten the decoy than the agent's actual values, since the decoy is exactly what the agent will visibly and cheaply cave on. It's a bit like a country ostentatiously fortifying a worthless border post so that invaders aim there instead of at the capital.

While working on this, I pulled my old copy of Thomas Schelling's "The Strategy of Conflict" off the shelf, because so much of the vocabulary here — credible commitment, tying your own hands, threats that work only because you've made yourself unable to back down — is recognizably his, just with the causality subtracted out. And that's the part I can't get past: Schelling's whole account of commitment depends on a rival being able to observe, or at least infer from real-world evidence, that you have actually limited your own options — burning a bridge where the enemy can see it burn, delegating authority to a subordinate under fixed instructions, and so on. The acausal version seems to dispense with all of that: the only "observation" available to the other side is its own prediction of your disposition, with no communication, no shared history, and not even necessarily a shared moment in time.

Schelling died in 2016, having won a Nobel largely for this body of work, so he never had to reckon with any of it directly — but it's exactly the family of problems he spent his career on. If you had to reconstruct his likely reaction, would he have recognized surrogate goals as a natural, if strange, extension of his own logic of commitment, or would he have said the acausal framing borrows the language of credible commitment while quietly discarding the observability that made commitment credible in his account in the first place? I'd also be curious whether his own point — that visibly strengthening your resolve can itself invite threats you wouldn't otherwise have faced — suggests that adopting a surrogate goal could attract exactly the kind of extortion it's meant to deter. I'm less interested in a balanced list of considerations than in your best reconstruction of how he would actually have come down on this.
