I've spent more years than I'd like to admit reading through the LessWrong and Overcoming Bias archives, and one topic that keeps resurfacing there without ever quite getting settled is anthropic reasoning about human extinction: the Carter–Leslie Doomsday Argument and its descendants. A discussion group I belong to, which mostly works through papers on decision theory and anthropics, spent a recent session on Bostrom's *Anthropic Bias*, and it ended in a disagreement between two members — I'll call them Marcus and Ines — that the rest of us couldn't settle and that has been nagging at me since.

(1) Marcus opened by defending the argument in something close to its classic form: adopt the Self-Sampling Assumption, reason as though you were a random draw from everyone who will ever exist in your reference class, and notice that finding yourself so near the beginning of that population is far more probable if humanity turns out to be short-lived than if it spreads across the galaxy for millions of years — so, prior to consulting any specific extinction-risk evidence, this alone should shift your credence toward an earlier rather than later end. (2) Ines answered with two objections at once: first, the conclusion is hostage to an arbitrary choice of reference class — Homo sapiens, every hominid, every entity that has ever pondered this very argument, per Bostrom's own more paranoid examples — with no principled way to fix it; second, even granting some reference class, Bostrom's own Self-Indication Assumption, which weights hypotheses by how many observers they predict, cancels out the doomsday-style update if you adopt it alongside SSA, so the gloomy conclusion isn't forced by the math, only by a silent and undefended decision to pair SSA with a rejection of SIA. (3) Marcus conceded that SIA cancels the update, but replied that accepting SIA is the worse bargain, since it is exactly what generates Bostrom's presumptuous philosopher, who claims he can already tell working cosmologists which of two competing theories is correct — making their planned experiment unnecessary — purely because one theory implies vastly more observers than the other; letting armchair anthropics override an experiment struck him as a worse bullet to bite than a modest update toward earlier extinction, so on balance keeping SSA and living with the Doomsday Argument is the lesser cost. (4) Ines said this "lesser of two evils" framing is a false choice: you can deny that you should reason as though you were randomly sampled from any class of observers, actual or merely possible, and hold instead that your own existence and position, being fixed rather than the output of a draw that could have gone otherwise, simply isn't the kind of fact that licenses an update in either direction — an objection with real pedigree, going back to some of the earliest published critiques of Carter and Leslie (Dennis Dieks among them) — on which the Doomsday Argument and the Presumptuous Philosopher commit exactly the same mistake, so there is no dilemma to resolve because neither inference was ever licensed to begin with.

Neither of them budged, and the group split down the middle. Could you go through moves (1) through (4) in order and say, for each, who has the better of it — a clean win, a partial point, or a wash where they are talking past each other — and flag anything important that both of them are missing? Then close with a verdict, on its own line starting with "Verdict:", on the question one level up: once all four moves are on the table, is there anything left of the original Doomsday Argument as a genuine, if weak, piece of Bayesian evidence, or does Ines's closing move dissolve it along with its presumptuous-philosopher rival?
