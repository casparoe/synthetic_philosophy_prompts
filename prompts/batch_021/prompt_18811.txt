I'm a philosophy PhD student working on formal epistemology and decision theory, and for the past few weeks I've been pulled into the rabbit hole of "acausal trade" — the idea, developed mostly outside mainstream academic philosophy (on LessWrong, in MIRI-adjacent writing on updateless and functional decision theory, and more recently in work rebranded as "evidential cooperation in large worlds") that agents with no causal channel between them whatsoever can still benefit from coordinating, simply by each reasoning about what the other would do and acting on that prediction, in the way two sufficiently similar reasoners might cooperate in a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma via Hofstadter-style superrationality even without any communication. My advisor thinks the whole thing is dressed-up wishful thinking with no more content than "I'll be nice in case someone I can't observe is watching," while a friend in an AI alignment reading group insists it has real teeth — e.g., for whether an updateless agent (or a lab designing one) ought to factor in the preferences of causally inaccessible agents at all, when nothing in the decision procedure seems to distinguish that move from pure speculation.

I'd like you to write a dialogue between two characters of equal philosophical caliber who disagree about exactly this: "Naomi," who thinks acausal trade is a serious and underappreciated consequence of taking updateless decision theory seriously, and that rejecting it just because it feels exotic is unprincipled given that we already accept superrationality in the idealized twin cases; and "Marcus," who accepts those idealized results but denies that real-world acausal trade proposals are action-guiding, because there is no non-arbitrary way to fix who the trading partners are, what they want, or what would even count as honoring the deal.

Please have them engage directly with each other's strongest moves rather than talk past one another. I'd expect Marcus to press some version of an underdetermination objection along those lines, and Naomi to try to answer it with a convergence argument about what sufficiently reflective consequentialist reasoners across a large or infinite universe would tend to want regardless of their starting values — and I want to see whether that move actually rescues her position or just relocates the arbitrariness one level up. Somewhere before the end, I'd also like the dialogue to surface the awkward relationship between acausal "trade" and acausal "blackmail" (à la Roko's basilisk), and whether Naomi's framework has any principled way of licensing the former while ruling out the latter. Feel free to let the exchange run as long as it needs to (a dozen or so turns, say) to get past restating positions and into real argumentative movement — I'm less interested in a balanced survey than in seeing whether either side can actually move the other.
