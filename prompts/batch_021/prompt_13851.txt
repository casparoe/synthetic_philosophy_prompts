I've spent the last few weeks of my graduate seminar on theories of truth working through the proof-theoretic case against deflationism, and I want to put the question we ended on directly to you. Deflationists typically hold that an adequate theory of truth should be conservative over whatever background theory it is added to: if T is some theory of arithmetic or syntax, and we extend it with a truth predicate governed only by disquotational or compositional axioms, the resulting theory should prove no new sentence in the original vocabulary of T that T could not already prove unaided. The rationale is straightforwardly deflationary: if "true" tracked some substantive, explanatorily loaded property of the world, adding it to our theory could extend our knowledge of that world, whereas if it is merely a logical device for disquotation and generalization, it shouldn't be able to do any such thing. The trouble, pressed influentially by Stewart Shapiro and Jeffrey Ketland in the late 1990s, is that natural truth theories are not actually conservative in this sense: once the induction schema is allowed to range over formulas that themselves contain "true," the resulting theory proves reflection principles for the base theory -- in effect, that whatever T proves is true -- that T could not have proved of itself without violating Gödel's second incompleteness theorem. One of my students dismissed all this as a curiosity for proof theorists with no real bearing on the philosophical debate; another thought it was close to decisive against deflationism, full stop. I'd like your own verdict.

Write an essay addressing whether the non-conservativeness of natural truth theories actually refutes deflationism about truth. Explain, in your own words, why conservativeness looked like a natural commitment for deflationism to begin with, and then work through the deflationist's main lines of response to the reflection-principle result: biting the bullet and arguing that non-conservativeness is compatible with truth remaining metaphysically thin, retreating to a weaker but genuinely conservative theory and owning whatever expressive costs that incurs, or denying that conservativeness was ever a commitment deflationism needed to accept in the first place. Weigh these responses against the anti-deflationist argument as charitably as you can, and don't end up on the fence: tell me which way you think the argument actually cuts, and justify it.
