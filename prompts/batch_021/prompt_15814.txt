I recently found myself in a good-natured disagreement with a colleague from the statistics department, and I have not been able to resolve it to my own satisfaction. Suppose all we are told is that a certain ferry crossing takes somewhere between one and two hours, with nothing more specific to go on, and we are asked how likely it is that the crossing takes less than ninety minutes. My instinct was that, since no duration in that range is favored over any other, each duration should be treated as equally likely, which yields a probability of one half. My colleague objected that duration is not obviously the quantity we should treat this way: since the distance covered is fixed, one could just as well say that no average speed is favored over any other, and treating speeds as equally likely yields a different probability for the very same event. Neither of us has been able to find a flaw in the other's reasoning, yet the two methods disagree. Is one of these clearly the right way to reason under this sort of ignorance, or does the disagreement point to some deeper problem with the whole approach?
