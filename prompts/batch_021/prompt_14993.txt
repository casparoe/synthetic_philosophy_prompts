So this happened at dinner after our philosophy of death seminar and I haven't been able to let it go. We'd just read Epicurus and Nagel, and two people in my cohort, Mara and Devon, spent basically the whole meal arguing about whether death can be bad for the person who dies. Mara took the Epicurean line: badness needs a subject, and once you're dead there's no subject left for anything to be bad for, so death itself isn't bad (dying, if it's painful, might be, but that's a different thing). Devon pushed back with Nagel's move: things can be bad for you comparatively, without you having to experience the loss -- like how a brain injury that leaves an adult happily reduced to an infant's mental level is still a terrible thing to happen to him, even though he's not aware of what he's missing. Mara granted that experience isnt required, but said this just relocates the problem: deprivation still needs a subject who HAS it, and Nagel owes us a time at which the dead guy is worse off -- which he can't give, since there's no moment where the actual person is worse off then his counterfactual self would've been. Devon said demanding a "when" is confused, because plenty of comparative facts (a city being bigger now than it was decades ago, say) aren't naturally the kind of thing that need their own separate timestamp beyond the two times already being compared. Mara's comeback was that this comparison is between two times the city actually existed, whereas stacking the actual dead person up against how well off he'd have been if he'd lived stacks something actual against something merely possible, and it's not obvious that move is innocent.

Nobody budged and I had to referee, badly, mid-bite. Can you go through this move by move and say who's got the better of each specific exchange -- not just an overall winner -- and point out anything important you think both of them are missing?
