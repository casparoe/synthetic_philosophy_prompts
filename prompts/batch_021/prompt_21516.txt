I've been part of a small monthly reading group that grew out of the comment section of a decision-theory blog -- the kind of group in which everyone has already read the Sequences and several of us have strong opinions about updateless decision theory. Last month we worked through some of Caspar Oesterheld's writing on evidential cooperation in large worlds (ECL), and the discussion ended in a genuine disagreement between two members, Ilana and Soren, that the rest of us couldn't settle and that I've kept turning over since.

(1) Ilana opened by defending the practical upshot of ECL: if the universe, or the wider Everettian multiverse, is as large as our best cosmology suggests, then there are enormous numbers of agents who are causally cut off from us but who reason in relevantly similar ways while ending up with very different terminal values; because our decision is correlated with theirs rather than causing theirs, we have the same kind of reason to cooperate with them on a compromise of values that we would have to cooperate with a psychological twin in a one-shot prisoner's dilemma, and in practice that means diverting some altruistic effort toward causes that make sense under the compromise rather than under our own values alone. (2) Soren objected that this move only works if you help yourself to evidential or functional decision theory exactly when it's convenient: correlation without causation is precisely what a causal decision theorist says gives you no reason to act, and if the group wants to reject causal decision theory it should defend that rejection on its own terms rather than smuggle it in through ECL. (3) Ilana replied that this was already settled business, since the group had spent an earlier session agreeing that functional-decision-theoretic reasoning handles Newcomb's problem and the twin prisoner's dilemma better than causal decision theory does, and ECL is simply that same move applied to a much larger population of correlated agents. (4) Soren granted the point for clean, symmetric cases -- literal twins, or provably identical algorithms -- but said ECL needs correlation with agents who might share nothing with you beyond a loose family resemblance, something like "being a reasoner who takes ECL seriously," and once correlation is allowed to be that thin, nothing constrains how many such agents exist or how much weight each deserves, so the supposed gains from trade can be made to justify almost anything depending on how generously the reference class is drawn. (5) Ilana conceded the reference-class worry but said it is the same difficulty that already sits inside moral uncertainty and anthropic reasoning more generally, and nobody proposes abandoning expected-value reasoning under moral uncertainty simply because weighting rival theories against one another is hard. (6) Soren's response was that the two cases are not parallel: moral uncertainty is uncertainty about one agent's own obligations, resolvable in principle through that agent's further reflection, whereas ECL rests on claims about how many correlated agents actually exist and how similar they really are -- claims we have no independent way of checking -- so the whole calculation looks less like ordinary reasoning under uncertainty and more like Pascal's Mugging in decision-theoretic dress.

Could you go through moves (1) through (6) and say who has the better of the argument at each stage, rather than simply declaring an overall winner? I would also value your own view on whether some version of ECL survives Soren's reference-class objection, or whether that objection is quietly fatal to the whole project.
