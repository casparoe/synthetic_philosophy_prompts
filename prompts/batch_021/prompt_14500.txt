Harry Frankfurt's 1969 argument against the Principle of Alternative Possibilities (PAP)—the claim that an agent is morally responsible for an act only if she could have done otherwise—opened a dialectic that keeps generating more sophisticated cases in response to more sophisticated objections. The first round is well known: a counterfactual intervener stands ready to force an agent's decision, but only if she first shows a sign of deciding otherwise, and so never has to act because the agent decides on her own. Critics replied that some "flicker of freedom" survives even so (she could at least have failed to show that sign), and, more seriously, pressed what has become known as the dilemma defense: if the sign deterministically guarantees the ensuing decision, the example simply presupposes that determined agents can be responsible, begging the question against incompatibilists; if the sign does not guarantee it, then before the intervener could possibly act, the agent genuinely could have decided otherwise—a robust alternative, not a mere flicker—so Frankfurt's own description of the case turns out to be false of it.

Consider a later-generation case designed to dodge that dilemma. Diana is deliberating about whether to report a large cash payment on her taxes. From the very first instant of her deliberation—not triggered partway through by any detectable sign—her brain is structured so that the neural process that would issue in a decision to hide the payment is fixed to run to completion before the rival process that would issue in a decision to report it, thereby preempting it. No signs are read and nobody intervenes from outside: the hiding-process simply prevails on its own, and Diana decides, through perfectly ordinary psychological causation, to hide the payment. Because there is no temporally prior sign for the dilemma defense to get a grip on, cases in this mold—in the spirit of "blockage" refinements due to philosophers such as Alfred Mele, David Robb, and Derk Pereboom—are often presented as immune to it.

Write a dialogue between two philosophers of action: one who holds that the Diana case succeeds where the original did not, finally showing PAP to be false, and the other who holds that it fails for reasons continuous with the dilemma defense even though no prior sign is involved. Both should be equally capable defenders of their positions, and each should press the other on the specific mechanics of the case—for instance, on whether the hiding-process's victory is itself determined by the antecedent state of Diana's brain plus the laws of nature, and on what follows for her responsibility either way—rather than simply restating the flicker-of-freedom or dilemma-defense objections in their original form. Have the two respond to each other's actual moves, each conceding what is genuinely forceful in the other's point before pushing back, and let the dialogue end with the disagreement narrowed to as precise a residual crux as they can manage, whether or not they resolve it.
