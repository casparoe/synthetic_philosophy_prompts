I'm a science journalist working on a long-form piece about the more exotic corners of logic, and for the last couple of weeks I've been reading my way into dialetheism — the view, most associated with Graham Priest, that some contradictions are actually true rather than merely apparent. I understand the basic motivation well enough: the Liar sentence, "This sentence is false," seems to force a contradiction no matter which way you turn, and classical logic can't simply shrug that off, because it lets you derive absolutely anything from a contradiction once you have one (the old principle of ex contradictione quodlibet). What I haven't been able to get a good feel for, from the introductory pieces I've found, is how the debate actually runs once both sides get technical. How does a dialetheist defend giving up disjunctive syllogism as a rule of inference in order to block that explosion, without the resulting logic feeling gerrymandered? How does treating the Liar sentence as a "glut" — both true and false — really compare to rivals that instead posit a truth-value "gap," or that follow Tarski in denying that any consistent language can contain its own truth predicate? And how does either side handle Curry's paradox, which seems to generate triviality out of a bare conditional sentence, with no negation or contradiction involved at all?

For the piece, I'd like to include a dramatized exchange rather than just paraphrasing journal articles, so readers can feel the actual shape of the disagreement rather than just its conclusion. Could you write a dialogue, set backstage immediately after a public debate, between a committed dialetheist and an equally formidable classical logician who has just finished arguing against him on stage? I'd like the dialetheist to explain why he thinks treating the Liar as a glut beats the gap-based and hierarchy-based alternatives, and to have a considered answer ready when his opponent brings up Curry's paradox and the "revenge" worry that any proposed fix seems to generate its own strengthened paradoxical sentence. The classical logician shouldn't simply insist that contradictions are unacceptable; she should press on the specific price of giving up disjunctive syllogism and make the dialetheist earn that concession. Above all, I'd like each of them to genuinely engage with whatever the other has just said, so that the exchange visibly builds across several turns rather than the two of them talking past each other.
