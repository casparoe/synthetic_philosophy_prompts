The Pasadena paradox, introduced by the philosopher Alan Hájek, concerns a wager in which a fair coin is tossed until it first lands heads; if the first head appears on toss n, the payoff is (-1)^(n+1) · (2^n/n) dollars. Since the probability of that outcome is 1/2^n, each term in the sum defining the expected value reduces to (-1)^(n+1)/n, so the expectation is exactly the alternating harmonic series -- convergent, but only conditionally, not absolutely. Please explain, at the level of intuition rather than by simply invoking the Riemann rearrangement theorem as a black box, why conditional convergence specifically is what makes this expectation undefined rather than merely infinite, and why that is a deeper problem for expected-value theory than the St. Petersburg paradox's mere divergence to infinity. Then consider a modified wager that adds a flat one-dollar bonus to every payoff, so that it pays strictly more than the original wager in every possible outcome: does the new wager thereby acquire a well-defined expectation, or does the same difficulty resurface -- and what does your answer imply about how statewise dominance should relate to expected-value maximization once undefined expectations are in play?
