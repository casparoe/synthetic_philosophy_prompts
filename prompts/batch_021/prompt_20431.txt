I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and next month I'm interviewing a philosopher of mind whose new book defends illusionism about phenomenal consciousness: the view that experience has, at most, "quasi-phenomenal" properties that introspection misrepresents as the intrinsic, ineffable qualia we naively believe in, and that solving Chalmers' meta-problem — why we're so convinced otherwise — is effectively the whole problem, not just a more tractable stand-in for it. Draft eight interview questions that push past the talking points she's used in print. I want at least one question on the standard "seeming" objection, that calling something an illusion still presupposes a subject to whom it seems a certain way, which looks like exactly the phenomenal fact being denied; one on why explaining our judgments about consciousness should settle the matter, given that a philosophical zombie would, by hypothesis, produce those same judgments for the same reasons; and one on whether an error theory this sweeping about introspection should undermine confidence in the introspective judgments illusionism itself is argued from. For each, add a follow-up keyed to the reply she's most likely to give, and close with a question about what would convince her that the meta-problem, even fully solved, still leaves something unexplained.
