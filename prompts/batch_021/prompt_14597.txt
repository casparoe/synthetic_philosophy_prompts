My father has spent the past year and a half consumed by the conviction that the closure of the small regional airport outside our hometown was not, as the county insists, a matter of maintenance costs, but a deliberate land grab engineered by a development consortium and quietly approved by officials he used to trust. He has filed public records requests, kept a growing binder of flight logs and zoning memos, and stopped speaking to two old friends who work for the county. What unsettles me is that he isn't a careless thinker: he brings the same meticulousness to this that he once brought to his engineering career, and he can point to specific anomalies in the record that I can't easily explain away.

Trying to make sense of this, I've been reading around the philosophical literature on conspiracy theories, and I keep running into the same fault line. "Generalists" think there is something epistemically defective about explanations that posit hidden, coordinated intentional action as such, independent of the specific evidence for them; "particularists" deny that this structural feature carries any special evidential weight and insist each theory be judged like any other empirical hypothesis, with something like Brian Keeley's distinction between warranted and unwarranted conspiracy theories doing the sorting instead. Write an essay that works through this generalist/particularist debate on its merits and reaches a defended verdict on whether, and under what conditions, it can be rational to believe a conspiracy theory. Please don't settle for "it depends on the theory" without cashing out exactly what it depends on—I would like enough precision that I could actually apply your criteria to my father's case, even though you needn't return to him once the problem is set up.
