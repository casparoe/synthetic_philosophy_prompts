I'm a philosophy professor serving as the humanities appointee on my university's Crisis Communications Review Board, which must approve official statements before they go out under the Provost's name during a crisis. Last night a chemical release in a teaching lab forced a building evacuation; no one was hospitalized, but this is the third such incident in the chemistry department in under two years, and the internal safety review ordered after the second incident was never completed. The communications office's draft statement, which needs to go out before local news covers the story tomorrow morning, reports the evacuation and the absence of injuries accurately, but frames the department's overall safety record as strong and says nothing about the recurrence or the stalled review. Two board members consider this ordinary, defensible message discipline. Two others, aware that I teach Arendt, want me to settle the disagreement by applying her distinction, in "Lying in Politics," between the traditional lie that merely conceals a fact and the modern political lie that manufactures a rival image of reality, together with her further worry that those who traffic in the latter risk coming to believe their own reassuring account, much as she argues the Vietnam-era policy planners she calls "problem-solvers" did. Please draft the short written recommendation I need to circulate to the full board by 9 a.m. — approve, reject, or approve with amendments — including a concrete release timeline, and make sure it engages seriously with whichever side's concern your recommendation does not adopt.
