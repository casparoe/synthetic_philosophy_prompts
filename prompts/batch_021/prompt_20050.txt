So I'm a retired geologist -- spent about 35 years logging rock cores for an oil company -- and in retirement I've fallen hard down a philosophy rabbit hole. Right now that means working through Tolstoy's odd little treatise "What is Art?", which a friend lent me after I admitted out loud that I'd stood in front of a big Rothko at the museum last month and felt precisely nothing. Turns out old Leo would not have been surprised: the book is basically him going after the entire Western canon for that exact failure. He rips into Shakespeare (there's apparently a whole separate essay where he goes to town on King Lear specifically), saves some of his nastiest pages for Wagner, argues that hardly anyone at a concert is really feeling "universal brotherhood" during Beethoven's Ninth even though that's supposedly the whole point of the piece, and -- this is the bit that really got me -- he even dumps most of his own novels, War and Peace included, into what he calls "counterfeit" art. Feel free to correct me if I've mangled any of this, I'm about two weeks into my Tolstoy phase, not two years.

As best I can reconstruct it, his actual test for good art has two separate parts, and people seem to mix them up alot: first, does a work "infect" an ordinary person with a feeling the artist truly and sincerely felt, transmitted with enough individuality and clarity that you don't need a docent explaining it to you; and second, is the feeling being transmitted one of the good, unifying kind (brotherly love, roughly) rather than something base, exclusive, or merely sensual or showy. A work can apparently pass the first test and still fail the second, and vice versa.

Since he died in 1910, Tolstoy never had to reckon with fully non-representational painting -- Kandinsky's later work, Malevich's Black Square, or the American abstract expressionists like Rothko and Pollock, where there's no picture of anything at all, just colour, shape, and gesture, and where you often need a fair bit of theory before it "clicks" (if it ever does for you -- see my Rothko experience above). Working strictly from his actual two-part test rather than his reputation as a humourless populist, what do you think his verdict on abstract painting would have been? I'd like you to run through both prongs separately rather than just giving me the one-line "he'd have hated it" answer: is there a version of his infection story where pure form and colour, with no depicted subject at all, could still count as individual, clear, and sincere, or does his insistence on immediate accessibility for the uneducated doom it no matter what the painter intended? And on the second prong, what would he do with someone like Kandinsky, who made explicit, quasi-religious claims that abstraction communicates exactly the kind of higher, unifying feeling Tolstoy wanted art to carry -- does that count in its favour by his own lights, or would he treat the whole theory-heavy manifesto apparatus around abstraction as proof that it's art made by and for a coterie, rather than genuine communication with ordinary people?
