I am a novelist currently writing a book that fictionalizes a real, identifiable person, for whom I invent private conversations and interior thoughts that never occurred. This has returned me to Augustine's account of lying, which defines a lie not by mere falsehood but by the conjunction of believing what one says is false and intending the hearer to take it as true — a definition that lets him, and the tradition following him, absolve fables, jests, and frank fiction of the charge, since no one is meant to mistake them for factual report. I would like a dialogue between two moral philosophers who both accept this Augustinian criterion without qualification but disagree sharply over whether it protects a book like mine. One should argue that Augustine's own test convicts me of lying regardless of the book's presentation as a novel, since I believe the invented scenes are false and intend many readers to come away believing something like them really happened. The other should argue that once a work is clearly signaled as fiction, the genre convention itself discharges the "no intent to deceive" requirement, so that no sentence-level intention regarding the invented particulars is needed. Have the two of them genuinely engage each other's strongest moves rather than talk past one another, and keep the dispute anchored in what Augustine's definition actually requires rather than in loose intuitions about honesty in art.
