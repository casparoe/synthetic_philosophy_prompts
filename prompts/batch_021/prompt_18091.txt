Since retiring from a career in analytical chemistry, I have spent the last year or so working through the philosophy of language, beginning naturally with Kripke's discussion of "water is H2O" in Naming and Necessity, since that identity was my own daily business for thirty years. More recently I have read Chalmers and Jackson on two-dimensional semantics, and something puzzles me: their apparatus seems to me to overturn Kripke's necessity claim rather than defend it. Once you bring in the primary intension of "water" -- the rule that fixes its reference to whatever watery stuff surrounds the speaker in a world considered as actual -- there is plainly a way for "water is H2O" to come out false (suppose our oceans and rivers had turned out to be full of XYZ rather than H2O), so the sentence must be contingent after all, and the older description theory of reference that Kripke was arguing against turns out to have been vindicated, merely re-expressed in fancier notation. Have I understood the two-dimensional framework correctly, and if so, doesn't this mean two-dimensionalism is best read as a repudiation of Kripke's semantics for natural-kind terms, rather than the friendly refinement of it that it is usually said to be?
