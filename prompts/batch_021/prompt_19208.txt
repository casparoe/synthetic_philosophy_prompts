So in my philosophy of science seminar (we're doing a unit on the Vienna Circle) we spent a whole class period on Otto Neurath's Isotype project, which honestly interested me way more than the actual protocol-sentence readings did. Apparently it started as what he called the "Vienna Method" at some workers' education museum in Red Vienna in the 1920s and only got renamed Isotype after he had to flee to the Netherlands and then England. The thing that really stuck with me is this one rule he was super strict about: if you want to show that, say, coal output doubled, you draw the little coal-wagon icon twice, side by side, at the exact same size - you never just draw one wagon twice as big - because resizing a symbol makes people misjudge the actual ratio. There was also this whole division of labor behind it that I thought was kind of wild: a "transformer" (his collaborator, and later wife, Marie Reidemeister) decided how a given set of statistics should get turned into pictures, while a totally seperate person, the artist Gerd Arntz, drew the actual standardized little figures, specifically so no individual artistic choices could sneak bias into what was supposed to be a neutral fact. On top of that Neurath was adamant that picture language could only ever display comparative facts, never an argument, a causal claim, or a value judgement - that still had to happen in words.

I brought this up with my housemate, who's a design major, and now I can't stop thinking about what Neurath would actually say if you sat him down in front of an average news website or an Instagram feed today. Data journalism violates his no-resizing rule constantly (bubble charts and icons scaled by height, where a 20% gap ends up looking like a 300% gap), and theres also this whole modern genre of "here's what you need to know about [crisis]" picture-and-text carousel slides that are absolutely making arguments and moral claims, not just displaying stats. So would he look at current infographic culture and see a messy, half-broken version of his own dream that finally went mainstream, or would he say the whole genre crossed his fact/argument line so completely it barely counts as "picture language" in his sense anymore? Can you actually work through this using his specific reasoning (the resizing rule, the transformer/artist split, the fact-not-argument limit) rather than giving me a generic "positivist who liked pictures would distrust hype" answer - that's basically the version of this I'm trying to do better than for my response paper this week.
