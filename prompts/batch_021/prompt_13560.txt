Okay, this has been bugging me since it happened at a meetup a few weeks back (small rationalist-adjacent decision theory reading group, the kind of place where people cite LW posts unprompted) and I can't let it go. Two people who both genuinely know their game theory, Marcus and Elena, got into a real argument about the absent-minded driver problem -- the one where you need to take the second of two exits that are completely indistinguishable from inside the car, so if you're prone to forgetting (imperfect recall), once you're actually sitting at an exit you have no way to tell whether it's the first one or the second. Taking the first exit by mistake is mildly annoying, taking the second (correct) one is great, and sailing past both is the worst outcome by far. Because the two exits look identical from the inside, whatever rule you use has to assign the same "probability of continuing" at either one -- there's a single number p, fixed in advance, that maximizes your expected payoff.

Marcus opened with: this basically proves that in-the-moment Bayesian updating can conflict with the plan you'd have picked ahead of time, which is exactly the sort of case that motivates updateless decision theory -- you have to commit to p in advance, because once you're standing at an exit and start "updating on the evidence that you're at some exit," ordinary expected-utility reasoning can talk you into drifting away from the optimal p. Elena shot back that this is only a problem if you botch the self-locating probability: work out P(this is the first exit) correctly, using the very same p you're currently evaluating (since p is what determines how often each exit even gets visited), and the in-the-moment calculation and the advance plan agree exactly, every time -- so the case shows you have to be careful what you condition on, and definately not that updating itself is unreliable or that you need anything as drastic as throwing it out. Marcus wasn't satisfied: doesn't that just relocate the problem one step back? To get "the correct" P(first exit) you already need to plug in the very strategy p you're supposedly still deciding on -- that's circular as an account of deliberation, fine as a consistency check on a plan you've already fixed, but not a story about how an amnesiac driver is supposed to arrive at p from the inside, from scratch, by conditionalizing on his evidence. The only way out of the circle is to have already committed, which is the updateless move under a different name. Elena's comeback was that this complaint would indict basically all equilibrium reasoning, including ordinary game theory played against a copy of yourself, and nobody thinks that requires exotic decision theories -- the driver can just use the fixed-point condition as his actual deliberation procedure, the way anyone solves for an equilibrium strategy, and what would really back up Marcus's view is multiple fixed points, or an unstable one, neither of which this problem has (the best-response-to-itself here is unique). Marcus's last word, before we had to clear the room, was that nothing about "maximize expected utility given your evidence," on its own, tells you to go hunting for a self-consistent fixed point instead of just using flat 50/50, since the exits are subjectively indistinguishable to you right this second -- the only reason to prefer the p-dependent credence over naive indifference is that you already know the answer you're trying to justify, which is still smuggling in the plan, just one level further down.

I could see something to both sides at every step and now I honestly can't tell who came out ahead. Can you go through the exchange move by move and say who's got the better of it at each stage, not just declare an overall winner, and flag anything important that both of them are still missing?
