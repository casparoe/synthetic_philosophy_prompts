I've spent a fair amount of time over the years on Bostrom's old anthropic-principle.com preprint archive and the associated Overcoming Bias and LessWrong threads on the Doomsday Argument, so when I sat down to write the final take-home question for the formal epistemology seminar I TA, I recast one of the classic thought experiments from that literature into a slightly different setting than its usual telling. I'd like a second opinion on how to grade one particular student's answer. Grading this fairly requires catching mistakes that are easy to wave through precisely because you already know the material, and I'd rather have an independent pass than rely purely on my own read.

Here is the question as I set it, worth 20 points total:

Two rival hypotheses about the Great Filter are on the table at an astrobiology conference. According to the Common Life hypothesis (H-common), the transition from simple chemistry to technologically capable life is not especially improbable, implying that across the whole of cosmic history, on the order of 10^12 such civilizations have existed. According to the Rare Life hypothesis (H-rare), that transition is astronomically improbable, implying that only a few dozen such civilizations have ever existed. Every biochemical and geological consideration currently available makes the two hypotheses roughly equally credible, and a long-awaited analysis of the organic chemistry in Enceladus's plume is expected to move our credence by no more than a percentage point or two in either direction. A philosopher in the audience objects that running the analysis is barely worth the trouble: she argues that we can already conclude, on general anthropic grounds and without needing the new data at all, that H-common is about ten billion times more probable than H-rare.

(a) [6 points] State which anthropic principle the philosopher is relying on, and reconstruct, in your own words, exactly how it generates her conclusion.
(b) [6 points] A rival anthropic principle blocks her inference completely, without disputing any of her figures. Name this principle and explain precisely why, on this view, learning that one exists as a member of a technologically capable civilization does not by itself favor H-common over H-rare.
(c) [8 points] Philosophers versed in this material will recognize the case as a variant of a classic thought experiment, and will also see a connection to the Doomsday Argument. Identify the thought experiment and who devised it, and explain the relationship between resisting the philosopher's conclusion here and the reasoning behind the Doomsday Argument. Are the two, at bottom, one and the same argument, or does treating them that way overlook something?

And here is the student's answer, reproduced verbatim:

(a) The philosopher is relying on the Self-Indication Assumption (SIA): given that you exist, you should weight each hypothesis about how many observers there are by how many observers like you it predicts, on top of whatever your prior credence in the hypothesis already was. Because H-common implies roughly 10^12 civilizations and H-rare implies only a few dozen, the ratio of observers is on the order of 10^10, and SIA converts that ratio directly into a likelihood ratio favoring H-common -- swamping whatever percentage-point shift the Enceladus data could ever provide.

(b) The principle that blocks this is the Self-Sampling Assumption (SSA): you should reason as though you are a random sample from the observers who actually exist in your reference class, conditional on a given hypothesis, rather than as a sample across hypotheses weighted by their populations. Since the philosopher's evidence is just "I am a member of a technologically capable civilization," and every member of that reference class has exactly that evidence under both H-common and H-rare alike, SSA assigns it probability 1 under each hypothesis: there is nothing about the raw fact of one's own existence, on this view, that discriminates between a hypothesis with many such civilizations and one with few. The Enceladus data would therefore be doing all the real work after all.

(c) This is a variant of the Presumptuous Philosopher, the thought experiment John Leslie devised to embarrass the Self-Indication Assumption by having a philosopher declare one cosmological theory a trillion times likelier than a rival merely because it contains a trillion times more observers. The connection to the Doomsday Argument is not just an analogy: the two are at bottom the very same argument. The Doomsday Argument gets its force from exactly the SSA-style refusal to let raw population size move your credence, applied to the temporal case rather than the spatial one. Once you accept, as in (b), that your own existence as a certain kind of observer cannot by itself tell you whether observers of your kind are common or rare across the universe, you have already accepted the premise that lets Carter and Leslie run the parallel argument across time instead of space, concluding that humanity is unlikely to have a long future ahead of it. Resisting the philosopher here and accepting the Doomsday Argument are therefore not two separate commitments but one.

Could you grade this out of 20, broken down by part (a)/(b)/(c), and give me the specific comments that justify each score rather than just an overall impression? I'd find it most useful as a JSON object with one key per sub-question, each holding the points awarded out of that part's maximum and a short comment, plus top-level "total" and "letter_grade" fields.
