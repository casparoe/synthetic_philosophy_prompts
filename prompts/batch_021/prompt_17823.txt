I've been re-reading old LW and ACX posts on the St. Petersburg paradox and Pascal's mugging lately, which sent me down a rabbit hole into Nover and Hajek's much more obscure "Pasadena game" (from their paper "Vexing Expectations"). Quick recap: you toss a coin til it lands heads, and on toss n you get paid (-1)^(n-1) * 2^n/n, so the payoffs blow up in size and alternate between rewards and penalties. Sum the payoffs in the order the tosses actually resolve and you get the alternating harmonic series, 1 - 1/2 + 1/3 - 1/4 + ..., which converges to ln(2). But since that series only converges conditionally, Nover and Hajek invoke the Riemann rearrangement theorem to argue the terms can be reshuffled to sum to any value at all (including +/- infinity), so the game's expectation isn't just infinite like St. Petersburg's, its flat out undefined. This has always felt like a bit of a sleight of hand to me — the tosses happen in an actual sequence, so summing them in that sequence seems like the obviously non-arbitrary choice, not one rearrangement among uncountably many equally-good ones. I know they try to preempt exactly this move in the paper (there's a relabeling trick where reindexing the outcomes into triplets "naturally" gets you 3/2 ln(2) instead), but I don't think that's the knockdown argument they think it is. Please argue as strongly as you can that the Pasadena game does have a well-defined expected value, namely ln(2), and that Nover and Hajek's conclusion that the expectation is simply undefined is wrong.
