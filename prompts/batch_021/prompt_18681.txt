In a seminar on moral responsibility, we spent the first few weeks on Harry Frankfurt's argument that an agent can be blameworthy (or praiseworthy) for what she does even though she could not have done otherwise, and then our instructor sent us back to the late seventeenth century, to Locke's chapter "Of Power" in the Essay Concerning Human Understanding, on the theory that Locke had already stumbled onto something like Frankfurt's insight without quite realizing what he had. Having now reread the relevant sections several times, I am no longer sure the comparison holds up, and I would like help thinking it through. Here is the passage (Book II, Chapter XXI, §§8 and 10):

"[§8] So far as a man has power to think or not to think, to move or not to move, according to the preference or direction of his own mind, so far is a man free. Wherever any performance or forbearance are not equally in a man's power; wherever doing or not doing will not equally follow upon the preference of his mind directing it, there he is not free, though perhaps the action may be voluntary. So that the idea of liberty is, the idea of a power in any agent to do or forbear any particular action, according to the determination or thought of the mind, whereby either of them is preferred to the other: where either of them is not in the power of the agent to be produced by him according to his volition, there he is not at liberty; that agent is under necessity.

[§10] Again: suppose a man be carried, whilst fast asleep, into a room where is a person he longs to see and speak with; and be there locked fast in, beyond his power to get out: he awakes, and is glad to find himself in so desirable company, which he stays willingly in, i.e. prefers his stay to going away. I ask, is not this stay voluntary? I think nobody will doubt it: and yet, being locked fast in, it is evident he is not at liberty not to stay, he has not freedom to be gone. So that liberty is not an idea belonging to volition, or preferring; but to the person having the power of doing, or forbearing to do, according as the mind shall choose or direct. Our idea of liberty reaches as far as that power, and no farther. For wherever restraint comes to check that power, or compulsion takes away that indifferency of ability to bear acting, there liberty, and our notion of it, presently ceases."

On first reading, the locked-room man looks like a Frankfurt case avant la lettre: he acts entirely from his own preference, he could not have done otherwise, and no reader is tempted to excuse him or to deny that the staying is fully his own. But what Locke actually concludes from the example seems to cut the other way. He does not say the man is free, still less that he is responsible for staying; he says only that liberty "presently ceases" the moment restraint takes hold, even though the action remains voluntary. Frankfurt's cases are built to establish the opposite conclusion: that responsibility survives the loss of alternatives. So it looks as though Locke and Frankfurt might be using structurally similar scenarios to defend opposite theses, or perhaps just to answer different questions that only look like the same question.

Could you help me get clear on this? Specifically: (1) given Locke's own definitions in §8, what exactly does the locked-room case establish — is it a claim about moral responsibility at all, or only about the relation between "voluntary" and "free"; (2) does it matter, for the comparison with Frankfurt, that Locke's restraint is a standing, categorical inability to leave rather than a merely counterfactual mechanism that would intervene only if the man tried to go; and (3) if Locke's example is not yet a genuine counterexample to the principle that responsibility requires the ability to do otherwise, what would need to be added to it to make it one, and would Locke's own framework even permit that addition?
