I've been rereading Nick Bostrom's 2011 paper "Infinite Ethics," and I keep getting stuck on what he calls "infinitarian paralysis": if the universe contains infinitely many value-bearing locations, standard aggregative consequentialism implies the total value is already infinite in both directions, so no finite action - even preventing a genocide - changes the cardinal sum, and every choice comes out ethically indifferent. His proposed patches (comparing worlds only when one dominates the other across every finite sub-region, or extending utility to the hyperreals via a non-principal ultrafilter) either fall silent in most realistic cases or turn out to hinge on an arbitrary choice of ultrafilter. He also suggests you don't even need certainty about an infinite universe for the problem to bite: merely assigning it non-negligible credence is enough to swamp any expected-value calculation, since it would take a near-miraculous cancellation for competing infinite possibilities to offset each other exactly. Given that Bostrom himself treats this as an open problem, what do you take to be its most important implications for how a longtermist effective altruist should actually divide a marginal donation between existential-risk work and nearer-term causes like global health or animal welfare?
