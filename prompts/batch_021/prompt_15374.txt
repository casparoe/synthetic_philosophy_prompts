I've been reporting a feature on the recent, widely covered wave of tic-like behaviour among teenagers that clinicians increasingly link to heavy social media use, alongside the parallel rise in adolescents self-diagnosing ADHD and even dissociative identity disorder after watching short-form videos about the conditions. Nearly every psychiatrist and philosopher of science I've interviewed eventually reaches for Ian Hacking's notion of "looping effects" — the idea that classifying someone as a certain kind of person can change how they behave, which then feeds back into the classification itself — and treats it as though this settles the underlying dispute. When I press for detail, though, the conversation usually stalls at the level of book titles, and I would like to see the disagreement it's supposed to resolve actually worked through by two people who take the empirical stakes seriously.

Could you write a dialogue between two philosophers of psychiatry, freshly out of the same workshop talk on the adolescent tic clusters, who take opposite sides of this question? One should argue that Hacking's apparatus — interactive versus indifferent kinds, dynamic nominalism, the picture of diagnostic categories as "moving targets" — shows that classifications like ADHD or dissociative identity disorder cannot be natural kinds even in principle, however far biomarker research advances. The other should grant that looping genuinely occurs but insist that it is a removable layer sitting on top of a real underlying kind that patient neuroscience, in the spirit of NIMH's RDoC program, will eventually expose. I'd like the realist to press her counterpart on exactly what it would take — short of completing neuroscience — to convince him that a given diagnostic category has stopped moving and become a stable natural kind, and I'd like him to explain in turn why the same looping story doesn't equally dissolve an uncontroversially biological case such as general paresis, which did turn out to track a real kind once its cause was identified. If they can, I'd like them to work out whether what actually divides them is a live empirical question or a prior, almost definitional disagreement about what is allowed to count as a "kind" at all.

Please keep both characters equally sharp and equally willing to make trouble for the other's position — I don't want either one to be a straw man the other simply knocks down, and I want them genuinely responding to each other's specific objections rather than talking past each other.
