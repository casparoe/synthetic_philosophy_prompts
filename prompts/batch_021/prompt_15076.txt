A friend and I spent an entire dinner arguing about coherent extrapolated volition -- the AI-alignment idea that a system should act on what we'd want if we knew more, thought faster, and were the people we wished we were, not on our stated wishes now. I called this a license for someone else's judgment to override yours while calling it your own; she called it the only honest way to honor a person's deeper interests when their in-the-moment and longer-run selves disagree. I now sit on the product ethics board for an AI personal-assistant company, and we're facing a scaled-down version of the same question. At issue is a feature that would let the assistant delay or flag -- never block -- a request that conflicts with a user's own long-term goals, as recorded in months of their budgeting notes and prior requests: a 2 a.m. transfer out of a savings account they've repeatedly called "untouchable," say. Half the board calls this exactly the judgment a trusted assistant should exercise; the other half calls it paternalism dressed up in respectable philosophy, since nothing tells us the reflective self deserves more authority than the one making the request at 2 a.m. Draft the memo I'll bring to the board: recommend approval, rejection, or approval with specific limits, and make the losing side's case well enough that they'd recognize it as fair, not a strawman.
