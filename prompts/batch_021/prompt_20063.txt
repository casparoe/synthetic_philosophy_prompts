I help maintain a mid-sized open-source project, and as it has grown, the maintainers have started debating whether to set up a more formal governance process, and if so, who should actually count as a voting "member": core contributors, anyone who has ever filed an issue, corporate sponsors who pay people to work on it, or downstream users who never touch the code but are heavily affected by our decisions. While reading around this question, I came across what political theorists call the boundary problem: the puzzle of how to justify the boundary of the demos — the group of people entitled to make a collective decision — which has to be settled before any question of how their votes get aggregated even arises. Two rival standards seem to be on offer: an all-affected-interests principle, under which anyone whose interests are shaped by a decision should get a say, and an all-subjected principle, which restricts the vote to those actually bound by the resulting rules; and, troublingly, there seems to be no way to choose between them, or to draw any boundary at all, without already presupposing some group entitled to do the choosing. Given this, please describe the most important implications of the boundary problem for how I should think about defining the electorate for governance decisions in a project like mine.
