In their 2016 paper "Logical Induction," Scott Garrabrant and coauthors at MIRI propose a computable procedure for assigning credences to sentences of a formal language -- including open mathematical conjectures -- that converges toward coherence and calibration over time. The construction is cast as a financial market: a countable collection of computable "traders" buy and sell shares in the truth of various sentences as a deductive process slowly enumerates theorems, and the logical induction criterion requires that no trader be able to exploit the resulting price sequence for unbounded, guaranteed profit. This has been offered as a genuine answer to the problem of logical omniscience: ordinary probability theory forces any coherent reasoner to assign probability 1 to every theorem of arithmetic, including ones nobody has proved, which is an absurd demand on any bounded reasoner, human or machine.

I've been sitting in on a reading group that has split roughly down the middle on how much this construction actually delivers philosophically. One camp is impressed that the framework provably learns statistical regularities in advance of proof, keeps its credences appropriately non-extreme on sentences that are independent or simply unsettled, and can even accommodate sentences that refer to its own future prices without collapsing into paradox -- properties no naive assignment of probabilities to logical sentences achieves. The other camp keeps coming back to the fact that every one of these guarantees is asymptotic and comes with no general bound on the rate of convergence: nothing in the theorems constrains what the market believes at any given finite stage, so a real, time-bound reasoner gets no more concrete guidance from logical induction than from being told that the truth will eventually out. They also argue that weighting the traders by some complexity measure, in order to make the market well-defined at all, just relocates the arbitrariness that a "logical prior" was supposed to remove, rather than eliminating it. Write an essay that lays out the strongest version of each side and then defends your own verdict on whether logical induction constitutes a genuine dissolution of the problem of logical uncertainty or a mathematically elegant construction that leaves the underlying epistemological problem essentially untouched. If your answer ends up conditional, say exactly what it's conditional on, but don't leave the question open.
