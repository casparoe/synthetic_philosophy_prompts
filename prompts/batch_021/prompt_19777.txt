I'm at work on a novel whose two central figures are Mira, a retired professor of mathematical logic, and her nephew Tomas, who has moved in with her for a few months while he negotiates a difficult divorce. Tomas keeps insisting that there must be some neutral, defensible way to split the marital assets — some baseline that isn't simply whoever argues harder walking away with more — and in the chapter I'm drafting, Mira reaches for an old probability puzzle to needle him out of that conviction. I want the scene to do real philosophical work rather than just gesture vaguely at "things are complicated," so before I go further I'd like a close read of the passage below.

Mira set two glasses of wine on the table and did not sit down. "You keep talking as if there's a fair split sitting out there somewhere, waiting to be discovered, if only the two of you were reasonable enough to find it. I want to show you why that's not obviously true, even in a case with no lawyers and no feelings in it at all."

"This ought to be good," Tomas said.

"Draw a circle." He did. "Now inscribe an equilateral triangle in it, touching the circle at three points. Here's the question: if you choose a chord of the circle at random — just a straight line connecting two points on the edge — what's the probability that the chord turns out longer than a side of the triangle?"

"I don't know. A half?"

"You can get a half. Pick a direction out from the center, slide a point uniformly along that radius, and draw the chord perpendicular to it through that point — the chord beats the triangle's side exactly when your point falls in the inner half of the radius, so: one-half. But choose the chord a different honest way — put both endpoints down independently, uniformly at random, on the circle itself — and the answer is one-third. Or pick the chord's midpoint uniformly at random from inside the whole disc, and it comes out one-quarter."

"Those can't all be right."

"They're all correct arithmetic about the method you chose. That's the point. 'At random' isn't one thing. Each of those three procedures has an equally good claim to being the neutral, no-thumb-on-the-scale way of picking a chord, and they hand you three different numbers. There's no vantage point above the three of them that tells you which procedure was the truly unbiased one, because 'unbiased' was never free of a procedure to begin with." She finally sat down. "So when you tell me there's a fair split of your marriage sitting out there waiting to be found, independent of any method for finding it, I want to know which of the three chords you think you're drawing — and why you trust it over the other two."

I'd welcome your comments on a few fronts. First, is the mathematics as I've rendered it actually sound — are those genuinely the three classical answers to this puzzle, and have I described each procedure correctly? Second, is Mira's inference fair philosophical play, or is she smuggling in a stronger conclusion — that there's no fact of the matter about unbiasedness at all — than the puzzle actually licenses? I have a nagging, possibly unreliable memory that some statistician or physicist argued one of the three procedures can be singled out as objectively correct on symmetry grounds, which would undercut her speech considerably if true; I'd like to know whether that memory is accurate, and if so, whether it genuinely damages her argument or whether she has a good rejoinder available. Finally, does her closing analogy to the divorce actually hold together, or is she quietly sliding from "there is no uniquely neutral procedure" to the much stronger "no split is any more defensible than any other"? I'd rather catch that kind of slippage now than have a sharp reader catch it later.
