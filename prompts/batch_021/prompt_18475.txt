I'm a junior at my high school, and this year I was elected to our Honor Council -- the student-faculty board that investigates academic integrity cases and sends the dean a recommended resolution for each one. We have a hearing this week that has split the council more than any case I've seen so far, and, probably because I took AP Psychology last year, I've ended up being the one asked to write the discussion memo we'll go over before Thursday's vote.

The case: during a chemistry final, a proctor who has monitored exams at our school for over a decade reported seeing a sophomore, Jordan, tilt his paper and glance toward his neighbor's desk twice. What complicates things is that, a few days earlier, a different teacher had mentioned to this proctor, informally, that Jordan was "someone to watch," based on a cheating rumor from last semester that later turned out to be baseless. Jordan's advocate argues that once that expectation was planted in the proctor's mind, the problem isn't just that she may have judged ambiguous behavior more harshly than she otherwise would have: there is real psychological research suggesting that what we expect to see can change what we actually perceive in the moment, not merely how we interpret it afterward, so her report might not amount to the "direct, firsthand observation" our Honor Code requires for a finding. Several other council members are unconvinced. They point out that no observation is ever expectation-free -- arguably that is the whole reason we station experienced proctors instead of substitutes who do not know the students -- so if we discount testimony whenever the observer had some prior belief about the student, we would have to throw out nearly every case that is not caught on camera.

Could you draft the memo I'll circulate to the council? It should recommend a specific resolution for Jordan's case, propose a general standard the council can use in future cases for weighing proctor testimony when expectation may have shaped what was seen, and take the "we can't function without some expectation-guided observation" worry seriously rather than wave it away.
