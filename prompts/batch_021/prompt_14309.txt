I'm writing a novel centered on Elizabeth Anscombe's 1956 pamphlet Mr. Truman's Degree, written during her unsuccessful campaign to stop Oxford from awarding Harry Truman an honorary degree, and a chapter I recently circulated to my manuscript group sparked a genuine argument between two of its members that I haven't been able to settle myself.

Helen took Anscombe's side: Truman chose to kill innocent civilians as the means of forcing Japan's surrender, and choosing to kill the innocent as a means to your ends is murder, whatever good comes of it afterward. Marcus objected that this only works if "killing civilians" and "ending the war" can be cleanly separated as two different things Truman intended, when in fact he performed a single act under many true descriptions, most of them innocuous — decide which description counts as the intention, and you've simply legislated your conclusion in advance. Helen replied that the targeting decisions settle the matter: planners selected largely intact city centers rather than the remaining military-industrial sites already identified, so the civilian deaths weren't incidental to striking something else — they were the mechanism by which surrender was to be extracted. Marcus came back that this proves too much, since the same reasoning damns the conventional firebombing of Tokyo, Hamburg, and Dresden, which were at least as indiscriminate and drew nothing like the same censure from Anscombe or anyone else at the time; either the "means" charge generalizes far beyond the bomb, or something other than intention is doing the real work in her argument.

Could you go through this move by move? Say who has the better of each of the four points, not just who wins overall, and close by noting anything important about Anscombe's actual position, or about the objections to it, that neither Helen nor Marcus raises.
