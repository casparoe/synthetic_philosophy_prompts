I'm putting together a collection of short fiction in which each piece grows out of a real episode in the history of ideas, and I'd like a draft built around Henry Sidgwick. For nearly two decades, Sidgwick was the guiding figure behind the Society for Psychical Research, bringing to séances and mediums the same scrupulous, self-doubting rigor he brought to his ethics. That rigor was rooted partly in a worry that morality could not be fully rational without some evidence bearing on the soul's survival -- a difficulty he named the dualism of practical reason. He died in 1900 still refusing to pronounce the evidence conclusive in either direction. Write a short story of roughly a thousand words, set at a sitting late in his life and told from close inside his perspective, that dramatizes the pull between his hunger for proof and his refusal to grant himself more certainty than the evidence allows. Resist the genre's usual shape: let the story close, as his research did, without ever settling whether anything real happened in that room.
