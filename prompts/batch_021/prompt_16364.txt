I'm an economics and philosophy double major, and this term I'm taking a seminar on the philosophy of economics. We recently spent a couple of sessions on Samuelson's revealed-preference program: the idea that "utility" should be defined entirely in terms of whatever consistent ordering rationalizes an agent's observed choices, with no leftover appeal to introspectible psychological states. A few of us stayed behind after the second session arguing about whether anything of substance survives that move. If utility just is the ordering that makes your choices consistent, is there still some further fact — about well-being, satisfaction, or strength of desire — that the word is supposed to track, or has the whole notion quietly turned into a bookkeeping device with no independent content?

I'd like to see that disagreement actually worked out rather than just gestured at, so could you write a dialogue between two characters on opposite sides of it. One should be a strict revealed-preference theorist in the Samuelsonian mold, who holds that utility talk is fully exhausted by whatever consistency conditions let choice data be represented by an ordering, and who treats any appeal to a further psychological or normative fact behind the choices as unnecessary metaphysics that only makes the theory harder to test. The other should be a mentalist or welfarist about utility, drawing on worries in the spirit of Sen's, who thinks a purely behavioural notion of rationality can't distinguish an agent's genuine interests from choices shaped by convention, framing, or the sheer menu of alternatives on offer, and who insists the theory needs some independent notion of well-being to do any explanatory or evaluative work at all.

Please have them fight this out over a specific case rather than staying at the level of slogans — for instance, a shopper who picks the mid-priced of three wines but would have picked the cheapest had only two been on the shelf (something like the "compromise effect" documented in choice research), or a comparably concrete case of your choosing. Let the revealed-preference theorist try the natural move of individuating the "alternatives" more finely so that consistency is preserved after all, and have the welfarist push back on whether that move is principled or just an ad hoc way of insulating the theory from falsification. Neither should be a pushover: give each side its strongest form, have them respond to each other's actual arguments over several exchanges rather than talking past one another, and end with them facing a further question squarely — whether anything like "behavioural welfare economics," which tries to infer an agent's own good from a cleaned-up version of her choices, can get off the ground at all without secretly presupposing the very psychological facts the revealed-preference theorist refuses to allow.
