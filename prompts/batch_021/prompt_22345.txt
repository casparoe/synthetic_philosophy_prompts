I'm a retired oceanographer (forty years on ocean circulation before I finally escaped committee meetings for good), and lately I've been chewing through Hegel's Philosophy of Right just to keep my brain busy in retirement. I'm using the old 1896 Dyde translation since its free online, and honestly the prose is a slog, but paragraph 324 - the bit on war - has got me stuck on something specific. Here's the passage:

"War is not to be regarded as an absolute evil. It is not a merely external accident, having its accidental ground in the passions of powerful individuals or nations, in acts of injustice, or in anything which ought not to be. Accident befalls that which is by nature accidental, and this fate is a necessity... It is necessary that what is finite, such as life and property, should have its contingent nature exposed, since contingency is inherent in the conception of the finite. This necessity has in one phase of it the form of a force of nature, since all that is finite is mortal and transient. But in the ethical life, that is to say, the state, this force and nature are separated. Necessity becomes in this way exalted to the work of freedom, and becomes a force which is ethical... Moreover, by it, as I have elsewhere expressed it, 'finite pursuits are rendered unstable, and the ethical health of peoples is preserved. Just as the movement of the ocean prevents the corruption which would be the result of perpetual calm, so by war people escape the corruption which would be occasioned by a continuous or eternal peace.'"

Here's my snag. He goes out of his way to say that in the ethical life of the state, "force and nature are separated" - the necessity behind war is supposed to get lifted up into something willed and free, not just blind natural process anymore. Fine, ok. But then, when he actually has to explain why war is good for a nation, the reason he reaches for is a completely natural-world image: wind stirring up the sea so it doesn't go stagnant. I spent my career on actual ocean circulation, and that's a real physical mechanism he's borrowing, not just a turn of phrase. So which is it? He wants the dignity of "this is freedom, not nature" and then wants the explanatory punch of a nature analogy for that exact same claim. Is that a genuine soft spot in his arguement, or is there something about how Hegel thinks nature and spirit relate that means this isn't the contradiction it looks like to me?
