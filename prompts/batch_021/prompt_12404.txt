Last week, the night before she sent off her final proofs, my friend Mara and I got into a two-hour argument over a single sentence in the preface to her first book. Mara is an epistemologist, and after four hundred pages establishing claim after claim about the norms of testimony, she wanted to end the preface with something like: "I believe every claim defended in this book, and I am equally confident that a book this long contains at least one error I failed to catch." I told her that was a strange thing to put in print, and pushed the obvious comparison: if she is willing to believe, of each of a thousand lottery tickets, that it will lose, while also believing that one of them will win, that is exactly the structure of Kyburg's old lottery paradox — and that is normally treated as a problem to be solved, not a stance to defend in your own preface.

Mara wouldn't concede the point. She agreed the two cases have the same shape — high confidence in each of many claims, paired with confidence that they cannot all be true — but she insisted they are not epistemically on a par: a thousand lottery tickets all rest on exactly the same shared statistical setup, she said, while her hundreds of claims about testimony each rest on their own separate, individually gathered evidence, and she saw no reason a solution suited to the first case must automatically suit the second. I pressed her for the rest of that argument for the better part of an hour, over increasingly cold coffee, and never got a version of it I could either cleanly accept or refute; she left the table just as unconvinced by my insistence that a contradiction is a contradiction regardless of its subject matter.

I am giving the toast at her launch party this Friday, in a room that will be roughly half fellow epistemologists — several of whom will recognize that preface sentence instantly as the small act of provocation it is — and half family, her editor, and friends from outside philosophy who have no idea any of this is contentious. Could you write me that toast, something I could actually deliver in about five minutes? I want it to be warm and genuinely funny rather than a lecture in disguise, but I do not want it to flatten our whole argument into a bland, toast-appropriate moral like "we are all fallible." It should explain the lottery paradox and the preface paradox clearly enough for the non-philosophers in the room to follow, be honest about why people have thought the two might come apart despite looking identical on paper, and be a little generous to Mara's side of things — because three days later, I have started to suspect she was closer to right than I was.
