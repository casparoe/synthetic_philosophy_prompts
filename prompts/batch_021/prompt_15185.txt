I'm several chapters into a historical novel that follows Frederick Douglass through the 1850s, and I've reached the point where I need to dramatize his rupture with William Lloyd Garrison over how to read the U.S. Constitution. Before I put words in anyone's mouth, I sketched out the notes below to fix the actual intellectual content of the dispute in my own mind. I'd be grateful if you could look them over and:

2. Tell me which of the specific moments or arguments below has the most potential as a scene of dialogue, and why;
3. Give me the strongest version of the Garrisonian counter-case that I'm currently shortchanging, since my Garrison character will be a strawman if I don't understand why a serious, morally uncompromising man held that view.

Notes toward the scene:

Douglass came up in the movement as a Garrisonian, and Garrison's reading of the Constitution was that its bargains with slaveholders — the three-fifths clause, the fugitive-slave clause, the twenty-year shield for the Atlantic slave trade — made it, in his own words, a covenant with death and an agreement with hell: a document to be renounced, not worked within. On this view, voting or holding federal office under such a compact was itself a form of complicity, and "disunion" was the only clean position left to a Northerner.

By the end of the 1840s, settled in Rochester with his own paper, Douglass was spending time with Gerrit Smith and reading the constitutional arguments of men like Lysander Spooner and William Goodell, who insisted that the document's text and Preamble, read on their own terms, said nothing that required a pro-slavery construction — that the Garrisonian reading was a choice, not a logical necessity. Douglass announced his change of mind in public in 1851, to Garrison's lasting anger, and the two were never really reconciled.

The fullest statements of the new position come in 1852, in the Rochester Fourth of July address, where he calls the Constitution, properly interpreted, "a glorious liberty document," and again in an 1860 lecture in Glasgow devoted entirely to the question of whether the Constitution is pro- or anti-slavery, where he presses the more technical point that the words "slave" and "slavery" appear nowhere in the text at all. He is explicit, by this point, that a legal document should be read by its words and not by guesswork about the private convictions of the men who drafted it — a principle that puts him, curiously, on the opposite side of the text-versus-intent question from Taney's opinion in Dred Scott only a few years earlier.

What I can't work out from my own reading is how much of this was arrived-at conviction and how much became convenient once Douglass had thrown in with Smith's political abolitionists and needed a Constitution a voter could use rather than only a moral witness could denounce — and that ambiguity is exactly what I want the scene to hold.
