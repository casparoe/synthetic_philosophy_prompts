At my school's philosophy club we've been discussing illusionism about consciousness: the view, associated with Keith Frankish and, in some moods, Daniel Dennett, that phenomenal consciousness — the raw, ineffable "feel" of experience — isn't a real feature of the world but an introspective illusion. Two members, Maya and Theo, got into an exchange our faculty advisor never quite settled.

(1) Maya defended illusionism: there is no extra felt quality to seeing red beyond the relevant brain processes; introspection just misrepresents a purely functional state as having some further intrinsic, ineffable character, and once that misrepresentation is subtracted, nothing phenomenal remains to explain.

(2) Theo objected that this only relocates the problem: even if the judgment "my experience has an ineffable quality" is false, undergoing that false impression is still something, so illusionism doesn't dissolve the hard problem, it just pushes it back a step, into the seeming itself.

(3) Maya answered by separating the representing from the represented: with the Müller-Lyer illusion, explaining why the lines seem unequal doesn't require the seeming-unequal itself to carry any special phenomenal ingredient beyond ordinary information processing, so why should introspection be any different?

(4) Theo replied that the analogy fails: with Müller-Lyer we can describe the whole illusion from outside, in third-person terms, with no residual question, but a purely functional story about introspection still owes an explanation of why the misrepresentation is felt by someone, rather than occurring unfelt, the way a thermostat "represents" temperature without anyone home to be fooled.

(5) Maya's last move was that "felt" just means "integrated into the kind of globally accessible, reportable processing a thermostat lacks," so given the right architecture, there's no further fact about feeling left over.
