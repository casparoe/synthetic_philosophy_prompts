I chair the three-person ethics panel for The Understory, a small literary quarterly I help run (a couple thousand subscribers, but we take our slush pile seriously, or did until this week). We're stuck on a plagiarism complaint that's turned into a real committee headache and I need an actual document to put in front of the other two panelists, not just my own gut feeling. Two years ago we rejected a submission from a writer I'll call R — a story about a small-town obituary writer who starts recieving anonymous letters that always arrive a few days before the letter-writer dies, specific enough that I don't buy two people landing on it independently. Last issue we published a story with more or less that exact premise by a different writer, M, who happened to be reading slush for us the same season R's story came through. R wants a public correction and is hinting hard at a lawyer if we say no.

Our bylaws only let us uphold a complaint like this if the panel finds BOTH that the accused had access to the original manuscript AND that the resemblance goes beyond premise into substantial similarity of actual expression — scenes, structure, language, not just the idea. Here's the fork: I think M clearly had access (she was logging into the same submissions system that whole season) but that what she shares with R is just the premise, not the execution, so I vote no violation. Our fiction editor thinks access is genuinely shaky (the software doesn't log which specific submissions a reader opens, and M swears she never saw it) but she's rattled by three scenes that track almost beat for beat, so she votes yes on similarity and no overall, because for her access is the sticking point. Our third panelist, a very literal-minded retired copyeditor, is convinced on both counts and votes to uphold. So: a straight yes/no poll of the three of us comes out 2-1 against upholding. But tally us issue by issue instead — access, then similarity, as separate questions — and a majority says yes on access (me and the copyeditor) and a separate majority says yes on similarity (the fiction editor and the copyeditor), and our own bylaws say access-plus-similarity equals a violation, full stop. I have one philosophy class from twenty years ago vaguely telling me this is A Whole Known Thing, and I brought it up at my writing group and watched everyone's eyes glaze over, but its a genuine fork, not one I've invented to sound clever, and I don't trust my own read on it since I'm not neutral here.

Could you draft the short memo I need to send the other two panelists by Friday, before we respond to R? It should explain, in a way I could actually defend out loud, why the two ways of counting our votes come apart instead of just noting that they do; recommend which count we should trust for this case and say plainly what that means for R and M; and propose a standing rule for how our panels should vote on complaints going forward, so we're not re-litigating the counting method every time. And please take seriously the objection I already know our copyeditor is going to raise if we go with a straight poll: that doing it that way lets my theory of the case and the fiction editor's completely different theory quietly cancel out the fact that a majority of us think M had access and a separate majority thinks the stories are substantially similar, which by our own rule is a violation — and that a straight poll just launders that away without anyone having to defend it on the merits.
