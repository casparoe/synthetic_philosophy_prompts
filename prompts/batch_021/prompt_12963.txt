I've been asked to give a short toast at the retirement dinner for my high school's physics teacher, who spent over thirty years coaching our Physics Olympiad team and built her entire junior-year unit around Bell's theorem. Her one inviolable classroom rule was that nobody could say Bell's theorem "proved Einstein wrong about spooky action at a distance": she'd remind us that Einstein used that exact phrase decades before Bell's theorem existed, and that what Bell actually showed is a dilemma, not a verdict -- you can keep locality, or you can keep the assumption that particles have definite properties before they're measured, but the experimental violations of Bell's inequality mean you cannot keep both. Half the room will be Olympiad alumni who can still recite the CHSH inequality from memory; the other half will be family and colleagues who know her only as "the terrifyingly intense physics teacher." Could you write me a four-to-five-minute toast that uses her insistence on that distinction as its throughline -- showing what it taught us beyond physics -- without turning into a lecture that loses the non-physicists in the room?
