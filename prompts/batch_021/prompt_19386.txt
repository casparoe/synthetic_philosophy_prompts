THE QUESTION:
Aquinas is sometimes summarized as having "endorsed tyrannicide," but his actual discussions of the topic, in the Commentary on the Sentences and in De Regno, are a good deal more qualified. Explain the distinction(s) Aquinas draws between kinds of tyranny, explain who he thinks is authorized to act against a tyrant and by what means in each case, and close with a short assessment of how fair the popular one-line summary actually is.

THE NOVICE'S ANSWER:
Aquinas's position is a lot more careful than the slogan "Aquinas said you can kill tyrants" suggests. He distinguishes two kinds of tyrant: the tyrant by usurpation, who has seized power with no legitimate title at all, and the tyrant merely in the exercise of rule, who holds legitimate title but governs unjustly. This distinction is what determines who may act.

For the usurper, Aquinas is relatively permissive: because such a man never had rightful authority to begin with, resisting or removing him isn't really an act against legitimate government -- it's closer to resisting a criminal, so a private person may licitly use force against him, especially where there's no public body able to act. He does add the caveat that this shouldn't be done from mere private presumption, since a botched attempt can easily make things worse than the tyranny itself.

For the legitimate ruler who has turned tyrannical, Aquinas's view is essentially the mirror image: because the ruler was legitimately given his position by the community, any private individual he wrongs keeps the natural right to resist him, and if necessary kill him in self-defense, exactly as one may resist any unjust aggressor -- the fact that the aggressor happens to be a king doesn't cancel the underlying natural-law right to self-preservation. What Aquinas insists on isn't that private individuals lack a right to act here, only that they should exhaust legal and communal channels, like an appeal to whoever installed the king, before resorting to force.

Aquinas's own final word, especially in De Regno, is actually more pessimistic about violent solutions generally: if no legal remedy is available, he thinks the community should fall back on patient endurance and prayer rather than rebellion, on the grounds -- original to him -- that a tyrant's rule can be a scourge a sinful people brings on itself, so killing him doesn't really fix the underlying problem.

So the one-line popular summary is misleading: on Aquinas's actual view, tyrannicide is something narrowly conditioned on the legitimacy of the ruler's title and the exhaustion of proper channels, not a blanket permission.
