I'm a second-year student in a bioethics MA program, and this term's clinical ethics practicum has placed me as a non-voting student member of my teaching hospital's death-determination policy subcommittee. It happens to line up with the unit we're currently on in my philosophy of medicine seminar: the "whole-brain" rationale for legal death, and Alan Shewmon's case-based challenge to it. His documentation of patients who pass every standard bedside test for brain death yet go on, sometimes for years, to show integrated bodily functions — many never develop diabetes insipidus, implying retained hypothalamic vasopressin secretion, and there are even a handful of reported cases of continued gestation — suggests that "irreversible cessation of all functions of the entire brain," the actual statutory phrase, is not what our tests really verify. We test for lost brainstem reflexes and lost consciousness, not literally every function of every part of the brain.

This stopped being a seminar question when the subcommittee received an actual proposal to vote on next month: replace "all functions of the entire brain" with cessation of a named shorter list of "critical functions" (consciousness, brainstem reflexes, capacity to breathe), and replace "irreversible" with "permanent" for the circulatory criterion, so that our organ-procurement team's short stand-off times are unambiguously compliant rather than resting on a technicality. Roughly half the committee, including the chair, sees this as an honest alignment of policy with what physicians have always meant in practice, closing a liability gap besides; the other half, including the hospital chaplain, worries this quietly redefines death to suit transplantation logistics while calling it a clarification, and that it will look exactly that cynical to a grieving family — or to a reporter — the first time a Shewmon-type case surfaces locally.

I've been asked to draft the discussion memo that goes to the full committee before the vote, and I'd like your help. The memo should take a clear, defensible position on whether to adopt the revised language, lay out a concrete implementation timeline together with any interim disclosure or family-communication safeguards you think adoption should be conditioned on, and engage honestly with the chaplain's "redefinition dressed as clarification" worry rather than setting it aside — I want to know why it is, or isn't, ultimately right about what determining someone's death should require.
