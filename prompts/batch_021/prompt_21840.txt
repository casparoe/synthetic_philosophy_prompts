In Jonathan Schaffer's 'trumping preemption' case, a major and a sergeant simultaneously order a corporal to advance, and by protocol the corporal's action always tracks the higher-ranking officer's order, regardless of what the sergeant does. Since the corporal would have advanced on the sergeant's order alone even had the major stayed silent, the advance does not counterfactually depend on the major's order — yet intuitively it is the major's order, not the sergeant's, that causes the advance. It can seem as though the interventionist, structural-equations approach has an easy win here over the plain counterfactual theory, since one can simply write the officers' priority into the structural equation governing the corporal's behavior. Yet on the standard Halpern–Pearl definition of actual causation, that priority-respecting equation still counts the sergeant's order as a cause too: the very device that rescues the major's own status as a cause — testing for dependence under contingencies in which the other officer's order is imagined away — equally rescues the sergeant's. Argue that this shows the structural-equations approach enjoys no principled advantage over the counterfactual theory when it comes to trumping: excluding the sergeant's order as a cause requires an independent ranking of which contingencies count as 'normal,' and such a ranking is no less stipulative than the priority a counterfactual theorist could simply posit by fiat. Defend your conclusion against the objection that needing further resources to handle hard cases is true of every theory of causation, and so is no special mark against interventionism in particular.
