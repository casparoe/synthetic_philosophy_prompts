I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and I'm reporting a piece on what philosophers call the "numbers problem" in rescue ethics. John Taurek's classic argument denies that we are obligated to save the greater number when we can only save one of two groups, since each person's loss is complete in itself and there is no larger, aggregate loss suffered by the bigger group; his own proposed remedy is to flip a coin between the two groups, giving each a fifty percent chance of being saved. Some philosophers writing in his wake have argued instead for a lottery weighted by numbers, in which a group's odds of rescue scale with how many people are in it. Please make the strongest case you can that Taurek's own premises — the separateness of persons and his commitment to equal concern and respect for each individual — actually commit him to the weighted lottery rather than the flat coin flip he endorsed, and then give the strongest reply a committed Taurekian could make.
