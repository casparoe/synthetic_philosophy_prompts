A friend of mine and I spent the better part of last night arguing about how much philosophical weight Harsanyi's aggregation theorem can bear. She holds that the theorem all but proves utilitarianism: if each individual's preferences over risky prospects satisfy the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms, and the social ranking does too while respecting the Pareto principle, then social welfare must be representable as a weighted sum of individual utilities — so anyone accepting such mild rationality assumptions is already committed to a utilitarian calculus. I pushed back with a version of Sen's objection: the theorem leaves the weights almost entirely free (nonnegative, but otherwise arbitrary), so it is just as compatible with wildly unequal weights as with equal ones, and even once the weights are fixed, nothing in the framework tells us that different people's utility units are commensurable — which is exactly the interpersonal comparison that summing utilities presupposes. Neither of us budged. Write an essay that adjudicates the dispute on its merits: state precisely what the theorem does and does not entail, weigh the strongest version of each side, and reach a defended verdict on whether it gives us good reason to accept some form of utilitarianism. A qualified verdict is fine, but don't retreat to "reasonable people can disagree."
