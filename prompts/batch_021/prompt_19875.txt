I've spent a long time teaching myself metaethics from whatever papers and articles I can track down, and I keep getting stuck on Sharon Street's evolutionary argument against moral realism. As I understand it, the realist faces a dilemma: either claim that natural selection favored our evaluative attitudes because they were true, which seems implausible, since fitness and independent moral truth don't obviously travel together the way fitness and accurate beliefs about predators do, or grant that it's a massive coincidence that our evolved attitudes match the independent moral facts, which undermines our right to trust those attitudes in the first place. What I can't get straight is how the "third-factor" replies to this dilemma are actually supposed to work, rather than just asserting that realism is true. My rough understanding is that the move involves pointing to some further, more general normative fact -- something like survival or flourishing being good -- that would explain both why evolution favored the relevant attitudes and why those attitudes are true, without either one causing the other. Could you explain how that's meant to block the dilemma, and then explain why several critics think it doesn't really succeed, but just relocates the coincidence onto the question of why that further, more general fact should itself line up with whatever evolution happened to select for?
