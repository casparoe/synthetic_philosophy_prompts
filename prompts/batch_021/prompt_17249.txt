I am writing a novel whose protagonist, a political philosopher, has built her career on a rigorous, textually faithful reading of Spinoza's political writings. In the scene I am drafting, a parliamentary committee asks her to respond to a proposal that would replace the elected legislature with continuous binding referenda: citizens voting directly on each bill through an app. I do not want the easy answer — "Spinoza liked democracy, so he'd like more of it" — but one grounded in his actual commitments: that natural right is simply coextensive with power (potentia); that fear and hope spread through a population by what he calls the imitation of the affects; and that he consistently prefers large deliberating councils to solitary rulers, because it is harder to move a whole body to something foolish or wicked than to move one man. Would he read these constant, app-mediated plebiscites as democracy finally achieved, or as the multitude's affects loosed from the very deliberative structure his argument for democracy depended on — and how should she answer when someone in the room notes that his own unfinished Tractatus Politicus breaks off in the middle of explaining why women specifically should not be citizens at all? Please work this out in Spinoza's own terms, rather than in the language of later liberalism.
