During training my supervisor explained the rule of thumb: if the whole genome comes back roughly 95% identical — something called "average nucleotide identity" — to a reference genome, we call it the same species, and below that cutoff it's a different one. I asked where the 95% actually came from and she basically said it's close to where the older lab methods used to draw the line, so it just stuck around. That felt so weirdly arbitrary to me. Back in school the definition of species I learned was "things that can interbreed and produce fertile offspring" (like how a horse and donkey can mate but the mule they get is sterile, so the parents count as different species) — but that whole idea is useless for bacteria, since they just split in two to reproduce and will happily swap genes with totally unrelated bacteria whenever it benefits them.

I brought it up with a coworker at lunch and he just shrugged and said "species isn't really a real thing for bacteria, its just a filing system we impose on them for our own convenience, don't overthink it." Something about that answer bugs me though. It feels like there should be some actual principled fact about when two bacterial populations count as meaningfully different kinds of thing, not just a percentage someone picked because it happened to line up with the old methods. So who's right here, me or him — is this genuinely just an arbitrary human bookkeeping decision, or is there a real, defensible line to draw?
