Then explain the role of vyāpti in Nyāya's theory of inference, illustrating both the structure of the five-membered syllogism (pañcāvayava) and one classic hetvābhāsa (fallacy of the reason)."

Here is the student's answer:

"Nyāya recognizes four pramāṇas — pratyakṣa, anumāna, upamāna, and śabda — and is in this respect more permissive than the Cārvākas, who admit only perception, and the Buddhist logicians Dignāga and Dharmakīrti, who admit only perception and inference. It is, however, more austere than the two branches of Mīmāṃsā: the Prābhākaras go furthest, adding both arthāpatti (postulation) and anupalabdhi (non-apprehension) to reach six pramāṇas, while Kumārila's Bhāṭṭa school stops one short of that, at five, admitting arthāpatti alone. Naiyāyikas reject both additions, holding that whatever legitimate cognitive work they do can be reconstructed as an implicit inference.

For Nyāya, an inference is sound only when the hetu (reason) stands in a relation of vyāpti, or invariable concomitance, with the sādhya (property to be proved), as smoke is invariably concomitant with fire. This is displayed in the udāharaṇa, or example-step, of the five-membered syllogism: (1) pratijñā — 'the hill has fire'; (2) hetu — 'because it has smoke'; (3) udāharaṇa — 'wherever there is smoke there is fire, as in a kitchen'; (4) upanaya — 'the hill has smoke'; (5) nigamana — 'therefore the hill has fire.' An inference such as 'the hill has fire because it is knowable' fails, since knowability has no real connection to fire; Naiyāyikas classify this as asiddha, the 'unestablished reason,' since a property like knowability could never be sufficient to establish the sādhya in the first place."
