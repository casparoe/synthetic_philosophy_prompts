I'm a second-year PhD student in philosophy, and this fall I'll be teaching my own discussion sections for our department's introductory symbolic logic course for the first time. The course leans heavily on Frege, both for the historical run-up to quantification theory and for the sense/reference distinction we cover before moving on to Russell and Tarski. Over the summer, while doing background reading for my dissertation prospectus (I work on the context principle and Frege's philosophy of arithmetic), I kept running into discussions of the private diary Frege kept in 1924, the year before he died, in which he apparently recorded virulently anti-Semitic, anti-parliamentary, and anti-democratic views, including something close to a wish for an authoritarian strongman to sweep away the Weimar Republic. From what I can tell, this was enough to visibly unsettle even Michael Dummett, the philosopher most responsible for rehabilitating Frege's reputation over the twentieth century.

I mentioned this to a couple of people in my cohort and got very different reactions, ranging from "that's just irrelevant biography, the logic stands or falls on its own" to "you can't so neatly quarantine a thinker's ideas from the person who produced them." Neither response fully satisfies me, and the case feels importantly different from the more familiar Heidegger controversy, since Frege's politics, as far as anyone has shown, never surface inside the technical papers themselves the way Heidegger's arguably do in Being and Time. If anything, the puzzle runs the other way for me: Frege spent enormous intellectual energy, especially in essays like "Der Gedanke," arguing for a "third realm" of objective thoughts entirely independent of the psychology, culture, or circumstances of whoever happens to be entertaining them, and I can't decide whether that impulse toward purity and rigor is simply beside the point here, or is in some tension with the diary, or is even quietly of a piece with it.

Before I settle on how to think about this myself, let alone how to handle it in front of a room of twenty-year-olds meeting the concept-script for the first time, I'd like a clearer sense of what's actually at stake. Could you describe the most important implications of the diary's contents for how philosophers should weigh this kind of biographical revelation against the standing of someone's technical work, and for what, concretely, I owe my students by way of context when I introduce them to Frege this term?
