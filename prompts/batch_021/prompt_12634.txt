Been down a bit of a rabbit hole this week — I'm working on a piece about how thinkers across history have talked about the relationship between law and raw power, and I keep circling back to that fragment from Pascal's Pensees where he says, more or less, that justice without force is powerless and force without justice is tyranny, and since people couldn't manage to make what's just also strong, they just went ahead and made whatever's strong count as just instead. Every time I reread it I think "wait, isnt this basically just Thrasymachus?" — you know, the guy in Book I of the Republic who tells Socrates that justice is nothing but the advantage of the stronger, that rulers make laws for their own benefit and then slap the label "just" on obedience to them. On the surface both seem to be cynically reducing justice to whoever's got the bigger stick.

A philosopher friend of mine insists they're actually doing pretty different things though — something about Pascal coming at it from his whole fallen-reason, we-can't-really-know-anything-for-certain angle, versus Thrasymachus just being a blunter sophist about power politics — and I want to get this right before it goes to print. Could you actually dig into a real comparison of the two positions for me? Specifically I'm curious whether they land in the same place for totally different reasons, and whether one of them (unlike the other) leaves room for some "true" justice existing out there that we're just too weak or corrupted to enforce, versus justice simply BEING whatever the powerful decide, full stop. Don't give me the soundbite version, I'd rather you get into the weeds on where exactly they diverge, if they do.
