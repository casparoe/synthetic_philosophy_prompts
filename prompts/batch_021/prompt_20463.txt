I'm a backend engineer at a logistics startup, and a genuinely strange argument has been circulating on my team for a couple of weeks now. Our ticketing system requires every ticket to be tagged, the moment it's filed, with a mandatory field called "Will Ship This Sprint," set to true or false, even though the sprint hasn't started and nobody can possibly know the answer yet. One of our product managers defends the policy on the grounds that the ticket will either ship this sprint or it won't -- there's no third option -- so one of those two claims has to be true right now, whether or not anyone is in a position to know which. On her view, tagging a ticket "unknown" instead of committing to true or false doesn't capture some real gap in the world; it only reports our ignorance, the same way saying "I don't know whether it's raining in Lagos right now" doesn't mean the weather over Lagos is somehow unsettled.

One of our engineers finds this quietly disturbing, for a different reason. If the PM is right that it is already true or false today whether the ticket ships, he argues, then nothing that happens between now and the end of the sprint -- the review that catches a blocking bug, the call to cut scope on Thursday, the outage that eats two days of someone's time -- can really be what makes the ticket ship or not, since the outcome was apparently fixed before any of that happened. On this view, sprint planning would be a ceremony in which we uncover a fact rather than help bring one about, and even the small side bet a few of us run each sprint on which tickets will slip would just be a wager on something already settled rather than a bet on an open question. The PM thinks this doesn't follow at all, and that he's smuggling in an extra assumption to get from "it's true in advance" to "nothing we do matters."

I have a computer science background, not a philosophy one, so I can tell something is off in this exchange without being able to locate exactly where. Is the PM right that a claim about the ticket's fate already has a definite answer today, even though nobody knows it yet? And if she is right about that, does the engineer's worry -- that none of our work during the sprint would really be what makes the outcome happen -- actually follow, or is he sneaking in a further assumption that she is free to reject? I'd like an honest verdict on who is arguing better here, not a diplomatic "both sides have a point."
