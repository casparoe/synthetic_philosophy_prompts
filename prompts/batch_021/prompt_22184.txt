A friend of mine who does AI alignment research was walking me through some bargaining theory over coffee last week, and she brought up something called the "commitment races problem" as one of the open issues in multi-agent AI safety. As I understood her explanation, it's basically an extension of that classic Schelling insight that in a standoff, whoever visibly commits first -- burns the bridge, throws the steering wheel out the window like in a game of chicken, builds the doomsday device -- wins, because the other side is left with no rational choice but to back down. So the "race" part is basically just the AI-era version of the arms race: whichever AI system (or whichever lab building one) locks in its demands or values first and most irreversibly comes out ahead in any future negotiation with rivals.

If I've got that right though, I'm confused why alignment researchers treat this as a problem to be solved rather than a strategy to be pursued. Shouldn't we want the safety-conscious labs to get their AI's committed to good values as early and as irreversibly as possible, before some reckless competitor locks in something worse? Seems like the sensible move is to just win the commitment race rather than figure out how to avoid it -- so what am I missing?
