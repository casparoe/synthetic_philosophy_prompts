I work in software and have no academic background in philosophy, but for the past year or so most of my spare reading has gone into interpretability research: the effort to work out what is actually happening inside large language models. Somewhere in the middle of that reading I got stuck on a philosophical question I can't quite shake, and I've written up my thinking as the short essay below. I'd be grateful for your comments, mainly on whether the central argument actually holds together or whether I'm quietly equivocating on "representation" somewhere without noticing, but also just on whether this is an interesting question at all or a well-trodden dead end I haven't run into yet.

**Detectors all the way down?**

Interpretability researchers have gotten quite good, over the last couple of years, at finding directions inside a language model's activations that correspond to specific, nameable concepts. Individual neurons mostly won't do this job; a given neuron tends to fire for a grab-bag of unrelated things, an effect usually blamed on the network packing more concepts into a layer than it has neurons to spare ("superposition"). The workaround that gets talked about most is dictionary learning with sparse autoencoders: train a second, wider network to re-describe a layer's activations as a sparse combination of "features," and a striking number of the resulting features turn out to be interpretable to a human. One fires on legal boilerplate, one on sycophantic praise, and one, in Anthropic's now-famous demonstration, on the Golden Gate Bridge. In that case the researchers did not just observe the feature; they turned it up. With the bridge direction clamped to an artificially high value, the model began bringing the bridge into nearly every answer, at one point describing itself as the bridge. That a single internal variable can be dialed up and reliably produce that behavior is offered, reasonably, as strong evidence that the feature is doing real causal work rather than merely correlating with bridge-talk after the fact.

What I can't work out is whether this causal evidence gets you all the way to what people actually mean when they say the model "represents" the Golden Gate Bridge, as opposed to merely detecting it, the way a smoke alarm detects smoke. A thermostat's bimetallic strip is reliably caused by temperature and reliably causes the furnace to switch off; nobody thinks it represents temperature in any interesting sense, even though the causal story is exactly analogous to the feature story, just shorter. I found my way, via an offhand reference in a blog post, to an older philosophy book that makes a version of this point about connectionist networks generally: Ramsey's *Representation Reconsidered*. A lot of what gets called "representation" in cognitive science, he argues, is really just this receptor-style causal relay, and the label only earns its keep when the internal item is used by the rest of the system the way a map or a model would be: as a stand-in that the system consults and that could in principle misrepresent, rather than a switch that merely gets flipped.

If that is right, then clamping and ablation, the whole causal toolkit interpretability relies on, look like exactly the kind of evidence a receptor notion of representation would also satisfy, since a receptor is by definition something whose manipulation changes downstream behavior in the right way. The toolkit seems well suited to showing that a feature is causally load-bearing, but not obviously suited to showing the further, more demanding thing: that it is consulted as a stand-in rather than merely triggered. I cannot tell whether any experiment, even in principle, could show that further thing, given that intervention is the only language we have for talking to these networks at all. Maybe the realists and the skeptics in this debate are not disagreeing about any fact that more interpretability work could settle, but are using "representation" in two different senses, and only one of them was ever something the causal toolkit could speak to.

I would rather hear where this goes wrong than have it politely agreed with. In particular: is the thermostat comparison actually fair to what interpretability researchers are claiming, or am I flattening a distinction they would insist on? And is the gap I am describing, between causal evidence and "real" representation, a known dead end in philosophy of mind with a standard answer I have simply not come across, or is it more open than that?
