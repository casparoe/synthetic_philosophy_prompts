So this happened in my graduate seminar on the epistemology of perception last week, and I want a second opinion because I couldn't fully settle it in class. We'd gone through the classic disjunctivist response to the argument from illusion -- Martin and Snowdon's line that a veridical perception and it's introspectively matching illusion (the stick that looks bent in the water, the coin that looks elliptical) don't share some common underlying mental state, so the argument's generalizing move -- from "in the bad case I'm not directly aware of the object" to "so I never am" -- doesn't have anywhere to get started. Then we read some of Pritchard's work on "epistemological disjunctivism," where he explicitly tries to hold onto the epistemic thesis (that in good cases your rational support for your perceptual belief is both factive and reflectively accessible to you) while officially staying neutral on that whole metaphysical question about the nature of experience.

Class split down the middle over whether this actually works. Half the room thinks Pritchard's helping himself to the interesting part of naive realism without paying for it, and that if he really stayed neutral on the metaphysics he'd have no explanation for why good and bad cases differ in epistemic standing at all. The other half thinks the epistemological and metaphysical questions really are separable, since the argument from illusion is at bottom an argument about justification, not about the deep nature of experience, so you don't need the full metaphysical package to block it. Can you write me something that actually argues for one of these and doesn't just lay out both sides -- does epistemological disjunctivism succeed in defusing the argument from illusion on its own terms, or is it quietly parasitic on the metaphysical thesis it claims not to need? I'd like it to deal honestly with the sharpest version of the objection (roughly: if hallucinating subjects only ever have non-factive support, on Pritchard's own view, what's doing the work of explaining that asymmetry if not something metaphysical?), and it doesn't need to be long -- a few tight paragraphs that commit to an answer beats a longer survey that doesn't.
