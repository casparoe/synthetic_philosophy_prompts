So, a bit of context: after a car nearly hit a kid outside my son's elementary school last spring, I ended up joining our neighborhood traffic safety committee (mostly out of guilt for not doing anything sooner, tbh). We've been pushing the city to redesign the intersection out front — basically a longer all-way pedestrian phase on the light so kids, older folks, and wheelchair users actually have enough time to get across before cars get the green again. Seemed like a slam dunk, until the city traffic engineer ran the numbers and now half the committee has quietly switched sides.

His argument goes like this: that intersection handles around 20,000 cars a day, and the new timing would add roughly 20 extra seconds of wait per car on average. Multiply that out and it comes to something like four and a half years of cumulative human time lost every single year, just sitting at a red light — versus, historically, maybe one serious pedestrian injury every five or six years at that same intersection. He's using some formula the state DOT apparently relies on for exactly this kind of tradeoff, one that puts a dollar value on time and a much bigger (but still finite) dollar value on a life, and by his math the "cost" of the delay blows the "benefit" of the safety fix out of the water. Not even close.

Everyone at the meeting kind of nodded along, because hey, the arithmetic checks out. But it still feels deeply wrong to me to weigh a huge pile of mildly annoyed commuters against one kid possibly dying, no matter how enormous you make the pile. I can't actually articulate why the math would be the wrong tool here though, if it even is. Is there a real flaw in his reasoning, or am I just letting emotion override a valid calculation because it happens to be my kids school? Curious how you'd untangle this one.
