I'm a novelist who eavesdrops on friends for material, and this argument from a dinner party a few weeks back is too good to waste, except I honestly can't tell who wins. Two friends of mine, Alina (a literary agent) and Jonah (an editor), have had a running bet since spring on whether our mutual friend Nate's novel, on submission right now, sells to a Big Five house by New Year's, and they keep trading updated percentages every couple weeks. Jonah finally said: "We've both been honest about our numbers this whole time, neither of us thinks the other's lying, and we're basically working off the same information at this point -- so by Aumann's agreement theorem we should've converged ages ago. If we haven't, one of us is kidding themselves." Alina said the theorem needs common priors, and theirs clearly aren't common -- she's placed a dozen debut literary novels, he's bought mostly commercial fiction, so of course they started from different places. Jonah said that's not what a common prior means in the theorem -- it means you'd have started in the same place given the same life experience, and if her placement history is doing the work here, that's just unshared evidence, which talking is supposed to wash out, not an excuse for a permanent gap. Alina said fine, but the theorem also needs common knowledge that the other's posterior really is what they claim, all the way up (I know you believe it, you know that I know it...), and in real life you never get further than a round of that. Jonah replied that they've been trading numbers for months, not once, and the whole point of the results building on Aumann's is that repeated exchange of posteriors should force convergence even without perfect common knowledge to start -- so a months-long gap is harder to explain away, not easier. Alina said that still assumes their flawless Bayesians with perfect memory of the whole exchange, when really they're just two tired people texting numbers between calls, and ordinary human sloppiness explains the gap just fine.

Can you go through this move by move, say who's got the better of each point -- not just who wins overall -- and flag anything you think both of them are missing about what the theorem actually licenses them to conclude here?
