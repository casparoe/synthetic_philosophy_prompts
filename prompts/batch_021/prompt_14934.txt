We did the standard tour -- Nagel's brain bisection essay, the classic Sperry/Gazzaniga case studies, Puccetti's old line that you were secretly two seperate people the whole time and the corpus callosum was just hiding it from you -- and then one of my students dug up some newer empirical stuff (I'm pretty sure its Pinto and colleagues, maybe ~2017?) showing split-brain patients actually integrate information across hemispheres way more than the classic "two totally separate streams" story would predict, at least for certain tasks. Now half the class thinks that basically sinks the two-minds reading and the other half thinks a good duality theorist can just absorb it as a minor wrinkle, and honestly I can't tell which side is being fairer to the other -- which is annoying, since this is more or less shaping up to be a chapter of my dissertation and I keep flip-flopping on it myself.

So could you write me a dialogue between two equally sharp philosophers of mind who run into each other at a conference right after a talk on that integration data, and end up hashing it out? One should defend a duality-style view (there are really two subjects of experience in there, before or after the surgery), the other a unity view (there's just one subject whose consciousness is weirdly, maybe intermittently, disunified, closer to the kind of thing Bayne and that crowd argue for). I don't want them to just re-run the 70s/80s debate at each other -- the newer behavioral evidence should actually force each of them to sharpen or slightly revise their position over the course of the conversation, and if one of them ends up ahead by the end, I want it to be because they made the better move in the actual exchange, not because you stacked the deck going in.
