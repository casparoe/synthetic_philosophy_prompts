Setup: a soldier named Judy is dropped into a war-games zone split into Red territory and Blue territory, each split again into an "HQ" half and a "Second Company" half -- four equally likely quadrants to start, 1/4 each. Command radios her: "can't tell if you've wandered into Red territory, but if you have, its 3-to-1 you're in their HQ area" -- then the radio dies. That message says literally nothing comparing Red to Blue, only how Red splits up internally. Richard Jeffrey has a famous rule for updating when evidence directly hands you new confidences over the options you care about, but this is a hedged, conditional claim about a sub-case that his rule doesn't obviously cover. The fix philosophers reached for -- pick the new probabilities that disturb your priors least, subject to that constraint (aka cross-entropy, or "infomin," updating) -- is supposed to extend Jeffrey's rule gracefully to cases like this. Except here it spits out something weird: Judy's confidence that she's safe in Blue rises, from 1/2 to about 0.53, purely from hearing how Red subdivides. (Apparently this traces back to an actual 1981 paper, inspired, of all things, by the Goldie Hawn movie Private Benjamin.)

Could you write me a dialogue between two philosophers hashing this out right as the radio cuts out, crouched over Judy's map? One defends the drift toward Blue as a real, if startling, requirement of updating honestly on evidence this messy. The other treats it as a reductio -- proof this whole strategy for patching Jeffrey conditionalization is broken, and something else is needed for cases like this. I want them actually landing blows on each other's specific reasoning, not just trading "that's counterintuitive," and don't let either one cave by the end -- honestly, nobody's fully settled this one.
