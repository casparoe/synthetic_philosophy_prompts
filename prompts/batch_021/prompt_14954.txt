I've spent the last few weeks reporting a feature on the rise of individualized risk scores in medicine and insurance — polygenic risk scores for heart disease, wearable-derived "biological age" calculators, underwriting algorithms that price a policy around a specific applicant rather than an age band. While preparing the piece, a biostatistician I interviewed brought up an old chestnut from the philosophy of probability called the reference class problem: the fact that any individual case belongs, at once, to indefinitely many reference classes. A given 54-year-old patient is simultaneously a "54-year-old," a "54-year-old smoker," a "54-year-old smoker with a family history of heart disease," and so on, and each of these classes can generate a different base-rate probability for the same future event, with no obviously principled way to decide which class's frequency counts as the patient's "real" probability. She mentioned that Reichenbach's classic fix — use the narrowest class for which reliable statistics are available — was something even he treated as a practical compromise rather than a genuine solution.

That got me wondering whether the puzzle, whatever its bite in Reichenbach's day, has essentially been overtaken by events. Today's individualized risk models don't have to settle for a handful of coarse, overlapping demographic buckets: they can condition on hundreds of a person's own measured traits at once, so that the reference class effectively shrinks down to something very close to the individual, and the old business of choosing among a few rival categories seems to fall away. Am I right that sufficiently granular, individualized data has essentially dissolved the reference class problem, turning it from a live conceptual puzzle into a solved, or at least solvable, engineering question? Or does something like the same puzzle reassert itself no matter how much data or algorithmic sophistication is brought to bear on a single case, just in a different guise?
