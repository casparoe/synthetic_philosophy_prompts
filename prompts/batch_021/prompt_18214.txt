(a) State the fairness criterion each side was implicitly relying on. (b) Using the relevant formal impossibility result(s), explain why a risk tool generally can't satisfy both criteria at once when the two groups have different base rates for the outcome being predicted. And here's the answer, word for word, just anonymized:

"ProPublica's charge, in the Machine Bias piece, was essentially that COMPAS violated equalized odds: black defendants who didn't reoffend were flagged high-risk at a much higher rate than white defendants who didn't reoffend (a higher false positive rate for black defendants), while white defendants who did reoffend were more often flagged low-risk (a higher false negative rate for white defendants). Northpointe's counter, echoed later by researchers like Flores et al., was that COMPAS was well-calibrated: within each risk score bucket, black and white defendants reoffended at roughly the same rate, so the tool satisfied predictive parity.

This is exactly the tension formalized in the Kleinberg-Mullainathan-Raghavan/Chouldechova impossibility theorem, which proves that calibration and equalized false positive/negative rates across groups can't both hold at once unless the tool is sufficiently accurate or the two groups happen to share the same base rate of reoffending. Since black and white defendants in the COMPAS sample had different base rates, and no risk tool is anywhere near perfect, ProPublica and Northpointe were bound to find different things wrong with the same tool — neither one made an error, calibration and equal error rates are just mathematically incompatible once base rates diverge.

I don't think this makes 'fairness' incoherent though. It shows fairness isn't one thing: calibration and equalized odds are both intuitively fairness-relevant, they just can't be jointly satisfied outside special cases, so which one you prioritize ends up being a substantive ethical choice about which kind of error (wrongly flagging someone as dangerous vs. wrongly clearing them) is worse to load onto a given group — not a technical question that better statistics could resolve."
