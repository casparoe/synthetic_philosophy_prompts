I'm this year's valedictorian, and I have about six minutes at graduation to say something to my class before we all scatter. This past year I did a lot of Lincoln-Douglas debate, and the topics kept pulling me into the literature on political obligation -- especially A. John Simmons's argument that none of the usual justifications (actual or hypothetical consent, the principle of fair play, gratitude, Rawls's natural duty of justice) actually succeed in grounding a general moral duty to obey the law simply because it is the law. Simmons calls the resulting position "philosophical anarchism," and he's careful to distinguish it from any call to revolt: you can accept that the state lacks the standing to demand obedience as such while still having plenty of ordinary reasons -- moral, prudential, communal -- to comply with most laws most of the time. I also worked through the sections of A Theory of Justice where Rawls lays out his much stricter conditions for when civil disobedience specifically is justified: it has to be nonviolent, public, a last resort after legal channels have failed, addressed to the community's shared sense of justice rather than to narrow self-interest, and undertaken with a willingness to accept the legal penalty.

When I ran the idea by my AP Government teacher, she said it might be too heavy for a graduation crowd, but I'd rather try and fail than get up and give the standard version of this speech -- "go out into the world, vote, obey the law, be good citizens" -- when I no longer think that argument actually works. At the same time, the school board and a lot of parents will be sitting right there, and I don't want six minutes that sounds like I'm handing three hundred eighteen-year-olds an excuse to ignore whatever rules are inconvenient to them. Could you draft the speech for me? I want it to take the philosophical anarchist conclusion seriously -- that there is no free-standing duty to obey a law just because it is a law -- and still arrive, honestly rather than by sleight of hand, at something worth telling my classmates about what they do owe each other and their communities, including where something like civil disobedience fits once you've given up the idea that breaking a law is always presumptively wrong. It should still sound like a graduation speech and not a philosophy lecture, and it has to work for the people in the audience who have never heard of Rawls or Simmons as well as for the two or three who have.
