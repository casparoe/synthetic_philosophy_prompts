Since retiring eighteen months ago after nearly forty years as a field ecologist, I have finally had the leisure to work through some of the philosophy I always set aside for "later," and I recently finished Cicero's De Senectute. I was struck by how Cato structures his defense of old age around four standard complaints: that it withdraws us from active pursuits, weakens the body, deprives us of pleasure, and brings us close to death. It is the reply to the first complaint that I keep returning to. Cato argues that the great business of the world — governing a state, commanding an army, steering public affairs — has never really depended on bodily strength or speed, but on judgment, deliberation, and the kind of authority that only accumulates with experience; on this view, old age does not so much lose the capacity for consequential action as change the form that action takes.

This argument lands rather close to home, since my own career consisted almost entirely of things I can no longer do myself — long expeditions, physically demanding survey work, hands-on laboratory analysis. These days, former students and junior colleagues still occasionally seek my opinion on a study design or a puzzling result, and Cato's argument offers a flattering description of that as a continuation of "real" contribution rather than a consolation prize for irrelevance. Yet I suspect the argument may be quietly circular: it seems to define away the loss by simply relabeling whichever capacities happen to remain to the old as the ones that "really" matter, so that old age comes out ahead almost by stipulation. I would be grateful if you could set out the most important implications of Cato's argument — both what is genuinely right about it and where, if anywhere, this circularity worry bites — for how I should regard my own shift from doing ecology to merely advising on it.
