As my newsroom's science correspondent, I also sit on its editorial standards committee, which is drafting guidance on how our reporters may describe studies of the effect of race on outcomes such as loan denial, disease risk, or sentencing length—studies that rely on statistical adjustment rather than randomized experiments. One camp wants a blanket rule against causal language ('associated with,' never 'causes'), since no study can change a person's race while holding everything else about them fixed, and it's doubtful the relevant counterfactual is even well defined. A second camp argues that the same standard, applied consistently, would also bar causal claims we make without hesitation (age causes cognitive decline, smoking causes cancer), and that it quietly relabels documented discrimination as mere correlation. A third wants to permit causal language only when a study manipulates something concrete and assignable, such as perceived race signaled on a resume or rental application, rather than race 'itself.' Please draft the committee's short guidance memo, due before next month's style-guide revision: give reporters a rule they can actually apply, and engage honestly with the strongest objection to whatever you recommend.
