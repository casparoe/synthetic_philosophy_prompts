At work, I've been building an internal coding agent that has to repeatedly decide, at various points while working on a task, whether to keep digging into a problem (running more tests, reading more of the codebase) or stop and commit a fix. Because we aggressively summarize and drop older tool calls to keep the prompt short, the model sometimes genuinely can't tell, at the moment it's making that stop-or-continue call, whether this is the first time it has hit this exact decision on the task or whether it already hit an identical-looking decision earlier, chose to keep digging, and has now simply looped back to what looks like the same juncture again. In a handful of production traces, this seems to be why the agent sometimes bails out far too early and other times never converges at all.

A teammate who minored in philosophy said this is basically a live version of something called the "absent-minded driver problem" from decision theory, where an agent can't tell whether it's facing the first or second occurrence of an identical choice point, and where the "correct" probability of stopping turns out to be computable in more than one defensible way, with those ways not obviously agreeing with each other. I don't know the field well enough to do much with that observation on my own, but it feels like more than a cute analogy — like it might actually bear on how we should configure the agent. Could you walk me through the most important implications of the absent-minded driver problem for a situation like this? I'm especially interested in whether it even makes sense to talk about a single "correct" stopping probability for the agent to use at each such decision point, and what, concretely, we should take away from this for how we design its decision procedure.
