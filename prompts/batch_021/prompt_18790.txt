For the past year I have helped run a small mutual-aid fund with two other volunteers. We approve requests for emergency help only when two conditions both hold: the need is genuine and unavoidable, and the amount asked for is modest enough that it will not strain our ability to help others later. Each of us judges both conditions separately for every case that comes before us.

On a recent request, I judged both conditions satisfied. One colleague judged only the first satisfied, and the other judged only the second. When we voted on each condition on its own and then applied our rule to the two majority verdicts, both conditions passed two-to-one, so the request qualified. Yet when we instead simply asked each other for a plain yes-or-no on funding the request itself, I was the lone "yes," and it failed two-to-one.

Both ways of reaching a verdict seem entirely reasonable on their own, yet they reversed each other using the very same three honest sets of opinions, and this was not the first time it happened. I suspect this is some kind of recognized trap that small groups fall into, but I do not know what it is called or whether there is a principled way out of it. What is really going on here, and which method should we trust from now on?
