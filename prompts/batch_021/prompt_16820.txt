This semester I'm taking a seminar on Kant's moral philosophy, and this week's session focused on his surprisingly thin treatment of non-human animals. As I understand it, Kant holds that only rational beings capable of setting their own ends count as ends in themselves, so animals—lacking self-consciousness and autonomy—cannot be wronged directly. Whatever duties we have "with regard to" animals are therefore indirect duties to ourselves: mistreating them is wrong not because it wrongs them, but because it dulls the very sympathetic feelings we rely on to properly discharge our direct duties toward other rational beings. Kant's own illustration is telling: a man who has his old dog shot once it can no longer work does no wrong to the dog, which has no standing to be wronged, but he does wrong himself, by coarsening a disposition that morality toward other humans depends on.

For my response paper, I'd rather stress-test this view than just summarize it. Could you write me a short story (roughly 700-900 words) in which a character who reasons explicitly along these Kantian lines is placed in a situation that puts real pressure on the line Kant draws between direct and indirect duty? I want the character's reasoning to stay recognizably Kantian—invoking rational agency, self-consciousness, and the capacity to set ends, rather than sliding into a generic "animals deserve kindness" sentiment—and I'd like it to be clear by the end exactly which of Kant's assumptions is doing the work in generating whatever tension or discomfort arises.
