So for my history of science seminar on the Scientific Revolution we've been slogging through bits of Kepler's Tertius Interveniens, and it's kind of wrecking my mental image of the guy. I always figured he either "believed in astrology" or "just did horoscopes for cash because he was the Imperial Mathematician," but he actually draws this weirdly specific line: he trashes the zodiac signs, the houses, and planetary rulerships as basically made-up nonsense with no real foundation, but he's dead serious that the aspects (the angles the planets make to each other) are physically real, because they're tied into his whole harmonic/geometry theory of the universe - like the earth has a kind of soul that responds to certain angle-ratios the same way our ear responds to a pleasing musical interval. He even invented a couple of brand new aspects because the harmonics demanded them, which I definately did not expect from a guy I'd only ever heard of for the laws of planetary motion.

This got me and my roommate arguing last night about what Kepler would've made of two things that happened to astrology way after he died, obviously none of which existed in 1630. On one side you've got the 20th century "no really, this is testable" camp: Gauquelin's Mars effect claiming a real statistical link between planet positions and eminent athletes, and then Shawn Carlson's big double-blind study in Nature in '85 where professional astrologers straight up failed to match birth charts to the right personality profiles better than chance. On the other side you've got the Jung-and-Rudhyar-descended "humanistic" astrology that gave up on causal claims entirely and treats charts as symbolic/archetypal tools for self-reflection (Jung's "synchronicity," meaningful but non-causal coincidence), which basically makes it immune to that kind of test by design.

Given how insistent Kepler was on there being an actual mechanism behind the aspects, even a semi-mystical one, and how much contempt he had for astrology he considered arbitrary and groundless, which of these two descendants do you think he'd find more objectionable, and why? Would he give the Gauquelin/Carlson crowd credit for at least playing the same game as him - making a real, checkable causal claim, even though the checking mostly went against them - or would he see the Jungian move as an even bigger cop-out than the "vulgar" astrology he was already fighting? I'd like an answer that actually engages his reasoning about aspects vs. signs rather than just shrugging "he's a 17th century guy, who knows."
