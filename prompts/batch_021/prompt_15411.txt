I co-teach a small graduate seminar that pairs philosophy students with students from our university's machine learning lab, and this week's session on inverse reinforcement learning produced an exchange I'd like a neutral third party to adjudicate, since I mostly sat back and let it run without weighing in myself. The assigned reading included a paper by Armstrong and Mindermann arguing that a simplicity prior cannot, by itself, pin down an agent's reward function once you allow for the possibility that the agent's "planner" — whatever procedure turns its reward into actual behavior — might itself be biased or less than fully rational, paired with some classic material on radical interpretation. Owen, who is finishing a master's in machine learning, and Nadia, who is writing a dissertation on philosophy of mind, ended up going back and forth for a good twenty minutes, and I've tried to reconstruct their exchange faithfully below.

(1) Owen opened by claiming that inverse reinforcement learning cannot, even in principle, recover a unique account of what an agent wants from a complete record of what it does: for any observed behavior and any reward function whatsoever, you can always construct some planner that would turn that reward into exactly that behavior, so the inference from behavior to reward is underdetermined in a way that no amount of additional behavioral data can fix by itself. (2) Nadia replied that this isn't a special curse on machine learning at all but the belief-desire holism that Quine and Davidson already identified: any stretch of behavior can be rationalized by trading off which beliefs and which desires you attribute to the agent, yet we interpret each other's actions constantly because we rely on something like Davidson's principle of charity — a default assumption that the other party is roughly rational and roughly right about their situation — and there's no reason an artificial system couldn't be built around some analogous default. (3) Owen answered that charity is not a free-floating axiom: when an interpreter relies on it, they are trading on a huge amount of shared context — common embodiment, a shared environment, overlapping needs and history — that makes "assume they are basically rational and basically right" a well-supported default rather than an arbitrary stipulation, and a learning system has no such context for free. Somebody has to choose its rationality model by hand, and reasonable-looking choices come apart sharply on exactly the cases we most want to get right, such as addiction or manipulated preferences, so fixing the planner just is deciding the answer in advance for the cases that matter most. (4) Nadia responded that this only shows the problem is empirically hard and normatively loaded, not that it's hopeless in principle: we make exactly these calls about friends and family all the time and revise them as we get more and different kinds of evidence — what someone says on reflection, what they do once circumstances change — and nothing stops a learning system from doing the same with richer data, such as corrections or comparisons, rather than raw behavioral traces alone. "Radically indeterminate," she said, overstates a problem that mostly calls for being more careful about what evidence you use and what you assume going in.

Neither of them budged, and I honestly can't tell who has the better of it at each step, or whether they're each half-right in ways the other hasn't quite acknowledged.
